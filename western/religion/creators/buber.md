# Martin Buber

**1878 – 1965** · Austrian-born Israeli  
Born: Vienna · Died: Jerusalem  
Movements & eras: [20th-century theology & ecumenism](../movements/twentieth-century-theology.md)  

> Austrian-Jewish philosopher of dialogue, author of I and Thou, who also brought Hasidic tales to a wide modern readership.

## Biography

Buber grew up partly in Lemberg (Lviv) with his grandfather Solomon Buber, a scholar of midrash, studied philosophy and art history in Vienna, Berlin and elsewhere, and became active in cultural Zionism. He taught at Frankfurt until the Nazis barred him and emigrated in 1938 to Jerusalem, where he was a professor at the Hebrew University and advocated a binational Arab-Jewish state. With Franz Rosenzweig he made a new German translation of the Hebrew Bible.

In I and Thou (1923) Buber distinguished the 'I–It' relation of use and experience from the 'I–Thou' relation of genuine encounter, in which God, the 'eternal Thou', is also met. His retellings of Hasidic stories shaped modern perceptions of Hasidism, though scholars such as Gershom Scholem criticised them as selective. His thought influenced Christian as well as Jewish theology, education and psychotherapy.

### I and Thou (Ich und Du) (1923)

- **Date:** 1923
- **Type:** Philosophical-religious treatise
- **Where written / held:** Leipzig
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=I%20and%20Thou%20%28Ich%20und%20Du%29%20Martin%20Buber)

In three parts written in aphoristic prose, Buber argues that human existence is lived in two primary relations. In I–It, we experience and use things and people as objects; in I–Thou, we meet the other in the fullness of presence, in mutuality and without purpose. Such encounters with nature, with other people and with spiritual forms are fleeting, and every Thou becomes an It again, but in each we glimpse the eternal Thou, God, who can only be addressed, not expressed. The book influenced Jewish and Christian theologians, existentialist philosophers, educators and psychotherapists. It remains under copyright and is available in English translations by Ronald Gregor Smith (1937) and Walter Kaufmann (1970).

### Tales of the Hasidim (1947–1948 (English; German edition 1949))

- **Date:** 1947–1948 (English; German edition 1949)
- **Type:** Retold stories
- **Where written / held:** New York (Schocken) and Zurich
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tales%20of%20the%20Hasidim%20Martin%20Buber)

From his first books in 1906 onward, Buber retold the stories of the Hasidic rabbis, from the Baal Shem Tov to the masters of the 19th century, in polished German prose. The two-volume Tales of the Hasidim (The Early Masters and The Later Masters) brings together his mature collection, with introductions explaining the movement's spirit. Buber presented Hasidism as a joyful hallowing of everyday life. Scholars such as Gershom Scholem argued that he neglected its theological and legal teachings, but the books shaped the image of Hasidism for generations of readers.
