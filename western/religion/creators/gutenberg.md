# Johannes Gutenberg

**c. 1400 – 1468** · German  
Born: Mainz · Died: Mainz  
Movements & eras: [Protestant Reformation](../movements/reformation.md)  

> Goldsmith of Mainz who developed printing with movable metal type in Europe and printed the Gutenberg Bible.

## Biography

Johannes Gensfleisch zur Laden zum Gutenberg was born in Mainz into a patrician family and trained, it seems, as a metalworker. In the 1430s he was in Strasbourg, where lawsuits show him working on secret projects with partners. Back in Mainz by 1448, he borrowed money from the merchant Johann Fust to develop a printing workshop, and by about 1455 had completed the great Latin Bible. A lawsuit then gave Fust control of much of the equipment, which he ran with Peter Schöffer. Gutenberg continued printing on a smaller scale and in 1465 was given a pension by the archbishop of Mainz.

Gutenberg's achievement lay in combining a hand mould for casting uniform metal type, an oil-based ink and an adapted screw press. The new technology spread rapidly across Europe; within fifty years millions of books had been printed. It transformed religion as well as learning, making the Reformation's flood of pamphlets, Bibles and catechisms possible.

## Key texts & works

### Gutenberg Bible (c. 1454–1455)

- **Date:** c. 1454–1455
- **Type:** Printed book
- **Where written / held:** Copies in the Library of Congress, British Library, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Mainz Gutenberg Museum and elsewhere
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gutenberg%20Bible%20Johannes%20Gutenberg)

Gutenberg's workshop in Mainz printed some 180 copies of the Latin Bible, about three-quarters on paper and the rest on vellum, in two large volumes of 1,286 pages, with two columns of usually 42 lines, in a Gothic type imitating the formal script of manuscripts. Production took several years and involved about 300 type designs. A letter by Enea Silvio Piccolomini, later Pope Pius II, reports seeing sheets at Frankfurt in 1454, and the books were finished by 1455. Initials, rubrics and borders were added by hand after printing, so every copy is different. Forty-nine copies, complete or substantial, survive. The Bible demonstrated that printing could match the beauty of the finest manuscripts and opened the age of the printed book, which would transform the spread of religious ideas.
