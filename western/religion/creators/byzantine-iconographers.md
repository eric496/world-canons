# Byzantine icon painters and mosaicists

**c. 6th–15th century** · Byzantine  
Born: Constantinople and the Byzantine world · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Byzantine Orthodoxy](../movements/byzantine-orthodoxy.md)  

> The largely anonymous artists, many of them monks, who painted the icons and mosaics through which Orthodox Christians have prayed for fifteen centuries.

## Biography

In the Orthodox tradition an icon is not a mere picture but a window onto the holy person it depicts, and icon painting is spoken of as 'writing'. The earliest surviving panel icons, from the 6th century, are preserved at St Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai, which escaped the destruction of the Byzantine iconoclast period (726–787 and 815–843). After the restoration of icons, celebrated as the 'Triumph of Orthodoxy' in 843, artists in Constantinople and the provinces developed a stable repertoire of types and compositions.

Most icon painters did not sign their work, seeing themselves as transmitting a tradition rather than expressing themselves. Byzantine icons and mosaic techniques spread to the Balkans, Russia, Italy and the Caucasus, and influenced early Italian painting, from Cimabue and Duccio onward.

### Christ Pantocrator of Sinai (6th century)

[![Christ Pantocrator of Sinai](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Spas_vsederzhitel_sinay.jpg/960px-Spas_vsederzhitel_sinay.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spas_vsederzhitel_sinay.jpg)

<sub>Christ Pantocrator, 6th century, St Catherine's Monastery, Sinai Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spas_vsederzhitel_sinay.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 6th century
- **Type:** Icon (encaustic on panel)
- **Where written / held:** St Catherine's Monastery, Mount Sinai, Egypt
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Christ%20Pantocrator%20of%20Sinai%20Byzantine%20icon%20painters%20and%20mosaicists)

This icon, about 84 centimetres high, shows Christ as Pantocrator, 'ruler of all', holding a jewelled Gospel book and raising his right hand in blessing. It was painted in encaustic, pigment mixed with hot wax, a technique inherited from Roman Egyptian portraiture, probably in Constantinople in the 6th century and perhaps donated by the emperor Justinian, who founded the monastery. The two sides of Christ's face differ noticeably, which has been interpreted as expressing his two natures, divine and human. Because St Catherine's lay outside the Byzantine Empire during iconoclasm, its icons escaped the destruction of images, and this one, rediscovered under later overpainting in the 1960s, is the model for the Pantocrator image found in countless later icons and church domes.

### Theotokos of Vladimir (c. 1130)

[![Theotokos of Vladimir](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/Virgin_of_Vladimir.jpg/960px-Virgin_of_Vladimir.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Virgin_of_Vladimir.jpg)

<sub>The Theotokos of Vladimir, c. 1130 (Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Virgin_of_Vladimir.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1130
- **Type:** Icon (tempera on panel)
- **Where written / held:** State Tretyakov Gallery (Church of St Nicholas in Tolmachi), Moscow
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Theotokos%20of%20Vladimir%20Byzantine%20icon%20painters%20and%20mosaicists)

Painted in Constantinople around 1130, this icon was sent to Kievan Rus' and taken in 1155 by Prince Andrei Bogolyubsky to Vladimir, where it gave the icon its name. It shows the Virgin and Child cheek to cheek, a type called Eleousa ('tenderness' or 'mercy'), with Mary's sorrowful gaze seeming to foresee the Passion. Only the faces are original; the rest was repainted over the centuries. Brought to Moscow in 1395, it was credited with protecting the city from Tamerlane and other invaders and became a national palladium, used at coronations. After the Revolution it entered the Tretyakov Gallery and is today displayed in the gallery's church so that it may be both seen and venerated. It is regarded as one of the masterpieces of Byzantine painting.

### Deësis mosaic, Hagia Sophia (c. 1261)

[![Deësis mosaic, Hagia Sophia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Deesis_mosaic_Hagia_Sophia.jpg/1280px-Deesis_mosaic_Hagia_Sophia.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Deesis_mosaic_Hagia_Sophia.jpg)

<sub>The Deësis mosaic in the south gallery of Hagia Sophia, Istanbul Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Deesis_mosaic_Hagia_Sophia.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1261
- **Type:** Mosaic
- **Where written / held:** Hagia Sophia, Istanbul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=De%C3%ABsis%20mosaic%2C%20Hagia%20Sophia%20Byzantine%20icon%20painters%20and%20mosaicists)

The Deësis ('supplication') mosaic in the south gallery of Hagia Sophia shows Christ in majesty between the Virgin and John the Baptist, who turn toward him in prayer on behalf of humankind. It is usually dated to around 1261, after Byzantine forces recovered Constantinople from the Latin crusaders, though some scholars have suggested other dates. The faces are modelled with exceptionally fine tesserae and subtle gradations of colour, giving them a softness and humanity that anticipate developments in Italian painting. Much of the lower part has been lost. After 1453 Hagia Sophia became a mosque and the mosaics were covered; they were uncovered and restored in the 1930s, when the building became a museum, and remain visible in the gallery.
