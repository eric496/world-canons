# Anonymous early Christian writers and scribes

**c. 1st–6th century** · Early Christian  
Born: Roman Empire · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Early Christianity & the New Testament](../movements/early-christianity.md), [Church Fathers & the Councils](../movements/church-fathers.md)  

> The unnamed authors, copyists and illuminators who produced early Christian texts such as the Didache and the great biblical codices of late antiquity.

## Biography

Much of the early Christian written heritage has no known author. Church orders such as the Didache, apocryphal gospels such as the Gospel of Thomas, and the Coptic codices buried at Nag Hammadi were composed or compiled by writers whose names are lost. Likewise, the scribes who copied the great 4th- and 5th-century parchment Bibles, Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Alexandrinus, are anonymous, though palaeographers can distinguish several hands in each.

Early Christians adopted the codex, the bound book, much more quickly than their pagan neighbours, and their manuscripts are a key witness to how the Bible was transmitted and how the canon took shape. Rediscoveries in the 19th and 20th centuries, from Sinaiticus to Nag Hammadi, reshaped scholarship on the New Testament text and on the diversity of early Christianity.

### Didache (c. AD 50–120)

[![Didache](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d7/Didache.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Didache.png)

<sub>A page of the Didache Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Didache.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 50–120
- **Type:** Church order
- **Where written / held:** Probably Syria; the only complete text is a manuscript of 1056, Jerusalem
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Didache%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

The Didache is a short anonymous Greek text in sixteen chapters. It begins with the 'two ways' of life and death, an ethical teaching with Jewish roots, then gives instructions for baptism, preferably in running water, for fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays, for saying the Lord's Prayer three times a day, and for the eucharist, with prayers of thanksgiving over the cup and bread. It also regulates the treatment of travelling apostles and prophets and ends with warnings about the last days. It was known by name to early writers but lost until Philotheos Bryennios, a Greek Orthodox metropolitan, found it in 1873 in a manuscript in Constantinople, publishing it in 1883. Its date is debated, many scholars placing it around the end of the 1st century, and it is a key witness to Christian practice in the generation after the apostles.

### Gospel of Thomas (c. AD 60–200)

[![Gospel of Thomas](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/42/El_Evangelio_de_Tom%C3%A1s-Gospel_of_Thomas-_Codex_II_Manuscritos_de_Nag_Hammadi-The_Nag_Hammadi_manuscripts.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:El_Evangelio_de_Tom%C3%A1s-Gospel_of_Thomas-_Codex_II_Manuscritos_de_Nag_Hammadi-The_Nag_Hammadi_manuscripts.png)

<sub>The opening of the Gospel of Thomas in Nag Hammadi Codex II Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:El_Evangelio_de_Tom%C3%A1s-Gospel_of_Thomas-_Codex_II_Manuscritos_de_Nag_Hammadi-The_Nag_Hammadi_manuscripts.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 60–200
- **Type:** Apocryphal gospel (sayings collection)
- **Where written / held:** Coptic version in the Nag Hammadi codices, Coptic Museum, Cairo; Greek fragments from Oxyrhynchus
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gospel%20of%20Thomas%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

The Gospel of Thomas presents itself as the 'secret sayings which the living Jesus spoke and which Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down'. It has no narrative of Jesus's birth, miracles, death or resurrection, only sayings, many introduced simply with 'Jesus said'. About half have parallels in the synoptic Gospels, such as the parable of the sower, while others have a mystical cast, speaking of finding the kingdom within and of knowing oneself. Greek fragments found at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt in the 1890s were identified only after the complete Coptic text came to light at Nag Hammadi in 1945. Scholars sharply debate its date and relation to the canonical Gospels: some see it as preserving independent early traditions, others as a 2nd-century work dependent on them. It was not accepted into the New Testament.

### Codex Vaticanus (c. 300–350)

[![Codex Vaticanus](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e9/Codex_Vaticanus_B%2C_2Thess._3%2C11-18%2C_Hebr._1%2C1-2%2C2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Codex_Vaticanus_B%2C_2Thess._3%2C11-18%2C_Hebr._1%2C1-2%2C2.jpg)

<sub>Codex Vaticanus, the end of 2 Thessalonians and the beginning of Hebrews Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Codex_Vaticanus_B%2C_2Thess._3%2C11-18%2C_Hebr._1%2C1-2%2C2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 300–350
- **Type:** Manuscript (Greek Bible codex)
- **Where written / held:** Vatican Apostolic Library, Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Codex%20Vaticanus%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

Codex Vaticanus (Vat. gr. 1209) is a parchment codex written in three columns per page by two or three scribes, dated by palaeographers to the first half of the 4th century. It contains most of the Septuagint and the New Testament up to Hebrews 9:14; the end of the New Testament, including the pastoral letters and Revelation, is lost, and later hands supplied some missing parts. It is recorded in the Vatican Library's first catalogue of 1475. Scholars restricted from studying it for centuries came to recognise its exceptional textual quality. The New Testament text of Westcott and Hort (1881), and through it most modern critical editions and translations, relies heavily on Vaticanus together with Sinaiticus.

### Codex Sinaiticus (c. 330–360)

[![Codex Sinaiticus](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4a/Sinaiticus_text.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sinaiticus_text.jpg)

<sub>A page of Codex Sinaiticus Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sinaiticus_text.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 330–360
- **Type:** Manuscript (Greek Bible codex)
- **Where written / held:** British Library, London; with leaves in Leipzig, St Catherine's Monastery (Sinai) and St Petersburg
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Codex%20Sinaiticus%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

Codex Sinaiticus was written on parchment by several scribes in the mid-4th century, perhaps in Caesarea or Egypt, in four columns per page in Greek capitals. It originally contained the whole Septuagint Old Testament, of which about half survives, and the complete New Testament, together with the Epistle of Barnabas and part of the Shepherd of Hermas. It was kept for centuries at St Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai. The German scholar Constantin von Tischendorf took leaves from the monastery in 1844 and the bulk of the codex in 1859, which was then presented to the Russian tsar; the circumstances remain contested by the monastery. In 1933 the Soviet government sold it to the British Museum. It bears thousands of later corrections, a valuable record of how the text was revised. In 2009 the dispersed parts were digitally reunited online, and it is one of the most important witnesses to the text of the Bible.

### Nag Hammadi library (4th century AD (copies of texts from the 2nd–3rd centuries))

[![Nag Hammadi library](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bb/Nag_Hammadi_Codex_II.jpg/1280px-Nag_Hammadi_Codex_II.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nag_Hammadi_Codex_II.jpg)

<sub>Nag Hammadi Codex II (Coptic Museum, Cairo) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nag_Hammadi_Codex_II.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 4th century AD (copies of texts from the 2nd–3rd centuries)
- **Type:** Manuscripts (codices)
- **Where written / held:** Coptic Museum, Cairo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nag%20Hammadi%20library%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

In December 1945 farmers digging near the town of Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt found a jar containing thirteen papyrus codices bound in leather, with some fifty texts translated into Coptic from Greek. They include the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip, the Gospel of Truth, the Apocryphon of John and other works, many associated with the varied movements later grouped as Gnosticism, which taught salvation through secret knowledge and often distinguished the supreme God from a lower creator. The codices were probably copied in the 4th century and may have been buried by monks of a nearby Pachomian monastery, though this is debated. Before the discovery these movements were known mainly through their opponents, such as Irenaeus. The find, published fully in the 1970s, transformed scholarship on the diversity of early Christianity.

### Rossano Gospels (6th century)

[![Rossano Gospels](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/RossanoGospelsFolio8vChristBeforePilate.jpg/1280px-RossanoGospelsFolio8vChristBeforePilate.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:RossanoGospelsFolio8vChristBeforePilate.jpg)

<sub>Rossano Gospels, folio 8v: Christ before Pilate Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:RossanoGospelsFolio8vChristBeforePilate.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 6th century
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** Diocesan Museum, Rossano, Calabria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rossano%20Gospels%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

The Codex Purpureus Rossanensis contains the Gospels of Matthew and most of Mark in Greek, written in silver and gold ink on parchment dyed purple, a colour associated with imperial luxury. It was probably made in the eastern Mediterranean, perhaps Syria or Constantinople, in the 6th century. Its fourteen surviving full-page miniatures show scenes such as the raising of Lazarus, the entry into Jerusalem, the Last Supper, Christ before Pilate and the parable of the wise and foolish virgins, often accompanied by Old Testament prophets pointing to verses that foretell the events. It came to Rossano in southern Italy, perhaps with Greek monks, and was rediscovered in the cathedral sacristy in 1879. It is listed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register and is a key work for the history of Christian book illustration.

### Rabbula Gospels (586)

[![Rabbula Gospels](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b1/Rabbula_Gospels_%289v_%26_10r%3B_Four_Evangelists%29.jpg/1280px-Rabbula_Gospels_%289v_%26_10r%3B_Four_Evangelists%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rabbula_Gospels_%289v_%26_10r%3B_Four_Evangelists%29.jpg)

<sub>Rabbula Gospels, folios 9v–10r, with decorated canon tables Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rabbula_Gospels_%289v_%26_10r%3B_Four_Evangelists%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 586
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rabbula%20Gospels%20Anonymous%20early%20Christian%20writers%20and%20scribes)

According to its colophon, this Syriac Gospel book was completed in 586 by the scribe Rabbula at the monastery of St John of Beth Zagba in northern Mesopotamia. Its decorated canon tables, which concord parallel passages of the four Gospels, are framed by arcades with birds, plants and small scenes, and several full-page miniatures show the Ascension, Pentecost and the Crucifixion, with Christ wearing a long purple robe between the two thieves. Some scholars think the full-page miniatures were added from another manuscript. It has been in Florence since the 15th century. It is an important witness both to Syriac Christianity, one of the great branches of the early church, and to the formation of Christian iconography later shared by East and West.
