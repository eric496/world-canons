## Biography

Bernard was born into a noble family near Dijon and in 1113 joined the new, austere monastery of Cîteaux, bringing some thirty relatives and friends with him. In 1115 he was sent to found the abbey of Clairvaux, which he led until his death, and under his influence the Cistercian order spread across Europe. He intervened decisively in the papal schism of 1130, secured the condemnation of Peter Abelard, and in 1146 preached the Second Crusade, whose failure grieved him. He was canonised in 1174.

Bernard's eighty-six sermons on the Song of Songs and his treatise On Loving God are classics of medieval mysticism, describing the soul's love for God in the language of the bride and bridegroom. He shaped Cistercian architecture's plainness through his criticism of extravagant decoration in his Apologia, and his devotion to the Virgin Mary was so famous that Dante made him his last guide in Paradise. His support for the crusades and his tone in controversy are also part of his legacy; he did, however, intervene to stop the massacre of Jews in the Rhineland in 1146.

### On Loving God (De diligendo Deo) (c. 1126–1141)

- **Date:** c. 1126–1141
- **Type:** Treatise
- **Where written / held:** Clairvaux Abbey, Burgundy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Loving%20God%20%28De%20diligendo%20Deo%29%20Bernard%20of%20Clairvaux)

Asked by Cardinal Haimeric why and how God should be loved, Bernard replies that the reason for loving God is God himself, and the measure is to love without measure. He describes four degrees of love: loving oneself for one's own sake, loving God for one's own sake, loving God for God's sake, and finally loving oneself only for God's sake, a state rarely reached in this life. The treatise is one of the most accessible and widely read works of medieval spirituality.

### Sermons on the Song of Songs (1135–1153)

- **Date:** 1135–1153
- **Type:** Sermons
- **Where written / held:** Clairvaux Abbey, Burgundy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sermons%20on%20the%20Song%20of%20Songs%20Bernard%20of%20Clairvaux)

Bernard began these sermons for his monks in 1135 and was still writing them at his death, having reached only the beginning of the third chapter of the Song. Using the poem's language of kisses, longing and union, he describes the soul as the bride of Christ, the Word, and traces the stages of its love, from repentance to contemplation. The sermons were polished literary compositions rather than transcripts, written in rich, rhythmic Latin full of scriptural echoes. They became a major source for later bridal mysticism, including the women mystics of the 13th century, and for devotional language in Catholicism and, through Luther and Calvin who admired Bernard, in Protestantism.
