# Jerome

**c. 345 – 420** · Roman (Illyrian)  
Born: Stridon, Dalmatia · Died: Bethlehem  
Movements & eras: [Church Fathers & the Councils](../movements/church-fathers.md), [Monasticism](../movements/monasticism.md)  

> Scholar, ascetic and translator whose Latin translation of the Bible, the Vulgate, became the standard Bible of the Western church for over a thousand years.

## Biography

Eusebius Hieronymus was born at Stridon, on the borders of Dalmatia and Pannonia, and studied in Rome, where he was baptised. After a period as a hermit in the Syrian desert, where he learned Hebrew, he became secretary to Pope Damasus in Rome, who commissioned him to revise the Latin Gospels. His ascetic teaching attracted a circle of aristocratic Roman women, including Paula and her daughter Eustochium, who followed him to Bethlehem in 386. There he founded monasteries and spent his last thirty-four years translating, commenting and corresponding.

Jerome's insistence on translating the Old Testament from the Hebrew rather than the Greek Septuagint, which he called the 'Hebrew truth', was controversial in his time, notably with Augustine. His Latin Bible, later called the Vulgate, together with his commentaries and vivid, often combative letters, made him one of the four great Latin doctors of the church. Renaissance painters frequently showed him as a scholar in his study with a lion, or as a penitent in the wilderness.

## Key texts & works

### Vulgate (c. 382–405)

- **Date:** c. 382–405
- **Type:** Scripture (Latin translation)
- **Where written / held:** Rome and Bethlehem; standard text of the Latin Church
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Vulgate%20Jerome)

In 382 Pope Damasus asked Jerome to revise the existing Old Latin versions of the Gospels, which varied widely. Jerome went further: in Bethlehem he translated most of the Old Testament directly from the Hebrew between about 390 and 405, an innovation that some, including Augustine, at first opposed. The other New Testament books in the Vulgate were revised by other, unknown scholars, and some books, such as the Psalms in their liturgical form, came from his earlier revisions based on the Greek. The resulting collection, later called the versio vulgata, 'common version', gradually displaced other Latin texts, and the Carolingians produced careful editions. The Council of Trent declared it authentic in 1546, and an official edition, the Sixto-Clementine Vulgate, followed in 1592. Its Latin shaped the language of the liturgy, theology, and much medieval European literature and art.
