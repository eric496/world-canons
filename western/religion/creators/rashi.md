## Biography

Shlomo Yitzchaki, known by the acronym Rashi, was born in Troyes in Champagne and studied in the Rhineland academies of Mainz and Worms before returning home to found his own school around 1070. Tradition says he supported himself by viticulture. He had no sons, but his daughters married scholars and his grandsons, including Rabbi Samuel ben Meir (Rashbam) and Rabbenu Tam, became leaders of the Tosafist school of talmudic commentary. His last years saw the massacres of Rhineland Jewish communities during the First Crusade in 1096.

Rashi's commentaries explain the plain sense (peshat) of the text in clear, economical Hebrew, drawing selectively on rabbinic midrash and sometimes glossing words in Old French written in Hebrew letters, a valuable source for the history of the French language. His Torah commentary was the first dated Hebrew book ever printed (1475), and his Talmud commentary is printed on the inner margin of every standard edition. Christian scholars, notably Nicholas of Lyra, used his work, and through Lyra it reached Luther.

### Commentary on the Torah (c. 1070–1105)

- **Date:** c. 1070–1105
- **Type:** Biblical commentary
- **Where written / held:** Troyes, Champagne; first dated Hebrew printed book, Reggio di Calabria, 1475
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Torah%20Rashi%20%28Shlomo%20Yitzchaki%29)

Rashi explains the Torah phrase by phrase, clarifying difficult words, grammar and context and answering questions a careful reader would raise, while weaving in selected rabbinic midrash where it helps the plain sense. His comments are brief and clear, and he occasionally translates a word into Old French, written in Hebrew letters. The commentary became the standard companion to the Torah text, studied by children and scholars alike; the custom of studying 'Chumash with Rashi' persists. It has generated hundreds of super-commentaries. Printed in Reggio di Calabria in 1475, it was the first dated Hebrew book. In Latin translation and through Nicholas of Lyra, it influenced Christian exegesis and the Reformation Bibles.

### Commentary on the Talmud (late 11th century)

- **Date:** late 11th century
- **Type:** Talmudic commentary
- **Where written / held:** Troyes, Champagne; printed in the inner margin of every standard Talmud edition
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Talmud%20Rashi%20%28Shlomo%20Yitzchaki%29)

Rashi's commentary on the Babylonian Talmud explains the flow of the argument, defines terms, supplies the background and identifies speakers, enabling students without a teacher to follow the dense Aramaic discussions. It covers most of the tractates; for a few, the commentary was completed by his students and grandsons. Since Daniel Bomberg's Venice edition of 1520–23, it has been printed in the inner column of every standard Talmud page, opposite the Tosafot, the critical glosses of Rashi's successors. Its distinctive semi-cursive script, used for commentaries, is called 'Rashi script'. It is essential to traditional Talmud study.
