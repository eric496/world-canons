## Biography

Pascal was a mathematical prodigy, educated at home by his father; as a young man he built a calculating machine, experimented on the vacuum and laid foundations of probability theory with Fermat. His family came under the influence of Jansenism, an Augustinian movement in French Catholicism centred on the convent of Port-Royal, where his sister Jacqueline became a nun. On the night of 23 November 1654 he had an intense religious experience, recorded on a parchment, the 'Memorial', which he sewed into his coat. He then devoted himself largely to religious writing until his early death in 1662.

In the Provincial Letters (1656–57) he defended the Jansenist Antoine Arnauld and mocked the moral reasoning of some Jesuit theologians. His notes for an apology for the Christian religion, published after his death as the Pensées, contain his famous wager argument and reflections on human greatness and wretchedness. Pascal's insistence that the heart has its reasons has made him a touchstone for religious thinkers from Kierkegaard onward.

### Provincial Letters (1656–1657)

<sub>An early edition of Les Provinciales Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Blaise_Pascal_-_Les_Provinciales.djvu).</sub>

- **Date:** 1656–1657
- **Type:** Polemical letters
- **Where written / held:** Published clandestinely in Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Provincial%20Letters%20Blaise%20Pascal)

When the Sorbonne moved to censure the Jansenist theologian Antoine Arnauld in 1656, Pascal wrote, under the pseudonym Louis de Montalte, a series of letters supposedly from a provincial gentleman to a friend. The early letters mock the disputes over grace; later ones turn to Jesuit casuists, quoting their manuals to show how they excused lying, duelling and usury through subtle distinctions. The letters were immensely popular and placed on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1657. They are regarded as a founding masterpiece of modern French prose. Historians note that Pascal's selections were sometimes unfair to the Jesuits' intentions, but the work made 'casuistry' a byword.

### Pensées (1657–1662; published 1670)

[![Pensées](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/Pascal_-_Pens%C3%A9es%2C_2e_%C3%A9dition_G._Desprez%2C_1670.djvu/page1-500px-Pascal_-_Pens%C3%A9es%2C_2e_%C3%A9dition_G._Desprez%2C_1670.djvu.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pascal_-_Pens%C3%A9es%2C_2e_%C3%A9dition_G._Desprez%2C_1670.djvu)

<sub>An edition of the Pensées published by Guillaume Desprez, 1670 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pascal_-_Pens%C3%A9es%2C_2e_%C3%A9dition_G._Desprez%2C_1670.djvu).</sub>

- **Date:** 1657–1662; published 1670
- **Type:** Fragments of Christian apologetics
- **Where written / held:** Paris; original manuscript fragments in the Bibliothèque nationale de France
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Pens%C3%A9es%20Blaise%20Pascal)

At his death Pascal left almost a thousand notes, some a few words, some several pages, for an apology for the Christian religion. His friends at Port-Royal published a selection in 1670, and the order of the fragments has been debated by editors ever since. Pascal describes the human condition as a mix of greatness and wretchedness, the human being as 'a reed, the feeblest thing in nature, but a thinking reed', and the restless diversions by which people avoid thinking of death. The famous wager argues that, since reason cannot decide whether God exists, one should bet on God, for if he exists the gain is infinite. Other fragments contrast the 'esprit de géométrie' with the 'esprit de finesse', and declare that 'the heart has its reasons which reason does not know'. The Pensées are a classic of French literature and of religious thought.
