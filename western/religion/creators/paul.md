# Paul the Apostle

<a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fresco_of_Saint_Paul_at_Ephesus.jpg"><img align="right" width="240" src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4a/Fresco_of_Saint_Paul_at_Ephesus.jpg" alt="Paul the Apostle" title="Saint Paul, fresco in the so-called Grotto of St Paul, Ephesus, c. 6th century"></a>

**c. AD 5 – c. 64–67** · Jewish, Roman citizen  
Born: Tarsus, Cilicia · Died: Rome  
Movements & eras: [Early Christianity & the New Testament](../movements/early-christianity.md)  

> Jewish Pharisee turned apostle of Christ to the gentiles, whose letters are the earliest Christian writings and the foundation of much Christian theology.

<sub>Portrait: Saint Paul, fresco in the so-called Grotto of St Paul, Ephesus, c. 6th century · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fresco_of_Saint_Paul_at_Ephesus.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Paul, originally called Saul, was born into a Jewish family in Tarsus in Asia Minor and, according to Acts, held Roman citizenship. He describes himself as a zealous Pharisee who persecuted the early followers of Jesus, until a vision of the risen Christ, traditionally placed on the road to Damascus, made him an apostle. For some two decades from the late 40s he travelled through Asia Minor, Greece and beyond, founding communities of largely gentile believers and writing to them. He was arrested in Jerusalem, taken to Rome, and according to early tradition was executed there under Nero in the mid-60s.

Thirteen New Testament letters bear Paul's name. Scholars broadly agree that seven are his own: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon, written roughly AD 50–60, while the authorship of the others is debated or widely doubted. In them Paul argues that gentiles may belong to God's people through faith in Christ without adopting the Jewish law, and develops ideas of grace, justification, the church as the body of Christ and the resurrection. His reading by Augustine, Luther and Barth made him decisive for Western theology, and his relation to Judaism remains a lively field of scholarship.

- **Date:** c. AD 48–55
- **Type:** Scripture (letter)
- **Where written / held:** Written to churches in Galatia, Asia Minor
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Epistle%20to%20the%20Galatians%20Paul%20the%20Apostle)

Galatians was written to churches in central Asia Minor that had been visited by teachers insisting that gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the Jewish law. Paul responds with an autobiographical account of his call and his dealings with the Jerusalem apostles, including a confrontation with Peter at Antioch, and argues that people are justified by faith, as Abraham was, so that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male and female. Its date is debated, some placing it among his earliest letters around 48 and others in the mid-50s. Luther called it his favourite letter, and it was central to Reformation debates about law and gospel.

### First Epistle to the Corinthians (c. AD 53–55)

[![First Epistle to the Corinthians](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/Ann_Arbor%2C_University_of_Michigan_P.Mich.inv._6238_%28Papyrus_46%29_fol._79v_1_Cor_2%2C_11%E2%80%933%2C_5.jpg/960px-Ann_Arbor%2C_University_of_Michigan_P.Mich.inv._6238_%28Papyrus_46%29_fol._79v_1_Cor_2%2C_11%E2%80%933%2C_5.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ann_Arbor%2C_University_of_Michigan_P.Mich.inv._6238_%28Papyrus_46%29_fol._79v_1_Cor_2%2C_11%E2%80%933%2C_5.jpg)

<sub>1 Corinthians 2–3 in Papyrus 46, c. 200 (University of Michigan) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ann_Arbor%2C_University_of_Michigan_P.Mich.inv._6238_%28Papyrus_46%29_fol._79v_1_Cor_2%2C_11%E2%80%933%2C_5.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 53–55
- **Type:** Scripture (letter)
- **Where written / held:** Written in Ephesus to the church in Corinth
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=First%20Epistle%20to%20the%20Corinthians%20Paul%20the%20Apostle)

Paul wrote this letter from Ephesus to the church he had founded in Corinth, a cosmopolitan Greek port, in response to reports of factions and to questions the Corinthians had sent him. He addresses rivalries among followers of different leaders, sexual morality, marriage and celibacy, eating food offered to idols, the conduct of worship and spiritual gifts such as speaking in tongues. Chapter 11 contains the earliest written account of the words of Jesus at the Last Supper, and chapter 15 lists witnesses of the risen Christ and argues for the bodily resurrection. Chapter 13, 'love is patient, love is kind', is among the best-known passages in the Bible. The letter offers a rare window into the life of an early Christian community. Some passages, notably on women keeping silent in church, are debated, with some scholars considering them later insertions.

### Epistle to the Romans (c. AD 57)

[![Epistle to the Romans](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e3/Uncial_0220_Rom-4.23-5.3.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Uncial_0220_Rom-4.23-5.3.jpg)

<sub>Uncial 0220, an early parchment leaf of Romans 4–5 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Uncial_0220_Rom-4.23-5.3.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 57
- **Type:** Scripture (letter)
- **Where written / held:** Written in Corinth to the church in Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Epistle%20to%20the%20Romans%20Paul%20the%20Apostle)

Paul wrote Romans, probably from Corinth around 57, to a church he had not founded, preparing for a visit on his way to Spain. He argues that all people, Jews and gentiles, have fallen short and are made righteous by God through faith in Christ, not by works of the law; that believers die and rise with Christ in baptism and live by the Spirit; and, in chapters 9–11, that God has not abandoned Israel, which will ultimately be saved. The letter ends with ethical exhortation and greetings to many named individuals, including women such as Phoebe and Junia. Romans was pivotal for Augustine's conversion and for Luther, Calvin and Barth. Since the 1970s the 'New Perspective on Paul' has argued that Paul was addressing the inclusion of gentiles more than individual guilt, a reading that remains debated.
