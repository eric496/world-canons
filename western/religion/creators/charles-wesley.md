# Charles Wesley

> Methodist preacher and the most prolific hymn writer in English, author of 'Hark! The Herald Angels Sing' and some six thousand other hymns.

<sub>Portrait: Portrait of Charles Wesley · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Wesley.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Charles Wesley, John's younger brother, founded the Oxford 'Holy Club', accompanied John to Georgia, and experienced his own evangelical conversion on 21 May 1738, three days before John. He preached as an itinerant for many years before settling in Bristol and then London. He was more attached than his brother to the Church of England and opposed steps that would separate the Methodists from it.

Charles wrote some six thousand hymns, giving the revival its voice and teaching its theology through song. Many, such as 'And Can It Be', 'Love Divine, All Loves Excelling' and 'Christ the Lord Is Risen Today', are sung in churches of many denominations. The Collection of Hymns for the Use of the People Called Methodists (1780), edited by John, contains mostly his work.

### Hark! The Herald Angels Sing (1739)

- **Date:** 1739
- **Type:** Hymn
- **Where written / held:** First published in Hymns and Sacred Poems, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hark%21%20The%20Herald%20Angels%20Sing%20Charles%20Wesley)

Charles Wesley's original hymn, published in 1739, began 'Hark how all the welkin rings'. George Whitefield altered the first lines in 1753 to the familiar version, and further changes followed. The hymn expresses the theology of the incarnation in compact verse, with lines on Christ 'veiled in flesh' and 'born that man no more may die'. In 1855 the English musician William H. Cummings adapted a melody from a cantata by Felix Mendelssohn to fit it, creating the tune now sung throughout the English-speaking world.

### A Collection of Hymns for the Use of the People Called Methodists (1780)

- **Date:** 1780
- **Type:** Hymnal
- **Where written / held:** London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Collection%20of%20Hymns%20for%20the%20Use%20of%20the%20People%20Called%20Methodists%20Charles%20Wesley)

In 1780 John Wesley gathered 525 hymns, the great majority by Charles, into a collection organised not by the church year but by the stages of Christian experience: exhortation to sinners, the pleasantness of religion, repentance, seeking for redemption, believers rejoicing, fighting, praying, watching, suffering and seeking full redemption. His preface called it 'a little body of experimental and practical divinity'. It includes hymns such as 'O for a thousand tongues to sing' and 'And can it be'. The collection shaped Methodist worship and theology, and through it the Wesleys' hymns entered the hymnals of many denominations.
