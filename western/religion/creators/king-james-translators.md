# Translators of the King James Bible

**1604–1611** · English  
Born: Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Puritanism & Pietism](../movements/puritanism-pietism.md)  

> The forty-seven scholars, organised in six companies, who produced the Authorised Version of the English Bible for James I.

## Biography

At the Hampton Court Conference of 1604, called to settle disputes between bishops and Puritans, King James I agreed to a new translation of the Bible. Some fifty scholars, of whom forty-seven are usually counted, were divided into six companies meeting at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge, each responsible for part of the text. They included Lancelot Andrewes, the Greek scholar John Bois and Puritans such as John Reynolds. They worked from the Hebrew and Greek, revising the Bishops' Bible and drawing heavily on Tyndale and the Geneva Bible.

Their translation, published in 1611, was slow to displace the Geneva Bible, but by the later 17th century it had become the Bible of the English-speaking world, a position it held for three centuries. Its rhythms and phrases deeply influenced English literature and speech.

- **Date:** 1611
- **Type:** Scripture (English translation)
- **Where written / held:** Printed by Robert Barker, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=King%20James%20Version%20%28Authorised%20Version%29%20Translators%20of%20the%20King%20James%20Bible)

Published in 1611 by the royal printer Robert Barker, the new translation was 'appointed to be read in churches'. Its translators worked from the Hebrew and Greek, with the Bishops' Bible as their base, and drew heavily on Tyndale and the Geneva Bible; their preface explains that they sought to make a good translation better. The first edition, a large folio, contains the Old and New Testaments and the Apocrypha. Early printings contained errors, and the text was standardised in the 18th century, most fully in the Oxford edition of 1769, which is the form usually read today. Although slow to replace the Geneva Bible, it became the Bible of English-speaking Protestants for three centuries, and its language profoundly influenced English literature, oratory and everyday speech, from 'the skin of my teeth' to 'a law unto themselves'.
