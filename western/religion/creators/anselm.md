## Biography

Anselm was born at Aosta in the Alps and, after a restless youth, came to the Norman abbey of Bec, drawn by its prior Lanfranc. He became a monk there in 1060, then prior and abbot. In 1093 he succeeded Lanfranc as archbishop of Canterbury. His insistence on the independence of the church brought him into conflict with the English kings William II and Henry I, especially over lay investiture, and he spent two periods in exile before an agreement was reached in 1107.

Anselm's motto, 'faith seeking understanding', expresses his aim of exploring by reason what is believed by faith. In the Proslogion he argued that God is 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived', an argument later called ontological and debated by philosophers ever since. In Cur Deus Homo he explained the incarnation as satisfaction for human sin owed to God's honour, a theory that shaped Western thinking about salvation. He was named a doctor of the church in 1720.

### Proslogion (1077–1078)

- **Date:** 1077–1078
- **Type:** Theological meditation
- **Where written / held:** Abbey of Bec, Normandy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Proslogion%20Anselm%20of%20Canterbury)

Written at Bec as a sequel to his Monologion, the Proslogion ('address') takes the form of a prayer seeking to understand what is believed, 'faith seeking understanding'. In its second chapter Anselm argues that God is 'something than which nothing greater can be thought'; since what exists in reality as well as in the mind is greater than what exists in the mind alone, such a being must exist. The monk Gaunilo replied that the same reasoning could prove the existence of a perfect island, and Anselm answered him; both texts circulated together. The argument was rejected by Aquinas, revived in different forms by Descartes and Leibniz, criticised by Kant, and is still debated by philosophers of religion.

### Cur Deus Homo (Why God Became Man) (1094–1098)

- **Date:** 1094–1098
- **Type:** Theological dialogue
- **Where written / held:** Canterbury and Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Cur%20Deus%20Homo%20%28Why%20God%20Became%20Man%29%20Anselm%20of%20Canterbury)

In this dialogue with his pupil Boso, Anselm argues 'leaving Christ out of view' that human sin dishonours God and requires satisfaction greater than anything humans can give, yet owed by humans; only one who is both God and man can make it, and Christ's freely offered death does so. The book rejected the older idea that Christ's death paid a ransom to the devil. Its 'satisfaction theory' strongly influenced medieval and Reformation teaching on atonement, including later theories of penal substitution, and has also been criticised, by Abelard in his own time and by modern theologians, for emphasising justice over love.
