# Israel ben Eliezer (the Baal Shem Tov)

**c. 1698 – 1760** · Polish-Lithuanian Jewish  
Born: Okopy, Podolia, Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth · Died: Medzhybizh, Podolia  
Movements & eras: [Evangelical revivals](../movements/evangelical-revivals.md)  

> Jewish mystic and healer in Podolia, revered as the founder of Hasidism; he left no writings of his own.

## Biography

Israel ben Eliezer was born in a small town in Podolia, now in Ukraine. Hagiographic tradition says he was orphaned young and lived in obscurity as a teacher's assistant and clay digger in the Carpathians before revealing himself around 1736 as a baal shem, a 'master of the name', a healer who used divine names and amulets. From about 1740 he lived in Medzhybizh, where a circle of disciples formed around him. Historical documents confirm his role as a recognised figure in the town, though many details of his life are known only from later stories.

The Baal Shem Tov taught that God is present everywhere, that joyful prayer and devotion (devekut) matter more than ascetic or scholarly achievement, and that simple people can draw close to God. His teachings survive through his disciples, notably Dov Ber of Mezeritch and Yaakov Yosef of Polonne. Hasidism spread rapidly across eastern Europe, meeting fierce opposition from the rabbinic establishment, and today forms a major part of Orthodox Judaism.

## Key texts & works

### Shivhei ha-Besht (In Praise of the Baal Shem Tov) (1814 (compiled c. 1790s))

- **Date:** 1814 (compiled c. 1790s)
- **Type:** Hagiographic tales
- **Where written / held:** First printed in Kopys, Belarus
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Shivhei%20ha-Besht%20%28In%20Praise%20of%20the%20Baal%20Shem%20Tov%29%20Israel%20ben%20Eliezer%20%28the%20Baal%20Shem%20Tov%29)

The Baal Shem Tov wrote no books. Shivhei ha-Besht, printed in Hebrew in 1814 and soon in Yiddish, collects some 250 stories about him gathered by Dov Ber of Linits, the son-in-law of the Baal Shem Tov's scribe, and edited by the printer Israel Jaffe. It tells of his hidden years, his revelation as a holy man, his miracles, healings and ascents of the soul, and his encounters with disciples, opponents and ordinary people. Historians use it with care, as legend mingles with memory, but recent research has shown that many figures in it are historical. It is a foundational work of Hasidic storytelling, which became a central genre of Hasidic culture and was later retold by Martin Buber and others.
