# Meister Eckhart

**c. 1260 – c. 1328** · German  
Born: Tambach, Thuringia (probably) · Died: Avignon or Cologne (probably)  
Movements & eras: [Medieval scholasticism & mysticism](../movements/scholasticism-mysticism.md), [Mendicant orders](../movements/mendicant-orders.md)  

> Dominican theologian and preacher whose daring German sermons on the birth of God in the soul made him the most celebrated of the medieval mystics.

## Biography

Eckhart von Hochheim joined the Dominicans at Erfurt as a young man and twice held the Dominican chair of theology at the University of Paris, an honour he shared only with Aquinas; hence the title Meister, 'Master'. He held senior offices in the order in Saxony and Bohemia and later preached and taught in Strasbourg and Cologne, often to communities of nuns and Beguines. In 1326 the archbishop of Cologne opened proceedings against him for heresy. He defended himself, appealed to the pope in Avignon, and died before the verdict. In 1329 Pope John XXII condemned twenty-eight propositions drawn from his works.

Eckhart wrote scholastic works in Latin but is best known for his German sermons, which speak of detachment, of the 'ground' of the soul where God and the soul are one, and of the birth of the Word in the soul. His followers Johannes Tauler and Henry Suso carried on his teaching more cautiously. Rediscovered in the 19th century, he has fascinated philosophers from Hegel to Heidegger as well as students of comparative mysticism; in 1992 the Dominican order stated that he had been rehabilitated, though no formal papal act has followed.

## Key texts & works

### German Sermons (c. 1300–1328)

- **Date:** c. 1300–1328
- **Type:** Sermons (Middle High German)
- **Where written / held:** Erfurt, Strasbourg and Cologne; preserved in many manuscripts
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=German%20Sermons%20Meister%20Eckhart)

Eckhart's German sermons, of which over a hundred are attributed to him, survive in notes and copies made by his hearers and followers rather than in his own edition, so their authenticity has had to be established by modern scholarship. Preached to Dominican nuns, Beguines and townspeople, they use paradox and striking images to speak of the soul's 'ground', where God is born, of 'detachment' (Abgeschiedenheit) from all created things and even from one's own will, and of a God beyond God, the silent desert of the Godhead. Some sentences were condemned by Pope John XXII in 1329, and Eckhart's followers preserved his teaching discreetly. Translated in the 19th and 20th centuries, the sermons have attracted philosophers, poets and readers interested in comparisons with Zen and Vedanta.
