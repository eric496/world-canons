## Biography

Cranmer was a Cambridge scholar who came to Henry VIII's notice by suggesting that the king's request to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon be put to university theologians. Made archbishop of Canterbury in 1533, he declared the marriage void and crowned Anne Boleyn. Under Henry he moved cautiously toward Protestantism, promoting the English Bible; under the young Edward VI (1547–53) he led a thorough Protestant reform of worship and doctrine. On the accession of the Catholic Mary I he was tried for heresy. He signed several recantations but, at his burning in Oxford in 1556, withdrew them and thrust first into the flames the hand that had signed them.

Cranmer's lasting monument is the liturgy of the Book of Common Prayer (1549 and 1552), whose cadenced English prose has shaped Anglican worship and the English language. He also drafted the Forty-two Articles, the basis of the Thirty-nine Articles of the Church of England.

## Key texts & works

### Book of Common Prayer (1549; revised 1552 and 1662)

[![Book of Common Prayer](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/21/Book_of_Common_Prayer%2C_1549_%282%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Book_of_Common_Prayer%2C_1549_%282%29.jpg)

<sub>The first Book of Common Prayer, 1549 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Book_of_Common_Prayer%2C_1549_%282%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1549; revised 1552 and 1662
- **Type:** Liturgy
- **Where written / held:** London; first printed by Edward Whitchurch and Richard Grafton, 1549
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Common%20Prayer%20Thomas%20Cranmer)

Under Edward VI, Cranmer replaced the various Latin service books of medieval England with a single book in English, combining services for morning and evening prayer, holy communion, baptism, marriage, burial and the church year, with a lectionary covering the Bible and the Psalter. The first Book of Common Prayer (1549) was conservative; the second (1552) was more firmly Protestant. Abolished under Mary I and restored under Elizabeth, it was revised in 1662, the version that remains a standard of the Church of England. Cranmer translated and adapted prayers from Latin sources and wrote new ones, in a cadenced English that gave the language phrases such as 'till death us do part' and 'ashes to ashes, dust to dust'. Versions of the prayer book shaped Anglican worship throughout the world.
