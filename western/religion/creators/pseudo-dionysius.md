# Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite

**fl. c. 500** · Syrian Greek  
Born: probably Syria · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Church Fathers & the Councils](../movements/church-fathers.md), [Byzantine Orthodoxy](../movements/byzantine-orthodoxy.md)  

> Anonymous Christian Neoplatonist writing under the name of a convert of St Paul, whose works on the divine names, hierarchies and mystical theology shaped medieval spirituality.

## Biography

A body of Greek writings, The Divine Names, The Mystical Theology, The Celestial Hierarchy, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy and ten letters, appeared in the early 6th century under the name of Dionysius the Areopagite, the Athenian converted by Paul in Acts 17. For a thousand years they were accepted as genuinely apostolic, giving them enormous authority. Renaissance scholars such as Lorenzo Valla and Erasmus raised doubts, and in 1895 two scholars independently showed that they depend on the 5th-century Neoplatonist Proclus. The author is now thought to have been a Syrian monk writing around 500, whose identity remains unknown.

The Dionysian corpus fused Christian theology with Neoplatonism. It describes God as beyond all names and being, approached by negation ('apophatic' theology) and ultimately in a 'divine darkness', and presents the angels and the church as ordered hierarchies through which divine light descends. Translated into Latin by John Scotus Eriugena in the 9th century, it influenced Aquinas, Eckhart, The Cloud of Unknowing and the Gothic theology of light, as well as Orthodox mystical theology.

### The Mystical Theology (c. 500)

- **Date:** c. 500
- **Type:** Mystical treatise
- **Where written / held:** Probably Syria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Mystical%20Theology%20Pseudo-Dionysius%20the%20Areopagite)

Only five short chapters long, The Mystical Theology begins with a prayer to the Trinity to lead the soul 'beyond unknowing' into the 'brilliant darkness' of a hidden silence. Using the image of Moses ascending Mount Sinai into the cloud, it teaches that the soul must leave behind sense and intellect to be united with God, who transcends every name, and that negations of what God is not are truer than affirmations. Accepted for a thousand years as the work of Paul's convert, it carried near-apostolic authority. Through the Latin translations of Eriugena and others it shaped medieval mysticism, including Bonaventure, Eckhart and the anonymous English Cloud of Unknowing, and Orthodox theology through Maximus the Confessor and Gregory Palamas.

### The Celestial Hierarchy (c. 500)

- **Date:** c. 500
- **Type:** Theological treatise
- **Where written / held:** Probably Syria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Celestial%20Hierarchy%20Pseudo-Dionysius%20the%20Areopagite)

The Celestial Hierarchy explains the biblical names of heavenly beings by arranging them into three ranks of three: seraphim, cherubim and thrones; dominions, powers and authorities; principalities, archangels and angels. Each rank receives divine illumination from the one above and passes it on below, so that the whole hierarchy draws creatures back toward God. Its companion, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, describes the church's sacraments and orders as reflecting the heavenly ones. The scheme was taken up by Gregory the Great, Aquinas and Dante, whose Paradiso follows it, and it shaped medieval art and ideas of order in church and society. Abbot Suger's Gothic rebuilding of Saint-Denis has often been linked to its theology of light, though the extent of that influence is debated.
