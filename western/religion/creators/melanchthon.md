## Biography

Philipp Schwartzerdt, who took the Greek form of his name, Melanchthon ('black earth'), was a great-nephew of the Hebrew scholar Johannes Reuchlin and a prodigy in classical languages. In 1518, aged twenty-one, he became professor of Greek at Wittenberg, where he became Luther's friend and ally. He reorganised schools and universities across Protestant Germany, earning the title 'teacher of Germany', and represented the Lutherans at imperial diets and in discussions with Catholics and Reformed.

His Loci communes (1521) was the first systematic presentation of Reformation theology, and his Augsburg Confession (1530) remains the basic statement of Lutheran faith. More conciliatory than Luther, he sought agreement with Catholics and with Calvinists, which led to bitter disputes among Lutherans after Luther's death.

### Loci communes (1521)

- **Date:** 1521
- **Type:** Systematic theology
- **Where written / held:** Wittenberg
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Loci%20communes%20Philip%20Melanchthon)

Loci communes rerum theologicarum ('common places of theology') grew out of Melanchthon's lectures on Romans. Rather than following the scholastic order, it focuses on the topics he saw as central to scripture, sin, law and grace, insisting that to know Christ is to know his benefits rather than to speculate about his natures. Luther praised it as worthy of being included in the canon. Melanchthon revised and expanded it many times until 1559, moving toward a fuller system and modifying some positions, for example on free will. It became the model for Protestant dogmatics.

### Augsburg Confession (1530)

- **Date:** 1530
- **Type:** Confession of faith
- **Where written / held:** Presented at the Diet of Augsburg, 25 June 1530
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Augsburg%20Confession%20Philip%20Melanchthon)

When Charles V summoned the Diet of Augsburg in 1530 to settle the religious question, the Lutheran princes and cities presented a statement of their faith drafted by Melanchthon, Luther being under imperial ban and remaining at Coburg. Its twenty-eight articles set out Lutheran teaching on God, sin, justification by faith, the church and sacraments, emphasising continuity with the Catholic tradition, and then describe abuses that had been corrected, such as communion in one kind and compulsory clerical celibacy. It was read aloud in German before the emperor on 25 June. The Catholic theologians replied with a Confutation, to which Melanchthon answered with his Apology. The Augsburg Confession remains the defining statement of Lutheran churches worldwide.
