# Moses Mendelssohn

**1729 – 1786** · German Jewish  
Born: Dessau, Anhalt · Died: Berlin  
Movements & eras: [Evangelical revivals](../movements/evangelical-revivals.md)  

> Philosopher of the German Enlightenment and pioneer of the Haskalah, the Jewish Enlightenment, who argued for religious freedom and Jewish civil rights.

## Biography

Mendelssohn was the son of a poor Torah scribe in Dessau and followed his teacher to Berlin in 1743, where he taught himself German, Latin and philosophy while working for a silk manufacturer. He became a celebrated philosopher, winning the Berlin Academy prize in 1763 ahead of Kant, and was the friend of the dramatist Lessing, whose play Nathan the Wise is thought to portray him. In 1769 a Swiss pastor, Johann Caspar Lavater, publicly challenged him to refute Christianity or convert, a controversy that pushed him to write on Judaism.

His German translation of the Torah in Hebrew letters, with a commentary (the Biur), helped Jews learn German, and his Jerusalem (1783) argued for separation of religious and state power and presented Judaism as a religion of revealed law rather than of compulsory dogma. He is regarded as the father of the Haskalah. His grandson was the composer Felix Mendelssohn.

### The Biur (German Torah translation and commentary) (1780–1783)

- **Date:** 1780–1783
- **Type:** Scripture (translation and commentary)
- **Where written / held:** Berlin; printed in Hebrew characters
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Biur%20%28German%20Torah%20translation%20and%20commentary%29%20Moses%20Mendelssohn)

Mendelssohn translated the Five Books of Moses into elegant High German, printing it in Hebrew characters so that Jews who read only Hebrew script could learn standard German through a familiar text. The accompanying Hebrew commentary, the Biur ('explanation'), written with collaborators such as Solomon Dubno and Naphtali Herz Wessely, explained the plain meaning using traditional commentaries and grammar. Some rabbis opposed it as an incitement to secular learning, while others approved it. It helped many central European Jews move into German culture and was a landmark of the Haskalah.

### Jerusalem, or on Religious Power and Judaism (1783)

- **Date:** 1783
- **Type:** Philosophical treatise
- **Where written / held:** Berlin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jerusalem%2C%20or%20on%20Religious%20Power%20and%20Judaism%20Moses%20Mendelssohn)

In the first part of Jerusalem, Mendelssohn argues that neither state nor church may coerce belief; the state may compel actions, but religion concerns convictions, so religious bodies should have no power of excommunication or punishment. In the second part, responding to the charge that Judaism itself was a coercive religion, he presents it as a revealed legislation, a law of actions given to Israel, whose eternal truths about God and morality are accessible to all people by reason. He urges Jews to keep the law while taking part in civil society, and calls on states to grant them civil equality. Kant praised the book. It became a founding text of modern Jewish thought and of liberal arguments for religious toleration.
