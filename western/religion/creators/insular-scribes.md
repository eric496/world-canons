# Insular monastic scribes and illuminators

**c. 650–850** · Irish and Anglo-Saxon  
Born: Ireland, Scotland and Northumbria · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Monasticism](../movements/monasticism.md)  

> The monks of Irish, Scottish and Northumbrian monasteries who created the great illuminated Gospel books of the 7th to 9th centuries, such as the Book of Kells.

## Biography

In the monasteries founded by Irish missionaries and their Anglo-Saxon converts, such as Iona, Durrow, Lindisfarne and Wearmouth-Jarrow, book production was a form of devotion. Most scribes are anonymous, though a colophon added to the Lindisfarne Gospels in the 10th century names Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne, as its maker. The style art historians call 'Insular' combines Celtic spirals and knotwork, Germanic interlaced animals and Mediterranean figure painting.

The Insular Gospel books, made for display on the altar as sacred objects as much as for reading, are among the summits of medieval art. Monks from these monasteries also carried learning to continental Europe, founding houses such as Luxeuil, Bobbio and St Gall, and Insular scribes played a part in the revival of learning under Charlemagne.

### Book of Durrow (c. 650–700)

[![Book of Durrow](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/IE_TCD_MS_57_f.86r.jpg/960px-IE_TCD_MS_57_f.86r.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:IE_TCD_MS_57_f.86r.jpg)

<sub>Book of Durrow, folio 86r (Trinity College Dublin, MS 57) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:IE_TCD_MS_57_f.86r.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 650–700
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** Trinity College Library, Dublin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Durrow%20Insular%20monastic%20scribes%20and%20illuminators)

The Book of Durrow is a small Gospel book in Latin, written in Insular majuscule and decorated with six carpet pages, symbols of the evangelists and decorated initials, in a limited palette of yellow, red, green and brown. Its ornament combines Celtic trumpet spirals, Germanic animal interlace like that on the Sutton Hoo metalwork, and Mediterranean motifs. It was kept at the monastery of Durrow in Ireland, founded by St Columba, but scholars debate whether it was made there, at Iona or in Northumbria. In the 17th century it was reportedly dipped in water by a farmer to cure sick cattle. It is the earliest of the great series that culminates in the Book of Kells.

### Lindisfarne Gospels (c. 715–720)

[![Lindisfarne Gospels](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/LindisfarneFol27rIncipitMatt.jpg/960px-LindisfarneFol27rIncipitMatt.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:LindisfarneFol27rIncipitMatt.jpg)

<sub>Lindisfarne Gospels, folio 27r: the opening of Matthew's Gospel Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:LindisfarneFol27rIncipitMatt.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 715–720
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** British Library, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lindisfarne%20Gospels%20Insular%20monastic%20scribes%20and%20illuminators)

The Lindisfarne Gospels was made at the monastery of Lindisfarne, on Holy Island off the Northumbrian coast, probably in honour of St Cuthbert. A colophon added in the 10th century by the priest Aldred names Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne from 698 to 721, as its scribe and artist, and most scholars accept that one person wrote and illuminated it. Each Gospel is preceded by an evangelist portrait, based on Mediterranean models, and a 'carpet page' of intricate interlace in the form of a cross, followed by a decorated initial page. Its pigments were made from local and imported materials. Aldred added a word-by-word translation into the Northumbrian dialect of Old English between the lines. The book was taken to Durham, then confiscated at the dissolution of the monasteries, and entered the British Library's collections through the library of Sir Robert Cotton.

### Codex Amiatinus (before 716)

[![Codex Amiatinus](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg/960px-CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg)

<sub>Codex Amiatinus, folio 5r: the prophet Ezra writing Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CodxAmiatinusFolio5rEzra.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** before 716
- **Type:** Manuscript (Latin Bible)
- **Where written / held:** Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Codex%20Amiatinus%20Insular%20monastic%20scribes%20and%20illuminators)

Codex Amiatinus is a giant single-volume Bible of over a thousand leaves, weighing more than 34 kilograms, one of three such pandects made at the twin monastery of Wearmouth-Jarrow under Abbot Ceolfrith, Bede's abbot. In 716 Ceolfrith set out to carry it to Rome as a gift for the pope but died on the way at Langres. The manuscript later reached the abbey of San Salvatore on Monte Amiata in Tuscany, from which it takes its name; its Northumbrian origin was recognised only in the 1880s, when a dedication altered to disguise its origin was deciphered. Written in a fine Italian-style uncial, it includes a famous image of the prophet Ezra writing at a book cupboard. It is the most important witness to the text of Jerome's Vulgate.

### Book of Kells (c. 800)

[![Book of Kells](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b1/KellsFol032vChristEnthroned.jpg/960px-KellsFol032vChristEnthroned.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:KellsFol032vChristEnthroned.jpg)

<sub>Book of Kells, folio 32v: Christ enthroned Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:KellsFol032vChristEnthroned.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 800
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** Trinity College Library, Dublin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Kells%20Insular%20monastic%20scribes%20and%20illuminators)

The Book of Kells contains the four Gospels in Latin, on 340 surviving vellum leaves. It was probably begun in the monastery of Iona, off the west coast of Scotland, around 800 and taken to Kells in Ireland after Viking raids, though where each part was made is debated. Its decoration is extraordinary: full-page portraits of Christ, the Virgin and Child and the evangelists, symbols of the four evangelists, and dense carpet-like pages of knotwork and spirals, most famously the Chi-Rho page, where the monogram of Christ's name swells to fill the page, with cats, mice and otters hidden in its ornament. Even ordinary text pages are enlivened with animals and figures. The manuscript, recorded at Kells in the 11th century, was stolen in 1007 and its gold cover lost. It came to Trinity College Dublin in the 17th century, where it is displayed, a page turned regularly.
