# Medieval Jewish scribes and illuminators

**c. 13th–15th century** · Jewish  
Born: Spain, Provence, Germany and Italy · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Medieval Jewish thought](../movements/medieval-jewish-thought.md)  

> The mostly anonymous scribes and artists who produced illuminated Hebrew manuscripts such as the great Haggadot of medieval Spain and Germany.

## Biography

Medieval Jewish communities commissioned richly decorated Hebrew Bibles, prayer books and above all Haggadot, the books read at the Passover meal. Scribes were often Jews, while illuminators could be Jewish or Christian, working in styles shared with the Gothic art around them. Some names survive in colophons, but most artists are anonymous, and art historians identify them by workshop and region.

These manuscripts, many of which survived expulsions and persecutions through extraordinary journeys, are among the treasures of Jewish art. They show that the biblical prohibition of images was understood to permit narrative illustration of sacred books, though some, such as the Birds' Head Haggadah, avoid showing human faces.

### Golden Haggadah (c. 1320–1330)

[![Golden Haggadah](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c8/Golden_Hag_BL_F60073-09b.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Golden_Hag_BL_F60073-09b.jpg)

<sub>A page of the Golden Haggadah (British Library) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Golden_Hag_BL_F60073-09b.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1320–1330
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** British Library, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Golden%20Haggadah%20Medieval%20Jewish%20scribes%20and%20illuminators)

The Golden Haggadah was made in or near Barcelona in the early 14th century. Its fourteen pages of miniatures, with scenes from Genesis and Exodus arranged in four panels per page against tooled gold backgrounds, are in the Gothic style of the time and show the involvement of at least two artists, perhaps working in a Christian workshop. Further images show Passover preparations, such as cleaning the house of leaven and distributing matzah. An inscription records that it was given as a wedding gift in Italy in 1602. It entered the British Museum in 1865 and is now in the British Library.

### Sarajevo Haggadah (c. 1350)

[![Sarajevo Haggadah](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/23/Sarajevska_hagada.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sarajevska_hagada.jpg)

<sub>The Sarajevo Haggadah Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sarajevska_hagada.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1350
- **Type:** Illuminated manuscript
- **Where written / held:** National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina, Sarajevo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sarajevo%20Haggadah%20Medieval%20Jewish%20scribes%20and%20illuminators)

The Sarajevo Haggadah was made in Catalonia, probably Barcelona, around 1350. Besides the text of the Passover service, it contains thirty-four full-page miniatures in gold and colour, from the creation of the world to the death of Moses, together with a picture of a Spanish synagogue. It probably left Spain with refugees after the expulsion of 1492, and notes in it show that it was in Italy in 1609, when a Catholic censor approved it. It was sold to the National Museum in Sarajevo in 1894. During the Second World War it was hidden from the Nazis, according to the best-known account, by the museum's Muslim librarian Derviš Korkut; it survived again during the siege of Sarajevo in the 1990s. Its story inspired Geraldine Brooks's novel People of the Book.
