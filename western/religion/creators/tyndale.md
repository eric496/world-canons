# William Tyndale

**c. 1494 – 1536** · English  
Born: Gloucestershire, England · Died: Vilvoorde, near Brussels  
Movements & eras: [Protestant Reformation](../movements/reformation.md)  

> English scholar who translated the New Testament and much of the Old from Hebrew and Greek, laying the foundation of the English Bible, and was executed for heresy.

## Biography

Tyndale studied at Oxford and probably Cambridge and became a priest. Inspired by Erasmus's Greek New Testament, he sought permission from the bishop of London to translate it into English; refused, he left England in 1524 and never returned. Working in Germany and the Low Countries, he published his New Testament in 1526 at Worms, and copies were smuggled into England, where the bishops had them burned. He went on to translate the Pentateuch and other books and wrote polemical works defending Reformation teaching. Betrayed in Antwerp in 1535, he was imprisoned at Vilvoorde castle and strangled and burned as a heretic in 1536.

Tyndale's translations, made directly from Hebrew and Greek, were absorbed into later English Bibles, including the Authorised (King James) Version of 1611, which scholars estimate keeps most of his New Testament wording. Phrases such as 'let there be light' and 'the powers that be' come from him. His plain, rhythmic English made him one of the shapers of the language.

## Key texts & works

### Tyndale New Testament (1526)

- **Date:** 1526
- **Type:** Scripture (English translation)
- **Where written / held:** Printed by Peter Schöffer, Worms; copies in the British Library and Stuttgart
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tyndale%20New%20Testament%20William%20Tyndale)

Tyndale's first attempt to print his New Testament in Cologne in 1525 was interrupted by a raid, and only a fragment of that edition survives. The first complete edition was printed in Worms in 1526 in a small octavo format, and about 3,000 copies were smuggled into England, where Bishop Cuthbert Tunstall bought and burned copies at St Paul's Cross. Only three copies of the 1526 edition are known today. Tyndale went on to translate the Pentateuch (1530), Jonah and a revised New Testament (1534). His clear and rhythmic English, with phrases such as 'the salt of the earth' and 'fight the good fight', passed largely into the Great Bible, the Geneva Bible and the King James Version.
