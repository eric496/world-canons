# John of Patmos

**1st century AD** · Early Christian  
Movements & eras: [Early Christianity & the New Testament](../movements/early-christianity.md)  

> Christian prophet who, exiled on the Aegean island of Patmos, wrote the Book of Revelation, the apocalypse that closes the New Testament.

## Biography

The author of Revelation calls himself John and says he was on Patmos 'for the word of God', probably in banishment, when he received his visions. Tradition from the 2nd century identified him with the apostle John, son of Zebedee, and with the author of the Fourth Gospel. Already in the 3rd century Dionysius of Alexandria argued on grounds of style that the two books had different authors, and most modern scholars agree, regarding John of Patmos as an otherwise unknown Jewish-Christian prophet active in Asia Minor.

Revelation is usually dated to the end of the reign of Domitian, around AD 95, though some scholars prefer a date in the late 60s. Its vivid imagery, steeped in the Hebrew prophets, was written to seven churches under pressure from Roman society. It has been read as coded commentary on Rome, as a prophecy of the end of history and as an allegory of the spiritual life, and has inspired movements, art, music and literature across two millennia.

- **Date:** c. AD 95
- **Type:** Scripture (apocalypse)
- **Where written / held:** Written on Patmos to seven churches of Asia Minor
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Revelation%20John%20of%20Patmos)

Revelation, or the Apocalypse, opens with letters from the risen Christ to seven churches in western Asia Minor and then unfolds a series of visions: the throne of God surrounded by living creatures, the Lamb opening a scroll with seven seals, trumpets and bowls of judgement, the woman clothed with the sun, the dragon and the beast whose number is 666, the fall of 'Babylon the great', the thousand-year reign of Christ, the last judgement and the descent of the New Jerusalem. Its imagery draws heavily on Ezekiel, Daniel and other prophets. Most scholars read it as addressed to Christians under the pressures of Roman imperial cult around 95, with Babylon standing for Rome. Its place in the canon was disputed for centuries, especially in the East. It has inspired countless interpretations of history and the end times, as well as Dürer's woodcuts, medieval tapestries and the language of Handel's Messiah.
