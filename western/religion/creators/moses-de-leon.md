# Moses de León

**c. 1240 – 1305** · Castilian Jewish  
Born: probably León, Castile · Died: Arévalo, Castile  
Movements & eras: [Medieval Jewish thought](../movements/medieval-jewish-thought.md)  

> Castilian kabbalist who circulated, and according to most scholars largely composed, the Zohar, the central book of Jewish mysticism.

## Biography

Moses ben Shem Tov de León lived in various towns of Castile, including Guadalajara and Ávila, and wrote a number of Hebrew works on Kabbalah under his own name in the 1280s and 1290s. He also circulated, as copies of an ancient manuscript, a large Aramaic work presenting the mystical teachings of the 2nd-century sage Shimon bar Yochai: the Zohar. A contemporary account reports that after his death his widow said he had written it himself, though the reliability of that story is itself debated.

Tradition holds the Zohar to be the ancient teaching of Shimon bar Yochai. Gershom Scholem, the founder of the modern academic study of Kabbalah, argued that Moses de León was its author; more recent scholars tend to see the Zohar as the work of a circle of Castilian kabbalists, with de León as a principal figure, assembled over time. Either way, his name is inseparable from the book that became, beside the Bible and Talmud, a sacred text of Judaism.

## Key texts & works

### Zohar (c. 1270s–1300 (traditional: Shimon bar Yochai, 2nd century))

[![Zohar](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/21/Zohar.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zohar.png)

<sub>An early printed edition of the Zohar Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zohar.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1270s–1300 (traditional: Shimon bar Yochai, 2nd century)
- **Type:** Mystical commentary
- **Where written / held:** Castile; first printed in Mantua and Cremona, 1558–60
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Zohar%20Moses%20de%20Le%C3%B3n)

The Zohar ('Radiance') is a vast collection of mystical interpretations of the Torah, the Song of Songs, Ruth and other texts, written in an artificial literary Aramaic. Its stories portray Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and his companions wandering through Galilee, discussing the hidden meanings of scripture. It develops the kabbalistic doctrine of the ten sefirot, the aspects through which the hidden God (Ein Sof) is manifest, and speaks of the divine feminine presence, the Shekhinah, of exile and redemption, and of how human deeds affect the divine realm. Tradition attributes it to bar Yochai. Most scholars since Gershom Scholem date it to late 13th-century Castile and link it to Moses de León, some seeing it as the work of a circle of kabbalists composed over time. It became a sacred text for many Jews, central to the Kabbalah of Safed and to Hasidism.
