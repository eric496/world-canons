# John Wycliffe

**c. 1328 – 1384** · English  
Born: Yorkshire, England · Died: Lutterworth, Leicestershire  
Movements & eras: [Medieval scholasticism & mysticism](../movements/scholasticism-mysticism.md)  

> Oxford theologian who criticised the wealth and authority of the church and inspired the first complete English Bible; often called the morning star of the Reformation.

## Biography

Wycliffe was a leading philosopher and theologian at Oxford, where he was master of Balliol College. From the 1370s he enjoyed the protection of John of Gaunt and argued that the church should give up its property and that civil rulers might remove corrupt clergy. He went on to attack papal authority and the doctrine of transubstantiation, and to teach that scripture is the supreme authority for Christians. Condemned by the pope and by an English council in 1382, he retired to his parish of Lutterworth, where he died in 1384. The Council of Constance later declared him a heretic, and in 1428 his remains were exhumed and burned.

Wycliffe's followers, known as Lollards, produced English translations of the whole Bible from the Latin Vulgate, and spread his ideas among clergy and lay people. His writings influenced Jan Hus in Bohemia, and through him the wider currents of reform. Historians debate how far Wycliffe himself took part in the translation that bears his name.

## Key texts & works

### Wycliffite Bible (c. 1382–1395)

- **Date:** c. 1382–1395
- **Type:** Scripture (English translation)
- **Where written / held:** Oxford and elsewhere; some 250 manuscripts survive, many in British and American libraries
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Wycliffite%20Bible%20John%20Wycliffe)

The Wycliffite Bible exists in two versions: an early, very literal translation of the Vulgate made around 1382, and a later, more idiomatic revision of the 1390s, associated with John Purvey, whose role scholars now question. Wycliffe's personal involvement is debated; the translation was the work of a team of Oxford scholars inspired by his teaching that scripture should be available to all. In 1409 the Constitutions of Oxford, under Archbishop Arundel, forbade translating or owning the Bible in English without episcopal approval, and Lollards were prosecuted. Nevertheless, some 250 manuscripts survive, more than of any other medieval English text, many apparently owned by orthodox readers. It was a forerunner of the Reformation Bibles.
