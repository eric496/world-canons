# Julian of Norwich

**c. 1343 – after 1416** · English  
Born: probably Norwich, England · Died: Norwich  
Movements & eras: [Medieval scholasticism & mysticism](../movements/scholasticism-mysticism.md)  

> English anchoress whose Revelations of Divine Love is the earliest surviving book in English known to have been written by a woman.

## Biography

Nothing is known for certain of Julian's early life, not even her real name; she is called after the church of St Julian in Norwich, where she lived as an anchoress, enclosed in a cell attached to the church. She records that in May 1373, aged thirty and a half and seemingly near death, she received sixteen 'showings' or visions of Christ's passion and of God's love. She recovered, and spent the rest of her life reflecting on them. Wills of the early 15th century leave money to her, and the mystic Margery Kempe describes visiting her for counsel around 1413.

Julian wrote a short account of her visions and later a much longer one, which together are called Revelations of Divine Love. Her theology is marked by optimism and tenderness: she speaks of God's love as motherly, sees sin as having no lasting substance, and records Christ's assurance that 'all shall be well'. Little read for centuries, her book was printed in 1670 and found a large audience in the 20th century; she is honoured in the Anglican, Lutheran and Catholic churches.

## Key texts & works

### Revelations of Divine Love (c. 1373–1410s)

- **Date:** c. 1373–1410s
- **Type:** Mystical writing
- **Where written / held:** Norwich; manuscripts in the British Library and Bibliothèque nationale de France
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Revelations%20of%20Divine%20Love%20Julian%20of%20Norwich)

Julian wrote a Short Text soon after her visions of May 1373 and, over some twenty years of reflection, a Long Text of eighty-six chapters. She describes seeing Christ's bleeding head and a small thing 'the quantity of an hazelnut' in her palm, which God told her is all that is made, kept in being by his love. She develops a theology of divine love that speaks of Jesus as our mother, of sin as having 'no manner of substance', and of the parable of a lord and servant explaining the fall. She records Christ's words that all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well. The Short Text survives in one manuscript and the Long Text in three later copies. Rediscovered in the 20th century, the book quoted by T. S. Eliot in Four Quartets, it is now one of the most read works of medieval spirituality.
