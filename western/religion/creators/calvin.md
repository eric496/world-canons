## Biography

Jean Cauvin was born in Noyon in Picardy and studied theology and law in Paris, Orléans and Bourges, absorbing humanist methods. Around 1533 he experienced what he called a 'sudden conversion' and had to flee France as persecution of Protestants grew. In Basel he published the first edition of the Institutes in 1536. That year Guillaume Farel persuaded him to stay in Geneva to lead its reform. Expelled in 1538, he spent three years in Strasbourg, then returned in 1541 and remained Geneva's chief pastor until his death, building a disciplined church order, a famous academy and an international network.

Calvin taught the sovereignty of God, the authority of scripture, the spiritual presence of Christ in the Lord's Supper and, most controversially, double predestination. His Geneva became a refuge and training centre for Reformers from France, the Netherlands, Scotland and England. Its record also includes the execution of the anti-Trinitarian Michael Servetus in 1553, which Calvin supported. Calvinist churches spread through Europe and to North America, and historians from Max Weber onward have debated their influence on modern society.

### Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536; final edition 1559)

[![Institutes of the Christian Religion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/ba/CalvinInstitutio.jpg/960px-CalvinInstitutio.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CalvinInstitutio.jpg)

<sub>Title page of an edition of Calvin's Institutio Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CalvinInstitutio.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1536; final edition 1559
- **Type:** Systematic theology
- **Where written / held:** First edition printed in Basel, 1536; final Latin edition Geneva, 1559
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Institutes%20of%20the%20Christian%20Religion%20John%20Calvin)

The first edition, of six chapters, was published in Basel in 1536 with a dedicatory letter to King Francis I of France defending persecuted French Protestants. Calvin expanded it repeatedly, and the final Latin edition of 1559, with a French translation in 1560 that helped shape French prose, is organised in four books following the Apostles' Creed: the knowledge of God the Creator; the knowledge of God the Redeemer in Christ; the way we receive the grace of Christ, including justification, the Christian life and predestination; and the church, sacraments and civil government. It opens with the claim that nearly all wisdom consists of knowing God and ourselves. The Institutes became the principal theological textbook of Reformed churches in Europe and North America.

### Genevan Psalter (1539–1562)

[![Genevan Psalter](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Psautier_huguenot_title_1539.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Psautier_huguenot_title_1539.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the first Strasbourg psalter of 1539 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Psautier_huguenot_title_1539.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1539–1562
- **Type:** Metrical psalter
- **Where written / held:** Geneva (complete edition 1562)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Genevan%20Psalter%20John%20Calvin)

Calvin believed that congregations should sing chiefly the Psalms, in the vernacular and in unison. Beginning with a small collection in Strasbourg in 1539, he worked with the poets Clément Marot and Théodore de Bèze, who versified the psalms in French, and with musicians such as Louis Bourgeois, who composed or adapted many of the melodies, to produce the complete Genevan Psalter of 1562. Printed in tens of thousands of copies, it was translated into Dutch, German, English and other languages and remains in use in Reformed churches. The melody now known as the 'Old Hundredth', sung in English to 'All people that on earth do dwell', comes from it.
