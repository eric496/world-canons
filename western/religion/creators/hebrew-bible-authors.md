# Authors and editors of the Hebrew Bible

**c. 10th–2nd century BC** · Israelite and Judean  
Born: Israel, Judah and Babylon · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Ancient Israel & the Hebrew Bible](../movements/ancient-israel.md)  

> The many anonymous writers, poets, prophets and editors whose work over several centuries became the Hebrew Bible, the Tanakh.

## Biography

Jewish and Christian tradition long ascribed the books of the Hebrew Bible to named authors: the Torah to Moses, the Psalms to David, Proverbs and the Song of Songs to Solomon, and the prophetic books to the prophets whose names they bear. Modern critical scholarship, developing since the 17th and especially the 19th century, sees most of these books as composite works shaped by many hands. Scholars debate the details, but there is broad agreement that older oral and written traditions were collected and edited by scribes and priests, with much of the material reaching its final form during or after the Babylonian exile of the 6th century BC. The latest book, Daniel, is generally dated to the 160s BC.

The process by which these writings became a fixed canon of scripture was gradual. The Torah seems to have been authoritative by the 5th or 4th century BC, the Prophets somewhat later, and the boundaries of the Writings were still discussed in the first centuries AD. The resulting collection of twenty-four books in Jewish reckoning is the foundation of Judaism and, as the Old Testament, of Christianity. Its narratives, laws, poetry and prophecy have shaped Western religion, ethics, law, literature and art more than perhaps any other body of writing.

### Book of Isaiah (8th–5th century BC)

- **Date:** 8th–5th century BC
- **Type:** Scripture (prophecy)
- **Where written / held:** Jerusalem and Babylon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Isaiah%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

Isaiah is the first and longest of the major prophetic books. Its earliest layers are associated with Isaiah son of Amoz, who prophesied in Jerusalem during the Assyrian crisis of the late 8th century BC, warning kings of Judah and calling for justice and trust in God. Since the late 18th century most scholars have held that chapters 40–55, which speak of Cyrus of Persia and the return from Babylonian exile, come from an anonymous prophet of the 6th century often called Second Isaiah, and that chapters 56–66 are later still, while traditional readers regard the whole book as the work of the one prophet. Its visions of the holy God enthroned in the temple, of swords beaten into ploughshares and of the suffering servant made it hugely influential: it is the prophetic book most cited in the New Testament, and Christians read many passages as foretelling Christ. A complete scroll of Isaiah is among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

### Torah (Five Books of Moses) (c. 10th–5th century BC (traditional: Moses))

- **Date:** c. 10th–5th century BC (traditional: Moses)
- **Type:** Scripture
- **Where written / held:** Ancient Israel and Babylon; read in synagogues from handwritten scrolls
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Torah%20%28Five%20Books%20of%20Moses%29%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

The Torah, also called the Pentateuch, is the first and most sacred division of the Hebrew Bible. It runs from the creation of the world and the stories of Adam and Eve, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, through the enslavement of the Israelites in Egypt, the Exodus led by Moses and the giving of the law at Mount Sinai, to the wanderings in the wilderness and Moses's death on the threshold of the promised land. Woven through the narrative are the Ten Commandments and extensive laws on worship, purity, justice and social life. Jewish and Christian tradition held Moses to be its author. From the 19th century the 'documentary hypothesis' proposed that it combined four main sources, often labelled J, E, D and P, written in different periods; scholars today continue to debate the number, nature and dating of its sources, but most agree it reached its final form around the 5th century BC, during or after the Babylonian exile. Handwritten on parchment scrolls by trained scribes, the Torah is read in a yearly cycle in synagogues and is the foundation of Jewish law and life.

### Book of Job (c. 6th–4th century BC)

- **Date:** c. 6th–4th century BC
- **Type:** Scripture (wisdom poetry)
- **Where written / held:** Ancient Judah or the exile
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Job%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

The Book of Job sets a long poetic debate inside a prose folk tale. In the frame story God allows 'the Satan', a heavenly accuser, to test the blameless Job by destroying his wealth, his children and his health. In the poetry Job curses the day of his birth, and three friends insist his suffering must be punishment for sin, which he denies, demanding a hearing from God. God finally answers from the whirlwind with a series of questions about the wonders of creation, without explaining Job's suffering, and in the prose ending restores his fortunes. Scholars usually date the book to the exilic or post-exilic period, while acknowledging that its language makes dating difficult. Its bold questioning of easy answers about suffering has fascinated theologians, philosophers and writers from Gregory the Great to Kierkegaard, Blake and modern novelists.

### Psalms (c. 10th–3rd century BC (traditional: David))

- **Date:** c. 10th–3rd century BC (traditional: David)
- **Type:** Scripture (poetry)
- **Where written / held:** Ancient Israel and Judah
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Psalms%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

The Book of Psalms collects 150 poems, divided into five books, many of them headed with the names of David, Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon or Moses. Tradition attributed most of them to King David. Modern scholars see the Psalms as having been composed over several centuries, some perhaps for worship in the First Temple and others in the exile and after, and compiled into the present collection in the Second Temple period, though the dating of individual psalms is much debated. They include hymns of praise, laments of individuals and of the people, royal psalms, songs of pilgrimage and meditations on the law. Their parallelism and imagery, from the shepherd of Psalm 23 to the exiles weeping by the rivers of Babylon in Psalm 137, have made them the most used part of the Bible in prayer. They are central to Jewish liturgy and to the daily offices of Christian monks, and have been set to music, versified and translated countless times.

### Book of Ruth (c. 6th–4th century BC (set in the time of the Judges))

- **Date:** c. 6th–4th century BC (set in the time of the Judges)
- **Type:** Scripture (narrative)
- **Where written / held:** Ancient Judah
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Ruth%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

Set in the period of the Judges, Ruth tells how Naomi, widowed and bereaved of her sons in Moab, returns to Bethlehem with her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth, who refuses to leave her, saying that Naomi's people will be her people and Naomi's God her God. Gleaning in the fields of Naomi's kinsman Boaz, Ruth wins his protection, and he marries her according to the custom of the kinsman-redeemer. Their son Obed is the grandfather of David. Scholars debate its date, from the monarchy to the post-exilic period; some read it as a gentle response to post-exilic hostility to foreign wives. In Jewish tradition Ruth is read at the festival of Shavuot and is the model of a convert; in the Christian Gospel of Matthew she appears in the genealogy of Jesus.

### Song of Songs (c. 10th–3rd century BC (traditional: Solomon))

- **Date:** c. 10th–3rd century BC (traditional: Solomon)
- **Type:** Scripture (love poetry)
- **Where written / held:** Ancient Israel
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Song%20of%20Songs%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

The Song of Songs, also called the Song of Solomon or Canticles, is a collection of lyrical poems in which two lovers, the woman's voice especially prominent, speak of desire, longing, separation and union, with rich imagery of gardens, vineyards, spices and animals. God is not named in it. Its heading attributes it to Solomon; scholars propose dates from the monarchy to the Hellenistic period, often favouring a late date on linguistic grounds. Rabbi Akiva is said to have called it the 'holy of holies' among the scriptures, and Jewish tradition read it as an allegory of the love between God and Israel; it is read at Passover. Christian commentators, from Origen to Bernard of Clairvaux, interpreted it as the love of Christ for the church or for the individual soul, and it became a principal source of mystical language. Modern readers have also valued it as celebration of human love.

### Book of Ecclesiastes (Qoheleth) (c. 4th–3rd century BC (traditional: Solomon))

- **Date:** c. 4th–3rd century BC (traditional: Solomon)
- **Type:** Scripture (wisdom literature)
- **Where written / held:** Judea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Ecclesiastes%20%28Qoheleth%29%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

Ecclesiastes presents the words of Qoheleth, 'the one who assembles', identified as a son of David and king in Jerusalem, hence the traditional attribution to Solomon. Its refrain, often translated 'vanity of vanities, all is vanity' (hevel, literally 'breath' or 'vapour'), introduces meditations on the limits of wisdom, the injustice of the world, the certainty of death and the cyclical nature of time, with the famous poem 'to every thing there is a season'. Against this background it repeatedly counsels enjoyment of food, work and love as gifts of God, and ends with an epilogue urging the fear of God. Most scholars date it, on linguistic grounds, to the Persian or early Hellenistic period. Its scepticism made its inclusion in the canon debated among the rabbis, and it has appealed to readers from Montaigne to modern existentialists.

### Book of Daniel (c. 167–164 BC (set in the 6th century BC))

- **Date:** c. 167–164 BC (set in the 6th century BC)
- **Type:** Scripture (apocalyptic)
- **Where written / held:** Judea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Daniel%20Authors%20and%20editors%20of%20the%20Hebrew%20Bible)

The first half of Daniel tells stories of Daniel and his companions, Jewish exiles at the courts of Babylon and Persia who stay faithful to God: the fiery furnace, the writing on the wall at Belshazzar's feast, and Daniel in the lions' den. The second half records visions of four beasts representing successive empires, of 'one like a son of man' coming with the clouds of heaven, and of a time of persecution followed by resurrection of the dead. Traditional readers take the book as written by Daniel in the 6th century BC. Most modern scholars date its final form to the persecution of the Jews by the Seleucid king Antiochus IV around 167–164 BC, since its visions describe those events in detail, while the court tales may be older. Daniel is the chief example of apocalyptic literature in the Hebrew Bible and deeply influenced the New Testament, Revelation and later speculation about the end of the world.
