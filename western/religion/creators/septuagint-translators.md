# Translators of the Septuagint

**3rd–1st century BC** · Hellenistic Jewish  
Born: Alexandria, Egypt · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Second Temple Judaism](../movements/second-temple-judaism.md)  

> The Greek-speaking Jewish scholars, mostly in Alexandria, who translated the Hebrew scriptures into Greek, beginning with the Torah in the 3rd century BC.

## Biography

According to the Letter of Aristeas, a Hellenistic Jewish text, seventy-two Jewish elders were brought to Alexandria under Ptolemy II Philadelphus (reigned 285–246 BC) to translate the Law of Moses into Greek for the royal library. Later tradition embroidered the story, and the round number gave the translation its name, from the Latin septuaginta, 'seventy'. Historians treat the legend with caution but agree that the Torah was translated in Alexandria in the 3rd century BC, probably to serve the needs of the large Greek-speaking Jewish community there. The other books followed over the next two centuries, by different translators of varying styles.

The Septuagint was the Bible of Hellenistic Judaism and of Philo, and became the Old Testament of the early Christian church: New Testament writers quote it extensively. It also contains books not in the later Hebrew canon, such as Wisdom of Solomon and the Maccabees, which remain in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. As Christians adopted it, Jews increasingly turned to other Greek versions and to the Hebrew. It remains the official Old Testament of the Greek Orthodox Church.

- **Date:** 3rd–1st century BC
- **Type:** Scripture (translation)
- **Where written / held:** Alexandria; preserved in codices such as Sinaiticus and Vaticanus
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Septuagint%20Translators%20of%20the%20Septuagint)

The Septuagint, often abbreviated LXX, is the collection of Greek translations of the Hebrew scriptures made by Jewish translators from the 3rd century BC, beginning with the Torah in Alexandria. The translation varies from very literal to quite free, and in some books, such as Jeremiah, it reflects a Hebrew text different from the later Masoretic one, as Dead Sea Scrolls fragments have confirmed. It also includes works composed in Greek or surviving only in Greek, such as the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach in translation, Judith, Tobit and the books of Maccabees. The New Testament writers usually quote scripture from it, and it remained the Old Testament of the Greek-speaking church, used today by the Orthodox. Its additional books were kept by Catholics and Orthodox but placed in an Apocrypha or removed by Protestants. The oldest complete copies are in the great 4th- and 5th-century Christian codices.
