# Judah Halevi

**c. 1075 – 1141** · Andalusian Jewish  
Born: Tudela or Toledo, Spain · Died: probably Egypt or Palestine  
Movements & eras: [Medieval Jewish thought](../movements/medieval-jewish-thought.md)  

> Poet and philosopher of Muslim and Christian Spain, the greatest Hebrew poet of the Middle Ages and author of the Kuzari.

## Biography

Judah Halevi was born in northern Spain and spent his youth in Andalusia, where he won fame as a poet and worked as a physician, living in Toledo, Córdoba and elsewhere. Disillusioned with the precarious position of Jews caught between Christian and Muslim powers, he resolved late in life to go to the Land of Israel. He sailed to Alexandria in 1140 and documents from the Cairo Geniza show him in Egypt in 1141; he probably died that year, either in Egypt or soon after reaching Palestine. Legend says he was killed at the gates of Jerusalem.

Halevi wrote some 800 Hebrew poems, secular and religious, including his 'Songs of Zion' expressing longing for Jerusalem, several of which entered the liturgy of the Ninth of Av. His philosophical dialogue, the Kuzari, defends Judaism against philosophy, Christianity and Islam, grounding faith in historical revelation rather than in reason. He remains a central figure for Jewish thought and was claimed as a forerunner by later Zionists.

## Key texts & works

### Kuzari (c. 1140)

[![Kuzari](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cc/1880_Warsaw_Kuzari2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1880_Warsaw_Kuzari2.jpg)

<sub>A page of the Kuzari, Warsaw edition, 1880 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1880_Warsaw_Kuzari2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1140
- **Type:** Philosophical dialogue
- **Where written / held:** Spain; written in Judeo-Arabic, translated into Hebrew by Judah ibn Tibbon in 1167
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kuzari%20Judah%20Halevi)

The Kuzari is built on the historical tradition that the ruling class of the Khazars, a Turkic people of the Caucasus steppes, converted to Judaism in the 8th or 9th century. In Halevi's book the king, told in a dream that his intentions are pleasing to God but not his actions, interviews a philosopher, a Christian scholar and a Muslim scholar before turning to a rabbi. The rabbi grounds his faith not in philosophical proofs but in God's revelation in history, witnessed publicly by the whole people at Sinai, and speaks of the special religious capacity of Israel and the holiness of the Land of Israel. The book closes with the rabbi's decision to go to Jerusalem, as Halevi himself did. It has remained a classic of Jewish thought.
