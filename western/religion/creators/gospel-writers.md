# The Evangelists (authors of the Gospels)

**1st century AD** · Early Christian  
Born: Roman Empire · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Early Christianity & the New Testament](../movements/early-christianity.md)  

> The authors of the four canonical Gospels, traditionally Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, who wrote accounts of the life, death and resurrection of Jesus in the later 1st century.

## Biography

The four Gospels are anonymous in their texts; the titles 'according to Matthew', 'Mark', 'Luke' and 'John' are attested from the 2nd century. Church tradition identified Matthew and John as apostles, Mark as a companion of Peter, and Luke as the companion of Paul who also wrote the Acts of the Apostles. Most modern scholars think the Gospels were written between about AD 65 and 100, in Greek, by Christians who drew on oral traditions and earlier written sources, and many doubt that any was written by an eyewitness. The prevailing view is that Mark was written first, around 70, and that Matthew and Luke used Mark and a shared collection of sayings known as Q, though alternative solutions to this 'synoptic problem' have defenders.

Each Gospel has a distinct character: Mark's urgent narrative, Matthew's links to Jewish scripture and long discourses, Luke's concern for the poor and outsiders, and John's meditative theology of Jesus as the incarnate Word. By the late 2nd century these four had been accepted as authoritative, while other gospels were set aside. Their portraits of Jesus are the foundation of Christian faith, liturgy and art, and their symbols, a man or angel, lion, ox and eagle, became familiar throughout medieval art.

### Gospel of Mark (c. AD 66–75)

[![Gospel of Mark](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8b/Mark_16_first_lines%2C_Codex_Sinaiticus.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mark_16_first_lines%2C_Codex_Sinaiticus.png)

<sub>Mark 16 in Codex Sinaiticus, 4th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mark_16_first_lines%2C_Codex_Sinaiticus.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 66–75
- **Type:** Scripture (gospel)
- **Where written / held:** Probably Rome or Syria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gospel%20of%20Mark%20The%20Evangelists%20%28authors%20of%20the%20Gospels%29)

Mark begins abruptly with John the Baptist and Jesus's baptism, and moves rapidly, often linking scenes with 'immediately', through Jesus's healings, exorcisms, parables and conflicts in Galilee to his journey to Jerusalem, his passion and his death on the cross. Throughout, Jesus tells those who recognise him to keep silent, a theme scholars call the 'messianic secret'. The earliest manuscripts end at 16:8, with women fleeing the empty tomb in fear; the longer endings found in later manuscripts are generally regarded as additions. Early tradition, reported by Papias in the 2nd century, held that Mark was Peter's interpreter in Rome; modern scholars date it around the Jewish war of 66–73, and debate whether it was written in Rome, Syria or Galilee. Most accept 'Markan priority', the view that Matthew and Luke used Mark as a source, which made this neglected Gospel central to modern study of the historical Jesus.

### Gospel of Matthew (c. AD 80–90)

[![Gospel of Matthew](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Oxford%2C_Sackler_Library_Ms_LXIV_4404_%28Papyrus_104%29_recto_Matt_21%2C_34-37.jpg/1280px-Oxford%2C_Sackler_Library_Ms_LXIV_4404_%28Papyrus_104%29_recto_Matt_21%2C_34-37.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oxford%2C_Sackler_Library_Ms_LXIV_4404_%28Papyrus_104%29_recto_Matt_21%2C_34-37.jpg)

<sub>Papyrus 104, an early fragment of Matthew 21 (Sackler Library, Oxford) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oxford%2C_Sackler_Library_Ms_LXIV_4404_%28Papyrus_104%29_recto_Matt_21%2C_34-37.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 80–90
- **Type:** Scripture (gospel)
- **Where written / held:** Probably Antioch, Syria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gospel%20of%20Matthew%20The%20Evangelists%20%28authors%20of%20the%20Gospels%29)

Matthew opens with a genealogy tracing Jesus to Abraham and David and with the stories of the Magi and the flight into Egypt. It arranges Jesus's teaching into five great discourses, beginning with the Sermon on the Mount, which contains the Beatitudes and the Lord's Prayer, and repeatedly notes how events fulfil the prophets. It ends with the risen Jesus commissioning his disciples to make disciples of all nations. Placed first in the New Testament, it was the most quoted Gospel in the early church. Tradition attributed it to the apostle Matthew, the tax collector; most scholars think its author was an anonymous Jewish-Christian writer around 80–90 who used Mark and the sayings source Q. Its sharp polemic against the Pharisees reflects tensions between the early church and the synagogue, and some of its verses were later used to justify Christian hostility toward Jews, a history that modern churches have confronted.

### Gospel of Luke and Acts of the Apostles (c. AD 80–95)

[![Gospel of Luke and Acts of the Apostles](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/22/Luke_13.29-35_and_14.10_%28CBL_BP_I%2C_f.15r%29.jpg/1280px-Luke_13.29-35_and_14.10_%28CBL_BP_I%2C_f.15r%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Luke_13.29-35_and_14.10_%28CBL_BP_I%2C_f.15r%29.jpg)

<sub>Luke 13–14 in a 3rd-century papyrus codex (Chester Beatty Library, Dublin) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Luke_13.29-35_and_14.10_%28CBL_BP_I%2C_f.15r%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 80–95
- **Type:** Scripture (gospel and history)
- **Where written / held:** Probably the Aegean or Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gospel%20of%20Luke%20and%20Acts%20of%20the%20Apostles%20The%20Evangelists%20%28authors%20of%20the%20Gospels%29)

Luke and Acts, addressed to a certain Theophilus, were written as a single work by the same author. The Gospel gives the fullest account of Jesus's birth, with the annunciation, the shepherds and the canticles of Mary and Zechariah, and includes parables found only in Luke, such as the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son, with a marked concern for the poor, women and outsiders. Acts continues with the ascension, the coming of the Spirit at Pentecost, the growth of the Jerusalem church, and the missions of Peter and above all Paul, ending with Paul preaching under house arrest in Rome. Tradition identified the author as Luke, the physician and companion of Paul; the 'we' passages in Acts have been read as eyewitness memory or as a literary device. Most scholars date the work to around 80–95. Acts is the main source, though one whose historical reliability is debated, for the first decades of the church.

### Gospel of John (c. AD 90–110)

[![Gospel of John](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/JRL19060215_%28cropped%29.jpg/960px-JRL19060215_%28cropped%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:JRL19060215_%28cropped%29.jpg)

<sub>Rylands Papyrus P52, a small early fragment of John 18 (John Rylands Library, Manchester) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:JRL19060215_%28cropped%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 90–110
- **Type:** Scripture (gospel)
- **Where written / held:** Traditionally Ephesus
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gospel%20of%20John%20The%20Evangelists%20%28authors%20of%20the%20Gospels%29)

John differs markedly from the other three, 'synoptic', Gospels. It begins with a hymn to the Logos, the Word who was with God and became flesh, and arranges Jesus's ministry around seven 'signs', such as turning water into wine at Cana and raising Lazarus, and long discourses with 'I am' sayings: the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd. Its chronology, with several visits to Jerusalem, also differs. The Gospel refers to an anonymous 'disciple whom Jesus loved' as its witness; tradition identified him with the apostle John, writing at Ephesus in old age. Many scholars instead think of a 'Johannine community' whose traditions were edited in stages and completed around 90–110. Its high Christology was decisive for later doctrine of the Trinity, and its depiction of 'the Jews' as opponents is much discussed in modern Jewish-Christian dialogue.
