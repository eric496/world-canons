# Jacobus de Voragine

> Dominican friar and archbishop of Genoa who compiled the Golden Legend, the most popular collection of saints' lives in the Middle Ages.

<sub>Portrait: Jacobus de Voragine, fresco in the Palazzo Trinci, Foligno · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PalazzoTrinci010_%28cropped%29.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Jacobus was born at Varazze near Genoa and entered the Dominican order in 1244. He was a noted preacher and teacher, served as provincial of the Dominicans in Lombardy, and in 1292 became archbishop of Genoa, where he worked to make peace between the city's warring factions. He also wrote a chronicle of Genoa and many model sermons.

His Legenda aurea, compiled around 1260, retells the lives of the saints and the feasts of the church year, drawing on earlier sources and including many miraculous stories. It survives in about a thousand manuscripts, was translated into every major European language and was among the earliest books printed; William Caxton's English version appeared in 1483. Renaissance humanists and Reformers criticised its credulity, but it was a principal source for the subjects of medieval and Renaissance religious art.

## Key texts & works

### Golden Legend (Legenda aurea) (c. 1260)

- **Date:** c. 1260
- **Type:** Hagiography
- **Where written / held:** Genoa and Lombardy; some 1,000 manuscripts survive; English edition by Caxton, 1483
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Golden%20Legend%20%28Legenda%20aurea%29%20Jacobus%20de%20Voragine)

Organised by the liturgical calendar from Advent onward, the Golden Legend gathers some 180 chapters on saints and feasts: the lives and martyrdoms of saints such as George and the dragon, Catherine of Alexandria and Christopher, along with explanations of feasts such as Christmas and the Assumption. Jacobus drew on earlier collections and often offered several versions of a story, with fanciful etymologies of saints' names. It was a handbook for preachers and became immensely popular, translated into most European languages and printed in many editions from the 1470s. Humanists such as Juan Luis Vives criticised its legends, and the Reformation and later Catholic critical scholarship reduced its authority. Its stories shaped the iconography of medieval and Renaissance art, providing the attributes by which saints are recognised in painting and stained glass.
