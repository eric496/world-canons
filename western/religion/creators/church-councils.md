# Church councils and synods

**325 onwards** · Christian  
Movements & eras: [Church Fathers & the Councils](../movements/church-fathers.md), [Catholic Reformation](../movements/catholic-reformation.md), [20th-century theology & ecumenism](../movements/twentieth-century-theology.md)  

> The assemblies of bishops and church leaders whose creeds, definitions and decrees, from Nicaea in 325 to the Second Vatican Council, defined Christian doctrine and practice.

## Biography

From the 4th century the church settled disputed questions in councils of bishops. The emperor Constantine summoned the first ecumenical (empire-wide) council at Nicaea in 325, and later councils at Constantinople (381), Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) defined the doctrines of the Trinity and of Christ's two natures. The Orthodox churches recognise seven ecumenical councils, ending with Nicaea II in 787; the Catholic Church counts twenty-one, including Trent (1545–63), Vatican I (1869–70) and Vatican II (1962–65). Some churches of the East, such as the Oriental Orthodox, do not accept Chalcedon.

Protestant churches likewise defined their faith through synods and assemblies, producing confessions and catechisms such as the Heidelberg Catechism and the Westminster Confession. Conciliar texts are corporate works, drafted by committees of theologians, debated and voted on, and often reflecting compromise. Their formulations remain the reference points of Christian orthodoxy and the main subject of ecumenical dialogue between churches.

### Nicene Creed (325; expanded 381)

[![Nicene Creed](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Nicaea_icon.jpg/960px-Nicaea_icon.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nicaea_icon.jpg)

<sub>Icon of the First Council of Nicaea, with the creed Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nicaea_icon.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 325; expanded 381
- **Type:** Creed
- **Where written / held:** Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nicene%20Creed%20Church%20councils%20and%20synods)

The Council of Nicaea, summoned by the emperor Constantine in 325, adopted a creed declaring the Son 'of one substance' (homoousios) with the Father, against Arius, who held that the Son was created. The creed recited today in churches is usually the longer form associated with the Council of Constantinople in 381, which added fuller statements on the Holy Spirit, the church, baptism and resurrection; scholars debate exactly how this form arose. In the Latin West the words 'and the Son' (filioque) were later added to describe the Spirit's procession, beginning in Spain in the 6th century and adopted in Rome in the 11th; this became a lasting point of dispute with the Orthodox churches. The creed is used by Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran and many other churches and has been set to music in countless masses as the Credo.

### Chalcedonian Definition (451)

- **Date:** 451
- **Type:** Doctrinal definition
- **Where written / held:** Council of Chalcedon, near Constantinople
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chalcedonian%20Definition%20Church%20councils%20and%20synods)

The Council of Chalcedon was called by the emperor Marcian in 451, after decades of conflict over how Christ is both God and man. Drawing on Cyril of Alexandria and on the Tome of Pope Leo I, it declared Christ to be one person 'acknowledged in two natures', fully divine and fully human. The definition became the standard of orthodoxy for the Greek and Latin churches and later for most Protestant churches. It was rejected by many Christians in Egypt, Syria and Armenia, who held to 'one nature' formulas; their churches, today called Oriental Orthodox (such as the Coptic, Syriac, Armenian and Ethiopian churches), separated from Constantinople and Rome. In recent decades theologians from both sides have agreed that the dispute was largely about terminology.

### Decrees of the Council of Trent (1545–1563)

[![Decrees of the Council of Trent](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Concilio_Trento_Museo_Buonconsiglio.jpg/1280px-Concilio_Trento_Museo_Buonconsiglio.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Concilio_Trento_Museo_Buonconsiglio.jpg)

<sub>A session of the Council of Trent, painting in the Castello del Buonconsiglio, Trento Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Concilio_Trento_Museo_Buonconsiglio.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1545–1563
- **Type:** Conciliar decrees
- **Where written / held:** Trento (Trent), Prince-Bishopric of Trent
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Decrees%20of%20the%20Council%20of%20Trent%20Church%20councils%20and%20synods)

The Council of Trent met in three periods between 1545 and 1563, interrupted by war, plague and politics. In its doctrinal decrees it affirmed that revelation is transmitted through scripture and unwritten tradition, declared the Vulgate authentic, taught that justification involves both grace and human cooperation, and reaffirmed the seven sacraments, transubstantiation and the sacrificial character of the Mass. Its reform decrees required bishops to reside in their dioceses, established seminaries for training priests, and regulated religious orders, marriage and indulgences. After the council, the papacy issued the Roman Catechism (1566), a revised Missal (1570) and Breviary. Historians debate the balance of reaction and renewal at Trent, but it shaped Catholicism until the Second Vatican Council.

### Heidelberg Catechism (1563)

[![Heidelberg Catechism](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Heidelberger_Katechismus_1563_%282%29.jpg/960px-Heidelberger_Katechismus_1563_%282%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Heidelberger_Katechismus_1563_%282%29.jpg)

<sub>The first edition of the Heidelberg Catechism, 1563 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Heidelberger_Katechismus_1563_%282%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1563
- **Type:** Catechism
- **Where written / held:** Heidelberg, Electoral Palatinate
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Heidelberg%20Catechism%20Church%20councils%20and%20synods)

Commissioned by the Elector Frederick III of the Palatinate and published in 1563, the Heidelberg Catechism was drafted mainly by the theologian Zacharias Ursinus, with Caspar Olevianus and others, though the extent of each person's contribution is debated. Its 129 questions open with 'What is your only comfort in life and death?', answered with the believer's belonging to Christ. It is organised in three parts: human misery, deliverance through Christ, and gratitude, expressed in the Ten Commandments and the Lord's Prayer, and divided into fifty-two 'Lord's Days' for preaching through the year. Approved by the Synod of Dort, it became one of the most widely used Reformed confessions in the Netherlands, Germany, Hungary and North America.

### Westminster Confession of Faith (1646)

[![Westminster Confession of Faith](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/Westminster_Confession_of_Faith_title_page.jpg/960px-Westminster_Confession_of_Faith_title_page.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Westminster_Confession_of_Faith_title_page.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the Westminster Confession of Faith Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Westminster_Confession_of_Faith_title_page.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1646
- **Type:** Confession of faith
- **Where written / held:** Westminster Assembly, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Westminster%20Confession%20of%20Faith%20Church%20councils%20and%20synods)

During the English Civil War, Parliament called an assembly of divines to Westminster Abbey in 1643 to reform the Church of England, with Scottish commissioners taking part. Its Confession of Faith, completed in 1646, sets out a classic Calvinist theology in thirty-three chapters, on scripture, God's decree and predestination, the covenants of works and grace, justification, the church, sacraments and civil government. The assembly also produced the Larger and Shorter Catechisms, the latter opening with the question 'What is the chief end of man?'. Adopted by the Church of Scotland in 1647, the Confession became the doctrinal standard of Presbyterian churches in Britain, America and beyond, and in modified form of some Congregational and Baptist churches.

### Documents of the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965)

- **Date:** 1962–1965
- **Type:** Conciliar constitutions and decrees
- **Where written / held:** St Peter's Basilica, Vatican City
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Documents%20of%20the%20Second%20Vatican%20Council%20Church%20councils%20and%20synods)

Convened by John XXIII and continued under Paul VI, the Second Vatican Council brought some 2,500 bishops to Rome in four sessions from 1962 to 1965, with Protestant and Orthodox observers present. Its sixteen documents include four constitutions: on the liturgy (Sacrosanctum concilium), which permitted the use of vernacular languages in the Mass; on the church (Lumen gentium), emphasising the church as the people of God and the collegiality of bishops; on revelation (Dei verbum); and on the church in the modern world (Gaudium et spes). The declaration Nostra aetate (1965) repudiated the charge that the Jews as a people were collectively guilty for Christ's death and spoke respectfully of Islam and other religions, and Dignitatis humanae affirmed religious freedom. How to interpret the council's reforms, as continuity or rupture, remains debated within Catholicism.
