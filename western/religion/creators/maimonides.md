## Biography

Moses ben Maimon, known in Hebrew by the acronym Rambam, was born in Córdoba, then under Muslim rule. When the Almohads conquered the city around 1148 and pressured Jews and Christians to convert, his family wandered through Spain and North Africa, settling in Fez and then, around 1166, in Egypt. After his brother David, a merchant who supported the family, drowned in the Indian Ocean, Maimonides became a physician, eventually serving at the court of Saladin's vizier. He was also the leader of the Jewish community of Fustat and answered legal questions from across the Jewish world.

His Commentary on the Mishnah included the Thirteen Principles of faith, later the basis of hymns in the prayer book; his Mishneh Torah codified the whole of Jewish law in clear Hebrew; and his Guide for the Perplexed, written in Arabic, sought to reconcile Torah and Aristotelian philosophy. His rationalism provoked bitter controversy after his death, but he became the most authoritative figure of medieval Judaism, and Aquinas and other Christian scholastics read the Guide in Latin.

### Commentary on the Mishnah (with the Thirteen Principles) (1168)

- **Date:** 1168
- **Type:** Commentary
- **Where written / held:** Written in Morocco and Egypt in Judeo-Arabic; autograph portions in the Bodleian Library, Oxford
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Mishnah%20%28with%20the%20Thirteen%20Principles%29%20Maimonides%20%28Moses%20ben%20Maimon%29)

Begun when Maimonides was twenty-three and completed in Egypt in 1168, the commentary, written in Arabic in Hebrew characters, explains every tractate of the Mishnah and summarises the conclusions of the Talmud. Its introductions became famous in their own right: the 'Eight Chapters' on ethics, preceding the tractate Avot, and the introduction to the chapter Helek of tractate Sanhedrin, where Maimonides sets out thirteen principles of faith, including God's existence, unity and incorporeality, the prophecy of Moses, the divine origin of the Torah, reward and punishment, the coming of the Messiah and the resurrection of the dead. The principles were debated by later Jewish thinkers, but versified as the hymn Yigdal and as the 'Ani Ma'amin' creed they entered the prayer book. Parts of Maimonides's autograph manuscript survive in Oxford.

### Mishneh Torah (1170–1180)

- **Date:** 1170–1180
- **Type:** Code of law
- **Where written / held:** Fustat, Egypt; illuminated manuscripts include the Kaufmann Mishneh Torah in Budapest
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mishneh%20Torah%20Maimonides%20%28Moses%20ben%20Maimon%29)

In the Mishneh Torah ('Repetition of the Torah') Maimonides set out to summarise all of Jewish law, including laws no longer practised such as those of the Temple and the king, so that anyone could know the law without wading through the Talmud. He organised it in fourteen books, from the fundamentals of faith and knowledge of God to the laws of kings and the Messiah, in a lucid Mishnaic Hebrew, usually without citing sources or recording debates. That omission provoked criticism, notably from Rabbi Abraham ben David of Posquières, whose critical glosses are printed with it, and fears that it would replace Talmud study. It became one of the pillars of Jewish law, together with the later Shulchan Aruch of Joseph Karo. Fine illuminated copies were made in medieval Spain, Italy and Germany.

### Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190)

[![Guide for the Perplexed](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/90/Manuscript_page_by_Maimonides_Arabic_in_Hebrew_letters.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Manuscript_page_by_Maimonides_Arabic_in_Hebrew_letters.jpg)

<sub>Manuscript page in Maimonides's hand, Arabic written in Hebrew letters Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Manuscript_page_by_Maimonides_Arabic_in_Hebrew_letters.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1190
- **Type:** Philosophical treatise
- **Where written / held:** Fustat, Egypt; written in Judeo-Arabic, translated into Hebrew by Samuel ibn Tibbon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Guide%20for%20the%20Perplexed%20Maimonides%20%28Moses%20ben%20Maimon%29)

Addressed to a student perplexed by apparent conflicts between scripture and philosophy, the Guide interprets the Bible's anthropomorphic language about God figuratively, argues that God can only be described by negations, discusses whether the world is eternal or created (favouring creation while admitting it cannot be proven), and explains prophecy, providence, evil and the reasons for the commandments. Maimonides warned that he wrote it deliberately obscurely in places, and readers have long debated its true teaching. Its rationalism provoked fierce controversy in the 13th century, with copies burned in Montpellier in 1232. In Latin translation it influenced Aquinas and other scholastics, and later Spinoza and Leibniz.
