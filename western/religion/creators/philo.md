# Philo of Alexandria

**c. 20 BC – c. AD 50** · Hellenistic Jewish  
Born: Alexandria, Egypt · Died: Alexandria  
Movements & eras: [Second Temple Judaism](../movements/second-temple-judaism.md)  

> Jewish philosopher of Alexandria who interpreted the Torah through Greek philosophy, especially Platonism, and deeply influenced early Christian theology.

## Biography

Philo was born into a wealthy and prominent Jewish family of Alexandria, then the greatest city of the Greek-speaking world and home to a large Jewish community. His brother Alexander was a leading official, and his nephew Tiberius Julius Alexander became prefect of Egypt. Philo received a full Greek education and wrote in Greek, reading scripture in the Septuagint. The one firmly dated event of his life is his leadership of a Jewish embassy to the emperor Caligula in AD 39–40, after anti-Jewish riots in Alexandria, which he described in The Embassy to Gaius.

Philo's many surviving treatises, mainly on the books of Moses, use allegorical interpretation to show that the Torah teaches the same truths as the best Greek philosophy. He described God as utterly transcendent and spoke of the divine Logos, or Word, as an intermediary in creation, an idea that resonates with the opening of John's Gospel. Philo had little influence on later rabbinic Judaism, but Christian writers such as Clement of Alexandria, Origen and Ambrose read him closely, and his works were preserved by the church.

### On the Creation of the World (De opificio mundi) (early 1st century AD)

- **Date:** early 1st century AD
- **Type:** Philosophical commentary
- **Where written / held:** Alexandria; preserved in Byzantine Greek manuscripts
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20Creation%20of%20the%20World%20%28De%20opificio%20mundi%29%20Philo%20of%20Alexandria)

In this treatise Philo interprets the first chapters of Genesis as a philosophical account of the cosmos. He argues that the six days are not a time sequence but an expression of order, that God first conceived an intelligible world of ideas, located in the divine Logos, and then made the visible world in its image, much as an architect plans a city before building it. The work, which draws on Plato's Timaeus and on Stoic and Pythagorean ideas, including number symbolism, opens Philo's series on the laws of Moses, presenting Moses as the supreme philosopher. Its approach of reading scripture alongside Greek philosophy was taken up by Christian writers such as Clement, Origen and Ambrose, and helped shape centuries of Christian commentary on creation.

### On the Contemplative Life (early 1st century AD)

- **Date:** early 1st century AD
- **Type:** Treatise
- **Where written / held:** Alexandria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20Contemplative%20Life%20Philo%20of%20Alexandria)

Philo describes a group he calls the Therapeutae ('healers' or 'worshippers'), who had left property and family to live in simple dwellings outside Alexandria, studying scripture allegorically during the week and gathering on the seventh day, men and women separately, with a great festival every fiftieth day including a night of choral singing. It is the only ancient description of this group. Eusebius in the 4th century believed they were early Christian monks, an identification no longer accepted; some modern scholars have even questioned whether Philo idealised them. The treatise is an important witness to Jewish ascetic piety in the Second Temple period and to the role of women in it, and it later fed Christian ideals of monastic life.
