# Qumran community and scribes

**c. 2nd century BC – AD 68** · Judean  
Born: Qumran, Judean Desert · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Second Temple Judaism](../movements/second-temple-judaism.md)  

> The Jewish group associated with the settlement at Qumran near the Dead Sea, and the scribes whose scrolls were found in nearby caves from 1947.

## Biography

Between 1947 and 1956 Bedouin shepherds and archaeologists found the remains of some 900 scrolls in eleven caves near Khirbet Qumran on the north-west shore of the Dead Sea. The settlement there was occupied from about the late 2nd or early 1st century BC until it was destroyed by Roman troops around AD 68. The majority view among scholars connects the site and many of the scrolls with the Essenes, a Jewish sect described by Philo, Josephus and Pliny the Elder, though this identification is debated, and some scholars argue the scrolls were brought from Jerusalem or other places.

The texts include the oldest known manuscripts of almost every book of the Hebrew Bible, as well as sectarian writings such as the Community Rule, the War Scroll and commentaries on the prophets that describe a community led by a priestly 'Teacher of Righteousness' and expecting an imminent final battle between light and darkness. They have transformed understanding of the textual history of the Bible and of the varieties of Judaism at the time of Jesus.

### Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) (c. 125 BC)

[![Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/The_Great_Isaiah_Scroll_MS_A_%281QIsa%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/1280px-The_Great_Isaiah_Scroll_MS_A_%281QIsa%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Great_Isaiah_Scroll_MS_A_%281QIsa%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

<sub>The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a), Israel Museum, Jerusalem Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Great_Isaiah_Scroll_MS_A_%281QIsa%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 125 BC
- **Type:** Manuscript (scroll)
- **Where written / held:** Israel Museum (Shrine of the Book), Jerusalem
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Great%20Isaiah%20Scroll%20%281QIsa-a%29%20Qumran%20community%20and%20scribes)

Found in Qumran Cave 1 in 1947, the Great Isaiah Scroll consists of seventeen sheets of parchment sewn together into a scroll over seven metres long, containing all sixty-six chapters of Isaiah in fifty-four columns. Palaeography and radiocarbon dating place it in the 2nd century BC. Its text agrees substantially with the medieval Masoretic Text, though it has thousands of minor variants in spelling and some significant differences in wording, illuminating how carefully, and with how much variation, scripture was copied. It was one of the first scrolls sold, through a dealer in Bethlehem, to the Syrian Orthodox metropolitan of Jerusalem, who later sold it to Israel in 1954. A facsimile is displayed in the Shrine of the Book, and the scroll can be viewed online in high resolution.

### Dead Sea Scrolls (c. 250 BC – AD 68)

[![Dead Sea Scrolls](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/Dead_Sea_Scroll%2C_Pesher_Isaiah%2C_from_Qumran_Cave_4._The_Jordan_Museum%2C_Amman.jpg/1280px-Dead_Sea_Scroll%2C_Pesher_Isaiah%2C_from_Qumran_Cave_4._The_Jordan_Museum%2C_Amman.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dead_Sea_Scroll%2C_Pesher_Isaiah%2C_from_Qumran_Cave_4._The_Jordan_Museum%2C_Amman.jpg)

<sub>Pesher (commentary) on Isaiah from Qumran Cave 4 (The Jordan Museum, Amman) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dead_Sea_Scroll%2C_Pesher_Isaiah%2C_from_Qumran_Cave_4._The_Jordan_Museum%2C_Amman.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 250 BC – AD 68
- **Type:** Manuscripts (scrolls)
- **Where written / held:** Israel Museum (Shrine of the Book), Jerusalem; Jordan Museum, Amman; and other collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dead%20Sea%20Scrolls%20Qumran%20community%20and%20scribes)

The first scrolls were found in 1946–47 by Bedouin shepherds in a cave near Khirbet Qumran; by 1956 eleven caves had yielded tens of thousands of fragments from roughly 900 manuscripts, mostly on parchment, in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek. They date from about the 3rd century BC to the 1st century AD. Around a quarter are copies of biblical books, every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther being represented, about a thousand years older than the previously known medieval manuscripts. Others are previously unknown texts: rules for a community, hymns, calendars, commentaries on prophets and apocalyptic works such as the War Scroll. The identity of the people who collected them, most often identified with the Essenes, is debated, as is the relationship between the scrolls and the nearby settlement. The slow publication of the fragments caused controversy until full access was granted in 1991. The scrolls have reshaped the study of the Hebrew Bible's text and of the Jewish world in which Christianity began.

### Community Rule (Serekh ha-Yahad) (c. 100–75 BC)

- **Date:** c. 100–75 BC
- **Type:** Manuscript (sectarian rule)
- **Where written / held:** Israel Museum (Shrine of the Book), Jerusalem
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Community%20Rule%20%28Serekh%20ha-Yahad%29%20Qumran%20community%20and%20scribes)

The best-preserved copy of the Community Rule, from Qumran Cave 1, was among the first scrolls discovered; further copies from Cave 4 show that the text developed over time. It describes how members entered a covenant, surrendered property to the community, passed through stages of probation, shared meals and study, and were punished for offences such as speaking against fellow members. A famous section, the Treatise on the Two Spirits, teaches that humanity is divided between a spirit of truth and a spirit of falsehood until God's final visitation. The text's parallels with ancient descriptions of the Essenes underlie the common identification of the Qumran group with them. Scholars have also compared its practices with those of the early Christian community in Acts, though direct links are not established.
