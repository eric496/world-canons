# Peter Lombard

**c. 1096 – 1160** · Italian  
Born: Novara, Lombardy · Died: Paris  
Movements & eras: [Medieval scholasticism & mysticism](../movements/scholasticism-mysticism.md)  

> Theologian of Paris and bishop whose Four Books of Sentences became the standard textbook of theology in medieval universities.

## Biography

Peter was born near Novara in Lombardy and studied at Bologna and Reims before coming to Paris around 1136 with a recommendation from Bernard of Clairvaux. He taught at the cathedral school of Notre-Dame for some twenty years and was made bishop of Paris in 1159, dying the following year.

His Sentences, completed around 1158, gathered quotations from scripture and the Church Fathers, above all Augustine, on every topic of doctrine, from the Trinity to the sacraments and the last things, and set out how to reconcile them. From the 13th century every candidate for a master's degree in theology had to lecture on the Sentences, so that commentaries on it by Aquinas, Bonaventure, Duns Scotus, Ockham and even the young Luther form a central body of medieval theology. Peter's list of seven sacraments became official Catholic teaching.

## Key texts & works

### Four Books of Sentences (c. 1155–1158)

- **Date:** c. 1155–1158
- **Type:** Theological textbook
- **Where written / held:** Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Four%20Books%20of%20Sentences%20Peter%20Lombard)

The Sentences arranges quotations, mainly from Augustine but also from other Fathers, under four headings: God and the Trinity; creation and sin; the incarnation and the virtues; and the sacraments and the last things. Where authorities seem to disagree, Peter offers his own resolutions. After the Fourth Lateran Council endorsed his teaching on the Trinity in 1215, the Sentences became the core of the theology curriculum: Alexander of Hales introduced lectures on it at Paris in the 1220s, and commenting on it became a stage in every theologian's career. Thousands of commentaries survive, and it was only displaced by Aquinas's Summa in the 16th century.
