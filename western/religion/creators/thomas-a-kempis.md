# Thomas à Kempis

**c. 1380 – 1471** · German  
Born: Kempen, Rhineland · Died: Mount Saint Agnes, near Zwolle  
Movements & eras: [Medieval scholasticism & mysticism](../movements/scholasticism-mysticism.md)  

> Augustinian canon of the Devotio Moderna movement, traditionally regarded as the author of The Imitation of Christ, the most widely read Christian devotional book.

## Biography

Thomas Hemerken was born at Kempen near Düsseldorf and educated at Deventer in the Netherlands under the Brethren of the Common Life, a community of the reform movement known as the Devotio Moderna, or 'modern devotion'. In 1399 he entered the house of Augustinian canons regular at Mount Saint Agnes near Zwolle, where he lived for seventy years as a priest, copyist, chronicler and novice master. He copied the whole Bible by hand, and wrote sermons, biographies and devotional works.

The Imitation of Christ circulated anonymously from about 1418, and its authorship was disputed for centuries, with candidates including the chancellor Jean Gerson. Most scholars now accept Thomas as author or compiler, partly because an autograph manuscript of 1441 in his hand survives, though some see the book as drawing on earlier Devotio Moderna writings. Its call to humility, inner life and personal devotion to Christ appealed to Catholics and Protestants alike.

## Key texts & works

### The Imitation of Christ (c. 1418–1427)

- **Date:** c. 1418–1427
- **Type:** Devotional book
- **Where written / held:** Mount Saint Agnes, near Zwolle; autograph of 1441 in the Royal Library, Brussels
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Imitation%20of%20Christ%20Thomas%20%C3%A0%20Kempis)

The Imitation of Christ consists of four books of short chapters: counsel for the spiritual life, on the inner life, on inner consolation in the form of a dialogue between Christ and the disciple, and on the sacrament of the eucharist. It urges humility, detachment from the world, self-knowledge and personal devotion to Christ, often in pithy sayings, and is sceptical of learning that does not lead to holiness. It circulated anonymously and was attributed to various authors before scholarly consensus settled on Thomas à Kempis, whose autograph copy of 1441 survives. It has been translated into more languages than almost any other book, and read by figures as different as Ignatius of Loyola, John Wesley and Thomas More.
