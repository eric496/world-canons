# The Masoretes

**c. 7th–10th century** · Jewish  
Born: Tiberias and Babylonia · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Rabbinic Judaism](../movements/rabbinic-judaism.md), [Medieval Jewish thought](../movements/medieval-jewish-thought.md)  

> Jewish scribe-scholars who fixed the vowels, accents and spelling of the Hebrew Bible, producing the Masoretic Text used in synagogues ever since.

## Biography

The Masoretes, from the Hebrew masorah, 'tradition', were families of Jewish scholars active between about the 7th and 10th centuries, chiefly in Tiberias in Galilee and in Babylonia. Hebrew was written without vowels, and the correct reading of scripture had been preserved orally. The Masoretes invented systems of vowel points and cantillation accents, and compiled marginal notes counting letters, words and unusual spellings to guard the text against error. The most famous family was that of Ben Asher in Tiberias, whose Aaron ben Asher is traditionally connected with the Aleppo Codex.

The Masoretic Text became the authoritative Hebrew Bible of Judaism and the basis of the Old Testament in most Protestant translations and of modern critical editions. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls showed that the consonantal text the Masoretes preserved goes back, in many books, to forms already current before the Christian era, while also revealing that other textual traditions once existed alongside it.

### Aleppo Codex (c. 930)

[![Aleppo Codex](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/Aleppo_Codex_Joshua_1_1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aleppo_Codex_Joshua_1_1.jpg)

<sub>Aleppo Codex, the opening of the Book of Joshua Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aleppo_Codex_Joshua_1_1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 930
- **Type:** Manuscript (Hebrew Bible codex)
- **Where written / held:** Israel Museum (Shrine of the Book), Jerusalem
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Aleppo%20Codex%20The%20Masoretes)

The Aleppo Codex is a parchment codex of the whole Hebrew Bible written around 930 in Tiberias. Its consonantal text was copied by the scribe Shlomo ben Buya'a, and the vowel points, accents and Masoretic notes were added, according to its colophon, by Aaron ben Asher, the leading Masorete of his day. Maimonides is believed to have used it when he set out rules for writing Torah scrolls, which gave it unique prestige. After passing through Jerusalem and Cairo it was kept for some five centuries by the Jewish community of Aleppo in Syria. During riots in December 1947 the synagogue was burned and large parts of the codex, including most of the Torah, went missing; the surviving leaves were smuggled to Israel in 1957–58. It is the basis of the Hebrew University Bible Project edition, and the fate of the missing pages remains the subject of investigation.

### Leningrad Codex (1008–1010)

[![Leningrad Codex](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Leningrad_Codex_Folio_474a.jpg/1280px-Leningrad_Codex_Folio_474a.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Leningrad_Codex_Folio_474a.jpg)

<sub>Leningrad Codex, folio 474a, a decorated 'carpet page' Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Leningrad_Codex_Folio_474a.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1008–1010
- **Type:** Manuscript (Hebrew Bible codex)
- **Where written / held:** National Library of Russia, St Petersburg
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Leningrad%20Codex%20The%20Masoretes)

Copied in Cairo around 1008–1010 by the scribe Samuel ben Jacob, who states that he corrected it according to manuscripts of the Ben Asher school, the Leningrad Codex contains the entire Hebrew Bible with vowels, accents and Masoretic notes. It also includes 'carpet pages' of geometric decoration, with micrographic Masoretic notes written in tiny letters forming patterns. The manuscript was acquired by the Karaite collector Abraham Firkovich and came to the Imperial Public Library in St Petersburg in the 19th century. Because the Aleppo Codex is incomplete, the Leningrad Codex is the basis of the scholarly editions Biblia Hebraica (from its third edition, 1937), Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia and Biblia Hebraica Quinta, and so of most modern translations of the Old Testament.
