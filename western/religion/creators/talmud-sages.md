# Sages of the Talmud (Amoraim and Savoraim)

**c. 200–600** · Jewish  
Born: Galilee and Babylonia · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Rabbinic Judaism](../movements/rabbinic-judaism.md)  

> The generations of rabbis in the academies of Galilee and Babylonia whose discussions of the Mishnah were compiled into the Jerusalem and Babylonian Talmuds.

## Biography

After the Mishnah, rabbis called Amoraim ('interpreters') debated and expanded its teachings in academies in Galilee, at Tiberias, Sepphoris and Caesarea, and in Babylonia, at Sura, Pumbedita and Nehardea. Famous names include Rav and Shmuel in 3rd-century Babylonia, Rabbi Yohanan in Tiberias, and Abaye and Rava in the 4th century. Their discussions, legal rulings, stories and scriptural interpretations were transmitted largely orally.

The Jerusalem Talmud was completed around 400 and the much larger Babylonian Talmud over the following two centuries; scholars attribute its final editing to anonymous later sages, the Stammaim or Savoraim, whose work is still being studied. Together with collections of midrash, these texts formed the core of Jewish learning, and the Babylonian Talmud became the central text of Jewish study for the next fifteen centuries.

### Jerusalem Talmud (c. 350–400)

[![Jerusalem Talmud](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/98/Yerushalmi_Talmud.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yerushalmi_Talmud.jpg)

<sub>A page of the Jerusalem Talmud Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yerushalmi_Talmud.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 350–400
- **Type:** Rabbinic commentary and law
- **Where written / held:** Tiberias and Sepphoris, Galilee; only complete manuscript, Leiden University Library
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jerusalem%20Talmud%20Sages%20of%20the%20Talmud%20%28Amoraim%20and%20Savoraim%29)

Despite its name, the Jerusalem Talmud (Talmud Yerushalmi) was compiled in Galilee, chiefly in Tiberias, in the late 4th century, when Jews were barred from Jerusalem. It records the discussions of the Amoraim of the Land of Israel on the Mishnah, in Western Aramaic and Hebrew, covering the first four orders and part of the sixth. It is especially important for laws connected with agriculture in the land of Israel. Its editing appears hurried and incomplete, perhaps because of political pressures in the late Roman period. Less studied than the Babylonian Talmud, it has been the subject of renewed scholarly attention. The only complete manuscript, copied in 1289, is in Leiden, and the first printed edition was published by Daniel Bomberg in Venice in 1523–24.

### Midrash Rabbah (c. 400–1200 (individual collections))

- **Date:** c. 400–1200 (individual collections)
- **Type:** Rabbinic biblical interpretation
- **Where written / held:** Land of Israel and elsewhere; first printed Constantinople and Venice, 16th century
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Midrash%20Rabbah%20Sages%20of%20the%20Talmud%20%28Amoraim%20and%20Savoraim%29)

Midrash is the rabbinic art of interpreting scripture, filling gaps in the biblical narrative, drawing out laws and morals, and linking distant verses. Midrash Rabbah is a set of ten collections on Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy and on the Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes and Esther. They were compiled at very different times: Genesis Rabbah and Leviticus Rabbah in the Land of Israel around the 5th century, others as late as the 12th. They were grouped together only in the printed editions of the 16th century. Their parables and stories, such as Abraham smashing his father's idols, became a common heritage of Jewish teaching and preaching.

### Babylonian Talmud (c. 500–600 (final editing debated))

[![Babylonian Talmud](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ca/Talmud-Druck_von_Daniel_Bomberg_und_Ambrosius_Froben.jpg/1280px-Talmud-Druck_von_Daniel_Bomberg_und_Ambrosius_Froben.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Talmud-Druck_von_Daniel_Bomberg_und_Ambrosius_Froben.jpg)

<sub>Pages from 16th-century printed Talmud editions by Daniel Bomberg and Ambrosius Froben Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Talmud-Druck_von_Daniel_Bomberg_und_Ambrosius_Froben.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 500–600 (final editing debated)
- **Type:** Rabbinic commentary and law
- **Where written / held:** Academies of Babylonia (Sura and Pumbedita); oldest complete manuscript, Munich, 1342
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Babylonian%20Talmud%20Sages%20of%20the%20Talmud%20%28Amoraim%20and%20Savoraim%29)

The Babylonian Talmud (Talmud Bavli) consists of the Mishnah together with the Gemara, the discussions of generations of Babylonian rabbis, in Aramaic and Hebrew, on thirty-seven of its tractates. Its debates move associatively from legal questions to biblical interpretation, ethics, medicine, folklore and stories of the sages, preserving minority opinions as well as rulings. Scholars debate how and when it took final shape; anonymous later editors are now thought to have shaped much of its argumentative structure. Through the Geonim of Babylonia it became authoritative throughout the Jewish world. The 1520–23 Venice edition of Daniel Bomberg fixed its page layout, with Rashi's commentary and the Tosafot around the text, and the famous Vilna edition of the 1880s standardised it further. Copies were burned in Paris in 1242 and elsewhere after Christian condemnations. Its study, in yeshivas and today in the worldwide daily-page cycle, is at the heart of Jewish religious culture.
