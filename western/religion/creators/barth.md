# Karl Barth

**1886 – 1968** · Swiss  
Born: Basel · Died: Basel  
Movements & eras: [20th-century theology & ecumenism](../movements/twentieth-century-theology.md)  

> Swiss Reformed theologian whose commentary on Romans and multi-volume Church Dogmatics made him the most influential Protestant theologian of the 20th century.

## Biography

Barth studied under leading liberal theologians in Germany and became a pastor in the Swiss village of Safenwil, where the support of his teachers for German war aims in 1914 and his encounter with Paul led him to break with liberalism. His commentary on Romans (1919, rewritten 1922) made him famous. He taught at Göttingen, Münster and Bonn, was a principal author of the Barmen Declaration (1934) of the Confessing Church against Nazi control of the churches, and was expelled from Germany in 1935. From then until his retirement he was professor in Basel.

Barth's theology, called dialectical or neo-orthodox, insisted that God is known only through his self-revelation in Jesus Christ, not through human religion, culture or natural reason. His unfinished Church Dogmatics runs to over nine thousand pages. He influenced Protestant and Catholic theology alike.

### The Epistle to the Romans (Der Römerbrief) (1919; rewritten 1922)

- **Date:** 1919; rewritten 1922
- **Type:** Biblical commentary
- **Where written / held:** Bern (1919) and Munich (1922)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Epistle%20to%20the%20Romans%20%28Der%20R%C3%B6merbrief%29%20Karl%20Barth)

Written while Barth was a village pastor in Safenwil, the commentary rejected the historical-critical approach and liberal theology of his teachers, reading Paul as speaking directly to the present about the 'infinite qualitative distinction' between God and humanity, a phrase borrowed from Kierkegaard. The completely rewritten second edition of 1922 was even more radical, describing God's revelation as a crisis that judges all human religion, culture and morality. The Catholic theologian Karl Adam said it fell 'like a bomb on the playground of the theologians'. It launched the movement known as dialectical theology or neo-orthodoxy.

### Barmen Theological Declaration (1934)

- **Date:** 1934
- **Type:** Confession of faith
- **Where written / held:** Synod of Barmen, Wuppertal, Germany
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Barmen%20Theological%20Declaration%20Karl%20Barth)

In May 1934 representatives of Lutheran, Reformed and United churches met at Barmen in Wuppertal to oppose the 'German Christians', who supported Nazi ideology and sought to bring the church under the Reich. Its six theses, largely drafted by Barth, declare that Jesus Christ is the one Word of God that the church must obey, rejecting other sources of revelation and the claim of the state to be the church's master. It does not explicitly mention the persecution of the Jews, an omission later criticised by many, including Barth himself. The Declaration became the founding document of the Confessing Church and has been adopted as a confessional standard by several churches.

### Church Dogmatics (Die kirchliche Dogmatik) (1932–1967 (unfinished))

- **Date:** 1932–1967 (unfinished)
- **Type:** Systematic theology
- **Where written / held:** Munich and Zurich
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Church%20Dogmatics%20%28Die%20kirchliche%20Dogmatik%29%20Karl%20Barth)

Barth began the Church Dogmatics in Bonn in 1932 and continued it in Basel until 1967, producing over 9,000 pages in four volumes divided into thirteen part-volumes: the Word of God, the doctrine of God, creation and reconciliation; a planned fifth volume on redemption was never written. Everything is centred on God's self-revelation in Jesus Christ. Among its most influential parts are its reconstruction of the doctrine of election, in which Christ is both the electing God and the elected human being, and its extensive small-print discussions of the Bible and the history of theology. It is widely studied by Protestant and Catholic theologians.
