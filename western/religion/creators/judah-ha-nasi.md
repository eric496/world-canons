# Judah ha-Nasi

**c. 135 – c. 217** · Jewish (Roman Judea)  
Born: Galilee · Died: Sepphoris or Beit She'arim  
Movements & eras: [Rabbinic Judaism](../movements/rabbinic-judaism.md)  

> Patriarch of the Jews of Roman Palestine, traditionally credited with compiling and editing the Mishnah, the first great code of rabbinic law.

## Biography

Judah, called ha-Nasi ('the Prince' or 'Patriarch') or simply Rabbi, was the head of the Jewish community in Roman Palestine in the late 2nd and early 3rd centuries. He was a descendant of the great sage Hillel, wealthy, and according to rabbinic tradition on friendly terms with a Roman emperor named Antoninus. He led the rabbinic academy and court at Beit She'arim and later Sepphoris in Galilee, and was buried in the necropolis of Beit She'arim.

Rabbinic tradition holds that Judah gathered and arranged the oral teachings of earlier sages, the Tannaim, into the Mishnah, completed around AD 200. Modern scholars accept his central editorial role while debating whether the Mishnah was written down in his time or long transmitted orally, and how much was added after him. His work became the basis of both Talmuds and of all later Jewish law.

## Key texts & works

### Mishnah (c. 200)

- **Date:** c. 200
- **Type:** Code of law
- **Where written / held:** Galilee; earliest complete manuscript, the Kaufmann Codex, in Budapest
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mishnah%20Judah%20ha-Nasi)

The Mishnah collects the legal teachings of the rabbis known as Tannaim, from roughly the 1st century BC to about 200 AD, edited according to tradition by Judah ha-Nasi. Written in a concise Hebrew, it is arranged in six orders covering agriculture and blessings, Sabbath and festivals, women and marriage, damages and civil law, holy things and sacrifices, and purity, divided into sixty-three tractates. It often records disagreements between named sages, such as the houses of Hillel and Shammai, alongside anonymous rulings. Remarkably, much of it discusses Temple rituals that could no longer be performed, showing how the rabbis preserved and reinterpreted the tradition. The tractate Avot, 'Sayings of the Fathers', is a widely loved collection of ethical maxims. The Mishnah became the basis of both Talmuds and of the study of Jewish law ever since.
