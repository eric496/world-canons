# Robert Venturi

**1925 – 2018** · American  
Born: Philadelphia · Died: Philadelphia  
Styles: [Postmodernism](../movements/postmodernism.md)  

> American architect and theorist whose book Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture and Vanna Venturi House launched postmodernism.

## Biography

Venturi studied at Princeton, won the Rome Prize, and worked for Eero Saarinen and Louis Kahn before opening his own practice in Philadelphia, where he was joined by Denise Scott Brown, his wife from 1967, and John Rauch. His mother's house in Chestnut Hill (1962–64), Guild House for the elderly, and the Sainsbury Wing of the National Gallery in London (1991) are among the firm's best-known works.

In Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) Venturi defended ambiguity and the richness of historical architecture against modernist purity, answering Mies's 'less is more' with 'less is a bore'. With Scott Brown and Steven Izenour he wrote Learning from Las Vegas (1972), which took the commercial strip seriously and distinguished buildings that are sculptural 'ducks' from 'decorated sheds'. He received the Pritzker Prize in 1991; that Scott Brown was not included became a long-running controversy.

## Key buildings

### Vanna Venturi House (1962–1964)

- **Date:** 1962–1964
- **Type:** House
- **Location:** Chestnut Hill, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Vanna%20Venturi%20House%20Robert%20Venturi)

Robert Venturi designed this house for his widowed mother, Vanna, revising it repeatedly. Its front façade is a broad gable, split in the middle above the entrance, with a thin applied arch and a strip moulding, and windows of different shapes placed according to the rooms behind, not the symmetry of the front. Inside, a fireplace and chimney compete with the staircase for the centre of the house, and the stair narrows awkwardly around it. Small in size, the house plays with the conventional image of home, full of the complexity and contradiction Venturi was championing in his book of 1966. It received the American Institute of Architects' Twenty-five Year Award in 1989 and is often called the first postmodern building.
