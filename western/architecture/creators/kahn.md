# Louis Kahn

**1901 – 1974** · American  
Born: Kuressaare, Estonia (then Russian Empire) · Died: New York City  
Styles: [Late & Expressive Modernism](../movements/late-modernism.md), [Brutalism](../movements/brutalism.md)  

> American architect of the Salk Institute, the Kimbell Art Museum and the National Assembly of Bangladesh, who brought monumental gravity and light back to modern architecture.

## Biography

Kahn emigrated to Philadelphia as a child, studied at the University of Pennsylvania in its Beaux-Arts tradition, and worked through the Depression on public housing. Only in his fifties, after a stay at the American Academy in Rome in 1950–51, did he find his own voice, with the Yale University Art Gallery and the Richards Medical Research Laboratories. His mature works include the Salk Institute in La Jolla (1959–65), the Indian Institute of Management in Ahmedabad, the National Assembly in Dhaka, the Kimbell Art Museum and the Yale Center for British Art. He died in debt in a station washroom in New York.

Kahn asked what a building wants to be, and answered with primary geometries, massive walls of concrete and brick, and a careful distinction between served and servant spaces. His buildings are shaped by daylight, which he called the giver of all presences. A revered teacher in Philadelphia, he influenced architects from Mario Botta to Tadao Ando.

### Salk Institute for Biological Studies (1959–1965)

[![Salk Institute for Biological Studies](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Salk_Institute_2.jpg/1280px-Salk_Institute_2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Salk_Institute_2.jpg)

<sub>Salk Institute for Biological Studies, La Jolla, California, United States Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Salk_Institute_2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1959–1965
- **Type:** Research institute
- **Location:** La Jolla, California, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Salk%20Institute%20for%20Biological%20Studies%20Louis%20Kahn)

Jonas Salk, developer of the polio vaccine, founded an institute for biological research on clifftops above the Pacific and asked Kahn for a place where scientists and artists might meet. Kahn designed two mirror-image laboratory buildings with column-free floors alternating with service floors for pipes and ducts, which can be changed as research changes. Facing an empty travertine courtyard are angled concrete towers with teak-panelled studies for the scientists. On the advice of the Mexican architect Luis Barragán, Kahn left the courtyard without trees, a 'façade to the sky', with a thin channel of water running toward the ocean. Built in finely finished concrete, the institute is among the most admired buildings of its era, and a meeting hall Kahn planned was never built.

### National Parliament House, Dhaka (Jatiya Sangsad Bhaban) (1962–1982)

[![National Parliament House, Dhaka (Jatiya Sangsad Bhaban)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/National_Assembly_of_Bangladesh_%2810%29.jpg/1280px-National_Assembly_of_Bangladesh_%2810%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:National_Assembly_of_Bangladesh_%2810%29.jpg)

<sub>National Parliament House, Dhaka (Jatiya Sangsad Bhaban), Dhaka, Bangladesh Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:National_Assembly_of_Bangladesh_%2810%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1962–1982
- **Type:** Parliament building
- **Location:** Dhaka, Bangladesh
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=National%20Parliament%20House%2C%20Dhaka%20%28Jatiya%20Sangsad%20Bhaban%29%20Louis%20Kahn)

In 1962 the government of Pakistan commissioned Kahn to design a second capital complex at Dhaka, then the capital of East Pakistan. After the war of independence of 1971 the new nation of Bangladesh continued the project as its parliament. The assembly building is a cluster of massive concrete volumes inlaid with bands of white marble, around the central chamber, and pierced by huge circles, triangles and rectangles that bring light into the interior and shade it from the tropical sun. A lake surrounds much of it. Kahn died in 1974, and the building was completed in 1982 by his former associates. Its story is told in his son Nathaniel Kahn's documentary My Architect (2003).
