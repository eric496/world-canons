# Greek master builders

**active 7th–4th century BC** · Greek  
Styles: [Ancient Greek](../movements/ancient-greek.md)  

> The architects of most Greek temples, theatres and gateways are unknown or known only by name from ancient writers. This entry gathers the buildings of the Greek world whose designers cannot be firmly identified.

## Biography

Greek architecture was the work of an architekton, a master builder who designed the building, drew up specifications, ordered stone and directed teams of quarrymen, masons and sculptors. A handful of names survive in inscriptions and in writers such as Vitruvius, Plutarch and Pausanias: Mnesicles for the Propylaea of the Acropolis, Polykleitos the Younger for the theatre at Epidaurus, and others. For many temples, including the great Doric temples of the Greek colonies in southern Italy and Sicily, no names are recorded at all.

What these builders created was a disciplined system rather than a sequence of individual styles. The Doric and Ionic orders fixed the relations of column, capital, frieze and cornice, and generations of architects refined proportions, spacing and optical corrections. Their temples, theatres and propylaea became the vocabulary that Rome adopted and that the Renaissance, Neoclassicism and even modern architects kept rediscovering.

### Second Temple of Hera, Paestum (c. 460 BC)

[![Second Temple of Hera, Paestum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fb/Veduta_di_Paestum_2010.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Veduta_di_Paestum_2010.jpg)

<sub>Second Temple of Hera, Paestum, Paestum, Campania, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Veduta_di_Paestum_2010.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 460 BC
- **Type:** Doric temple
- **Location:** Paestum, Campania, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Second%20Temple%20of%20Hera%2C%20Paestum%20Greek%20master%20builders)

Paestum, the Greek colony of Poseidonia south of Naples, preserves three Doric temples. The largest and best preserved, built around 460 BC and traditionally called the Temple of Neptune, is now usually identified as a second temple of Hera, though its dedication is uncertain. It has six by fourteen massive fluted columns with a pronounced swelling, or entasis, and its interior still carries two tiers of smaller columns that supported the roof. Built of local travertine, once stuccoed and painted, it shows the mature Doric order shortly before the Parthenon. The site was abandoned in the Middle Ages and rediscovered in the eighteenth century, when engravings by Piranesi and visits by architects such as Soufflot made its heavy, primitive-seeming columns a powerful influence on the Greek Revival.

### Propylaea of the Acropolis (437–432 BC)

[![Propylaea of the Acropolis](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/East_Facade_of_the_Propylaea_on_July_23%2C_2019.jpg/1280px-East_Facade_of_the_Propylaea_on_July_23%2C_2019.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:East_Facade_of_the_Propylaea_on_July_23%2C_2019.jpg)

<sub>Propylaea of the Acropolis, Acropolis, Athens, Greece Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:East_Facade_of_the_Propylaea_on_July_23%2C_2019.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 437–432 BC
- **Type:** Monumental gateway
- **Location:** Acropolis, Athens, Greece
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Propylaea%20of%20the%20Acropolis%20Greek%20master%20builders)

The Propylaea, designed according to Plutarch by the architect Mnesicles, is the grand marble gateway at the west end of the Acropolis, begun as soon as the Parthenon was complete. Facing the ascending visitor are two Doric porches like temple fronts, and the central passage through which processions climbed is lined with Ionic columns, an early combination of the two orders in one building. A wing to the north, the Pinakotheke, displayed paintings. The building was never finished: work stopped with the Peloponnesian War, and lifting bosses still project from some blocks. Later used as a palace and a gunpowder store, it inspired monumental gateways from the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin to the Propyläen in Munich.

### Erechtheion (421–406 BC)

[![Erechtheion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/Erechtheum_Acropolis_Athens.jpg/1280px-Erechtheum_Acropolis_Athens.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Erechtheum_Acropolis_Athens.jpg)

<sub>Erechtheion, Acropolis, Athens, Greece Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Erechtheum_Acropolis_Athens.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 421–406 BC
- **Type:** Ionic temple
- **Location:** Acropolis, Athens, Greece
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Erechtheion%20Greek%20master%20builders)

The Erechtheion, on the north side of the Acropolis, housed the ancient olive-wood statue of Athena Polias and shrines to Poseidon, the legendary king Erechtheus and other cults on a site holding sacred marks of the contest between Athena and Poseidon. Its unknown architect, sometimes thought to be Mnesicles, resolved a sloping site and multiple functions in an asymmetrical building with porches on three sides and floors at different levels. The finely carved Ionic columns and the south porch, where six maidens, the Caryatids, serve as columns, made it the model for the Ionic order in later centuries. Building accounts inscribed in stone record the wages of the citizens, foreigners and slaves who worked on it. One Caryatid is in the British Museum; the others are in the Acropolis Museum and copies stand in their place.

### Theatre of Epidaurus (c. 340–300 BC)

[![Theatre of Epidaurus](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/Theatre_of_Epidaurus_OLC.jpg/1280px-Theatre_of_Epidaurus_OLC.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Theatre_of_Epidaurus_OLC.jpg)

<sub>The theatre of Epidaurus seen from the upper rows Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Theatre_of_Epidaurus_OLC.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 340–300 BC
- **Type:** Theatre
- **Location:** Epidaurus, Peloponnese, Greece
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Theatre%20of%20Epidaurus%20Greek%20master%20builders)

The theatre in the sanctuary of Asclepius at Epidaurus, a healing centre that drew pilgrims from across Greece, was built in the late fourth century BC and extended in the second. Pausanias attributed it to Polykleitos the Younger, an attribution modern scholars treat with caution. Its limestone seats, for around 13,000 spectators, rise in a slightly more than semicircular bowl around a full circular orchestra, set into a natural hillside. The theatre is famous for its acoustics, which carry even a whisper from the orchestra to the upper rows, an effect researchers have linked to the way the rows of seats filter low-frequency noise. Buried for centuries and excavated from 1881, it is again used for performances of ancient drama each summer.
