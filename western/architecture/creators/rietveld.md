# Gerrit Rietveld

**1888 – 1964** · Dutch  
Born: Utrecht · Died: Utrecht  
Styles: [Modernism & the International Style](../movements/modernism.md)  

> Dutch furniture maker and architect of De Stijl, designer of the Red and Blue Chair and the Rietveld Schröder House in Utrecht.

## Biography

Rietveld trained in his father's furniture workshop and set up as a cabinetmaker in Utrecht in 1917. His Red and Blue Chair, made around 1918 and painted in primary colours in the early 1920s, brought him into the De Stijl group of Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. In 1924, with his client Truus Schröder-Schräder, he designed the Schröder House, his first building. He later designed social housing, furniture for mass production such as the Zig-Zag chair, and the Dutch pavilion at the Venice Biennale; the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam was completed after his death.

Rietveld translated Mondrian's paintings into three dimensions: planes, lines and primary colours that seem to slide past each other in space. The Schröder House, with sliding partitions that open its upper floor into a single space, is the only building fully realised on De Stijl principles and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Key buildings

### Rietveld Schröder House (1924)

- **Date:** 1924
- **Type:** House
- **Location:** Utrecht, Netherlands
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rietveld%20Schr%C3%B6der%20House%20Gerrit%20Rietveld)

Truus Schröder-Schräder, a widow with three children, commissioned Rietveld to design a house at the end of a row of brick houses on the edge of Utrecht, and she took an active part in the design. The house is composed of planes, lines and coloured elements, white and grey walls, red, yellow, blue and black lines, that seem to slide apart, with balconies and roof slabs projecting beyond the walls. The ground floor has conventional rooms, but the upper floor can be opened as a single space or divided into rooms by sliding and folding partitions. Windows open at right angles to the façade so that the corner disappears. Schröder lived there until her death in 1985. It is a museum and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
