**1404 – 1472** · Florentine  
Born: Genoa · Died: Rome

> Humanist, writer and architect whose treatise On the Art of Building was the first Renaissance theory of architecture, and whose façades and churches applied ancient forms with new rigour.

<sub>Portrait: Alberti, self-portrait plaquette, c. 1435, National Gallery of Art, Washington · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Leon_Battista_Alberti%2C_Self-Portrait%2C_c._1435%2C_NGA_43845.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Alberti was born in exile, the illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant family, and studied law at Bologna before entering the papal bureaucracy. A true universal man, he wrote dialogues on the family, Latin comedies, a treatise on painting that codified perspective, and works on sculpture, cryptography and surveying. From the late 1440s he advised popes, the Malatesta of Rimini, the Rucellai of Florence and the Gonzaga of Mantua on their buildings, usually providing designs that others executed.

His De re aedificatoria, completed around 1452, recast Vitruvius for the modern world and defined beauty as a harmony of parts to which nothing can be added or taken away. His buildings put the theory into practice: the classical orders layered on the Palazzo Rucellai, the Roman triumphal arch adapted for the churches of Rimini and Mantua, and the geometric façade of Santa Maria Novella. Alberti established the architect as an intellectual who designs rather than builds.

### Palazzo Rucellai (c. 1446–1451)

[![Palazzo Rucellai](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/Via_della_Vigna_Nuova_18%2C_palazzo_rucellai%2C_02%2C0.jpg/1280px-Via_della_Vigna_Nuova_18%2C_palazzo_rucellai%2C_02%2C0.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Via_della_Vigna_Nuova_18%2C_palazzo_rucellai%2C_02%2C0.jpg)

<sub>Palazzo Rucellai, Florence, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Via_della_Vigna_Nuova_18%2C_palazzo_rucellai%2C_02%2C0.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1446–1451
- **Type:** Palace
- **Location:** Florence, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Palazzo%20Rucellai%20Leon%20Battista%20Alberti)

The merchant Giovanni Rucellai commissioned a new façade for his family's houses on the Via della Vigna Nuova, and it is generally attributed to Alberti, with Bernardo Rossellino as builder. For the first time since antiquity, a façade is articulated by three storeys of flat pilasters with entablatures, a sequence derived from the Colosseum, over a smooth, gently rusticated wall. Paired windows sit between the pilasters, and a heavy cornice crowns the top. The base imitates the diamond-pattern opus reticulatum of Roman walls. The façade was apparently left unfinished, its right edge still ragged. Its systematic use of the orders on a domestic building became a model for Renaissance palaces, especially in Rome.

### Façade of Santa Maria Novella (1456–1470)

[![Façade of Santa Maria Novella](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Florence%2C_Santa_Maria_Novella%2C_facade.jpg/1280px-Florence%2C_Santa_Maria_Novella%2C_facade.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Florence%2C_Santa_Maria_Novella%2C_facade.jpg)

<sub>Façade of Santa Maria Novella, Florence, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Florence%2C_Santa_Maria_Novella%2C_facade.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1456–1470
- **Type:** Church façade
- **Location:** Florence, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fa%C3%A7ade%20of%20Santa%20Maria%20Novella%20Leon%20Battista%20Alberti)

The Dominican church of Santa Maria Novella was built in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and its façade had been begun in Gothic style with marble arches and tomb niches. Giovanni Rucellai paid for its completion to Alberti's design, and his name is inscribed in the frieze. Alberti kept the Gothic lower parts but added a classical portal, a tall attic and a pediment-crowned upper storey linked to the lower by great scrolls, or volutes, that hide the aisle roofs, an invention imitated on hundreds of churches. The whole façade is based on squares and simple ratios. The inlaid white and green marble belongs to the Tuscan Romanesque tradition of San Miniato. Inside, the church holds Masaccio's Trinity and frescoes by Ghirlandaio.

### Sant'Andrea, Mantua (1472–1790)

[![Sant'Andrea, Mantua](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/MantovaBasilicaSantAndrea_cutnpaste_over_intrusions.jpg/960px-MantovaBasilicaSantAndrea_cutnpaste_over_intrusions.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:MantovaBasilicaSantAndrea_cutnpaste_over_intrusions.jpg)

<sub>Sant'Andrea, Mantua, Mantua, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:MantovaBasilicaSantAndrea_cutnpaste_over_intrusions.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1472–1790
- **Type:** Basilica
- **Location:** Mantua, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sant%27Andrea%2C%20Mantua%20Leon%20Battista%20Alberti)

Ludovico Gonzaga, marquis of Mantua, commissioned Alberti to design a church to house a relic of Christ's blood, and building began in 1472, the year of Alberti's death. Alberti replaced aisles with a single, huge barrel-vaulted nave lined by alternating large chapels and smaller piers, a rhythm repeated on the façade, which combines a temple front with a triumphal arch. The coffered vault deliberately evokes Roman baths and basilicas. Construction continued for centuries: the dome by Filippo Juvarra was completed in the eighteenth century. The single-nave, chapel-lined plan anticipated the Gesù in Rome and Counter-Reformation churches. The painter Andrea Mantegna is buried in one of its chapels.
