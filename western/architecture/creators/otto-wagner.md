# Otto Wagner

**1841 – 1918** · Austrian  
Born: Penzing, near Vienna · Died: Vienna  
Styles: [Art Nouveau & Modernisme](../movements/art-nouveau.md), [Modernism & the International Style](../movements/modernism.md)  

> Viennese architect and teacher who moved from historicism to the Secession and pioneered a functional modern architecture, as in the Postal Savings Bank.

## Biography

Wagner studied in Vienna and Berlin and spent decades building apartment houses in the historical styles of the Ringstrasse. In 1894 he became professor at the Vienna Academy of Fine Arts and was commissioned to design the stations, bridges and viaducts of the Vienna Stadtbahn railway. His book Moderne Architektur (1896) argued that architecture must reflect modern life, materials and construction. He joined the Vienna Secession in 1899, and built the Majolikahaus, the church of St Leopold at Steinhof (1904–07) and the Austrian Postal Savings Bank (1904–06, 1910–12).

Wagner's later buildings are clad in thin panels of marble or tile fixed with visible bolts, and his interiors use aluminium, glass and steel with a new frankness. As a teacher he formed a generation of architects, including Josef Hoffmann, Joseph Maria Olbrich and Jože Plečnik. He bridges the decorative Secession and the functional modernism of the twentieth century.

## Key buildings

### Austrian Postal Savings Bank (1904–1906; 1910–1912)

- **Date:** 1904–1906; 1910–1912
- **Type:** Bank
- **Location:** Vienna, Austria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Austrian%20Postal%20Savings%20Bank%20Otto%20Wagner)

The Postsparkasse, the Austrian postal savings bank founded in 1883, held a competition for its headquarters on the Ringstrasse, won by Otto Wagner. The façade is clad in thin marble slabs fixed by aluminium-capped bolts that make the means of attachment visible, while aluminium figures and acroteria crown the cornice. The main banking hall has a glass ceiling carried on slender steel columns, with a glass floor bringing light to the level below, and aluminium heating ducts and fittings, then a new material, all designed by Wagner. The building expresses his argument that architecture should be shaped by modern needs and materials. It is still used by the bank and houses a museum.
