# Ictinus

**active mid-5th century BC** · Greek (Athenian)  
Styles: [Ancient Greek](../movements/ancient-greek.md)  

> Athenian architect who, with Callicrates, designed the Parthenon on the Acropolis under the direction of Phidias. Ancient sources also credit him with the temple of Apollo at Bassae and the hall of the Mysteries at Eleusis.

## Biography

Almost nothing is known of Ictinus's life. Ancient writers name him as an architect of the Parthenon, built between 447 and 432 BC as part of Pericles's programme to rebuild the Acropolis after the Persian sack, and Vitruvius records that he wrote a book on the temple together with one Carpion; it is lost. The travel writer Pausanias says Ictinus designed the temple of Apollo Epicurius at Bassae in Arcadia, and Plutarch and Strabo connect him with the Telesterion, the initiation hall at Eleusis.

The Parthenon shows an architect working with exceptional sophistication. It combines a Doric exterior with Ionic elements such as a continuous sculpted frieze, and its floor, columns and entablature are subtly curved and inclined so that the building reads as alive rather than rigid. Bassae, with its early Corinthian capital and inward-facing half-columns, suggests the same interest in experiment. Ictinus has stood ever since as the archetype of the classical architect.

## Key buildings

### Parthenon (447–432 BC)

[![Parthenon](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/da/The_Parthenon_in_Athens.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Parthenon_in_Athens.jpg)

<sub>Parthenon, Acropolis, Athens, Greece Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Parthenon_in_Athens.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 447–432 BC
- **Type:** Doric temple
- **Location:** Acropolis, Athens, Greece
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Parthenon%20Ictinus)

The Parthenon was built under Pericles to house Phidias's gold and ivory statue of Athena Parthenos, replacing an earlier temple destroyed by the Persians in 480 BC. Ictinus and Callicrates designed it, and Phidias oversaw its sculpture: the pediments, ninety-two metopes and a continuous Ionic frieze, probably showing the Panathenaic procession. The temple has eight by seventeen Doric columns in Pentelic marble, and almost nothing in it is straight: the platform curves upward, the columns lean slightly inward and swell, and the corner columns are thicker, refinements intended to correct optical illusions and give the building life. Over the centuries it served as a church, a mosque and a gunpowder store; a Venetian shell blew up its centre in 1687, and in the early nineteenth century Lord Elgin removed much of the sculpture to London, where its return remains a matter of dispute. Restoration has been under way since the 1970s.
