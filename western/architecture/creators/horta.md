# Victor Horta

**1861 – 1947** · Belgian  
Born: Ghent · Died: Brussels  
Styles: [Art Nouveau & Modernisme](../movements/art-nouveau.md)  

> Belgian architect whose Hôtel Tassel in Brussels (1893) is regarded as the first Art Nouveau building.

## Biography

Horta studied at the academies of Ghent and Brussels and worked for Alphonse Balat, architect to King Leopold II, on the royal greenhouses at Laeken. His Hôtel Tassel (1892–93) was followed by a series of town houses for Brussels industrialists and professionals, including the Hôtel Solvay, the Hôtel van Eetvelde and his own house and studio (1898–1901), and by the Maison du Peuple (1896–99) for the Belgian Workers' Party, demolished in 1965. After the First World War, spent partly in the United States, he turned to a more austere style, as at the Palais des Beaux-Arts in Brussels.

Horta used exposed iron and glass in domestic architecture and let the whiplash curves of the iron spread into mosaics, wall paintings, banisters and door handles, designing every detail. His open, light-filled plans around central staircases were as new as his ornament. Four of his Brussels houses are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Key buildings

### Hôtel Tassel (1892–1893)

[![Hôtel Tassel](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/34/Victor_Horta_Hotel_Tassel.JPG/960px-Victor_Horta_Hotel_Tassel.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Victor_Horta_Hotel_Tassel.JPG)

<sub>Hôtel Tassel, Brussels, Belgium Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Victor_Horta_Hotel_Tassel.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1892–1893
- **Type:** Town house
- **Location:** Brussels, Belgium
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=H%C3%B4tel%20Tassel%20Victor%20Horta)

Émile Tassel, a professor of descriptive geometry at the Free University of Brussels, commissioned his friend Victor Horta to build a house on a narrow plot in the Rue Paul-Émile Janson. Horta abandoned the conventional corridor plan of Brussels houses and arranged the rooms around an octagonal hall and a central staircase lit from above, with levels shifting by half-storeys. Exposed iron columns branch at the top like plant stems, and their whiplash curves continue in the painted walls, mosaic floor, stair rail and stained glass, all designed by Horta. The stone façade swells into a central bay window carried on iron. The house's unity of structure and ornament defined Art Nouveau. Divided into flats and altered in the twentieth century, it was restored in the 1980s and is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site with three other Horta houses.
