# Roman imperial builders

**active 1st century BC – 4th century AD** · Roman  
Styles: [Ancient Roman](../movements/ancient-roman.md)  

> The engineers, architects and workforces of Rome, mostly anonymous, who built the temples, amphitheatres, baths, aqueducts and basilicas of the empire.

## Biography

Roman building was organised on a vast scale. Emperors, magistrates and wealthy citizens commissioned public works; architects, often Greek or trained in Greek traditions, designed them; and military engineers, contractors, slaves and free craftsmen carried them out. Only a few architects are known by name, such as Rabirius for Domitian's palace or Apollodorus of Damascus for Trajan, and the designers of even the most famous buildings, including the Colosseum and Hadrian's Pantheon, are not recorded.

The Roman achievement was above all structural. Roman concrete, made with volcanic pozzolana, allowed builders to cast arches, barrel and groin vaults and domes of unprecedented size, and to standardise construction across an empire. Romans combined these vaulted spaces with Greek columns and pediments, inventing building types from the amphitheatre to the bath complex. Their ruins, studied by every later classicist, made Rome the great school of Western architecture.

### Maison Carrée (c. 16 BC – AD 4)

[![Maison Carrée](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Maison_Carree_in_Nimes_%2816%29.jpg/1280px-Maison_Carree_in_Nimes_%2816%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Maison_Carree_in_Nimes_%2816%29.jpg)

<sub>Maison Carrée, Nîmes, France Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Maison_Carree_in_Nimes_%2816%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 16 BC – AD 4
- **Type:** Roman temple
- **Location:** Nîmes, France
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Maison%20Carr%C3%A9e%20Roman%20imperial%20builders)

The Maison Carrée ('square house') at Nîmes, in the Roman province of Gallia Narbonensis, was built under Augustus and rededicated to his grandsons and adopted heirs Gaius and Lucius Caesar. It is a classic Roman temple: raised on a high podium, approached by a frontal stair, with a deep porch of six Corinthian columns and half-columns engaged in the walls of the cella along the sides and back. Its survival almost intact is due to continuous use as a meeting hall, a stable, a church and a museum. Thomas Jefferson, who saw it in 1787 and said he gazed at it for hours, used it as the model for the Virginia State Capitol, and it inspired the Madeleine in Paris. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.

### Pont du Gard (c. AD 40–60)

[![Pont du Gard](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Pont_du_Gard_BLS.jpg/1280px-Pont_du_Gard_BLS.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pont_du_Gard_BLS.jpg)

<sub>Pont du Gard, Vers-Pont-du-Gard, near Nîmes, France Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pont_du_Gard_BLS.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 40–60
- **Type:** Aqueduct bridge
- **Location:** Vers-Pont-du-Gard, near Nîmes, France
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Pont%20du%20Gard%20Roman%20imperial%20builders)

The Pont du Gard carried the aqueduct that brought water about fifty kilometres from springs near Uzès to the Roman city of Nemausus (Nîmes). It is usually dated to the mid-first century AD. Three tiers of arches, built of huge limestone blocks laid without mortar, rise nearly fifty metres above the Gardon river, and the water channel on top descends by only a few centimetres across the bridge, part of a gradient for the whole aqueduct of about twenty-five centimetres per kilometre. Projecting stones and ridges left in the masonry supported scaffolding. The aqueduct fell out of use by the sixth century, but the bridge survived because it served as a road crossing, and it became a celebrated stop on the Grand Tour. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### Colosseum (Flavian Amphitheatre) (AD 70–80)

[![Colosseum (Flavian Amphitheatre)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Colosseo_2020.jpg/1280px-Colosseo_2020.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Colosseo_2020.jpg)

<sub>Colosseum (Flavian Amphitheatre), Rome, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Colosseo_2020.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** AD 70–80
- **Type:** Amphitheatre
- **Location:** Rome, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Colosseum%20%28Flavian%20Amphitheatre%29%20Roman%20imperial%20builders)

The emperor Vespasian began the Flavian Amphitheatre around AD 70 on the site of the lake of Nero's Golden House, and his son Titus inaugurated it in 80 with a hundred days of games; Domitian completed it. An ellipse about 189 by 156 metres, it held perhaps 50,000 spectators, seated by rank and admitted through eighty numbered entrances. Its outer wall stacks three storeys of arches framed by Tuscan, Ionic and Corinthian half-columns beneath an attic of Corinthian pilasters, a sequence that became a rule for Renaissance architects. Under the arena, the hypogeum held animals, gladiators and lifts. After the games ended in the sixth century it served as housing, fortress and quarry; earthquakes and stone robbing reduced it to its present state. It remains the symbol of Rome.

### Pantheon (c. AD 113–125)

[![Pantheon](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Pantheon_%28Rome%29_-_Right_side_and_front.jpg/1280px-Pantheon_%28Rome%29_-_Right_side_and_front.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pantheon_%28Rome%29_-_Right_side_and_front.jpg)

<sub>Pantheon, Rome, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pantheon_%28Rome%29_-_Right_side_and_front.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 113–125
- **Type:** Temple, later church
- **Location:** Rome, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Pantheon%20Roman%20imperial%20builders)

The Pantheon was rebuilt under Hadrian on the site of a temple built by Marcus Agrippa, whose name is still inscribed on the portico; its architect is unknown, though Apollodorus of Damascus has sometimes been suggested. A traditional temple porch of sixteen granite Corinthian columns leads into a vast rotunda whose height and diameter are equal, about 43 metres, so that a sphere would fit within it. The concrete of the coffered dome grows lighter toward the top, with pumice in the aggregate, and the dome is open at the crown in an oculus nearly nine metres across, the only source of light. Consecrated as the church of Santa Maria ad Martyres in 609, it has been in use ever since and contains the tombs of Raphael and the kings of Italy. It is the most influential surviving building of antiquity, the ancestor of domed buildings from Florence to Washington.

### Hadrian's Villa (c. AD 118–138)

[![Hadrian's Villa](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Villa_Hadriana_%28Villa_Adriana_Tivoli%29_1000_03.jpg/1280px-Villa_Hadriana_%28Villa_Adriana_Tivoli%29_1000_03.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Villa_Hadriana_%28Villa_Adriana_Tivoli%29_1000_03.jpg)

<sub>Hadrian's Villa, Tivoli, near Rome, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Villa_Hadriana_%28Villa_Adriana_Tivoli%29_1000_03.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 118–138
- **Type:** Imperial villa
- **Location:** Tivoli, near Rome, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hadrian%27s%20Villa%20Roman%20imperial%20builders)

Hadrian, the most architecturally minded of Roman emperors and possibly involved in the design himself, built a retreat below the hills of Tivoli covering more than a hundred hectares. It contains over thirty buildings: palaces, baths, libraries, theatres, guest quarters and gardens, laid out on varying axes. The so-called Maritime Theatre is a round island villa within a moat, and the Canopus is a long pool framed by colonnades and statues, said to recall places Hadrian had visited in Egypt. Its domes and vaults on curved and lobed plans show Roman concrete architecture at its most inventive. Plundered for marble and sculpture after antiquity, it was excavated from the Renaissance onward and studied by Piranesi and later architects. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### Baths of Caracalla (AD 212–216)

[![Baths of Caracalla](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Baths_of_Caracalla%2C_facing_Caldarium.jpg/1280px-Baths_of_Caracalla%2C_facing_Caldarium.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Baths_of_Caracalla%2C_facing_Caldarium.jpg)

<sub>Baths of Caracalla, Rome, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Baths_of_Caracalla%2C_facing_Caldarium.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** AD 212–216
- **Type:** Public baths
- **Location:** Rome, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Baths%20of%20Caracalla%20Roman%20imperial%20builders)

The Baths of Caracalla, built under the emperors Septimius Severus and Caracalla, were among the largest public baths of Rome, used by several thousand people at a time. A central bathing block, with a cold-water hall, a warm room and a circular hot room, the caldarium, under a great dome, stood within a precinct of gardens, libraries and exercise areas. Behind the scenes, furnaces, aqueduct-fed cisterns and underground passages for slaves heated and supplied the complex. The baths functioned until the Gothic siege of 537 cut the aqueducts. Their immense brick-faced concrete ruins influenced Renaissance and modern architects; the waiting hall of New York's demolished Pennsylvania Station was modelled on them. Operas are performed in the ruins in summer.
