# Thomas Jefferson

**1743 – 1826** · American  
Born: Shadwell, Colony of Virginia · Died: Monticello, Virginia  
Styles: [Palladianism & Neoclassicism](../movements/neoclassicism.md)  
Also in: [History](../../history/creators/jefferson.md)  

> Author of the Declaration of Independence and third US president, and a self-taught architect who designed Monticello, the Virginia State Capitol and the University of Virginia.

## Biography

Jefferson learned architecture from books, above all Palladio's Four Books, and began building his hilltop house at Monticello in 1769, rebuilding it after his years in France. As minister to France (1784–89) he saw the Maison Carrée at Nîmes, which he took as the model for the Virginia State Capitol in Richmond, the first modern public building in the form of a classical temple. As secretary of state and president he promoted classicism for Washington. In retirement he founded and designed the University of Virginia, which opened in 1825.

For Jefferson architecture was a civic art: the forms of Roman republican buildings would educate the citizens of a new republic. His 'academical village', a lawn flanked by pavilions and colonnades with a Rotunda modelled on the Pantheon at its head, is one of the great American architectural achievements. His legacy is entangled with slavery: Monticello and the university were built and maintained largely by enslaved people, a history the sites now present alongside the architecture.

### Monticello (1769–1809)

<sub>Monticello, Charlottesville, Virginia, United States Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Monticello_reflected.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1769–1809
- **Type:** Plantation house
- **Location:** Charlottesville, Virginia, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Monticello%20Thomas%20Jefferson)

Jefferson began building on his 'little mountain' near Charlottesville in 1769, following Palladio's books. After his years in Paris, where he admired the domed Hôtel de Salm, he largely demolished and rebuilt the house from 1796 to 1809, adding the octagonal dome over the west front, the first on an American house. Inside are his own inventions: a two-faced clock, a dumbwaiter and a revolving bookstand. Service rooms, kitchens and stables were placed in wings sunk into the hillside under terraces, so the house appears to stand alone; Mulberry Row along the slope held the workshops and dwellings of the enslaved people who built and ran the plantation, among them the Hemings family. The house appears on the US nickel. With the University of Virginia, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### The Rotunda and Lawn, University of Virginia (1817–1826)

[![The Rotunda and Lawn, University of Virginia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/University_of_Virginia_Rotunda_2006.jpg/1280px-University_of_Virginia_Rotunda_2006.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:University_of_Virginia_Rotunda_2006.jpg)

<sub>The Rotunda and Lawn, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, Virginia, United States Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:University_of_Virginia_Rotunda_2006.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1817–1826
- **Type:** University campus
- **Location:** Charlottesville, Virginia, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Rotunda%20and%20Lawn%2C%20University%20of%20Virginia%20Thomas%20Jefferson)

Jefferson conceived the University of Virginia as an 'academical village', a community of scholars instead of a single great building. A long terraced lawn is flanked by two rows of five pavilions, each a professor's house and classroom in a different classical order taken from ancient Roman examples, linked by colonnades in front of student rooms; behind are gardens and further ranges. At the head stands the Rotunda, the library, a half-scale version of the Pantheon in Rome, completed in 1826. Enslaved workers built much of it. Its brick and white-columned style became the model of American campuses. The Rotunda burned in 1895 and was rebuilt by Stanford White, and was later restored to Jefferson's design. In 1976 members of the American Institute of Architects voted it the proudest achievement of American architecture of the previous two hundred years.
