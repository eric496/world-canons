> Raphael's chief assistant, painter and architect to the Gonzaga court at Mantua, whose Palazzo Te is the defining building of architectural Mannerism.

<sub>Portrait: Titian, Portrait of Giulio Romano, c. 1536 · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Titian_-_Portrait_of_Giulio_Romano_-_WGA22947.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Giulio Pippi, called Romano, grew up in Raphael's workshop, rising to become his principal assistant on the Vatican frescoes and, after Raphael's death in 1520, his artistic heir. In 1524 he moved to Mantua to serve Federico II Gonzaga, and for the rest of his life he directed nearly every artistic project in the city: palaces, churches, festivals, tapestries and the rebuilding of the cathedral. He was the only living artist named by Shakespeare, in The Winter's Tale.

At the Palazzo Te, Giulio played with the expectations of educated visitors who knew the classical rules: triglyphs slip out of place, keystones bulge, rough rustication meets smooth stone. Inside, the Sala dei Giganti is painted so that the whole room seems to collapse on the viewer. His witty, theatrical treatment of the orders made Mantua a centre of Mannerism and influenced Palladio and later architects across Europe.

## Key buildings

### Palazzo Te (1524–1534)

[![Palazzo Te](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Mantova_104.jpg/1280px-Mantova_104.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mantova_104.jpg)

<sub>Palazzo Te, Mantua, Italy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mantova_104.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1524–1534
- **Type:** Suburban villa
- **Location:** Mantua, Italy
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Palazzo%20Te%20Giulio%20Romano)

Federico II Gonzaga built the Palazzo Te on an island at the edge of Mantua, where his family had stables, as a villa for leisure and for meeting his mistress. Giulio Romano designed the building and its decoration. A low, square building around a courtyard, it plays with classical rules: in the courtyard, triglyphs drop out of the frieze as if slipping, rough and smooth stone alternate, and keystones project unexpectedly, jokes intended for viewers who knew the rules. Inside, frescoes by Giulio and his workshop include the Sala di Psiche, with erotic scenes from the story of Cupid and Psyche, and the Sala dei Giganti, painted from floor to vaulted ceiling with Jupiter's thunderbolts striking down giants as columns collapse around the viewer. It is now a museum.
