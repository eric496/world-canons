# Daniel Libeskind

**1946** · Polish-American  
Born: Łódź, Poland · Died: ?  
Styles: [Deconstructivism & Digital Form](../movements/deconstructivism.md)  

> Polish-American architect whose Jewish Museum Berlin turned architecture into a medium of memory.

## Biography

Libeskind, the son of Holocaust survivors, studied music in Israel and New York before turning to architecture at the Cooper Union. He was known for theoretical drawings and teaching until he won the competition for the Jewish Museum Berlin in 1989, which opened in 2001. His later works include the Imperial War Museum North in Manchester, the extension of the Denver Art Museum and the Royal Ontario Museum, and in 2003 he won the competition for the master plan of the World Trade Center site in New York.

Libeskind's buildings are made of sharp, intersecting volumes and slashed openings, intended to express rupture and absence. He was one of the architects shown at the 1988 Deconstructivist Architecture exhibition at MoMA.

## Key buildings

### Jewish Museum Berlin (1992–1999; opened 2001)

<sub>The Libeskind building beside the Baroque old building of the Jewish Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Berlin_J%C3%BCdisches_Museum_und_der_Libeskind-Bau.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1992–1999; opened 2001
- **Type:** Museum
- **Location:** Kreuzberg, Berlin, Germany
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jewish%20Museum%20Berlin%20Daniel%20Libeskind)

Daniel Libeskind won the competition in 1989 for an extension to the Berlin Museum dedicated to Jewish history. The building is a zigzag in plan, like a broken Star of David, clad in zinc and cut by slashing windows. It has no entrance of its own: visitors descend from the Baroque old building into underground axes representing continuity, exile and the Holocaust, the last ending in a tall, cold, unheated tower lit only by a slit. A line of empty concrete voids cuts through the whole building and can be seen but not entered. In the Garden of Exile, visitors walk on a tilted floor among forty-nine concrete pillars with trees growing from their tops. Before any exhibits were installed, the empty building opened to visitors in 1999, and many thought it should remain empty; the museum opened in 2001.
