# Frank Lloyd Wright

**1867 – 1959** · American  
Born: Richland Center, Wisconsin · Died: Phoenix, Arizona  
Styles: [Modernism & the International Style](../movements/modernism.md), [Late & Expressive Modernism](../movements/late-modernism.md)  

> American architect of the Prairie houses, Fallingwater and the Guggenheim Museum, and the prophet of an 'organic architecture' rooted in landscape.

## Biography

Wright studied briefly at the University of Wisconsin and worked for Louis Sullivan in Chicago before opening his own practice in Oak Park in 1893. His Prairie houses, culminating in the Robie House, and Unity Temple made him famous, and the Wasmuth portfolio of his work, published in Berlin in 1910–11, influenced European modernists. Personal scandal and tragedy, including the murder of his partner Mamah Borthwick and six others at Taliesin in 1914, interrupted his career; he built the Imperial Hotel in Tokyo and concrete-block houses in California. From the mid-1930s came a remarkable late flowering: Fallingwater, the Johnson Wax headquarters, the Usonian houses, Taliesin West and the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, completed after his death.

Wright sought an architecture that grew from its site, with low horizontal lines, open plans flowing around a central hearth, natural materials, and a continuity between inside and outside. He designed furniture, glass and textiles for his buildings. Over a career of some seventy years he completed more than five hundred works, and eight of them form a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### Frederick C. Robie House (1909–1910)

- **Date:** 1909–1910
- **Type:** House
- **Location:** Hyde Park, Chicago, Illinois, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Frederick%20C.%20Robie%20House%20Frank%20Lloyd%20Wright)

Frederick Robie, a businessman who made bicycles and then car parts, commissioned Wright to design a house on a narrow corner lot near the University of Chicago. Wright stretched it horizontally, with long bands of Roman brick, low hipped roofs projecting far beyond the walls on steel beams and art-glass windows, so that it seems rooted in the flat prairie. The living and dining rooms form a single long space on the upper floor, divided only by a central fireplace, with doors onto balconies. Wright designed furniture, lighting and glass. The house was threatened with demolition several times, and its rescue in the 1950s and 1960s became an early landmark of preservation. It is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site of Wright's twentieth-century architecture.

### Fallingwater (1936–1939)

- **Date:** 1936–1939
- **Type:** Country house
- **Location:** Mill Run, Pennsylvania, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fallingwater%20Frank%20Lloyd%20Wright)

Edgar J. Kaufmann, a Pittsburgh department store owner, asked Wright for a weekend house near a waterfall on Bear Run, expecting a view of the falls. Wright placed the house over them, anchoring it to the rock with a stone chimney core from which reinforced-concrete terraces cantilever above the stream. Inside, flagstone floors, a boulder rising through the living room hearth and stone walls continue the landscape, and glass walls and corner windows open to the woods; a stair descends from the living room to the water. The cantilevers sagged over time and were reinforced with post-tensioning in 2002. Kaufmann's son gave the house to the Western Pennsylvania Conservancy in 1963. It is a museum and part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site of Wright's architecture.

### Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum (1943–1959)

- **Date:** 1943–1959
- **Type:** Art museum
- **Location:** Fifth Avenue, New York City, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Solomon%20R.%20Guggenheim%20Museum%20Frank%20Lloyd%20Wright)

Solomon R. Guggenheim and the artist Hilla Rebay, director of his foundation for non-objective art, commissioned Wright in 1943 to design a museum. After years of revisions, delays and conflicts with building officials, it opened in October 1959, six months after Wright's death. The building is a concrete spiral, wider at the top than the bottom, facing Central Park. Inside, a continuous ramp winds up around a central atrium under a glass dome, so visitors see the art as they descend and look across the open space at other works. Many artists and critics objected that its sloping floors and curved walls made paintings hard to hang. A tower by Gwathmey Siegel was added in 1992. The museum is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site of Wright's architecture.
