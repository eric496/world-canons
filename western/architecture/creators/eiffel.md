# Gustave Eiffel

**1832 – 1923** · French  
Born: Dijon · Died: Paris  
Styles: [Iron, Glass & Engineering](../movements/iron-and-glass.md)  

> French engineer whose firm built viaducts, bridges and railway stations across the world and the Eiffel Tower for the Paris Exposition of 1889.

## Biography

Eiffel graduated from the École Centrale des Arts et Manufactures in 1855 and specialised in metal structures, founding his own firm in 1866. It built railway bridges and viaducts such as the Maria Pia bridge in Porto and the Garabit viaduct in France, stations in Budapest and elsewhere, and the internal iron framework of Bartholdi's Statue of Liberty. For the Exposition Universelle of 1889, celebrating the centenary of the Revolution, his company designed and built the 300-metre tower, from a concept by his engineers Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier with the architect Stephen Sauvestre. Eiffel's reputation suffered from the Panama Canal scandal of 1893, and he then devoted himself to research in meteorology and aerodynamics.

Eiffel's structures are calculated lattices of wrought iron in which every member answers to the forces of weight and wind, and his tower was the tallest structure in the world until 1930. Many artists and writers publicly protested against it, but it became the symbol of Paris and of modern engineering. He used it for experiments in radio, which helped save it from demolition.

## Key buildings

### Eiffel Tower (1887–1889)

- **Date:** 1887–1889
- **Type:** Lattice tower
- **Location:** Champ de Mars, Paris, France
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Eiffel%20Tower%20Gustave%20Eiffel)

For the Exposition Universelle of 1889, celebrating the centenary of the Revolution, the government held a competition for a tower 300 metres high. Eiffel's company won with a design developed by its engineers Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier and the architect Stephen Sauvestre. About 18,000 wrought-iron parts, designed and prefabricated in the company's workshops, were joined with some 2.5 million rivets in just over two years. Four curved legs, shaped to resist the wind, rise to three platforms. Prominent artists and writers protested in 1887 against what they called a useless and monstrous tower. It was meant to stand only for twenty years, but its usefulness for radio transmission saved it. The tallest structure in the world until the Chrysler Building of 1930, it became the symbol of Paris and one of the most visited monuments in the world.
