# Augustus Welby Northmore Pugin

<sub>Portrait: John Rogers Herbert, portrait of Augustus Pugin, National Portrait Gallery, London · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Augustus_Welby_Northmore_Pugin_from_NPG.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Pugin was the son of the French draughtsman Auguste Charles Pugin, who published studies of Gothic architecture, and he was designing furniture for Windsor Castle at fifteen. He converted to Catholicism in 1835 and in Contrasts (1836) set noble medieval towns beside squalid modern ones, arguing that Gothic was the only true Christian architecture. Working with great speed, he designed churches, cathedrals and houses across Britain and Ireland, including St Giles' at Cheadle and his own house and church at Ramsgate, and from 1844 the interiors, furniture, tiles, metalwork and clock tower of the Palace of Westminster. Overwork led to a breakdown, and he died at forty.

Pugin's True Principles of Pointed or Christian Architecture (1841) argued that ornament should enrich structure and that a building should honestly express its construction and purpose. Those ideas outlived the Gothic style and passed through Ruskin and William Morris to the Arts and Crafts movement and early modernism. He made the Gothic Revival a serious architectural language rather than a picturesque fashion.

### St Giles' Catholic Church, Cheadle (1841–1846)

- **Date:** 1841–1846
- **Type:** Parish church
- **Location:** Cheadle, Staffordshire, England
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=St%20Giles%27%20Catholic%20Church%2C%20Cheadle%20Augustus%20Welby%20Northmore%20Pugin)

John Talbot, 16th Earl of Shrewsbury, the richest English Catholic, funded a parish church at Cheadle near his estate at Alton Towers, and Pugin was given a free hand. He designed it in the Decorated Gothic style of the early fourteenth century, which he considered the purest, with a tall spire visible across the countryside. Inside, every surface is coloured and gilded: the walls stencilled with patterns, the floor tiled by Minton, the screen and the Easter sepulchre carved, the windows filled with stained glass by William Wailes, the metalwork by John Hardman. Pugin called it 'perfect Cheadle', and it remains the most complete expression of his ideal of a revived medieval parish church.

### Elizabeth Tower (Big Ben) (1843–1859)

[![Elizabeth Tower (Big Ben)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Elizabeth_Tower_and_the_north_front_of_the_Palace_of_Westminster%2C_London.jpg/960px-Elizabeth_Tower_and_the_north_front_of_the_Palace_of_Westminster%2C_London.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Elizabeth_Tower_and_the_north_front_of_the_Palace_of_Westminster%2C_London.jpg)

<sub>Elizabeth Tower (Big Ben), Palace of Westminster, London, England Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Elizabeth_Tower_and_the_north_front_of_the_Palace_of_Westminster%2C_London.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1843–1859
- **Type:** Clock tower
- **Location:** Palace of Westminster, London, England
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Elizabeth%20Tower%20%28Big%20Ben%29%20Augustus%20Welby%20Northmore%20Pugin)

The great clock tower at the north end of the Palace of Westminster, known as the Clock Tower until 2012 and now as the Elizabeth Tower, was designed by Pugin in 1852, the last major design before his breakdown and death; he based it on an earlier tower he had designed. About 96 metres high, it is of brick clad in stone, with a cast-iron spire. The clock mechanism, designed by Edmund Beckett Denison and built by Edward John Dent and his stepson, began keeping time in 1859 and was famous for its accuracy. The name Big Ben properly belongs to the largest bell, which cracked soon after installation and was given a quarter turn so its hammer would strike elsewhere. A restoration was completed in 2022.
