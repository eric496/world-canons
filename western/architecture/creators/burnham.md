# Daniel Burnham

**1846 – 1912** · American  
Born: Henderson, New York · Died: Heidelberg, Germany  
Styles: [Chicago School](../movements/chicago-school.md), [Beaux-Arts & Historicism](../movements/beaux-arts.md)  

> Chicago architect and planner, partner of John Wellborn Root, director of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition and designer of the Flatiron Building in New York.

## Biography

Burnham grew up in Chicago and formed a partnership with John Wellborn Root in 1873. Burnham and Root designed pioneering tall buildings such as the Monadnock and the Rookery, and after Root's death in 1891 the firm, as D. H. Burnham and Company, built the glass-walled Reliance Building in Chicago, the Flatiron Building in New York (1902) and Union Station in Washington. Burnham directed the design of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893, whose white classical 'White City' inspired the City Beautiful movement, and he led plans for Washington, San Francisco, Manila and Chicago.

Burnham was an organiser of genius whose large office became a model of the modern architectural firm. His buildings range from the structural directness of the Chicago School to grand Beaux-Arts classicism, and his 1909 Plan of Chicago, with its lakefront parks, shaped the city. Words often attributed to him, urging planners to make no little plans, are probably a later paraphrase.

## Key buildings

### Flatiron Building (1901–1902)

<sub>Flatiron Building, New York City, United States Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Edificio_Fuller_%28Flatiron%29_en_2010_desde_el_Empire_State_crop_boxin.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1901–1902
- **Type:** Office building
- **Location:** New York City, United States
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Flatiron%20Building%20Daniel%20Burnham)

The Fuller Company commissioned D. H. Burnham and Company to design a twenty-two-storey office building on the triangular plot formed by Broadway and Fifth Avenue at 23rd Street. Its steel frame, one of the earliest in New York for so tall a building, is clad in limestone and terracotta, organised like a classical column into base, shaft and capital, with Renaissance ornament. The narrow point is only about two metres wide. The building's shape, likened to a clothes iron, gave it its popular name, and the wind gusts around it became a legend. Photographers such as Alfred Stieglitz and Edward Steichen made it an icon of the modern city. Long used as offices, it is undergoing conversion to apartments.
