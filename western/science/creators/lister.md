# Joseph Lister

**1827 – 1912** · English  
Born: Upton, Essex · Died: Walmer, Kent  
Eras & fields: [Public health, germ theory & modern medicine](../movements/germ-theory.md)  

> Surgeon who applied Pasteur's germ theory to surgery, using carbolic acid to prevent wound infection, and so founded antiseptic surgery.

## Biography

Lister was born into a Quaker family; his father, a wine merchant, was an expert on microscope lenses. He studied medicine at University College London and became a surgeon in Edinburgh and then professor of surgery at Glasgow, later returning to Edinburgh and then King's College London. He was surgeon to Queen Victoria and in 1897 became the first medical man raised to the peerage.

At Glasgow Royal Infirmary Lister saw that many patients died of infection after operations. Reading Pasteur's work on microbes, he reasoned that germs from the air and hands caused wound sepsis, and from 1865 treated wounds, dressings and instruments with carbolic acid, reporting a sharp fall in deaths. His methods were resisted at first but spread rapidly, and developed into today's aseptic surgery. The bacterial genus Listeria and the mouthwash Listerine are named after him.

## Key works & discoveries

- **Date:** 1867
- **Type:** Medical papers
- **Published / where:** The Lancet, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20Antiseptic%20Principle%20in%20the%20Practice%20of%20Surgery%20Joseph%20Lister)

In the mid nineteenth century almost half of patients who had a limb amputated in some hospitals died of infection, and 'hospital gangrene' was widely blamed on bad air. At Glasgow Royal Infirmary, Lister read Pasteur's work showing that fermentation and putrefaction are caused by microbes, and reasoned that germs entering wounds caused sepsis. In August 1865 he treated a boy's compound fracture of the leg, in which the bone had broken the skin, with lint soaked in carbolic acid, a chemical used to treat sewage, and the wound healed without infection. In a series of articles in The Lancet in 1867 he reported his results with compound fractures and abscesses, and in a lecture that year described a striking fall in deaths on his wards. He later sprayed carbolic acid in the operating theatre, a method he eventually abandoned. Antisepsis met resistance, especially in Britain, but spread through Germany and elsewhere, and developed into modern aseptic surgery with sterilised instruments, gowns and gloves.
