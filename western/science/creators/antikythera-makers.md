# Makers of the Antikythera mechanism

**fl. c. 150–60 BC** · Greek  
Eras & fields: [Hellenistic science & mathematics](../movements/hellenistic-science.md)  

> The unknown Greek craftsmen and astronomers who designed and built the Antikythera mechanism, the oldest known geared calculating device.

## Biography

Nothing is known of the people who made the Antikythera mechanism. Its inscriptions and calendar suggest links with Corinth or its colonies, possibly Syracuse, and with the astronomical school of Rhodes associated with Hipparchus and later Posidonius, but every attribution is debated. Its date of construction is variously put between about 200 and 60 BC.

Whoever they were, they combined Babylonian cycles, Greek geometrical astronomy and fine bronze gear-work into a portable machine of a complexity not seen again in Europe until the astronomical clocks of the Middle Ages. The mechanism shows that ancient science was not only theoretical, and that a tradition of precision instrument-making existed of which this is the only survivor.

## Key works & discoveries

### Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–60 BC)

- **Date:** c. 150–60 BC
- **Type:** Geared astronomical calculator (bronze)
- **Published / where:** National Archaeological Museum, Athens
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Antikythera%20mechanism%20Makers%20of%20the%20Antikythera%20mechanism)

The Antikythera mechanism was recovered in 1901 by sponge divers from a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera, as a lump of corroded bronze and wood in 82 fragments. Decades of study, culminating in X-ray tomography and surface imaging from 2005, revealed at least thirty bronze gears in a box about the size of a shoebox. Turning a handle moved pointers on the front showing the positions of the Sun and Moon in the zodiac and the phase of the Moon, with a pin-and-slot gear reproducing the Moon's varying speed as described by Hipparchus; inscriptions suggest that it also showed the planets. Spiral dials on the back tracked the 19-year Metonic calendar, the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games and the 18-year Saros cycle for predicting eclipses. Its date is debated, with proposals from the late third to the early first century BC. Nothing of comparable complexity survives from the ancient world, and it has transformed views of ancient technology.
