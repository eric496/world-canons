# Francis Bacon

**1561 – 1626** · English  
Born: London · Died: Highgate, London  
Eras & fields: [Scientific Revolution](../movements/scientific-revolution.md)  
Also in: [Philosophy](../../philosophy/eras/03-early-modern/francis-bacon.md)  

> Statesman and philosopher who called for a new science based on systematic observation, experiment and induction, carried out collectively for the benefit of humanity.

## Biography

Bacon was the son of Elizabeth I's Lord Keeper of the Great Seal and studied at Trinity College, Cambridge. A lawyer and member of Parliament, he rose under James I to become Attorney General and Lord Chancellor, but in 1621 he was convicted of accepting bribes and removed from office. He spent his last years writing, and is said to have died of a chill caught while experimenting with stuffing a chicken with snow.

In the Novum Organum and other works Bacon attacked reliance on ancient authority and on deduction from first principles, and proposed a method of gathering natural histories and reasoning step by step to general laws, guarding against the 'idols' that distort human judgement. His utopia New Atlantis imagined a state research institution. Though he made no great discoveries himself, the founders of the Royal Society in 1660 claimed him as their inspiration, and his vision of organised, useful science has remained powerful.

## Key works & discoveries

### Novum Organum (1620)

- **Date:** 1620
- **Type:** Treatise on scientific method
- **Published / where:** London: John Bill
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Novum%20Organum%20Francis%20Bacon)

Novum Organum was published as part of a vast unfinished project, the Great Instauration, for the renewal of all knowledge. Its title challenges Aristotle's logical works, known as the Organon, and its frontispiece shows a ship sailing beyond the Pillars of Hercules, the limits of the ancient world, with a motto from the Book of Daniel promising that many shall pass to and fro and knowledge shall increase. In a series of aphorisms Bacon criticises hasty generalisation and describes four kinds of 'idols', the illusions of human nature, of the individual, of language and of philosophical systems, that mislead the mind. He then proposes a method of collecting natural histories and arranging instances in tables of presence, absence and degree to arrive step by step at the causes of phenomena, illustrated with an inquiry into heat, which he concludes is a kind of motion. His method was rarely followed literally, but his ideals inspired the Royal Society.
