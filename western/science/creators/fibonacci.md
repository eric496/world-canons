# Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci)

**c. 1170 – c. 1250** · Italian  
Born: Pisa · Died: Pisa (probably)  
Eras & fields: [Medieval Latin scholarship](../movements/medieval-latin-science.md)  

> Merchant mathematician of Pisa whose Liber Abaci introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals and their arithmetic to European commerce. He was the most original European mathematician of the Middle Ages.

<sub>Portrait: Nineteenth-century statue of Fibonacci, Pisa · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Leonardo_Fibonacci.JPG)</sub>

## Biography

Leonardo was the son of a Pisan merchant and customs official stationed at Bugia (Béjaïa) in North Africa, where as a boy he learned to calculate with the Indian numerals used by Arab merchants. He travelled around the Mediterranean, to Egypt, Syria, Byzantium and Sicily, studying the methods of the mathematicians he met. Back in Pisa he wrote a series of books and was honoured by the city with a salary; he also met and corresponded with the scholars at the court of Emperor Frederick II. The nickname Fibonacci, 'son of Bonacci', was popularised in the nineteenth century.

His Liber Abaci of 1202 taught the new numerals, place value and zero through hundreds of practical problems, and helped them displace Roman numerals and the counting board in Italian business. His other works, on geometry and on number theory, contain original results. A problem about breeding rabbits gave the sequence named after him, which turns up throughout mathematics and nature.

## Key works & discoveries

### Liber Abaci (1202; revised 1228)

[![Liber Abaci](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/04/Liber_abbaci_magliab_f124r.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Liber_abbaci_magliab_f124r.jpg)

<sub>Page from a manuscript of Liber Abaci, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Liber_abbaci_magliab_f124r.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1202; revised 1228
- **Type:** Mathematical treatise
- **Published / where:** Pisa
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Liber%20Abaci%20Leonardo%20of%20Pisa%20%28Fibonacci%29)

Liber Abaci, the Book of Calculation, opens by introducing the nine Indian figures and the sign 0, which in Arabic is called zephirum, and teaches how to read, write and calculate with them. Its fifteen chapters then apply the new arithmetic to practical commerce, including prices, currency exchange, barter, partnerships, alloys and interest, and to recreational problems and algebra drawn from Arabic sources. One problem asks how many pairs of rabbits will be produced in a year from one pair, if each pair produces a new pair every month from its second month; the answer generates the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 and so on, later named after Fibonacci, though it was known earlier to Indian mathematicians. The book, surviving in a revised version of 1228, inspired the abacus schools of Italian merchant cities, where generations learned to calculate in the new numerals, and helped make Italy the centre of European mathematics.
