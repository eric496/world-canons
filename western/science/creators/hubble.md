# Edwin Hubble

**1889 – 1953** · American  
Born: Marshfield, Missouri · Died: San Marino, California  
Eras & fields: [Cosmology & the expanding universe](../movements/cosmology.md)  

> Astronomer who showed that the spiral nebulae are galaxies far beyond the Milky Way and that more distant galaxies recede faster, the observational basis of the expanding universe.

## Biography

Hubble grew up in Missouri and Illinois, studied mathematics and astronomy at the University of Chicago and law at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, and briefly taught high school. After a doctorate at Chicago and service in the First World War, he joined the Mount Wilson Observatory in California in 1919, where he had use of the 100-inch Hooker telescope, then the largest in the world. He worked there for the rest of his life, apart from war service in ballistics during the Second World War.

In 1923–24 Hubble identified Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda nebula and used them to show that it lies far outside our galaxy, settling the debate over whether the universe contains other galaxies. He devised the classification of galaxies by shape still used today. In 1929, combining his distances with redshifts measured largely by Vesto Slipher and with Milton Humason's work, he showed that galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance. Lemaître had found the relation two years earlier, and since 2018 it has been officially called the Hubble–Lemaître law. The Hubble Space Telescope is named after him.

## Key works & discoveries

### Hubble's law of galaxy recession (1929)

- **Date:** 1929
- **Type:** Scientific paper
- **Published / where:** Mount Wilson Observatory; published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hubble%27s%20law%20of%20galaxy%20recession%20Edwin%20Hubble)

In 1924–25 Hubble had shown that the spiral nebulae are distant galaxies by finding Cepheid variable stars in them, whose regular pulsations reveal their true brightness and hence their distance. In a short paper of 1929, A Relation between Distance and Radial Velocity among Extra-Galactic Nebulae, he plotted the distances of 24 galaxies against their velocities, most of them measured from redshifts in their spectra by Vesto Slipher at Lowell Observatory, and found a roughly linear relation: the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it is receding. With Milton Humason he extended the relation to much greater distances in 1931. Hubble was cautious about the interpretation, but the relation was soon understood as evidence that the universe is expanding, as Georges Lemaître had predicted from general relativity in 1927. Hubble's distances were too small by a large factor, giving too young an age for the universe, a problem corrected in the 1950s. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended calling it the Hubble–Lemaître law.
