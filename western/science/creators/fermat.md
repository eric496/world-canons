# Pierre de Fermat

**1601 – 1665** · French  
Born: Beaumont-de-Lomagne, France · Died: Castres, France  
Eras & fields: [Scientific Revolution](../movements/scientific-revolution.md)  

> Lawyer and amateur mathematician who founded modern number theory, invented analytic geometry independently of Descartes and helped create probability theory and calculus.

<sub>Portrait: Portrait of Pierre de Fermat · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pierre_de_Fermat.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Fermat was the son of a wealthy merchant and studied law, probably at Orléans. He spent his career as a councillor of the parliament of Toulouse, a judicial post, and did mathematics in his leisure. His birth year is uncertain, given as 1601 or about 1607. He published almost nothing, communicating his results in letters to Mersenne, Pascal and others, often without proofs, and his work became widely known only when his son published his papers after his death.

Fermat developed a method of finding maxima, minima and tangents that anticipated the differential calculus, devised coordinate geometry at the same time as Descartes, and in letters with Pascal in 1654 laid the foundations of probability. In optics he stated the principle that light takes the path of least time. Above all he revived number theory, with results such as his 'little theorem' on primes, and left the famous marginal note claiming a proof of what became known as Fermat's Last Theorem.

### Fermat's Last Theorem (marginal note) (c. 1637; published 1670)

[![Fermat's Last Theorem (marginal note)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/47/Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg)

<sub>Problem II.8 in the 1670 edition of Diophantus, with Fermat's note Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1637; published 1670
- **Type:** Mathematical conjecture
- **Published / where:** Note in Fermat's copy of Diophantus' Arithmetica; published Toulouse, 1670
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fermat%27s%20Last%20Theorem%20%28marginal%20note%29%20Pierre%20de%20Fermat)

Around 1637, reading Claude Bachet's 1621 Latin edition of the Arithmetica of Diophantus, Fermat wrote in the margin beside a problem about dividing a square into two squares that it is impossible to divide a cube into two cubes, or any higher power into two like powers, adding that he had found a truly marvellous proof which the margin was too narrow to contain. His original copy is lost; the note is known because his son Samuel published an edition of Diophantus with his father's notes in 1670. Fermat did leave a proof for the case of fourth powers, but no general proof was ever found, and most mathematicians doubt he had one. Attempts to prove it over three centuries drove the development of algebraic number theory. The theorem was finally proved by Andrew Wiles in 1994, with help from Richard Taylor, using methods far beyond anything available to Fermat.

### Correspondence with Pascal on probability (1654)

- **Date:** 1654
- **Type:** Letters
- **Published / where:** Toulouse and Paris; published in Fermat's Varia Opera, 1679
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Correspondence%20with%20Pascal%20on%20probability%20Pierre%20de%20Fermat)

In the summer of 1654 Pascal and Fermat exchanged a series of letters about problems posed by the gambler Antoine Gombaud, the Chevalier de Méré. The central one, known since the fifteenth century, was the problem of points: how to divide the stakes fairly between two players when a game is broken off before either has won. Fermat solved it by listing all the possible ways the remaining rounds could turn out and counting those favourable to each player; Pascal used a recursive method based on expected values and his arithmetical triangle. Their letters agreed on the answers and established the idea that uncertain outcomes can be treated mathematically by counting equally possible cases. Huygens, hearing of their work in Paris, wrote the first published book on probability in 1657, and the subject was developed by Jacob Bernoulli, de Moivre and Laplace.
