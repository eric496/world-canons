# Avicenna (Ibn Sina)

**c. 980 – 1037** · Persian  
Born: Afshana, near Bukhara · Died: Hamadan, Persia  
Eras & fields: [Islamic science & the translation movement](../movements/islamic-science.md)  
Also in: [Philosophy](../../philosophy/eras/02-medieval/avicenna.md)  

> Persian physician and philosopher whose Canon of Medicine was the standard medical textbook of the Islamic world and of European universities for some five hundred years.

## Biography

Ibn Sina was born near Bukhara and, by his own account, had mastered logic, mathematics and medicine by his late teens; curing the Samanid ruler gave him access to the royal library. Political upheavals sent him moving between the courts of Persia, where he served as physician and sometimes as vizier, writing at night and in hiding. He died at Hamadan in 1037.

His Canon of Medicine organised Greek, Persian and Indian medical knowledge, with his own observations, into a clear systematic whole, including rules for testing drugs. His philosophical encyclopedia, the Book of Healing, made him the most influential philosopher of the Islamic world. Translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona in the twelfth century, the Canon was taught in European medical schools into the seventeenth century. Islamic science will be covered in its own section.

## Key works & discoveries

### The Canon of Medicine (1025)

<sub>Page from a manuscript of the Canon of Medicine Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Canon_of_Medicine.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1025
- **Type:** Medical encyclopedia
- **Published / where:** Persia; Latin translation by Gerard of Cremona, Toledo, 12th century; printed Milan, 1473
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Canon%20of%20Medicine%20Avicenna%20%28Ibn%20Sina%29)

Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb is a five-book encyclopedia completed around 1025. The first book covers the principles of medicine, from the humours and anatomy to the causes of disease and general therapy; the second lists some 800 simple drugs, with rules for testing their effects on the body; the third and fourth deal with diseases of each organ and those affecting the whole body, such as fevers; and the fifth with compound drugs. It synthesised Galen, Hippocrates, Aristotle and Persian and Indian sources into a clear, logical system, adding original observations, for example on the contagious nature of tuberculosis and the spread of disease through water and soil. Translated into Latin in Toledo in the twelfth century, it became the core of the medical curriculum at universities such as Montpellier and Leuven, with dozens of printed editions after 1473, and was still being taught in some places in the seventeenth century.
