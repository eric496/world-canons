# Robert Koch

**1843 – 1910** · German  
Born: Clausthal, Kingdom of Hanover · Died: Baden-Baden, Germany  
Eras & fields: [Public health, germ theory & modern medicine](../movements/germ-theory.md)  

> Physician and microbiologist who identified the bacteria causing anthrax, tuberculosis and cholera and established methods and criteria for proving that a microbe causes a disease.

## Biography

Koch was the son of a mining official in the Harz mountains and studied medicine at Göttingen. While working as a country doctor in Wollstein, in Prussian Poland, he set up a laboratory in his home and in 1876 described the life cycle of the anthrax bacillus. He moved to the Imperial Health Office in Berlin, then became professor at Berlin and director of a new institute for infectious diseases, now the Robert Koch Institute. He won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1905.

Koch's laboratory introduced techniques that made bacteriology a precise science: growing pure cultures on solid media, staining and photographing bacteria. In 1882 he announced that tuberculosis, then the leading cause of death in Europe, is caused by a bacillus, and in 1883–84 he identified the cholera bacterium in Egypt and India. His criteria for linking a microbe to a disease are known as Koch's postulates. His claim to have found a cure for tuberculosis, tuberculin, proved a failure, though it became useful as a diagnostic test.

## Key works & discoveries

### Discovery of the tubercle bacillus (1882)

- **Date:** 1882
- **Type:** Medical discovery
- **Published / where:** Imperial Health Office, Berlin; announced to the Berlin Physiological Society, 24 March 1882
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discovery%20of%20the%20tubercle%20bacillus%20Robert%20Koch)

Tuberculosis, also known as consumption, killed about one in seven people in nineteenth-century Europe, and many thought it hereditary. Koch developed a new staining method that made a tiny rod-shaped bacterium visible in tubercular tissue from humans and animals, grew it in pure culture on coagulated blood serum, which took weeks because it grows very slowly, and showed that injecting the culture into guinea pigs produced the disease. He announced the discovery at a meeting of the Berlin Physiological Society on 24 March 1882, a date now marked as World Tuberculosis Day, and published it as Die Ätiologie der Tuberculose. His systematic chain of proof became the model for linking a microbe to a disease, later formalised as Koch's postulates. His later claim that a substance from the bacilli, tuberculin, could cure the disease proved a costly failure, though it became a diagnostic test. Effective treatment came only with antibiotics after the Second World War.
