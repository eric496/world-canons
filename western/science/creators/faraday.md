# Michael Faraday

**1791 – 1867** · English  
Born: Newington Butts, Surrey · Died: Hampton Court, Middlesex  
Eras & fields: [Electromagnetism & classical physics](../movements/electromagnetism.md)  

> Self-taught experimenter at the Royal Institution who discovered electromagnetic induction, built the first electric motor and generator, and introduced the idea of the field.

## Biography

Faraday was the son of a blacksmith and had little schooling. Apprenticed to a bookbinder, he educated himself by reading the books he bound, and after attending lectures by Humphry Davy he obtained a post as Davy's laboratory assistant at the Royal Institution in London in 1813. He spent his whole career there, becoming its director of the laboratory and Fullerian Professor of Chemistry, and was a devout member of a small Christian sect, the Sandemanians. He refused a knighthood and lived his last years in a house at Hampton Court given by Queen Victoria.

Faraday made important chemical discoveries, including benzene and the laws of electrolysis, but his greatest work was in electricity and magnetism. In 1821 he made a wire rotate around a magnet, the first electric motor, and in 1831 he discovered that a changing magnetic field induces a current, the principle of the dynamo and the transformer. Lacking mathematics, he thought in terms of lines of force filling space, an idea that Maxwell turned into field theory. His Christmas lectures for young people, still held today, made him a celebrated popular teacher.

### Discovery of electromagnetic induction (1831)

- **Date:** 1831
- **Type:** Experimental discovery
- **Published / where:** Royal Institution, London; published in the Philosophical Transactions, 1832
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discovery%20of%20electromagnetic%20induction%20Michael%20Faraday)

After Hans Christian Ørsted showed in 1820 that an electric current deflects a compass needle, many tried to find the reverse effect, electricity from magnetism. On 29 August 1831 Faraday wound two coils of insulated wire on opposite sides of an iron ring and found that when he switched on or off the current in one coil, a brief current flowed in the other, but none while the current was steady. Over the following weeks he showed that moving a magnet into or out of a coil also produced a current, and that a copper disc rotating between the poles of a magnet gave a continuous current, the first dynamo. He reported the results to the Royal Society in November as the first series of his Experimental Researches in Electricity. Induction is the principle of the electric generator and the transformer, on which electric power systems depend. Joseph Henry in America discovered it independently at about the same time. The induction ring survives at the Royal Institution.

### The Chemical History of a Candle (1848; published 1861)

- **Date:** 1848; published 1861
- **Type:** Lectures for young people
- **Published / where:** Royal Institution, London; published London: Griffin, Bohn
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Chemical%20History%20of%20a%20Candle%20Michael%20Faraday)

Faraday gave the Royal Institution's Christmas lectures for young people nineteen times, and his series on The Chemical History of a Candle, first given in 1848 and repeated in 1860–61, became the most famous. In six lectures he used the burning candle to explain capillary action, the shape of the flame, the products of combustion, the composition of water and air, the properties of hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide, and finally respiration, comparing the slow burning of carbon in our bodies to the candle. Each lecture was full of demonstrations. The lectures were taken down in shorthand by William Crookes and published in 1861, and have been in print ever since in many languages. Faraday ended by wishing that his listeners might, like the candle, shine as lights to those about them. The Christmas lectures continue today, broadcast on television.
