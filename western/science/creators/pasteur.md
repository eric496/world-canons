# Louis Pasteur

**1822 – 1895** · French  
Born: Dole, Jura, France · Died: Marnes-la-Coquette, near Paris  
Eras & fields: [Public health, germ theory & modern medicine](../movements/germ-theory.md)  

> Chemist and microbiologist who showed that fermentation and many diseases are caused by microorganisms, disproved spontaneous generation and developed vaccines against anthrax and rabies.

<sub>Portrait: Albert Edelfelt, Louis Pasteur in his laboratory, 1885, Musée d'Orsay · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Albert_Edelfelt_-_Louis_Pasteur_-_1885.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Pasteur was the son of a tanner in the Jura. He studied at the École normale supérieure in Paris and made his name as a chemist with the discovery that some molecules exist in mirror-image forms. He was professor at Strasbourg and Lille and then at the École normale in Paris. Despite a stroke in 1868 that left him partly paralysed, he continued working, and in 1888 the Institut Pasteur was founded in his honour by public subscription.

Studying fermentation for the wine and beer industries, Pasteur showed that it is the work of living microorganisms, and that gentle heating, now called pasteurisation, could prevent spoilage. His experiments with swan-necked flasks discredited spontaneous generation. He helped save the French silk industry from silkworm diseases and championed the germ theory of disease. With his collaborators he developed vaccines by weakening microbes, against chicken cholera, anthrax and, in 1885, rabies, which made him a national hero. Studies of his notebooks have shown that his public accounts sometimes simplified what he actually did.

### Swan-neck flask experiments (1859–1862)

[![Swan-neck flask experiments](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Swan-necked_flask_used_by_Pasteur._Wellcome_M0012521.jpg/1280px-Swan-necked_flask_used_by_Pasteur._Wellcome_M0012521.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Swan-necked_flask_used_by_Pasteur._Wellcome_M0012521.jpg)

<sub>Swan-necked flask used by Pasteur Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Swan-necked_flask_used_by_Pasteur._Wellcome_M0012521.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1859–1862
- **Type:** Experiment
- **Published / where:** École normale supérieure, Paris; Institut Pasteur, Paris (original flasks)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Swan-neck%20flask%20experiments%20Louis%20Pasteur)

The question of whether living things can arise spontaneously from non-living matter was revived in 1859 by the naturalist Félix Pouchet, and the French Academy of Sciences offered a prize for experiments that would settle it. Pasteur boiled nutrient broth in flasks whose necks he had drawn out into long S-shaped curves, open to the air. Dust and microbes settled in the bend and did not reach the liquid, which remained clear indefinitely; when the neck was broken off or the flask tilted so that broth touched the trapped dust, it soon teemed with life. He also exposed flasks to air at different altitudes, including on a glacier near Mont Blanc, and found fewer contaminations in pure mountain air. He won the prize in 1862 and announced in a lecture at the Sorbonne in 1864 that spontaneous generation would never recover. The experiments supported his germ theory of fermentation and disease. Some of the original flasks, still sterile, are kept at the Institut Pasteur.

### Rabies vaccine (1885)

- **Date:** 1885
- **Type:** Vaccine
- **Published / where:** Laboratory of the École normale supérieure, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rabies%20vaccine%20Louis%20Pasteur)

Rabies was then always fatal once symptoms appeared. Pasteur and his collaborators, especially Émile Roux, could not see the infectious agent, which is a virus, but they passed it through rabbits and weakened it by drying the infected spinal cords in sealed flasks for different numbers of days. Because rabies takes weeks to develop, they reasoned that a series of injections of increasingly virulent material might produce immunity after a bite. On 6 July 1885 a nine-year-old Alsatian boy, Joseph Meister, was brought to Paris two days after being badly bitten by a rabid dog, and Pasteur, who was not a physician, had doctors give him thirteen injections over ten days. The boy survived, as did a young shepherd, Jean-Baptiste Jupille, treated in October. News spread, and patients came from around the world. The success led to the founding of the Institut Pasteur in 1888. Historians studying his notebooks have shown that the vaccine had been less thoroughly tested in animals than he publicly implied.
