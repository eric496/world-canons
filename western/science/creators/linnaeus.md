## Biography

Linnaeus was the son of a Lutheran pastor and keen gardener. He studied medicine at Lund and Uppsala, made a botanical expedition to Lapland in 1732, and took his medical degree in the Dutch Republic in 1735, where he published the first edition of Systema Naturae. Returning to Sweden, he practised medicine in Stockholm and from 1741 was professor at Uppsala, where he restored the botanical garden. He sent his students, the 'apostles', on voyages around the world to collect specimens, and was ennobled as Carl von Linné in 1761.

Linnaeus classified plants by the number and arrangement of their sexual organs, an artificial system that was easy to use, and from 1753 consistently gave each species a binomial name such as Homo sapiens, a term he coined. His system of nested categories made it possible to catalogue the flood of new organisms reaching Europe, and binomial names remain the basis of biological nomenclature, though his classification has long been replaced by evolutionary ones.

### Systema Naturae (1735)

[![Systema Naturae](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ae/Linnaeus1758-title-page.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Linnaeus1758-title-page.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the tenth edition, 1758 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Linnaeus1758-title-page.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1735
- **Type:** Taxonomic treatise
- **Published / where:** Leiden: Theodor Haak
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Systema%20Naturae%20Carl%20Linnaeus)

The first edition of Systema Naturae, printed in Leiden when Linnaeus was a 28-year-old medical student, was just eleven large pages of tables classifying the three kingdoms of nature, animal, vegetable and mineral, into classes, orders, genera and species. Plants were sorted by their sexual system, the number and arrangement of their stamens and pistils, which made identification easy but caused offence to some readers for its frankness. Linnaeus revised and expanded it through twelve editions in his lifetime. The tenth edition of 1758–59, which consistently used two-word names for animals, is the official starting point of zoological nomenclature, as his Species Plantarum of 1753 is for plants. In it he placed humans with apes and monkeys in the order Primates under the name Homo sapiens. The hierarchy of ranks and the binomial system survive in modern biology, though the classification itself is now based on evolutionary relationships.

### Species Plantarum (1753)

[![Species Plantarum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c4/Species_plantarum_001.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Species_plantarum_001.jpg)

<sub>Title page, 1753 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Species_plantarum_001.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1753
- **Type:** Botanical catalogue
- **Published / where:** Stockholm: Laurentius Salvius
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Species%20Plantarum%20Carl%20Linnaeus)

Species Plantarum, The Species of Plants, published in two volumes in 1753, describes about 5,900 species, everything Linnaeus knew of the world's flora, arranged by his sexual system. For each species he gave a short Latin descriptive phrase and, in the margin, a 'trivial name', a single word that combined with the genus name formed a convenient two-word label such as Bellis perennis for the daisy. Linnaeus meant these names as a practical shorthand, but they proved so useful that they replaced the long phrases. International rules of botanical nomenclature take 1 May 1753, the nominal publication date of this book, as the starting point for the names of most plants, so that many names still used today date from it. The specimens he used are preserved by the Linnean Society of London and are consulted to fix the meaning of names.
