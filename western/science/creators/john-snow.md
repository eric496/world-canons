# John Snow

**1813 – 1858** · English  
Born: York · Died: London  
Eras & fields: [Public health, germ theory & modern medicine](../movements/germ-theory.md)  

> London physician who showed that cholera spreads through contaminated water, and a pioneer of anaesthesia. He is regarded as a founder of epidemiology.

## Biography

Snow was the son of a labourer in York. He was apprenticed to a surgeon in Newcastle, where he saw the first cholera epidemic of 1831–32, and then studied in London, setting up practice in Soho. He became the leading expert in anaesthesia with ether and chloroform, and gave chloroform to Queen Victoria at the births of two of her children. He died of a stroke at forty-five.

At a time when most doctors believed cholera was spread by bad air, or miasma, Snow argued that it was caused by something swallowed in contaminated water. During the 1854 outbreak in Soho he mapped deaths clustering around a pump in Broad Street and persuaded the parish to remove its handle. His larger study comparing households supplied by two water companies provided strong statistical evidence. His ideas were slow to be accepted, but he is now honoured as a founder of modern epidemiology.

## Key works & discoveries

### On the Mode of Communication of Cholera and the Broad Street map (1849; 2nd ed. 1855)

- **Date:** 1849; 2nd ed. 1855
- **Type:** Epidemiological study and map
- **Published / where:** London: John Churchill
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20Mode%20of%20Communication%20of%20Cholera%20and%20the%20Broad%20Street%20map%20John%20Snow)

Snow first set out his theory that cholera is spread by a poison or agent in contaminated water, not by bad air, in a pamphlet of 1849. When a violent outbreak killed over five hundred people in a few weeks in Soho in late August and September 1854, he investigated house by house and found that the deaths were concentrated around a public water pump in Broad Street, now Broadwick Street, while workers at a nearby brewery who drank beer and a workhouse with its own well were largely spared. He persuaded the local board to remove the pump's handle on 8 September, by which time the outbreak was already waning. In the second edition of his book in 1855 he published a map marking each death with a bar, which became a famous image, and the results of his 'grand experiment' comparing death rates among households supplied by two water companies, one drawing from sewage-polluted Thames water. The local curate Henry Whitehead, at first sceptical, traced the source to a baby's nappies washed into a leaking cesspool next to the well.
