> Natural philosopher and founder member of the Royal Society who made chemistry an experimental science and discovered the law relating the pressure and volume of a gas.

<sub>Portrait: Johann Kerseboom, the 'Shannon portrait' of Robert Boyle · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Shannon_Portrait_of_the_Hon_Robert_Boyle.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Boyle was the fourteenth child of Richard Boyle, 1st Earl of Cork, one of the richest men in Britain. Educated at Eton and on a long tour of the Continent, he settled first in Dorset and then in Oxford, where from about 1656 he worked with a circle of experimenters and hired the young Robert Hooke as his assistant. He was a founder of the Royal Society in 1660 and lived his last decades in London with his sister Katherine, Lady Ranelagh. Deeply pious, he funded Bible translations and endowed lectures defending Christianity.

With Hooke's improved air pump Boyle carried out a famous series of experiments on air and vacuum, finding that the volume of a gas varies inversely with its pressure. In The Sceptical Chymist he attacked both the Aristotelian elements and the alchemists' principles, arguing for a corpuscular view of matter tested by experiment. He wrote in detail about his procedures so that others could repeat them, and his idea of the experimental report and the credible witness shaped the practice of modern science.

### New Experiments Physico-Mechanical, Touching the Spring of the Air (1660; 2nd ed. 1662)

- **Date:** 1660; 2nd ed. 1662
- **Type:** Experimental report
- **Published / where:** Oxford: H. Hall for Thomas Robinson
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=New%20Experiments%20Physico-Mechanical%2C%20Touching%20the%20Spring%20of%20the%20Air%20Robert%20Boyle)

In New Experiments, Boyle described 43 experiments made with an air pump designed with Robert Hooke, a glass globe from which air could be largely removed. He showed that sound does not travel through a vacuum, that a feather and a coin fall at the same rate, that candles go out and small animals die without air, and that the mercury in a barometer placed inside falls as the air is pumped out, confirming that air pressure supports it. The second edition of 1662, answering critics, included measurements with a J-shaped tube showing that the volume of trapped air varies inversely with the pressure on it, the relation now known as Boyle's law; it had also been found by Richard Towneley and Henry Power, and was independently published by Edme Mariotte in 1676. Boyle's detailed, circumstantial style of reporting, written so that readers could virtually witness the experiments, became a model for scientific writing.

### The Sceptical Chymist (1661)

[![The Sceptical Chymist](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/Sceptical_chymist_1661_Boyle_Title_page_AQ18_%283%29.jpg/960px-Sceptical_chymist_1661_Boyle_Title_page_AQ18_%283%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sceptical_chymist_1661_Boyle_Title_page_AQ18_%283%29.jpg)

<sub>Title page, 1661 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sceptical_chymist_1661_Boyle_Title_page_AQ18_%283%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1661
- **Type:** Chemical treatise (dialogue)
- **Published / where:** London: J. Cadwell for J. Crooke
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Sceptical%20Chymist%20Robert%20Boyle)

The Sceptical Chymist: or Chymico-Physical Doubts & Paradoxes is a dialogue among several speakers in which Carneades, Boyle's spokesman, questions the evidence for the two ruling theories of the composition of matter: the four Aristotelian elements, earth, water, air and fire, and the three principles of the followers of Paracelsus, salt, sulphur and mercury. Boyle argues that analysis by fire does not reveal the true components of bodies and that experiments do not support either theory. He favours a corpuscular philosophy in which matter is made of tiny particles whose size, shape and motion explain chemical properties, and gives a definition of elements as simple bodies not made of other bodies, while doubting that any such elements had yet been found. The book helped make chemistry a respectable branch of natural philosophy, though Boyle himself remained interested in alchemical transmutation all his life.
