## Biography

Nightingale was born into a wealthy English family and named after the city of her birth. Against her family's wishes she trained as a nurse, and in 1854 led a team of nurses to the British military hospital at Scutari during the Crimean War, where she became famous as 'the Lady with the Lamp'. On her return she worked tirelessly, though often bedridden, for reform of army medicine, hospital design and nursing, founding a nursing school at St Thomas' Hospital in 1860.

Nightingale had been taught mathematics as a girl and worked with the statistician William Farr. She collected mortality figures and presented them in striking graphics, notably her polar-area 'rose' diagrams, to persuade officials that sanitary conditions were killing far more soldiers than wounds. In 1858 she became the first woman elected a fellow of the Royal Statistical Society. She held to a miasma view of disease long after germ theory arose, but her use of evidence made her a pioneer of public health statistics.

After the Crimean War, Nightingale worked with the statistician William Farr to analyse the deaths of British soldiers in the military hospitals at Scutari and in the Crimea. To make the findings unmistakable to officials and the public, she designed coloured diagrams, now usually called rose or coxcomb diagrams, in which each month is a wedge of a circle and the area of coloured sections shows the number of deaths from wounds, from other causes, and from 'zymotic', infectious, diseases such as cholera, typhus and dysentery. The large blue areas of preventable disease dwarfed the red of wounds, and the diagrams showed how deaths fell after a Sanitary Commission improved drains, ventilation and water in 1855. Polar-area charts had been used before, but her diagrams became the most famous early example of statistical graphics used for persuasion, and they helped bring about reforms in army health and the design of hospitals.
