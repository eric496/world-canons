# Kurt Gödel

**1906 – 1978** · Austrian-American  
Born: Brünn (Brno), Austria-Hungary · Died: Princeton, New Jersey  
Eras & fields: [Logic & the foundations of mathematics](../movements/logic-foundations.md)  

> Logician who proved that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, transforming the foundations of mathematics.

## Biography

Gödel was born into a German-speaking family in Brno and studied mathematics and physics at the University of Vienna, where he took part in meetings of the Vienna Circle of philosophers. He proved the completeness of first-order logic in his doctoral thesis and his incompleteness theorems in 1931. After the annexation of Austria he emigrated in 1940 to the United States, crossing Siberia and the Pacific, and joined the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, where he became a close friend of Einstein. He suffered from paranoia and died of malnutrition, having refused to eat for fear of poisoning.

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem showed that in any consistent formal system that includes arithmetic there are statements that can neither be proved nor disproved within it; the second showed that such a system cannot prove its own consistency. This ended the hope of Hilbert's programme in its original form. He also showed that the continuum hypothesis cannot be disproved from the usual axioms of set theory, and found solutions to Einstein's equations that allow travel into the past.

## Key works & discoveries

### On Formally Undecidable Propositions (incompleteness theorems) (1931)

- **Date:** 1931
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik, Vienna
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Formally%20Undecidable%20Propositions%20%28incompleteness%20theorems%29%20Kurt%20G%C3%B6del)

Gödel's paper Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme, received in November 1930 and published in 1931, answered a central question of Hilbert's programme. Gödel showed how statements about a formal system, such as 'this formula is provable', can be encoded as statements about numbers within the system itself, by assigning a number to every symbol, formula and proof. He then constructed a statement that in effect says of itself that it is not provable. If the system is consistent, this statement can be neither proved nor disproved within it, and so is true but unprovable. This is the first incompleteness theorem. The second shows that such a system cannot prove its own consistency. The results apply to any system strong enough to express basic arithmetic, including the Principia Mathematica of Whitehead and Russell. They ended the hope of a complete and self-certifying foundation for mathematics, influenced Turing's work on computability and remain among the most discussed results in logic and philosophy.
