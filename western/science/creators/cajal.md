# Santiago Ramón y Cajal

**1852 – 1934** · Spanish  
Born: Petilla de Aragón, Navarre, Spain · Died: Madrid  
Eras & fields: [Public health, germ theory & modern medicine](../movements/germ-theory.md)  

> Spanish histologist who showed that the nervous system is made of separate cells, neurons, and drew them with unmatched skill. He is regarded as the founder of modern neuroscience.

## Biography

Cajal was the son of a country doctor and anatomy teacher in Aragon. A rebellious boy who wanted to be an artist, he trained in medicine at Zaragoza, served as an army doctor in Cuba, where he caught malaria, and became professor of anatomy at Valencia, then of histology at Barcelona and Madrid. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1906 with Camillo Golgi.

Using and improving Golgi's silver staining method, Cajal studied the brain, spinal cord and retina and argued that nerve tissue is not a continuous network, as Golgi believed, but a mass of distinct cells that communicate across small gaps, and that signals flow in one direction through each cell. This neuron doctrine was confirmed by electron microscopy in the 1950s. His thousands of drawings remain both scientific evidence and admired works of art.

## Key works & discoveries

### Drawings of neurons and the neuron doctrine (1888–1904)

- **Date:** 1888–1904
- **Type:** Histological drawings and treatises
- **Published / where:** Instituto Cajal, Madrid (Legado Cajal)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Drawings%20of%20neurons%20and%20the%20neuron%20doctrine%20Santiago%20Ram%C3%B3n%20y%20Cajal)

In 1887 Cajal learned of the 'black reaction' discovered by Camillo Golgi, a silver stain that colours just a few nerve cells completely, revealing their branching shapes against a clear background. He improved it and applied it to the brains of young animals and birds, where cells were less tangled. In 1888 he published evidence, from the cerebellum, that the endings of nerve fibres touch other cells but do not fuse with them, so the nervous system is made of independent units, later called neurons, rather than a continuous net as Golgi and most others held. He proposed that nerve impulses travel in one direction, from the branching dendrites through the cell body to the axon. He set out his findings in his great Textura del sistema nervioso del hombre y de los vertebrados of 1899–1904, illustrated with thousands of drawings in ink and pencil, of the retina, cortex, spinal cord and other tissues, which combine scientific accuracy with remarkable beauty. The neuron doctrine was definitively confirmed by electron microscopy in the 1950s.
