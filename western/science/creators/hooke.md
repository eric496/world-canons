# Robert Hooke

**1635 – 1703** · English  
Born: Freshwater, Isle of Wight · Died: London  
Eras & fields: [Scientific Revolution](../movements/scientific-revolution.md)  

> Experimenter, architect and first curator of experiments of the Royal Society, whose Micrographia revealed the microscopic world and named the cell.

## Biography

Hooke was the son of a curate on the Isle of Wight. He went to Westminster School and Oxford, where he became assistant to Robert Boyle and built his air pump. In 1662 he was appointed curator of experiments to the new Royal Society, charged with demonstrating several experiments at each meeting, and he later became professor of geometry at Gresham College. After the Great Fire of 1666 he was a City surveyor and worked with Christopher Wren on the rebuilding of London.

Hooke's ideas ranged across microscopy, elasticity, where Hooke's law describes springs, astronomy, geology, horology and the theory of light. He suggested that gravity weakens with the square of distance and quarrelled bitterly with Newton over credit. Micrographia made him famous, and its plates of insects and plant tissue are among the most celebrated scientific images ever published. No authenticated portrait of him survives.

## Key works & discoveries

### Micrographia (1665)

[![Micrographia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Flea_Micrographia_Hooke.png/1280px-Flea_Micrographia_Hooke.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flea_Micrographia_Hooke.png)

<sub>Hooke's engraving of a flea Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flea_Micrographia_Hooke.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1665
- **Type:** Illustrated book of observations
- **Published / where:** London: John Martyn and James Allestry, printers to the Royal Society
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Micrographia%20Robert%20Hooke)

Micrographia, or Some Physiological Descriptions of Minute Bodies Made by Magnifying Glasses, was the first important publication of the Royal Society. Hooke described about sixty observations made with a compound microscope he had improved, of objects from the point of a needle and the edge of a razor to snowflakes, moulds, mineral crystals and insects, and some telescopic observations of the Moon and stars. In observing a slice of cork he saw tiny box-like compartments which he called cells, the origin of the biological term, though he saw only the empty walls of dead plant tissue. The large fold-out engravings, mostly from Hooke's own drawings, of a flea and a louse caused a sensation; Samuel Pepys sat up until two in the morning reading it and called it the most ingenious book he had ever read. Hooke also put forward ideas on light as a wave, combustion and the origin of fossils.
