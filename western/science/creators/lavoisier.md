## Biography

Lavoisier was born into a wealthy Parisian family and trained in law before turning to science. He joined the Royal Academy of Sciences in 1768 and bought a share in the Ferme générale, the private company that collected taxes, which financed his laboratory. His wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, translated English chemistry for him, kept his records and drew the plates of his books. He also directed the royal gunpowder administration and worked on agriculture and the metric system. Because of his role as a tax farmer he was arrested during the Revolution and guillotined in May 1794.

Using precise balances and sealed vessels, Lavoisier showed that combustion and the rusting of metals involve combination with a component of the air, which he named oxygen, overturning the theory of phlogiston. He showed that water is a compound of oxygen and hydrogen, and he treated chemical reactions as balanced equations in which mass is conserved. With colleagues he introduced the systematic chemical nomenclature still in use, and his Traité élémentaire de chimie of 1789 defined an element as a substance that cannot be further decomposed.

## Key works & discoveries

### Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789)

- **Date:** 1789
- **Type:** Chemical textbook
- **Published / where:** Paris: Cuchet
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Trait%C3%A9%20%C3%A9l%C3%A9mentaire%20de%20chimie%20Antoine%20Lavoisier)

The Elementary Treatise of Chemistry, published in the year the French Revolution began, presented Lavoisier's new chemistry as a coherent system for students. Using the reformed nomenclature that he and his colleagues had introduced in 1787, it explained combustion, respiration and the calcination of metals as combination with oxygen, rejecting the phlogiston theory. It defined a chemical element practically, as any substance that has not yet been decomposed, and listed 33 of them, including oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen and carbon, but also light and caloric, the supposed fluid of heat. It stated clearly that in every operation the quantity of matter is the same before and after, the principle of conservation of mass that underlies chemical equations. The second part describes the apparatus of the laboratory, illustrated by thirteen plates engraved from drawings by Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier. It was translated into English in 1790 and won over most chemists within a decade.
