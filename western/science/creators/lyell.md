## Biography

Lyell was the eldest son of a wealthy Scottish landowner and botanist. He studied at Oxford, where William Buckland's lectures drew him to geology, and trained as a barrister, but soon devoted himself to science. Field trips in France, Italy and Sicily, including the volcano Etna, convinced him of the power of gradual change. He was professor of geology at King's College London for a short time, and travelled widely in Europe and North America.

In the Principles of Geology, Lyell argued against catastrophist theories that the Earth's features were made by sudden upheavals, and claimed that present-day causes acting over vast periods were sufficient, a view later called uniformitarianism. Darwin read the first volume on the Beagle and later became Lyell's close friend. Lyell was long reluctant to accept evolution, especially for humans, but in his later work he came to support Darwin's theory.

## Key works & discoveries

### Principles of Geology (1830–1833)

[![Principles of Geology](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9e/Charles_Lyell_-_Pillars_of_Pozzuoli.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Lyell_-_Pillars_of_Pozzuoli.jpg)

<sub>Frontispiece: the columns of the 'Temple of Serapis' at Pozzuoli Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Lyell_-_Pillars_of_Pozzuoli.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1830–1833
- **Type:** Geological treatise in three volumes
- **Published / where:** London: John Murray
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Principles%20of%20Geology%20Charles%20Lyell)

Principles of Geology, being an Attempt to Explain the Former Changes of the Earth's Surface, by Reference to Causes now in Operation, was published in three volumes and revised through twelve editions in Lyell's lifetime. Against geologists who explained mountains, valleys and fossil extinctions by past catastrophes far more violent than any seen today, Lyell argued that ordinary processes, erosion, sedimentation, earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, acting over immense periods of time, were sufficient to explain everything. The frontispiece shows the columns of the so-called Temple of Serapis at Pozzuoli near Naples, bored by marine molluscs, as evidence that the land had slowly sunk beneath the sea and risen again. The second volume discussed the distribution and extinction of species, though Lyell then rejected transmutation. Darwin took the first volume on the Beagle and saw the land through Lyell's eyes; the long timescale Lyell established was essential to the theory of evolution.
