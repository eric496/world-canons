# Bernhard Riemann

**1826 – 1866** · German  
Born: Breselenz, Kingdom of Hanover · Died: Selasca, Italy  
Eras & fields: [Mathematics in the age of rigour](../movements/nineteenth-century-mathematics.md)  

> Mathematician whose geometry of curved spaces provided the mathematics of general relativity, and whose hypothesis about prime numbers remains the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics.

## Biography

Riemann was the son of a Lutheran pastor in a village near the Elbe and was shy and often unwell. He studied at Göttingen and Berlin and took his doctorate under Gauss in 1851. He taught at Göttingen, succeeding Dirichlet as professor in 1859. Tuberculosis forced him to seek the Italian climate, and he died on Lake Maggiore at thirty-nine.

In a few highly original papers Riemann transformed complex analysis, introduced the Riemann integral and Riemann surfaces, and in his 1854 lecture on the foundations of geometry described spaces of any number of dimensions whose curvature can vary from point to point. Einstein used this geometry for general relativity. His single paper on prime numbers of 1859 linked their distribution to the zeros of a function, the zeta function, and his conjecture about those zeros, the Riemann hypothesis, is still unproved.

### On the Hypotheses which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry (1854; published 1868)

- **Date:** 1854; published 1868
- **Type:** Lecture
- **Published / where:** University of Göttingen; published in the Abhandlungen der Königlichen Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20Hypotheses%20which%20Lie%20at%20the%20Foundations%20of%20Geometry%20Bernhard%20Riemann)

For his habilitation, the qualification to teach in a German university, Riemann had to give a lecture on a topic chosen by the faculty from three he proposed, and Gauss, against custom, chose the third, on the foundations of geometry. Delivered on 10 June 1854 before an audience that mostly could not follow it, the lecture contained almost no formulas. Riemann described manifolds, spaces of any number of dimensions, and showed how their geometry could be determined by a rule for measuring distances between nearby points, so that curvature could vary from place to place. Euclidean geometry and the non-Euclidean geometries of Lobachevsky and Bolyai became special cases. He suggested that the geometry of physical space was a question for experience and might depend on the forces acting in it. The lecture was published only after his death, in 1868. The mathematics it opened, developed by Christoffel, Ricci and Levi-Civita, provided the language Einstein needed for general relativity.

### On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude (1859)

- **Date:** 1859
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Monatsberichte der Berliner Akademie, Berlin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20Number%20of%20Primes%20Less%20Than%20a%20Given%20Magnitude%20Bernhard%20Riemann)

Riemann wrote this eight-page paper on his election to the Berlin Academy. Taking the zeta function, which Euler had shown is related to the prime numbers, he extended it to complex numbers and showed how an exact formula for the number of primes up to any given size could be written in terms of the zeros of this function. He remarked, almost in passing, that it was very likely that all the non-trivial zeros lie on a single line in the complex plane, but that he had put aside the search for a proof. This statement, the Riemann hypothesis, would give the most precise possible information about how the primes are distributed. It was the eighth of Hilbert's problems in 1900 and is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems; billions of zeros have been checked by computer, but it remains unproved. The paper's methods were used in 1896 to prove the prime number theorem.
