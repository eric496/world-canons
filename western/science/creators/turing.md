# Alan Turing

**1912 – 1954** · English  
Born: Maida Vale, London · Died: Wilmslow, Cheshire  
Eras & fields: [Computing & information](../movements/computing.md), [Logic & the foundations of mathematics](../movements/logic-foundations.md)  

> Mathematician who defined the theoretical idea of the universal computer, led the breaking of German naval codes in the Second World War, and posed the question of machine intelligence.

## Biography

Turing studied mathematics at King's College, Cambridge, and wrote his paper on computable numbers in 1936, then took a doctorate at Princeton under Alonzo Church. During the Second World War he worked at Bletchley Park, designing the electromechanical Bombe used to break Enigma messages and leading the attack on German naval codes. After the war he designed the ACE computer at the National Physical Laboratory and worked on early computers at Manchester. In 1952 he was convicted of homosexual acts and subjected to chemical castration; he died of cyanide poisoning in 1954, ruled a suicide. He received a posthumous royal pardon in 2013.

Turing's 1936 paper described a simple abstract machine that can perform any computation that can be precisely specified, and a universal machine that can imitate any other, the theoretical basis of the stored-program computer; he used it to show that some mathematical problems cannot be solved by any mechanical procedure. In 1950 he proposed the imitation game, now called the Turing test, as a way of asking whether machines can think, and in his last years he did pioneering work on the mathematics of biological pattern formation.

### On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem (1936)

- **Date:** 1936
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Computable%20Numbers%2C%20with%20an%20Application%20to%20the%20Entscheidungsproblem%20Alan%20Turing)

Hilbert had asked whether there is a definite mechanical procedure that can decide, for any statement of mathematical logic, whether it is provable, the Entscheidungsproblem or decision problem. To answer, Turing, aged twenty-three, analysed what a human computer does when calculating with pencil and paper, and reduced it to a simple abstract machine that reads and writes symbols on an endless tape, one square at a time, following a finite table of rules. He showed that there is a universal machine that, given the description of any other machine on its tape, can imitate it, the essential idea of the stored-program computer. He then proved that no machine can decide, for every machine, whether it will ever print a certain symbol, a result related to what is now called the halting problem, and concluded that the decision problem has no solution. Alonzo Church had reached the same conclusion by a different method shortly before. The Turing machine became the standard definition of computation and the foundation of computer science.

### The Bombe (1940)

- **Date:** 1940
- **Type:** Electromechanical codebreaking machine
- **Published / where:** Bletchley Park, Buckinghamshire (working reconstruction at The National Museum of Computing)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Bombe%20Alan%20Turing)

The German armed forces enciphered their messages with Enigma machines whose rotors and plugboard settings were changed daily, giving an astronomical number of possibilities. Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski had broken early Enigma traffic and built a machine called the bomba, and shared their methods with Britain and France in July 1939. At Bletchley Park, Turing designed a much more powerful and general machine that tested possible rotor settings against a 'crib', a guessed fragment of plain text, using chains of logical deductions to eliminate impossible settings quickly. Gordon Welchman added the 'diagonal board', which greatly increased its power. The first Bombe, named Victory, began work in March 1940, built by the British Tabulating Machine Company, and over two hundred were in use by the end of the war, operated mostly by members of the Women's Royal Naval Service. The intelligence obtained, code-named Ultra, was vital in the Battle of the Atlantic. All the Bombes were dismantled after the war; a working reconstruction was completed in 2006.

### Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950)

- **Date:** 1950
- **Type:** Philosophical paper
- **Published / where:** Mind, Oxford
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Computing%20Machinery%20and%20Intelligence%20Alan%20Turing)

Turing opens this paper by proposing to replace the question 'Can machines think?', which he regarded as too vague to discuss, with a game: an interrogator communicates by typed messages with a human and a machine and tries to tell which is which. If the machine can play the imitation game as well as a human, he suggests, there is no good reason to deny that it thinks. He then explains what digital computers are and why one could in principle imitate any discrete machine, and answers nine objections, including theological ones, the argument from consciousness, the mathematical objection from Gödel's theorem and Lady Lovelace's objection that a machine can only do what it is told. He predicts that by about the end of the century machines would play the game well enough that general opinion would accept talk of machines thinking. He ends by proposing that, rather than programming an adult mind, it would be better to build a 'child machine' and educate it, an early statement of the idea of machine learning. The paper is a founding text of artificial intelligence.
