## Biography

Harrison was the son of a carpenter and grew up in Barrow upon Humber in Lincolnshire, where he built wooden clocks with remarkable innovations to reduce friction and the effects of temperature. In 1714 the British Parliament offered a prize of up to £20,000 for a practical method of finding longitude at sea, and Harrison moved to London and devoted the rest of his life to the problem, supported by the clockmaker George Graham.

Over some thirty years he built three large sea clocks, H1 to H3, and then the large watch H4, which on trials to Jamaica and Barbados in the 1760s kept time well enough to fix longitude within the terms of the prize. The Board of Longitude was slow to pay, partly because many favoured an astronomical method, and Harrison received his reward only in stages, with the help of George III. His work led to the marine chronometer, essential for navigation until the age of radio.

## Key works & discoveries

### Marine chronometer H4 (1759)

[![Marine chronometer H4](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0d/Harrison_H4_chronometer.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Harrison_H4_chronometer.jpg)

<sub>H4, National Maritime Museum, Greenwich Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Harrison_H4_chronometer.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1759
- **Type:** Marine timekeeper
- **Published / where:** National Maritime Museum, Royal Observatory, Greenwich
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Marine%20chronometer%20H4%20John%20Harrison)

To find longitude at sea, a navigator needs to know the time at a reference place such as Greenwich while measuring local time by the Sun; each hour of difference corresponds to fifteen degrees. Pendulum clocks are useless on a rolling ship, and Harrison's first three sea clocks, H1 to H3, were large and complex machines with balances and springs. In H4, completed in 1759, he turned to a watch about thirteen centimetres across, with a fast-beating balance, diamond pallets and a mechanism to compensate for temperature. On a voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62 it lost only five seconds in 81 days, and a second trial to Barbados in 1764 confirmed its accuracy. The Board of Longitude demanded further proof and copies, and Harrison was fully rewarded only after appealing to George III and Parliament in 1773. Larcum Kendall's copy, K1, accompanied Captain Cook on his second voyage, and Harrison's ideas led to the chronometers that guided ships for two centuries. H1 to H4 are displayed at Greenwich.
