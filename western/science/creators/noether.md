# Emmy Noether

**1882 – 1935** · German  
Born: Erlangen, Bavaria · Died: Bryn Mawr, Pennsylvania  
Eras & fields: [Relativity & quantum mechanics](../movements/relativity-quantum.md), [Mathematics in the age of rigour](../movements/nineteenth-century-mathematics.md)  

> Mathematician who discovered the deep link between symmetries and conservation laws in physics and was a founder of modern abstract algebra.

## Biography

Noether was the daughter of the mathematician Max Noether in Erlangen, where women were allowed to enrol at the university only from 1904. She took her doctorate there in 1907 and worked for years without pay. In 1915 Hilbert and Klein invited her to Göttingen, but faculty opposition to a woman lecturer meant she could only teach under Hilbert's name until 1919, and she was never given a full professorship. Dismissed in 1933 because she was Jewish, she moved to Bryn Mawr College in the United States, where she died after surgery two years later.

Noether's theorem of 1918 shows that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity: symmetry in time gives conservation of energy, symmetry in space conservation of momentum. It became a guiding principle of twentieth-century physics. In the 1920s her work on rings, ideals and modules transformed algebra into the abstract, structural subject it is today, and she inspired a large circle of students, the 'Noether boys'.

## Key works & discoveries

### Invariante Variationsprobleme (Noether's theorem) (1918)

- **Date:** 1918
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Nachrichten von der Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Invariante%20Variationsprobleme%20%28Noether%27s%20theorem%29%20Emmy%20Noether)

Hilbert and Klein had asked Noether to help them understand a puzzle in general relativity: energy did not seem to be conserved in the usual way. In this paper, presented to the Göttingen society by Klein in July 1918 because she was not a member, Noether proved two general theorems about systems described by a principle of least action. The first shows that every continuous symmetry of such a system corresponds to a conserved quantity: if the laws are unchanged by shifts in time, energy is conserved; by shifts in space, momentum; by rotations, angular momentum. The second explains why in theories like general relativity, whose symmetries depend on arbitrary functions, conservation laws take a different form. The first theorem became one of the most important tools of twentieth-century physics, used throughout quantum field theory and the Standard Model of particle physics, where conserved quantities such as electric charge are traced to symmetries. Einstein praised her penetrating mathematical thinking.
