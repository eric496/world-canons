## Biography

Gauss was the son of a poor bricklayer and gardener in Brunswick. His precocious gift for calculation came to the attention of the Duke of Brunswick, who paid for his education at the Collegium Carolinum and the University of Göttingen. In 1796 he showed that a regular seventeen-sided polygon can be constructed with ruler and compass, and in 1801 published his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae. From 1807 until his death he was director of the Göttingen observatory and professor of astronomy.

Gauss made fundamental contributions to number theory, algebra, where he gave a proof of the fundamental theorem, complex numbers, differential geometry and statistics, where the normal distribution is often named after him. He calculated the orbit of the newly found asteroid Ceres from a handful of observations using his method of least squares, surveyed the Kingdom of Hanover, and with Wilhelm Weber studied the Earth's magnetism and built an early electric telegraph. Cautious and perfectionist, he left many discoveries, including non-Euclidean geometry, unpublished, guided by the motto 'few, but ripe'.

### Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801)

<sub>Title page, 1801 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Disqvisitiones-800.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1801
- **Type:** Mathematical treatise
- **Published / where:** Leipzig: Gerhard Fleischer
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Disquisitiones%20Arithmeticae%20Carl%20Friedrich%20Gauss)

The Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, Arithmetical Investigations, was written in Latin when Gauss was twenty-one and published in 1801 with funding from the Duke of Brunswick. It turned number theory, previously a collection of isolated results by Fermat, Euler, Lagrange and Legendre, into a coherent discipline. It introduced the notation of congruence, writing a ≡ b (mod m) when two numbers leave the same remainder on division by m, which made 'clock arithmetic' a powerful tool. It gave the first complete proof of the law of quadratic reciprocity, which Gauss called the golden theorem, developed the theory of quadratic forms, and in its final section explained which regular polygons can be constructed with ruler and compass, including the 17-sided polygon he had discovered at eighteen. Its compressed style made it hard reading, but it set the agenda of number theory throughout the nineteenth century.

### Theoria motus corporum coelestium (1809)

[![Theoria motus corporum coelestium](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b6/Gauss%2C_Carl_Friedrich_%E2%80%93_Theoria_motus_corporum_coelestium_in_sectionibus_conicis_solem_ambientium%2C_1809_%E2%80%93_BEIC_12217180.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gauss%2C_Carl_Friedrich_%E2%80%93_Theoria_motus_corporum_coelestium_in_sectionibus_conicis_solem_ambientium%2C_1809_%E2%80%93_BEIC_12217180.jpg)

<sub>Title page, 1809 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gauss%2C_Carl_Friedrich_%E2%80%93_Theoria_motus_corporum_coelestium_in_sectionibus_conicis_solem_ambientium%2C_1809_%E2%80%93_BEIC_12217180.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1809
- **Type:** Astronomical treatise
- **Published / where:** Hamburg: Friedrich Perthes and I. H. Besser
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Theoria%20motus%20corporum%20coelestium%20Carl%20Friedrich%20Gauss)

On 1 January 1801 Giuseppe Piazzi discovered a small new planet, Ceres, but lost it behind the glare of the Sun after observing it for only about forty days. Gauss, using new methods, calculated its orbit from these few observations and predicted where it would reappear, and astronomers found it there in December 1801, making him famous. The Theory of the Motion of Heavenly Bodies Moving about the Sun in Conic Sections, published in 1809, set out his methods for determining orbits. It included the method of least squares, for finding the best fit to observations affected by error, with a justification based on the assumption that errors follow what is now called the normal or Gaussian distribution. Legendre had published least squares in 1805, and Gauss's claim to have used it since 1795 led to a priority dispute. The method became the standard tool for analysing measurements in all the sciences.
