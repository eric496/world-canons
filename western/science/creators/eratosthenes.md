# Eratosthenes of Cyrene

**c. 276 BC – c. 195 BC** · Greek  
Born: Cyrene, Libya · Died: Alexandria  
Eras & fields: [Hellenistic science & mathematics](../movements/hellenistic-science.md)  

> Scholar and chief librarian of Alexandria who measured the circumference of the Earth with remarkable accuracy and founded scientific geography.

## Biography

Born in the Greek city of Cyrene in North Africa, Eratosthenes studied in Athens and was invited to Alexandria by Ptolemy III, where he became head of the great Library. He wrote on an astonishing range of subjects, from literary criticism and chronology to poetry, mathematics and geography, and his rivals nicknamed him 'Beta', second best at everything. He is said to have starved himself to death after losing his sight in old age.

His best-known achievement is the measurement of the Earth's circumference from the angle of the Sun's shadow at Alexandria and Syene. He also drew a world map with lines of latitude and longitude, coined the word geography, compiled a chronology of Greek history, and devised the sieve of Eratosthenes for finding prime numbers. His works survive only in fragments and in the reports of later authors.

## Key works & discoveries

### Measurement of the Earth's circumference (c. 240 BC)

- **Date:** c. 240 BC
- **Type:** Geodetic measurement
- **Published / where:** Alexandria and Syene (Aswan), Egypt
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Measurement%20of%20the%20Earth%27s%20circumference%20Eratosthenes%20of%20Cyrene)

Eratosthenes' treatise On the Measurement of the Earth is lost, but the Greek astronomer Cleomedes describes his method. At Syene, near the Tropic of Cancer, the Sun stood directly overhead at noon on the summer solstice and lit the bottom of a well, while at Alexandria, believed to lie due north, a vertical rod cast a shadow showing the Sun about one-fiftieth of a circle from the vertical. Since the Sun is so far away that its rays are effectively parallel, the arc between the two cities must be one-fiftieth of the Earth's circumference. Taking the distance as 5,000 stadia, he obtained 250,000 stadia, later adjusted to 252,000. How accurate this was depends on the length of the stadion he used, which is debated, but estimates fall within a few per cent to about fifteen per cent of the true value. The method is a classic of scientific reasoning and is still repeated by schoolchildren.
