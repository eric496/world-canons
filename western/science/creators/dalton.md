# John Dalton

> Quaker teacher in Manchester who turned the ancient idea of atoms into a quantitative chemical theory, with each element made of atoms of a characteristic weight.

## Biography

Dalton was the son of a Quaker weaver in Cumberland and was teaching school by the age of twelve. In 1793 he moved to Manchester to teach at a dissenting academy, and spent the rest of his life there as a private tutor and an active member of the Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society. He kept a daily weather diary for over fifty years, and his account of his own colour blindness was the first scientific description of the condition, sometimes called Daltonism.

From studies of gases and the weather, Dalton came to the view that each chemical element consists of identical, indestructible atoms with a particular weight, and that compounds form when atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios. He set out the theory, with the first table of relative atomic weights and circular symbols for the elements, in his New System of Chemical Philosophy from 1808. Many of his values were wrong and his symbols did not catch on, but the atomic theory became the foundation of nineteenth-century chemistry.

## Key works & discoveries

### A New System of Chemical Philosophy (1808–1827)

- **Date:** 1808–1827
- **Type:** Chemical treatise
- **Published / where:** Manchester: R. Bickerstaff (London) and S. Russell (Manchester)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20New%20System%20of%20Chemical%20Philosophy%20John%20Dalton)

In the first part of A New System of Chemical Philosophy, published in 1808, Dalton set out the atomic theory he had been developing since about 1803. Each chemical element, he proposed, is made of identical atoms that cannot be created, destroyed or divided in chemical reactions, the atoms of different elements differ in weight, and compounds are formed when atoms combine in fixed simple ratios. This explained why elements combine in definite proportions, and why, when two elements form more than one compound, the amounts of one combining with a fixed amount of the other are in simple whole-number ratios, the law of multiple proportions. A plate showed his symbols for elements, circles with dots, lines or letters, and diagrams of compound atoms. Taking hydrogen as one, he gave a table of relative weights. Because he assumed the simplest formulas, such as HO for water, many of his weights were wrong, and it took half a century to agree on correct values, but his theory became the foundation of chemistry.
