## Biography

Augusta Ada Byron was the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron, who left the family weeks after her birth. Her mother, Annabella Milbanke, had her taught mathematics and science, partly to steer her away from her father's temperament. She met Charles Babbage in 1833 and became a close friend and correspondent. In 1835 she married William King, later Earl of Lovelace. She studied mathematics with Augustus De Morgan, suffered long periods of illness and died of cancer at thirty-six.

In 1843 Lovelace translated an Italian article by Luigi Menabrea on the Analytical Engine and added notes three times its length. They explained how the engine could be instructed and included a table of operations for calculating Bernoulli numbers, often called the first computer program, and speculated that it might compose music. How much of the mathematics was her own and how much Babbage's is debated, but her vision of the computer as a general symbol-manipulating machine was unique in her time.

## Key works & discoveries

### Notes on the Analytical Engine (Note G) (1843)

- **Date:** 1843
- **Type:** Translation with notes
- **Published / where:** Scientific Memoirs, vol. 3, London: Richard and John E. Taylor
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Notes%20on%20the%20Analytical%20Engine%20%28Note%20G%29%20Ada%20Lovelace)

In 1842 the Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea published in French a description of the Analytical Engine based on lectures Babbage had given in Turin. Ada Lovelace translated it into English and, at Babbage's suggestion, added seven notes, labelled A to G, signed only with her initials A.A.L. In them she explained the difference between the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine, how the engine could be instructed through cards, and the use of repeated sequences of operations. Note G gave a detailed table of the steps by which the engine could calculate Bernoulli numbers, often described as the first published computer program, though how much she and Babbage each contributed is debated. Most strikingly, she argued that the engine could act on any objects whose relations could be expressed by symbols, so that it might compose elaborate music, while adding that it could originate nothing and only do what it was ordered to perform, a remark that Alan Turing discussed as 'Lady Lovelace's objection'.
