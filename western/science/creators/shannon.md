# Claude Shannon

**1916 – 2001** · American  
Born: Petoskey, Michigan · Died: Medford, Massachusetts  
Eras & fields: [Computing & information](../movements/computing.md)  

> Mathematician and engineer who founded information theory and showed that Boolean logic could be used to design switching circuits.

## Biography

Shannon grew up in Gaylord, Michigan, studied electrical engineering and mathematics at the University of Michigan and went to MIT, where he worked on Vannevar Bush's differential analyser. His 1937 master's thesis showed that relay circuits could be analysed and designed with Boole's algebra. He worked at Bell Telephone Laboratories from 1941, including wartime work on cryptography, and from 1956 taught at MIT. He was also known for his juggling, unicycling and playful machines.

Shannon's 1948 paper 'A Mathematical Theory of Communication' defined information as a measurable quantity, measured in bits, and showed that every communication channel has a capacity below which messages can be sent with as few errors as desired using suitable codes. It laid the foundation for digital communication, data compression and error-correcting codes. He also wrote early papers on programming a computer to play chess.

## Key works & discoveries

### A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948)

- **Date:** 1948
- **Type:** Scientific paper
- **Published / where:** Bell System Technical Journal, New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Mathematical%20Theory%20of%20Communication%20Claude%20Shannon)

Published in two parts in the Bell System Technical Journal in July and October 1948, Shannon's paper set out a general mathematical model of communication: a source produces a message, a transmitter encodes it into a signal, the signal passes through a channel where noise may corrupt it, and a receiver decodes it for its destination. He defined the information produced by a source as a measure of its uncertainty, which he called entropy by analogy with physics, and measured it in binary digits, or bits, a term he credited to John Tukey. He proved that messages can be compressed down to their entropy and no further, and that every noisy channel has a capacity: below that rate, information can be transmitted with an arbitrarily small rate of error by suitable coding, while above it reliable communication is impossible. These results gave engineers the limits within which they worked and the goals to aim for, and they underlie data compression, error-correcting codes, mobile telephones, the internet and space communication. The paper was republished as a book with Warren Weaver in 1949.
