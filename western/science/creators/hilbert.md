# David Hilbert

**1862 – 1943** · German  
Born: Königsberg, Prussia · Died: Göttingen, Germany  
Eras & fields: [Logic & the foundations of mathematics](../movements/logic-foundations.md)  

> Leading mathematician of the early twentieth century, who set the agenda of modern mathematics with his 23 problems and sought to place it on secure formal foundations.

## Biography

Hilbert was born and educated in Königsberg, where he formed a lifelong friendship with Hermann Minkowski. In 1895 he moved to Göttingen, which under him and Felix Klein became the world centre of mathematics, attracting students and visitors from everywhere, including Emmy Noether, whom he fought to have appointed. After the Nazis expelled many of his colleagues in 1933, the institute was a shadow of itself, and he died in Göttingen during the Second World War.

Hilbert did fundamental work in invariant theory, number theory, the axioms of geometry, integral equations and the mathematics of physics, where the Hilbert space is named after him. At the 1900 International Congress in Paris he presented a list of problems that shaped twentieth-century research. His programme to prove mathematics consistent and complete by finite formal methods was shown by Gödel in 1931 to be unachievable as he had hoped, but it gave birth to modern logic and proof theory.

## Key works & discoveries

### Hilbert's problems (1900)

- **Date:** 1900
- **Type:** Lecture and list of problems
- **Published / where:** International Congress of Mathematicians, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hilbert%27s%20problems%20David%20Hilbert)

On 8 August 1900, at the second International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris, Hilbert gave a lecture on the future of mathematics, arguing that every well-posed problem has a solution and that problems are the lifeblood of the subject. He presented ten problems in the lecture and published twenty-three in the printed version. They included Cantor's continuum hypothesis, the consistency of the axioms of arithmetic, the Riemann hypothesis, the axiomatisation of physics and the existence of an algorithm to decide whether a Diophantine equation has whole-number solutions. Over the following century work on them produced major advances: Gödel's and Paul Cohen's work showed that the continuum hypothesis cannot be decided from the usual axioms, Gödel's incompleteness theorems bore on the second problem, and Yuri Matiyasevich showed in 1970 that the tenth has no general solution. Some are solved, some were too vague to be settled, and some, including the Riemann hypothesis, remain open.
