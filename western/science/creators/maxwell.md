## Biography

Maxwell grew up on the family estate of Glenlair in Galloway and published his first scientific paper, on oval curves, at fourteen. He studied at Edinburgh and Cambridge, then taught at Aberdeen and King's College London, where he did his most important work on electromagnetism. After some years at Glenlair he became the first Cavendish Professor of Experimental Physics at Cambridge in 1871 and planned the Cavendish Laboratory. He died of abdominal cancer at forty-eight.

Building on Faraday's lines of force, Maxwell developed a set of equations describing electric and magnetic fields and showed that they predict waves travelling at the speed of light, concluding that light is an electromagnetic wave. Heinrich Hertz produced such waves in 1887, opening the way to radio. Maxwell also explained Saturn's rings, made the first colour photograph with his three-colour method, and with Boltzmann founded statistical mechanics. Einstein kept his picture on the wall of his study, alongside those of Newton and Faraday.

### First colour photograph (tartan ribbon) (1861)

[![First colour photograph (tartan ribbon)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7f/Tartan_Ribbon.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tartan_Ribbon.jpg)

<sub>The tartan ribbon, projected by Maxwell in 1861 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tartan_Ribbon.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1861
- **Type:** Three-colour photographic demonstration
- **Published / where:** Royal Institution, London; plates at the James Clerk Maxwell Foundation, Edinburgh
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=First%20colour%20photograph%20%28tartan%20ribbon%29%20James%20Clerk%20Maxwell)

Maxwell's studies of colour vision showed that any colour the eye perceives can be matched by mixing three primary lights, red, green and blue. In 1855 he suggested that a colour photograph could be made by taking three black-and-white photographs through red, green and blue filters, and projecting them superimposed through the same filters. For a lecture at the Royal Institution in May 1861, the photographer Thomas Sutton photographed a tartan ribbon in this way, and the projected image reproduced its colours. The result was better than it should have been, because the photographic emulsions of the time were barely sensitive to red; a century later it was found that the red filter had passed ultraviolet light reflected by the red dyes. The three-colour principle underlies colour photography, television and digital screens.

### A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field (1865)

- **Date:** 1865
- **Type:** Scientific paper
- **Published / where:** Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Dynamical%20Theory%20of%20the%20Electromagnetic%20Field%20James%20Clerk%20Maxwell)

Maxwell read this paper to the Royal Society in December 1864 and published it in 1865. Earlier he had modelled Faraday's lines of force with an imaginary mechanism of spinning vortices, and had found that it predicted waves travelling at the speed of light. In this paper he dropped the mechanical model and described the electromagnetic field directly by twenty equations relating electric and magnetic forces, currents and charges, including the displacement current he had introduced. From them he derived waves of electric and magnetic disturbance travelling at a speed that matched the measured speed of light, and concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic disturbance. Oliver Heaviside later condensed the equations into the four now known as Maxwell's equations. Heinrich Hertz produced and detected electromagnetic waves in 1887–88, leading to radio, and the theory raised the questions about the speed of light that Einstein answered with relativity.

### A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism (1873)

- **Date:** 1873
- **Type:** Treatise in two volumes
- **Published / where:** Oxford: Clarendon Press
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Treatise%20on%20Electricity%20and%20Magnetism%20James%20Clerk%20Maxwell)

In the two volumes of the Treatise, Maxwell gathered the whole of electricity and magnetism, experimental and theoretical, into a single system based on the field. He covered electrostatics, electric currents, magnetism and electromagnetism, treated mathematical methods such as potential theory and spherical harmonics, described measuring instruments, and set out his electromagnetic theory of light. He predicted that light would exert a pressure on surfaces it falls on, which was later confirmed. The book was difficult and in places obscure, and much of the work of the following generation, by Heaviside, Hertz, George FitzGerald, Oliver Lodge and others, the so-called Maxwellians, consisted of clarifying and simplifying it. Einstein later called the change from action at a distance to fields the most profound since Newton.
