# Nicole Oresme

<a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oresme.jpg"><img align="right" width="240" src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/Oresme.jpg/330px-Oresme.jpg" alt="Nicole Oresme" title="Nicole Oresme in a medieval manuscript miniature"></a>

**c. 1320 – 1382** · French  
Born: Normandy (probably near Caen) · Died: Lisieux  
Eras & fields: [Medieval Latin scholarship](../movements/medieval-latin-science.md)  

> Paris scholar and bishop who graphed changing quantities, analysed motion and argued that the daily rotation of the Earth could not be disproved. He was one of the most original thinkers of the fourteenth century.

<sub>Portrait: Nicole Oresme in a medieval manuscript miniature · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oresme.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Oresme studied at the University of Paris and became master of the College of Navarre. He was an adviser to King Charles V of France, for whom he translated Aristotle's works on ethics, politics and the heavens into French, coining many French scientific words in the process, and he wrote an important treatise on money. He ended his life as bishop of Lisieux.

In his treatise on the configurations of qualities he represented changing quantities by geometrical figures, a forerunner of the graph, and gave a geometric proof of the mean-speed rule for uniformly accelerated motion later used by Galileo. In his commentary on Aristotle's On the Heavens he argued that no observation or argument could prove that the Earth does not rotate daily, though he concluded, as faith required, that it stands still. He also attacked astrology and proved that the harmonic series diverges.

## Key works & discoveries

### Le Livre du ciel et du monde (1377)

[![Le Livre du ciel et du monde](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Oresme_Spheres.jpg/1280px-Oresme_Spheres.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oresme_Spheres.jpg)

<sub>Miniature of the celestial spheres from a manuscript of Oresme Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oresme_Spheres.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1377
- **Type:** Translation and commentary
- **Published / where:** Paris; written for King Charles V
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Le%20Livre%20du%20ciel%20et%20du%20monde%20Nicole%20Oresme)

Oresme translated Aristotle's On the Heavens into French for King Charles V and added a long commentary, finished in 1377. In its most famous passage he examines the question whether the Earth rotates daily while the heavens are at rest. He argues that all observations would look the same either way, since motion is relative, answering objections about falling stones and arrows shot into the air by pointing out that they share the Earth's motion. He adds that a rotating Earth would be simpler and more economical than turning the vast heavens. Yet he ends by saying that he believes, as is commonly held, that the heavens move and the Earth does not. The text also discusses the possibility of other worlds and compares the universe to a clock set going by God. Whether Copernicus knew Oresme's arguments is uncertain, but they show that medieval thinkers had already undermined some arguments against the Earth's motion.
