# William Harvey

<a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:William_Harvey_2.jpg"><img align="right" width="240" src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/42/William_Harvey_2.jpg" alt="William Harvey" title="Portrait of William Harvey"></a>

**1578 – 1657** · English  
Born: Folkestone, Kent · Died: Roehampton, Surrey  
Eras & fields: [Scientific Revolution](../movements/scientific-revolution.md)  

> Physician who demonstrated that the blood circulates continuously around the body, pumped by the heart. His work began modern physiology.

<sub>Portrait: Portrait of William Harvey · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:William_Harvey_2.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Harvey was the son of a prosperous yeoman in Kent. He studied at Cambridge and then at Padua, the leading medical school of the day, under the anatomist Fabricius, who had described the valves of the veins. Returning to London, he became physician at St Bartholomew's Hospital, a lecturer at the College of Physicians and physician to James I and Charles I, whom he accompanied during the Civil War.

Through dissection, vivisection of many animals and simple quantitative reasoning, Harvey showed that the heart pumps far more blood than the body could ever produce from food, and that the blood must therefore return to the heart in a circuit, through the arteries and back by the veins. His De motu cordis of 1628 met fierce opposition but was widely accepted within a generation. He also studied the development of embryos, arguing that all animals come from an egg.

## Key works & discoveries

### De motu cordis (1628)

- **Date:** 1628
- **Type:** Physiological treatise
- **Published / where:** Frankfurt: William Fitzer
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=De%20motu%20cordis%20William%20Harvey)

Exercitatio anatomica de motu cordis et sanguinis in animalibus is a short book of 72 pages printed in Frankfurt for the book fair. Harvey described his observations of the beating heart in many animals, including cold-blooded ones whose heartbeat is slow, and showed that the heart contracts actively to expel blood. He estimated how much blood the heart pumps with each beat and showed that in an hour it far exceeds the weight of the whole body, so blood cannot be continually made from food in the liver and consumed, as Galen taught, but must circulate. With simple experiments on ligatures on the arm, illustrated in the book, he showed that the valves in the veins allow blood to flow only towards the heart. Harvey could not see the connections between arteries and veins, which Marcello Malpighi observed with the microscope in 1661. Despite early ridicule, the circulation was widely accepted within a few decades and is the foundation of modern physiology.
