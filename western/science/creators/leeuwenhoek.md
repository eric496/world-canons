## Biography

Leeuwenhoek had no university education and knew no Latin. He ran a drapery business in Delft and held minor civic posts. Probably inspired by Hooke's Micrographia and by the use of lenses to check cloth, he began grinding or blowing tiny lenses and mounting them in small brass and silver holders, reaching magnifications of over two hundred times. From 1673 until his death he sent hundreds of letters describing his observations to the Royal Society in London, which elected him a fellow in 1680.

Leeuwenhoek saw and described single-celled organisms in pond water, bacteria from his own teeth, red blood cells, the flow of blood in capillaries and spermatozoa, and he argued against the spontaneous generation of small creatures. He kept his lens-making techniques secret, so few could match his results for more than a century. He is regarded as the founder of microbiology.

## Key works & discoveries

### Letter on the 'little animals' (1676)

- **Date:** 1676
- **Type:** Letter to the Royal Society
- **Published / where:** Delft; published in the Philosophical Transactions, London, 1677
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Letter%20on%20the%20%27little%20animals%27%20Antonie%20van%20Leeuwenhoek)

In a long letter dated 9 October 1676 to Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society, Leeuwenhoek described tiny living creatures that he had seen through his single-lens microscopes in rainwater, well water, sea water and water in which he had steeped pepper. He estimated their sizes, observed their movements and calculated that thousands would fit in a grain of sand. These 'animalcules' were protozoa and, in some cases, almost certainly bacteria. His claims were so astonishing that the Society asked Robert Hooke to confirm them, which he did in 1677 with witnesses' signatures. In later letters Leeuwenhoek described bacteria in the plaque of his own teeth, spermatozoa, red blood cells and capillaries. His instruments, of which about a dozen survive, were small brass plates holding a tiny lens, with which he achieved magnifications well above those of the compound microscopes of his day.
