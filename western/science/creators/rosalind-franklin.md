# Rosalind Franklin

**1920 – 1958** · English  
Born: Notting Hill, London · Died: Chelsea, London  
Eras & fields: [Genetics & molecular biology](../movements/molecular-biology.md)  

> Chemist and X-ray crystallographer whose diffraction images and measurements of DNA were essential to the discovery of the double helix. She also did pioneering work on coal and viruses.

## Biography

Franklin came from a prominent London Jewish family and studied chemistry at Newnham College, Cambridge. During the Second World War she studied the structure of coal for the British Coal Utilisation Research Association, and from 1947 to 1950 worked in Paris, where she mastered X-ray diffraction. In 1951 she joined King's College London to study DNA, in a difficult working relationship with Maurice Wilkins. In 1953 she moved to Birkbeck College, where she led important studies of the structure of viruses. She died of ovarian cancer at thirty-seven.

At King's, Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling distinguished two forms of DNA and produced sharp diffraction images, including Photo 51, together with precise measurements showing that the phosphate backbone lies on the outside. Without her knowledge, Wilkins showed Photo 51 to James Watson, and a report containing her data reached Francis Crick; these were crucial to their model. Her contribution was long underplayed, partly because she died before the 1962 Nobel Prize was awarded, and she has since become a symbol of overlooked women in science.

## Key works & discoveries

### Photo 51 (1952)

- **Date:** 1952
- **Type:** X-ray diffraction photograph
- **Published / where:** King's College London; published in Nature, 25 April 1953
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Photo%2051%20Rosalind%20Franklin)

At King's College London, Franklin and her doctoral student Raymond Gosling found that DNA fibres take two forms depending on humidity, a dry crystalline A form and a wet B form, and they made very fine X-ray diffraction photographs of each. Photograph number 51, of the B form, was taken in May 1952 after an exposure of many hours. Its striking X-shaped pattern of spots indicated a helical structure, and the spacing of the layers gave its dimensions. In January 1953, after Franklin had decided to leave King's, Gosling passed the photograph to Maurice Wilkins, who showed it to James Watson without Franklin's knowledge. Watson later wrote that his mouth fell open and his pulse began to race. With Franklin's measurements, which reached Crick through a Medical Research Council report, it helped Watson and Crick build their model. The photograph was published with an article by Franklin and Gosling in the same issue of Nature as the Watson and Crick paper, and has become a symbol both of the discovery and of the debate about credit for it.
