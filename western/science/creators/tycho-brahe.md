## Biography

Tycho was born into the high Danish nobility and studied at Copenhagen, Leipzig and other German universities, losing part of his nose in a duel and wearing a metal replacement for the rest of his life. His observation of a new star in 1572 made him famous, and King Frederick II gave him the island of Hven, where he built the observatory-palace Uraniborg and the underground observatory Stjerneborg. After quarrels with Frederick's successor he left Denmark in 1597 and became imperial mathematician to Rudolf II in Prague, where he died in 1601.

Tycho's large quadrants, sextants and armillary spheres, used systematically over twenty years with attention to error, measured positions to about a minute of arc. He showed that the new star of 1572 and the comet of 1577 lay beyond the Moon, undermining the idea of unchanging heavens. He proposed a compromise system in which the planets go around the Sun and the Sun around a stationary Earth. His greatest legacy was the observational record he left to his assistant Kepler.

### De nova stella (1573)

[![De nova stella](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8c/Tycho_Cas_SN1572.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tycho_Cas_SN1572.jpg)

<sub>Tycho's chart of Cassiopeia with the new star marked I, from De nova stella Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tycho_Cas_SN1572.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1573
- **Type:** Astronomical treatise
- **Published / where:** Copenhagen: Lorentz Benedicht
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=De%20nova%20stella%20Tycho%20Brahe)

On 11 November 1572 Tycho noticed a brilliant new star in Cassiopeia, as bright as Venus. Over the following months he measured its position with a large sextant and found that it showed no daily parallax, the shift that a nearby object would show as the Earth turned, and no motion against the stars. It must therefore lie beyond the Moon, among the fixed stars, contradicting the Aristotelian doctrine that the heavens are perfect and unchangeable. The star faded and disappeared by early 1574. Tycho's short book On the New Star, published in 1573, made his reputation and led to royal support for his observatory on Hven. The object is now known to have been a supernova, an exploding star, and is called Tycho's Supernova; its remnant has been studied with modern telescopes.

### Astronomiae instauratae mechanica (1598)

[![Astronomiae instauratae mechanica](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bc/Tycho-Brahe-Mural-Quadrant.jpg/1280px-Tycho-Brahe-Mural-Quadrant.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tycho-Brahe-Mural-Quadrant.jpg)

<sub>The mural quadrant at Uraniborg, from Astronomiae instauratae mechanica Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tycho-Brahe-Mural-Quadrant.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1598
- **Type:** Illustrated book of instruments
- **Published / where:** Wandsbek, near Hamburg (printed by Tycho's own press)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Astronomiae%20instauratae%20mechanica%20Tycho%20Brahe)

Instruments for the Restored Astronomy was printed in 1598 after Tycho had left Denmark, as a lavish presentation volume to win the patronage of Emperor Rudolf II. It describes and illustrates with handsome hand-coloured woodcuts and engravings the instruments he had developed at Uraniborg and Stjerneborg, including great quadrants, sextants, armillary spheres and a large brass celestial globe, together with views of the buildings, and his autobiography. The most famous plate shows the mural quadrant, a large arc fixed to a wall, with Tycho pointing at the sky and his assistants at work in the observatory behind. The book explains how he improved accuracy, for example with new sights and transversal scales, and how errors could be checked by multiple instruments. It is a key document of the rise of precision measurement.
