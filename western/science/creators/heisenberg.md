# Werner Heisenberg

**1901 – 1976** · German  
Born: Würzburg, Bavaria · Died: Munich, West Germany  
Eras & fields: [Relativity & quantum mechanics](../movements/relativity-quantum.md)  

> Physicist who created matrix mechanics, the first complete form of quantum mechanics, and formulated the uncertainty principle.

## Biography

Heisenberg was the son of a professor of Greek in Munich. He studied with Arnold Sommerfeld, took his doctorate at twenty-two, and worked with Max Born in Göttingen and with Bohr in Copenhagen. In 1927 he became professor at Leipzig, and he received the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1932. He stayed in Germany under the Nazis and led the German nuclear research programme during the Second World War; how far he worked for or against a bomb remains intensely debated. After the war he directed the Max Planck Institute for Physics.

On the island of Heligoland in 1925 Heisenberg found a way to describe atomic systems purely in terms of observable quantities, which Born and Pascual Jordan recognised as matrix algebra. In 1927 he showed that certain pairs of quantities, such as position and momentum, cannot both be known precisely at once, the uncertainty principle. These ideas, with Bohr's, formed the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. He later worked on nuclear physics and the theory of elementary particles.

## Key works & discoveries

### Matrix mechanics and the uncertainty principle (1925–1927)

- **Date:** 1925–1927
- **Type:** Scientific papers
- **Published / where:** Zeitschrift für Physik, Berlin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Matrix%20mechanics%20and%20the%20uncertainty%20principle%20Werner%20Heisenberg)

Suffering from hay fever, Heisenberg went to the treeless North Sea island of Heligoland in June 1925 and there worked out a new mechanics for atoms that used only observable quantities, such as the frequencies and intensities of spectral lines, rather than unobservable electron orbits. His paper, published in July 1925, contained a strange rule of multiplication in which the order of factors mattered; Max Born recognised it as matrix algebra, and Born, Heisenberg and Pascual Jordan developed matrix mechanics into a complete theory by the end of the year. In 1927, working in Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, Heisenberg showed that the theory implies that the more precisely the position of a particle is determined, the less precisely its momentum can be known, and vice versa, with the product of the uncertainties never smaller than a quantity set by Planck's constant. The uncertainty principle became a cornerstone of the Copenhagen interpretation, and Heisenberg received the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1932.
