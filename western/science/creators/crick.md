# Francis Crick

**1916 – 2004** · English  
Born: Weston Favell, Northamptonshire · Died: San Diego, California  
Eras & fields: [Genetics & molecular biology](../movements/molecular-biology.md)  

> Physicist turned biologist who co-discovered the structure of DNA with James Watson and did more than anyone to work out how the genetic code is read.

## Biography

Crick studied physics at University College London, and his doctoral research was interrupted by the Second World War, when he designed magnetic and acoustic mines for the Admiralty. Inspired partly by Schrödinger's What Is Life?, he moved into biology and in 1949 joined the Medical Research Council unit at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, where he met Watson in 1951. He spent most of his career in Cambridge and from 1976 at the Salk Institute in California, where he turned to the study of consciousness.

After the double helix of 1953, Crick set out the 'sequence hypothesis' and the 'central dogma' that information flows from nucleic acid to protein and not back, proposed the existence of adaptor molecules, later found to be transfer RNA, and with Sydney Brenner showed in 1961 that the genetic code is read in groups of three bases. He shared the 1962 Nobel Prize with Watson and Wilkins. He was the leading theorist of molecular biology in its classic period.

## Key works & discoveries

### On Protein Synthesis (the central dogma) (1958)

- **Date:** 1958
- **Type:** Scientific lecture and paper
- **Published / where:** Symposia of the Society for Experimental Biology, Cambridge
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Protein%20Synthesis%20%28the%20central%20dogma%29%20Francis%20Crick)

In a lecture given at University College London in September 1957 and published in 1958, Crick set out a framework for how the information stored in DNA is used to make proteins, at a time when the details were almost entirely unknown. The sequence hypothesis proposed that the order of bases in a stretch of nucleic acid specifies the order of amino acids in a protein, and that this sequence alone determines how the protein folds. The central dogma, as he called it, stated that once information has passed into protein it cannot get out again: information may flow from nucleic acid to nucleic acid or from nucleic acid to protein, but not from protein to protein or from protein back to nucleic acid. He also proposed that small 'adaptor' molecules carry amino acids to the template, a prediction soon fulfilled by the discovery of transfer RNA. The framework guided the research of the following decade, in which the genetic code was deciphered. The discovery of reverse transcription in 1970 added a flow from RNA to DNA that Crick had allowed for.
