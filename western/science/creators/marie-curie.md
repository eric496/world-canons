# Marie Curie

**1867 – 1934** · Polish-French  
Born: Warsaw, Congress Poland, Russian Empire · Died: Passy, Haute-Savoie, France  
Eras & fields: [X-rays, radioactivity & the atom](../movements/radioactivity-atom.md)  

> Physicist and chemist who pioneered the study of radioactivity, discovered polonium and radium, and was the first person to win two Nobel Prizes.

## Biography

Maria Skłodowska was the daughter of teachers in Russian-ruled Warsaw, where women could not attend university. She worked as a governess to pay for her sister's medical studies, then in 1891 went to Paris to study physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne. In 1895 she married the physicist Pierre Curie. After Pierre's death in a street accident in 1906 she took over his chair, becoming the first woman professor at the Sorbonne. During the First World War she organised mobile X-ray units for the front. She died of aplastic anaemia, almost certainly caused by her long exposure to radiation.

Following Henri Becquerel's discovery of uranium rays, Curie found that the intensity of the radiation depended only on the amount of uranium, suggesting it came from the atom itself, and she coined the word radioactivity. With Pierre she discovered polonium and radium in 1898, and after years of processing tonnes of pitchblende she isolated radium salts. She shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre and Becquerel, and won the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone. Her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie also won a Nobel Prize.

## Key works & discoveries

### Discovery of polonium and radium (1898)

- **Date:** 1898
- **Type:** Discovery of chemical elements
- **Published / where:** École municipale de physique et chimie industrielles, Paris; announced in the Comptes rendus de l'Académie des sciences
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discovery%20of%20polonium%20and%20radium%20Marie%20Curie)

For her doctoral research Marie Curie chose to study the rays from uranium discovered by Henri Becquerel in 1896. Using an electrometer designed by Pierre Curie and his brother Jacques, she measured the faint electric currents the rays produced in air, and found that thorium also emits them and that the intensity depends only on the amount of the element, suggesting that it is a property of the atom; she called it radioactivity. Pitchblende, a uranium ore, was more active than its uranium content could explain, so she and Pierre, who abandoned his own research to join her, chemically separated it. In July 1898 they announced polonium, named after her occupied homeland, and in December, with Gustave Bémont, radium. Working in a leaky shed, Marie processed several tonnes of pitchblende residues to obtain a tenth of a gram of radium chloride in 1902 and determine its atomic weight. Radium became a sensation and was used in cancer treatment. Her notebooks are still so radioactive that they are kept in lead-lined boxes at the Bibliothèque nationale de France.
