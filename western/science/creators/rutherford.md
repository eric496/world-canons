# Ernest Rutherford

**1871 – 1937** · New Zealander  
Born: Brightwater, New Zealand · Died: Cambridge, England  
Eras & fields: [X-rays, radioactivity & the atom](../movements/radioactivity-atom.md)  

> New Zealand-born physicist who explained radioactivity as the transformation of elements, discovered the atomic nucleus and first split an atom by artificial means.

## Biography

Rutherford was the son of a flax-miller and farmer in New Zealand. A scholarship took him from Canterbury College to the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge in 1895, where he worked under J. J. Thomson. He was professor at McGill University in Montreal, then at Manchester, and from 1919 director of the Cavendish Laboratory, where many future Nobel laureates trained under him. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1908 and was made Baron Rutherford of Nelson.

At McGill, with Frederick Soddy, Rutherford showed that radioactive atoms spontaneously turn into other elements, and he named alpha and beta radiation. At Manchester, experiments by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden firing alpha particles at gold foil led him in 1911 to propose that the atom's mass is concentrated in a tiny, positive nucleus. In 1917–19 he transformed nitrogen into oxygen by alpha bombardment, the first artificial nuclear reaction, and he identified the proton.

## Key works & discoveries

### Rutherford model of the atom (1911)

- **Date:** 1911
- **Type:** Theory and experiment
- **Published / where:** University of Manchester; published in the Philosophical Magazine
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rutherford%20model%20of%20the%20atom%20Ernest%20Rutherford)

In 1909 at Manchester, Hans Geiger and the student Ernest Marsden, working under Rutherford, fired alpha particles from a radioactive source at a very thin gold foil and counted the flashes they made on a fluorescent screen. Most passed straight through, but about one in eight thousand bounced back at large angles. The prevailing 'plum pudding' model of J. J. Thomson, with electrons scattered through a diffuse sphere of positive charge, could not explain this; Rutherford later said it was as if a fifteen-inch shell had bounced back from tissue paper. In a paper of May 1911 he showed mathematically that the results fitted an atom with almost all its mass and positive charge concentrated in a minute central nucleus, with electrons at a great distance, so that the atom is mostly empty space. Geiger and Marsden confirmed his scattering formula in detail in 1913. The model was unstable according to classical physics, a problem that Bohr resolved with quantum theory, and it opened the way to nuclear physics.
