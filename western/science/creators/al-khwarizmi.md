# Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi

**c. 780 – c. 850** · Persian  
Born: Khwarazm, Central Asia (probably) · Died: Baghdad (probably)  
Eras & fields: [Islamic science & the translation movement](../movements/islamic-science.md)  

> Scholar of the House of Wisdom in Baghdad whose books gave the world algebra and spread Indian decimal numerals. The words algebra and algorithm come from his book title and his name.

## Biography

Al-Khwarizmi's name suggests that his family came from Khwarazm, south of the Aral Sea. He worked in Baghdad under the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun, as a scholar associated with the House of Wisdom, a centre where Greek, Persian and Indian works were translated and studied. Besides mathematics he wrote on astronomy, producing astronomical tables, and on geography, revising Ptolemy's coordinates.

His treatise on calculation by completion and balancing taught the systematic solution of linear and quadratic equations, and its key term al-jabr became 'algebra'. A second work, on calculating with Indian numerals, survives only in a Latin translation whose opening words 'Dixit Algorizmi' gave us 'algorithm'. Through twelfth-century translations in Spain, his books taught Europe the place-value numerals with zero and the beginnings of algebra. Islamic science will be covered in its own section.

### The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing (Al-jabr) (c. 820)

- **Date:** c. 820
- **Type:** Mathematical treatise
- **Published / where:** Baghdad; Latin translation by Robert of Chester, Segovia, 1145
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Compendious%20Book%20on%20Calculation%20by%20Completion%20and%20Balancing%20%28Al-jabr%29%20Muhammad%20ibn%20Musa%20al-Khwarizmi)

Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala was written in Baghdad for the caliph al-Ma'mun as a practical handbook. Al-Khwarizmi reduced all linear and quadratic equations to six standard types and gave rules for solving each, justified by geometrical diagrams of completing the square. Al-jabr, restoring, refers to moving subtracted terms to the other side of an equation, and al-muqabala, balancing, to cancelling like terms. Everything is written in words, without symbols, and the book continues with applications to measurement, trade and the Islamic law of inheritance. It drew on Babylonian, Greek and Indian methods but organised them as an independent discipline. Latin translations by Robert of Chester in 1145 and Gerard of Cremona introduced the subject to Europe, where 'algebra' entered the vocabulary of mathematics.

### On Calculation with Hindu Numerals (Algoritmi de numero Indorum) (c. 825)

- **Date:** c. 825
- **Type:** Arithmetic treatise
- **Published / where:** Baghdad; survives in a 12th-century Latin translation
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Calculation%20with%20Hindu%20Numerals%20%28Algoritmi%20de%20numero%20Indorum%29%20Muhammad%20ibn%20Musa%20al-Khwarizmi)

Al-Khwarizmi's book on arithmetic with Indian numerals is lost in Arabic and survives only in a twelfth-century Latin version, probably made in Spain, which begins 'Dixit Algorizmi', thus spoke al-Khwarizmi. It explains the nine Indian digits and the small circle for zero, how place value works, and methods for addition, subtraction, halving, doubling, multiplication, division and square roots, as well as calculation with fractions. Indian mathematicians had developed decimal place value with zero by around the sixth century, and the system reached Baghdad by the eighth. Through this and related Latin treatises, the Hindu-Arabic numerals spread among European scholars, and the methods of calculating with them became known as algorism, the origin of the modern word algorithm. They took centuries to displace Roman numerals in everyday use.
