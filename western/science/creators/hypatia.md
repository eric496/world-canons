## Biography

Hypatia was the daughter of the mathematician Theon of Alexandria, who prepared editions of Euclid and Ptolemy. She became a celebrated teacher of philosophy and mathematics in her own right, with pupils from across the eastern Mediterranean, and was an influential figure in the city's public life. In 415, amid a bitter political conflict between the city's prefect and its bishop Cyril, she was attacked and killed by a Christian mob.

None of her writings survives under her name. Ancient sources credit her with commentaries on the Arithmetica of Diophantus and the Conics of Apollonius and with an astronomical table, and scholars think she worked with her father on his edition of Ptolemy. Her life and violent death were later turned into a symbol of the end of classical learning, a picture that historians now regard as simplified.

## Key works & discoveries

### Edition of Ptolemy's Almagest (with Theon) (c. 380–400)

- **Date:** c. 380–400
- **Type:** Mathematical commentary and edition
- **Published / where:** Alexandria
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Edition%20of%20Ptolemy%27s%20Almagest%20%28with%20Theon%29%20Hypatia%20of%20Alexandria)

None of Hypatia's works survives under her own name, but the heading of the third book of Theon of Alexandria's commentary on Ptolemy's Almagest describes it as an edition revised by his daughter, the philosopher Hypatia. Scholars debate whether this means she edited the commentary or prepared the text of the Almagest itself, and some think the surviving text of Ptolemy may descend from her version. Ancient sources, especially the Byzantine Suda, also credit her with commentaries on the Arithmetica of Diophantus, a pioneering work on number problems, and on the Conics of Apollonius, and with an astronomical canon. Her pupil Synesius of Cyrene wrote to her about building an astrolabe and a hydrometer. Through such teaching and editing, the work of Greek mathematicians was preserved for Byzantine and later Arabic scholars.
