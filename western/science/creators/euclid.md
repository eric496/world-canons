# Euclid of Alexandria

**fl. c. 300 BC** · Greek  
Born:  · Died: Alexandria (presumed)  
Eras & fields: [Hellenistic science & mathematics](../movements/hellenistic-science.md)  

> Mathematician of Alexandria whose Elements set out geometry and number theory as a chain of proofs from a few definitions and axioms. It became the most influential mathematics textbook ever written.

## Biography

Euclid's life is almost a blank. Later writers say that he taught in Alexandria in the time of the first Ptolemy, around 300 BC, and he probably learned his mathematics from students of Plato's Academy. Stories about him, such as his reply that there is no royal road to geometry, come from much later and may not be true.

Besides the Elements, works on optics, astronomy, music and conic sections are attributed to him, some surviving and some lost. His achievement was less to discover new results than to organise the mathematics of his predecessors into a single deductive system, starting from definitions, postulates and common notions. That model of rigorous proof shaped mathematics, philosophy and even political writing, from Islamic scholars to Newton, Spinoza and the authors of the American Declaration of Independence.

## Key works & discoveries

### Elements (c. 300 BC)

- **Date:** c. 300 BC
- **Type:** Mathematical treatise
- **Published / where:** Alexandria; first printed edition Venice, 1482
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Elements%20Euclid%20of%20Alexandria)

Euclid's Elements, in Greek Stoicheia, gathers the mathematics of the Greek world into thirteen books. It starts from twenty-three definitions, five postulates and five common notions and proves 465 propositions from them in strict logical order: plane geometry, including Pythagoras' theorem, in the first books; proportion, probably following Eudoxus; number theory, with the proof that there are infinitely many primes and the Euclidean algorithm; incommensurable magnitudes; and solid geometry, ending with the five regular solids. The fifth or parallel postulate, which seemed less evident than the others, puzzled mathematicians for two thousand years until non-Euclidean geometries were found in the nineteenth century. Translated into Arabic in Baghdad and from Arabic into Latin by Adelard of Bath in the twelfth century, it was printed in Venice in 1482 and has appeared in more editions than any book except the Bible. It was the standard geometry textbook into the twentieth century.
