# Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

**1646 – 1716** · German  
Born: Leipzig, Saxony · Died: Hanover  
Eras & fields: [The Newtonian age](../movements/newtonian-age.md)  
Also in: [Philosophy](../../philosophy/eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md)  

> Philosopher and mathematician who invented the calculus independently of Newton, with the notation still used today, and worked out binary arithmetic.

## Biography

Leibniz was the son of a professor of moral philosophy at Leipzig and studied law. He entered the service of the Elector of Mainz and in 1672–76 was in Paris on a diplomatic mission, where Huygens taught him modern mathematics and where he developed the calculus. From 1676 he was librarian, historian and adviser to the dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg in Hanover. He founded the Berlin Academy of Sciences, corresponded with hundreds of scholars and died somewhat neglected in Hanover.

Leibniz published his differential calculus in 1684 and his integral calculus in 1686; his symbols dx and the integral sign proved so effective that they became standard. The bitter priority dispute with Newton's followers divided British and Continental mathematics for a century. He also built a calculating machine that could multiply, explored binary numbers, and dreamed of a universal logical language in which reasoning could be done by calculation, an idea later realised in symbolic logic and computing.

### Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis (1684)

- **Date:** 1684
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Acta Eruditorum, Leipzig
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nova%20methodus%20pro%20maximis%20et%20minimis%20Gottfried%20Wilhelm%20Leibniz)

Leibniz's six-page paper, A New Method for Maxima and Minima, and for Tangents, which is not hindered by fractional or irrational quantities, appeared in the Leipzig journal Acta Eruditorum in October 1684. In compressed and sometimes obscure form, it gave rules for finding the differentials of sums, products, quotients, powers and roots, and showed how to use them to find tangents, maxima, minima and points of inflection, with applications to optics and geometry. Two years later he published the integral calculus, introducing the elongated S sign for summation. Leibniz had developed these methods in Paris in 1675–76, about a decade after Newton's unpublished work on fluxions. The bitter priority dispute that erupted around 1700, in which the Royal Society under Newton found Leibniz guilty of plagiarism, is now regarded as a case of independent discovery. Leibniz's notation, spread by the Bernoulli brothers and their pupils, became universal.

### Explication de l'arithmétique binaire (1703)

- **Date:** 1703
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Mémoires de l'Académie Royale des Sciences, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Explication%20de%20l%27arithm%C3%A9tique%20binaire%20Gottfried%20Wilhelm%20Leibniz)

Leibniz had worked on counting with only two digits since the 1670s, and in this paper for the Paris Academy he explained how all numbers can be written using only 0 and 1, and how addition, subtraction, multiplication and division can be done in this system, with remarkable regularities in the patterns. He had recently learned from the Jesuit missionary Joachim Bouvet in China about the 64 hexagrams of the Yijing, the Book of Changes, built from broken and unbroken lines, and argued that they matched the binary numbers from 0 to 63, which he took as evidence of ancient Chinese wisdom. Leibniz also saw religious meaning in binary, with all things created from one and nothing. The binary system had little practical use until the twentieth century, when Boolean logic and electronic switching made it the basis of all digital computers.
