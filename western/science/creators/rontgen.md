# Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen

**1845 – 1923** · German  
Born: Lennep, Prussia · Died: Munich, Germany  
Eras & fields: [X-rays, radioactivity & the atom](../movements/radioactivity-atom.md)  

> Physicist who discovered X-rays in 1895 and was awarded the first Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Biography

Röntgen was the only child of a cloth merchant and grew up in the Netherlands. Expelled from school for a prank he did not commit, he studied mechanical engineering at the Zurich Polytechnic and then physics. He held posts at Strasbourg, Hohenheim and Giessen, and in 1888 became professor of physics at Würzburg, later moving to Munich. He received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901 and gave the prize money to his university.

In November 1895, experimenting with cathode-ray tubes, Röntgen noticed that a fluorescent screen across the room glowed even when the tube was covered. He spent weeks investigating the invisible, penetrating radiation, which he called X-rays, and made an image of the bones in his wife's hand. Within months X-rays were being used by doctors around the world. Röntgen refused to patent the discovery, believing it belonged to humanity. The discovery also launched the research that led to radioactivity and atomic physics.

## Key works & discoveries

### Discovery of X-rays (Hand mit Ringen) (1895)

- **Date:** 1895
- **Type:** Discovery and first radiograph
- **Published / where:** University of Würzburg; published in the Sitzungsberichte der Physikalisch-Medizinischen Gesellschaft zu Würzburg
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discovery%20of%20X-rays%20%28Hand%20mit%20Ringen%29%20Wilhelm%20Conrad%20R%C3%B6ntgen)

On 8 November 1895, working alone in his laboratory at Würzburg with a cathode-ray tube covered in black cardboard, Röntgen noticed a faint glow on a screen coated with barium platinocyanide lying nearby. He spent seven weeks investigating the unknown rays, which he called X-rays, showing that they travel in straight lines, are not deflected by magnets, pass through paper, wood and flesh but are stopped by metals and bone, and darken photographic plates. On 22 December he made an image of the hand of his wife, Anna Bertha, showing her bones and wedding ring; she is said to have exclaimed that she had seen her death. His paper On a New Kind of Rays, submitted on 28 December, and copies of the image sent to colleagues caused an international sensation. Within weeks X-rays were being used to locate bullets and set fractures. Their nature as short-wavelength electromagnetic radiation was shown by Max von Laue's diffraction experiments in 1912, which also founded X-ray crystallography. The dangers of overexposure were learned only slowly.
