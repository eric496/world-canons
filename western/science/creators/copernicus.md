# Nicolaus Copernicus

**1473 – 1543** · Polish  
Born: Toruń, Royal Prussia, Kingdom of Poland · Died: Frombork, Royal Prussia  
Eras & fields: [Renaissance anatomy & astronomy](../movements/renaissance-science.md)  

> Astronomer and church administrator who proposed that the Earth and planets move around the Sun, the starting point of modern astronomy.

## Biography

Copernicus was born into a merchant family in Toruń and raised by his uncle, later Prince-Bishop of Warmia. He studied at Kraków and then for years in Italy, at Bologna, Padua and Ferrara, taking a doctorate in canon law and studying medicine. Back in Warmia he served as a canon of Frombork cathedral, administering estates, practising medicine and writing on currency reform, while pursuing astronomy in his spare time.

Around 1510 he circulated a short sketch of a Sun-centred system, but held back his full treatise for decades. Encouraged by the young Lutheran mathematician Rheticus, he finally allowed De revolutionibus to be printed in 1543, and is said to have received a copy on his deathbed. His model still used circular orbits and was no more accurate than Ptolemy's, but it set the stage for Kepler, Galileo and Newton, and 'Copernican revolution' became a phrase for any fundamental change of view.

## Key works & discoveries

### Commentariolus (c. 1510–1514)

- **Date:** c. 1510–1514
- **Type:** Manuscript treatise
- **Published / where:** Circulated in manuscript; first printed Vienna, 1878
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentariolus%20Nicolaus%20Copernicus)

The Commentariolus, Little Commentary, was a short text that Copernicus circulated among friends without a title or his name some time before 1514, when a Kraków professor listed it in his library. It sets out seven assumptions: that there is no single centre for all the celestial spheres, that the centre of the Earth is not the centre of the universe, that the Sun is near the centre of the planetary spheres, that the distance to the stars is immeasurably greater than that to the Sun, that the daily motion of the heavens is due to the Earth's rotation, that the Sun's apparent annual motion results from the Earth's orbit, and that the apparent backward loops of the planets are caused by the Earth's own motion. It then describes the models with small epicycles that would replace Ptolemy's equant. Only three manuscript copies are known, rediscovered in the nineteenth century.

### De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543)

- **Date:** 1543
- **Type:** Astronomical treatise
- **Published / where:** Nuremberg: Johannes Petreius
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=De%20revolutionibus%20orbium%20coelestium%20Nicolaus%20Copernicus)

De revolutionibus was printed in Nuremberg in 1543, after Copernicus' disciple Rheticus had persuaded him to publish and taken the manuscript to the printer. In six books, modelled on Ptolemy's Almagest, it argues that the Earth spins daily on its axis and orbits the Sun yearly with the other planets, then provides the mathematics and tables to compute their positions. The system explained naturally why Mercury and Venus are always near the Sun, why the planets seem to loop backwards, and gave their order and relative distances, but still used circles and epicycles and was no more accurate than Ptolemy's. A preface added without permission by the Lutheran theologian Andreas Osiander presented it as a calculating hypothesis rather than physical truth. The Catholic Church placed it on the Index pending correction in 1616. The historian Owen Gingerich's census of surviving copies showed that it was widely read and annotated by astronomers.
