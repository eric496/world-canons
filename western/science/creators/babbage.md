# Charles Babbage

**1791 – 1871** · English  
Born: London · Died: London  
Eras & fields: [Computing & information](../movements/computing.md)  

> Mathematician and inventor who designed the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine, a mechanical general-purpose computer conceived a century before electronic ones.

## Biography

Babbage was the son of a wealthy London banker and studied at Cambridge, where he helped introduce Continental calculus notation. He was Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge from 1828 to 1839, though he rarely lectured, and was a founder of the Astronomical and Statistical Societies. He wrote on manufacturing, economics and the decline of British science, campaigned against street musicians and held famous Saturday evening parties in London.

Frustrated by errors in printed mathematical tables, Babbage designed in 1822 a Difference Engine to calculate and print them mechanically, and obtained government funds to build it; it was never completed. He then conceived the Analytical Engine, with a separate store and mill, the equivalent of memory and processor, programmed by punched cards like a Jacquard loom. Neither machine was built in his lifetime, but in 1991 the Science Museum in London completed a working Difference Engine No. 2 from his drawings.

### Difference Engine (1822–1849; built 1991)

- **Date:** 1822–1849; built 1991
- **Type:** Mechanical calculator
- **Published / where:** Science Museum, London; Computer History Museum, Mountain View (Difference Engine No. 2)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Difference%20Engine%20Charles%20Babbage)

Mathematical tables of logarithms, astronomical positions and navigation were calculated by hand and full of errors. In 1822 Babbage proposed a machine that would compute tables by the method of finite differences, which needs only repeated addition, and print the results to avoid copying mistakes. With government money he employed the engineer Joseph Clement to build Difference Engine No. 1, but after disputes and costs of over £17,000 work stopped in 1833, leaving only a demonstration section of about 2,000 parts, which Babbage showed at his parties. Between 1847 and 1849 he designed a simpler and more elegant Difference Engine No. 2. Its plans lay unused until the Science Museum in London built it, completing the calculating section in 1991 for the bicentenary of Babbage's birth and the printer in 2002. It weighs about five tonnes, has some 8,000 parts and works exactly as he intended, proving that the design was sound. The Swedish engineers Georg and Edvard Scheutz built a working difference engine of their own in the 1850s.

### Analytical Engine (1834–1871)

- **Date:** 1834–1871
- **Type:** Design for a mechanical general-purpose computer
- **Published / where:** Science Museum, London (trial model of the mill and printing mechanism)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Analytical%20Engine%20Charles%20Babbage)

From 1834 Babbage worked on a far more ambitious machine than the Difference Engine. The Analytical Engine was to have a 'store' holding a thousand numbers of fifty digits each, and a 'mill' that would perform arithmetic on numbers brought from the store, the equivalent of a modern computer's memory and central processor. It would be programmed by punched cards, borrowed from the Jacquard loom that wove patterns in silk, with separate cards for operations and for the addresses of variables, and it could make decisions by branching according to the results of calculations, and repeat sequences in loops. Output would be printed or punched. The engine was never built, partly for lack of funding; Babbage made thousands of pages of drawings and notes and built only small parts, and his son Henry assembled a piece of the mill after his death. It was a general-purpose computer in principle, a century before electronic computers, though there is little evidence that its designers in the 1940s were influenced by it.
