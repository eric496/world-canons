# George Boole

**1815 – 1864** · English  
Born: Lincoln, England · Died: Ballintemple, Cork, Ireland  
Eras & fields: [Logic & the foundations of mathematics](../movements/logic-foundations.md)  

> Self-taught mathematician who turned logic into algebra. Boolean algebra later became the mathematical basis of digital circuits and computing.

## Biography

Boole was the son of a Lincoln shoemaker with a passion for science and instruments. Unable to afford university, he taught himself mathematics and languages and opened his own school at nineteen. His papers on differential equations and invariants won him a gold medal of the Royal Society, and in 1849, without a degree, he became the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork. He died at forty-nine after catching a chill walking to a lecture in the rain.

In The Mathematical Analysis of Logic of 1847 and The Laws of Thought of 1854, Boole showed that logical reasoning could be expressed as algebraic equations in which variables stand for classes and take only the values 0 and 1. In 1937 Claude Shannon showed that this algebra could describe switching circuits, and Boolean logic became the foundation of digital electronics and computer programming.

## Key works & discoveries

### The Laws of Thought (1854)

- **Date:** 1854
- **Type:** Treatise on logic and probability
- **Published / where:** London: Walton and Maberly; Cambridge: Macmillan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Laws%20of%20Thought%20George%20Boole)

An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are Founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities developed ideas Boole had first published in 1847. He represented classes of things, or propositions, by letters such as x and y, the combination 'x and y' by multiplication and 'either x or y' by addition, with 1 standing for the universe of discourse and 0 for nothing. Because a class combined with itself is unchanged, x times x equals x, which holds only for the numbers 0 and 1, so logic becomes an algebra of those two values. With this system the syllogisms of traditional logic, and far more complex arguments, could be worked out by calculation. Boole also applied it to probability. His algebra was simplified by later logicians such as William Stanley Jevons and Charles Sanders Peirce, and in 1937 Claude Shannon showed that it describes electrical switching circuits, making it the foundation of digital computer design.
