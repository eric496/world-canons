## Biography

Euler was the son of a pastor near Basel and studied under Johann Bernoulli. In 1727 he joined the new Academy of Sciences in Saint Petersburg, moved in 1741 to Frederick the Great's academy in Berlin, and returned to Saint Petersburg in 1766, where he spent the rest of his life. He lost the sight of one eye in the 1730s and became almost totally blind around 1771, yet his output increased, as he dictated to assistants and his sons.

Euler wrote some 850 papers and books on almost every branch of pure and applied mathematics, from infinite series and differential equations to the mechanics of fluids and rigid bodies, the motion of the Moon, optics and ship design. He solved the Königsberg bridge problem, founding graph theory, and introduced or popularised the notations f(x), e, i and the summation sign. His textbooks taught the calculus to Europe and the remark attributed to Laplace, that Euler was the master of us all, sums up his influence.

### Solution of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg (1736)

[![Solution of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e5/Bridges_of_Konigsberg.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bridges_of_Konigsberg.png)

<sub>The seven bridges of Königsberg Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bridges_of_Konigsberg.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1736
- **Type:** Mathematical paper
- **Published / where:** Commentarii Academiae Scientiarum Petropolitanae, Saint Petersburg (published 1741)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Solution%20of%20the%20Seven%20Bridges%20of%20K%C3%B6nigsberg%20Leonhard%20Euler)

The city of Königsberg in Prussia, now Kaliningrad, was built on both banks of the river Pregel and on two islands, joined by seven bridges, and its citizens wondered whether it was possible to walk through the city crossing each bridge exactly once. In a paper presented to the Saint Petersburg Academy in 1736, Euler showed that the answer depends only on how the land areas are connected, not on distances or shapes. Representing each area and its bridges, he showed that such a walk is possible only if no more than two areas have an odd number of bridges, and in Königsberg all four did. The reasoning, though Euler did not draw the diagram of points and lines now used, is regarded as the first theorem of graph theory and an early step towards topology, the study of properties unchanged by stretching. Two of the bridges were destroyed in the Second World War.

### Introductio in analysin infinitorum (1748)

[![Introductio in analysin infinitorum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum._T._1_-_Leonhard_Euler%2C_1748_%28title_page%29.jpg/1280px-Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum._T._1_-_Leonhard_Euler%2C_1748_%28title_page%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum._T._1_-_Leonhard_Euler%2C_1748_%28title_page%29.jpg)

<sub>Title page of volume 1, 1748 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum._T._1_-_Leonhard_Euler%2C_1748_%28title_page%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1748
- **Type:** Mathematical textbook
- **Published / where:** Lausanne: Marc-Michel Bousquet
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Introductio%20in%20analysin%20infinitorum%20Leonhard%20Euler)

In the two volumes of the Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite, Euler reorganised mathematics around the concept of the function, which he defined as an analytic expression of a variable quantity. The first volume treats exponential and logarithmic functions, defining them through infinite series and products, and establishes the number e, using that letter, as the base of natural logarithms. It presents Euler's formula connecting the exponential function with sine and cosine for imaginary numbers, from which follows the identity relating e, i, pi, 1 and 0. It also contains his solution of the Basel problem, the sum of the reciprocals of the squares, and the product formula linking the zeta function to prime numbers. The second volume covers analytic geometry of curves and surfaces. Written with great clarity, it was the model for mathematical textbooks for a century, and has been compared in importance to Euclid's Elements.
