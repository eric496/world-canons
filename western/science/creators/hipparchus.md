# Hipparchus of Nicaea

**c. 190 BC – c. 120 BC** · Greek  
Born: Nicaea, Bithynia · Died: Rhodes (probably)  
Eras & fields: [Hellenistic science & mathematics](../movements/hellenistic-science.md)  

> Astronomer who turned Greek astronomy into a precise, predictive science, discovered the precession of the equinoxes and compiled the first known star catalogue.

## Biography

Hipparchus was born in Nicaea in Asia Minor and made most of his observations on the island of Rhodes between about 147 and 127 BC. Only one of his works survives, a critical commentary on an astronomical poem, so most of what is known about him comes from Ptolemy's Almagest, which drew on him heavily.

He used Babylonian eclipse records alongside Greek observations, built geometrical models of the motions of the Sun and Moon that could predict eclipses, and developed an early form of trigonometry, with a table of chords, to do the calculations. Comparing his own star positions with older ones, he detected the slow shift of the equinoxes now called precession. His star catalogue, which ranked stars by brightness in a forerunner of the magnitude system, became the basis of Ptolemy's.

## Key works & discoveries

### Discovery of the precession of the equinoxes (c. 130 BC)

- **Date:** c. 130 BC
- **Type:** Astronomical discovery and star catalogue
- **Published / where:** Rhodes; reported in Ptolemy's Almagest
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discovery%20of%20the%20precession%20of%20the%20equinoxes%20Hipparchus%20of%20Nicaea)

Hipparchus measured the positions of stars relative to the equinoxes, the points where the Sun's path crosses the celestial equator, and compared them with observations made by Timocharis and Aristyllus in Alexandria about 150 years earlier. He found that the longitude of the star Spica had increased by about two degrees, and concluded that the equinoxes move slowly westward against the stars, by at least one degree a century; the true value is about one degree in 72 years. This also showed that the solar year measured by the seasons is slightly shorter than the year measured by the stars. His works On the Displacement of the Solstitial and Equinoctial Points and his star catalogue are lost, and we know them through Ptolemy. Precession is now explained, following Newton, as a slow wobble of the Earth's axis caused by the pull of the Sun and Moon on its equatorial bulge, with a period of about 26,000 years.
