## Biography

Descartes was educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche and took a law degree at Poitiers. As a young man he served as a gentleman soldier in the Dutch and Bavarian armies, and in 1619 had the dreams that, he said, revealed his mission to found a unified science. He spent most of his working life in the Dutch Republic, and died in Stockholm in 1650, a few months after going to teach philosophy to Queen Christina.

His Discourse on Method of 1637 was published with three scientific essays, on optics, meteorology and geometry. La Géométrie showed how to describe curves by equations and solve geometrical problems by algebra, introducing the notation still in use. In physics he described the universe as matter in motion filling space, with planets carried by vortices, and he gave an early statement of the law of inertia and of the law of refraction. His mechanical philosophy was later displaced by Newton's, but it set the agenda for seventeenth-century science.

## Key works & discoveries

### La Géométrie (1637)

[![La Géométrie](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/35/GeometryDescartes.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:GeometryDescartes.JPG)

<sub>Opening page of La Géométrie, 1637 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:GeometryDescartes.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1637
- **Type:** Mathematical treatise
- **Published / where:** Leiden: Jan Maire (appendix to the Discourse on Method)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=La%20G%C3%A9om%C3%A9trie%20Ren%C3%A9%20Descartes)

La Géométrie was the third of three essays published in French with the Discourse on Method in 1637, to show the power of Descartes's method. In three books he showed how geometrical problems can be translated into algebraic equations by assigning letters to lengths, and how equations can in turn be represented by curves, the basis of analytic or coordinate geometry; the familiar x and y axes were developed later by his followers. He solved the problem of Pappus, an ancient challenge, classified curves by the degree of their equations, and introduced the convention of using letters from the end of the alphabet for unknowns and from the beginning for known quantities, and superscripts for powers. Fermat developed similar ideas at the same time. Frans van Schooten's Latin edition with commentaries made it widely accessible, and it was the foundation from which Newton and Leibniz developed the calculus.
