# Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen)

**c. 965 – c. 1040** · Arab  
Born: Basra, Iraq · Died: Cairo  
Eras & fields: [Islamic science & the translation movement](../movements/islamic-science.md)  

> Mathematician and physicist of Basra and Cairo whose Book of Optics showed that vision occurs when light enters the eye, and set out a programme of controlled experiment.

## Biography

Born in Basra, Ibn al-Haytham moved to Cairo under the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim. Later biographers tell that he offered to regulate the Nile floods, realised the scheme was impossible and feigned madness to escape the caliph's anger; whether true or not, he spent much of his life in Cairo writing on mathematics, astronomy and optics. Close to a hundred works are attributed to him.

His Book of Optics rejected the ancient theory that the eye sends out rays, combined geometry with the anatomy of the eye, and tested claims about reflection, refraction and the camera obscura by experiment. Translated into Latin around 1200 as De aspectibus under the name Alhazen, it shaped the optics of Roger Bacon, Witelo and Kepler. He also wrote a critique of Ptolemy's astronomy. Islamic science will be covered in its own section.

## Key works & discoveries

### Book of Optics (Kitab al-Manazir) (1011–1021)

[![Book of Optics (Kitab al-Manazir)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/45/Book_of_Optics_Cover_Page.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Book_of_Optics_Cover_Page.jpg)

<sub>Frontispiece of Risner's Latin edition, Opticae thesaurus, 1572 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Book_of_Optics_Cover_Page.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1011–1021
- **Type:** Treatise on optics
- **Published / where:** Cairo; Latin translation De aspectibus c. 1200; printed Basel, 1572
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Optics%20%28Kitab%20al-Manazir%29%20Ibn%20al-Haytham%20%28Alhazen%29)

In the Kitab al-Manazir Ibn al-Haytham combined the mathematical optics of Euclid and Ptolemy, the medical anatomy of the eye from Galen and the physical theories of the philosophers into a single new theory. He argued that every point of a visible object sends rays of light in all directions, and that vision occurs when the rays falling perpendicularly on the eye form an image of the object, rejecting the ancient idea that the eye emits rays. He investigated reflection and refraction in plane and curved mirrors and lenses, explained the camera obscura and visual illusions, and insisted that claims be tested by controlled experiments with tubes, screens and instruments. Translated into Latin around 1200 as De aspectibus or Perspectiva, under the name Alhazen, it was the foundation of medieval European optics in Roger Bacon, Witelo and John Pecham, and was printed by Friedrich Risner in 1572, when Kepler read it.
