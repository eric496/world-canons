## Biography

Marcel Proust was born in 1871 in Auteuil, then on the edge of Paris, the son of an eminent doctor and a cultivated mother from a wealthy Jewish family. He suffered from severe asthma from the age of nine. He studied law and philosophy, frequented the aristocratic salons of the Faubourg Saint-Germain and published a book of stories and sketches, Pleasures and Days (1896). He translated John Ruskin and worked for years on an abandoned novel, Jean Santeuil. He was an ardent Dreyfusard. After the deaths of his parents he withdrew increasingly from society, writing at night in a cork-lined bedroom on the Boulevard Haussmann. Swann's Way, the first volume of In Search of Lost Time, was published at his own expense in 1913. Within a Budding Grove won the Prix Goncourt in 1919. He died of pneumonia in 1922, still revising; the last three volumes appeared after his death.

Proust's novel follows its narrator from childhood through the high society of the Belle Époque and the First World War to his discovery that involuntary memory, triggered by sensations such as the taste of a madeleine dipped in tea, can recover the past, and that art can preserve it. His long, intricate sentences trace the movement of thought and feeling, and his analyses of jealousy, snobbery, desire and homosexuality were revolutionary. He influenced novelists and thinkers across the century and remains a touchstone for writing about memory.

## Key works

### In Search of Lost Time (1913–1927)

- **Date:** 1913–1927
- **Type:** Novel (seven volumes)
- **First published:** Paris, Grasset (vol. 1, 1913) and Gallimard (vols 2–7, 1919–1927)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=In%20Search%20of%20Lost%20Time%20Marcel%20Proust)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=In+Search+of+Lost+Time+Proust)

À la recherche du temps perdu, also translated as Remembrance of Things Past, was published in seven volumes: Swann's Way (1913), at Proust's own expense after rejections, including by André Gide at Gallimard; Within a Budding Grove (1919), which won the Prix Goncourt; The Guermantes Way; Sodom and Gomorrah; and, after Proust's death in 1922, The Captive, The Fugitive and Time Regained, edited from his manuscripts. Its narrator, Marcel, recalls his childhood in the village of Combray, brought back whole by the taste of a madeleine dipped in lime-blossom tea; the earlier love affair of the elegant Charles Swann with the courtesan Odette; his own adolescent love for Gilberte and later for the elusive Albertine; his entry into the aristocratic world of the Guermantes; and the figures of the Baron de Charlus and the painter Elstir. At a final reception after the First World War, he realises through involuntary memory that his vocation is to write the book we have just read. Its long sentences and its analysis of time and consciousness made it one of the defining works of Modernism.
