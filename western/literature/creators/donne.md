## Biography

John Donne was born in London in 1572 into a Roman Catholic family at a time when Catholics were persecuted; his brother died in prison for harbouring a priest. He studied at Oxford and Cambridge, though as a Catholic he could not take a degree, and at the Inns of Court, and sailed with the Earl of Essex on the raids on Cádiz and the Azores. He became secretary to the Lord Keeper, Sir Thomas Egerton, but his secret marriage in 1601 to Egerton's young niece Anne More cost him his post and briefly his liberty, and years of poverty followed. Having conformed to the Church of England, he was ordained in 1615 at the urging of James I, became a celebrated preacher and was appointed Dean of St Paul's Cathedral in 1621. He died in 1631.

Donne's poems, mostly published after his death in 1633, combine passionate feeling with intricate argument and far-fetched comparisons, the conceits for which Samuel Johnson later named him and his followers the metaphysical poets. His Songs and Sonnets range from cynical wit to intense devotion, and his Holy Sonnets wrestle with death and salvation. His sermons and Devotions upon Emergent Occasions are among the finest English prose of the age. Neglected in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, he was championed by T. S. Eliot and the modernists and is now one of the most admired English poets.

## Key works

The Holy Sonnets, also called the Divine Meditations, are nineteen sonnets mostly written around 1609–1610, during a period of illness, poverty and religious crisis before Donne's ordination. They circulated in manuscript and were first printed in his posthumous Poems of 1633, in a sequence and number that differ between manuscripts. The poems bring the urgent, dramatic voice and intellectual daring of Donne's love poetry to devotion: the speaker contemplates the last judgement, confronts his sin and fear of damnation, and addresses God in startling terms. Death be not proud defies death itself, and Batter my heart, three-personed God asks God to overthrow and ravish the speaker so that he may be free. The sonnets show the influence of Ignatian meditation and the tensions between Donne's Catholic upbringing and his Anglican faith. Benjamin Britten set nine of them to music, and they are among the finest religious poems in English.
