## Biography

William Wordsworth was born in 1770 in Cockermouth in the Lake District and lost both parents as a boy. He studied at St John's College, Cambridge, and in 1790–92 travelled in revolutionary France, where he was swept up in republican enthusiasm and fathered a daughter with Annette Vallon. War between Britain and France separated them, and the Terror disillusioned him. From 1795 he lived with his sister Dorothy, whose journals often inspired his poems, and formed a close friendship with Coleridge, with whom he published Lyrical Ballads in 1798. In 1799 he settled at Dove Cottage in Grasmere, married Mary Hutchinson and later moved to Rydal Mount. His political views became conservative, and he succeeded Southey as Poet Laureate in 1843. He died in 1850; his great autobiographical poem The Prelude was published that year.

In the preface to Lyrical Ballads, Wordsworth called for poetry in the real language of men and defined poetry as the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings, recollected in tranquillity. His best work, written roughly between 1797 and 1807, explores the growth of the mind, childhood memory and the healing power of landscape, in poems such as Tintern Abbey, the Immortality Ode and The Prelude. He changed what English poetry could be about and influenced not only later poets but also the way people value nature and wilderness.

### Lyrical Ballads (1798)

[![Lyrical Ballads](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/70/Lyrical_Ballads.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lyrical_Ballads.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the first edition, 1798 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lyrical_Ballads.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1798
- **Type:** Poetry collection (with Coleridge)
- **First published:** Bristol, published by Joseph Cottle; enlarged edition with Preface, London 1800
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lyrical%20Ballads%20William%20Wordsworth)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Lyrical+Ballads+Wordsworth)

Lyrical Ballads, with a Few Other Poems was published anonymously in 1798, the fruit of the close collaboration of Wordsworth and Coleridge in Somerset. According to Coleridge's later account, he was to write poems of the supernatural made believable, while Wordsworth would give the charm of novelty to things of every day. Coleridge contributed The Rime of the Ancient Mariner and three other pieces, Wordsworth the rest, including poems about rural poor people, children and outcasts such as We Are Seven, Simon Lee and The Thorn, and the meditative Lines Written a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey. The second edition of 1800, in two volumes under Wordsworth's name, added his Preface, which argued for poetry in the real language of men and defined it as the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings. The book received mixed reviews, but it is now regarded as the founding document of English Romantic poetry.

### The Prelude (1798–1839; published 1850)

- **Date:** 1798–1839; published 1850
- **Type:** Autobiographical poem
- **First published:** London, published posthumously by Edward Moxon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Prelude%20William%20Wordsworth)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Prelude+Wordsworth)

The Prelude, or Growth of a Poet's Mind was intended as an introduction to a vast philosophical poem, The Recluse, that Wordsworth never completed. He wrote a two-book version in 1798–99, a thirteen-book version addressed to Coleridge in 1805, and revised it for the rest of his life; it was published as The Prelude by his widow in 1850, months after his death. In blank verse, it traces his childhood in the Lake District, including famous spots of time such as stealing a boat at night, his years at Cambridge, a walking tour of the Alps, his residence in London and in France during the Revolution, his political disillusion and crisis, and his recovery through nature and the imagination, culminating in a vision on Mount Snowdon. The poem made the poet's own mental development into the subject of epic, and it is regarded as one of the masterpieces of English poetry. Modern editors often prefer the 1805 text to the more conservative revision of 1850.
