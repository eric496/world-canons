# Nikolai Gogol

**1809 – 1852** · Russian (Ukrainian-born)  
Born: Sorochyntsi, Poltava Governorate (now Ukraine) · Died: Moscow  
Movements & eras: [Russian Golden Age](../movements/russian-golden-age.md)  

> The grotesque comic genius of Russian literature, author of The Government Inspector, The Overcoat and Dead Souls.

## Biography

Nikolai Vasilyevich Gogol was born in 1809 in the village of Sorochyntsi in the Poltava region of Ukraine, into a family of minor gentry. He moved to Saint Petersburg in 1828, failed as a civil servant, actor and history lecturer, and found fame with Evenings on a Farm near Dikanka (1831–32), stories of Ukrainian folk life admired by Pushkin. His comedy The Government Inspector (1836), a satire on provincial corruption, was approved by Nicholas I himself but caused uproar. Gogol spent much of the following decade in Rome, where he wrote Dead Souls, published in 1842. In later years he fell under the influence of a fanatical priest, burned the manuscript of the second part of Dead Souls and starved himself, dying in Moscow in 1852.

Gogol's world is absurd, fantastic and pathetic at once: a nose that leaves its owner's face, a poor clerk who dies for the loss of an overcoat, a swindler buying the names of dead serfs. His prose, full of digressions, odd details and verbal fireworks, combines satire with a deep sense of the strangeness of reality. Dostoevsky is said to have remarked that the Russian writers all came out from under Gogol's overcoat, and Nabokov, Bulgakov and Kafka have been compared with him.

### Dead Souls (1842)

- **Date:** 1842
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Moscow
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dead%20Souls%20Nikolai%20Gogol)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Dead+Souls)

Dead Souls was published in 1842; the censor insisted on the title The Adventures of Chichikov, or Dead Souls, objecting that souls are immortal. Gogol called it a poem, imagining it as the first part of a trilogy modelled on Dante's Divine Comedy. Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, a smooth former civil servant, arrives in a provincial town and visits the surrounding landowners with an odd proposal: to buy the serfs, called souls, who have died since the last census but still count as alive for tax purposes, so that he can mortgage them as property. His visits introduce a gallery of grotesque landowners, the sentimental Manilov, the suspicious widow Korobochka, the brawling Nozdryov, the bearlike Sobakevich and the miser Plyushkin. When rumours spread, Chichikov flees in his troika, and the narrator compares Russia itself to a speeding troika. Gogol wrote a second part but burned most of it shortly before his death. The novel's satire, digressions and comic exuberance made it a foundation of Russian prose.

### The Overcoat (1842)

- **Date:** 1842
- **Type:** Short story
- **First published:** Saint Petersburg, in Gogol's Collected Works
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Overcoat%20Nikolai%20Gogol)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Overcoat)

The Overcoat was published in 1842 in the third volume of Gogol's collected works. Akaky Akakievich Bashmachkin is a timid, elderly copying clerk in a Saint Petersburg government department, mocked by his colleagues and content only when copying documents. When his threadbare coat can no longer be patched, he saves for months, going without tea and candles, and orders a new overcoat from a one-eyed tailor. The coat transforms his life, and his colleagues throw a party in its honour, but on the way home he is robbed of it. His pleas to a pompous Very Important Person for help end in a humiliating scolding, and he dies of fever. His ghost then haunts the city, stealing overcoats, until it seizes that of the Important Person. Blending comedy, pathos and the fantastic, the story became a founding text of Russian realism and its sympathy for the little man; the remark that Russian writers all came out from under Gogol's overcoat is traditionally attributed to Dostoevsky.
