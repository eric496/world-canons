# Lewis Carroll (Charles Lutwidge Dodgson)

**1832 – 1898** · English  
Born: Daresbury, Cheshire · Died: Guildford, Surrey  
Movements & eras: [Realism](../movements/realism-literature.md)  

> Oxford mathematician, logician and photographer who wrote Alice's Adventures in Wonderland and Through the Looking-Glass.

## Biography

Charles Lutwidge Dodgson was born in 1832 at Daresbury in Cheshire, the eldest son among eleven children of a clergyman, and entertained his siblings with games, puzzles and magazines. He studied at Christ Church, Oxford, and spent the rest of his life there as a lecturer in mathematics, publishing works on geometry and symbolic logic and becoming an accomplished amateur photographer. On a boat trip in July 1862 he improvised a story for Alice Liddell, the daughter of the dean of Christ Church, and her sisters. Written down and expanded, it was published as Alice's Adventures in Wonderland in 1865 under the pen name Lewis Carroll, with illustrations by John Tenniel. Through the Looking-Glass followed in 1871 and the nonsense poem The Hunting of the Snark in 1876. He died in Guildford in 1898.

Carroll's Alice books turned children's literature away from moral instruction towards play, and their nonsense, puns, parodies of improving verse and logical paradoxes delight adults as much as children. Characters such as the Mad Hatter, the Cheshire Cat and Humpty Dumpty became part of common culture, and his invented words, such as chortle, entered the language. Logicians, philosophers, Surrealists and Joyce have all found something in his work.

### Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

- **Date:** 1865
- **Type:** Children's novel
- **First published:** London, published by Macmillan, with illustrations by John Tenniel
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Alice%27s%20Adventures%20in%20Wonderland%20Lewis%20Carroll%20%28Charles%20Lutwidge%20Dodgson%29)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Alice%27s+Adventures+in+Wonderland)

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland began as a story Charles Dodgson told to the three Liddell sisters on a rowing trip from Oxford on 4 July 1862. He wrote it out as Alice's Adventures Under Ground, with his own illustrations, as a gift for Alice Liddell, and then expanded it for publication by Macmillan in 1865 with forty-two illustrations by the Punch cartoonist John Tenniel. The first printing was withdrawn because Tenniel was unhappy with its quality. Bored on a riverbank, Alice follows a White Rabbit with a pocket watch down a rabbit hole and finds herself growing and shrinking after eating and drinking, swimming in a pool of her own tears and meeting the Caterpillar, the Duchess, the Cheshire Cat, the Mad Hatter and March Hare at their tea party, and the Queen of Hearts, who demands that everyone's head be cut off. The book ends with a trial and Alice waking up. Its puns, parodies and logical absurdities revolutionised children's literature, and it has been translated into well over a hundred languages.

### Through the Looking-Glass (1871 (dated 1872))

- **Date:** 1871 (dated 1872)
- **Type:** Children's novel
- **First published:** London, published by Macmillan, with illustrations by John Tenniel
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Through%20the%20Looking-Glass%20Lewis%20Carroll%20%28Charles%20Lutwidge%20Dodgson%29)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Through+the+Looking-Glass)

Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There was published in December 1871 as a sequel to Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, again with illustrations by John Tenniel. Playing with her kitten on a winter evening, Alice climbs through the mirror over the fireplace into a reversed world where she must run fast to stay in the same place. The landscape is a giant chessboard, and Alice joins the game as a white pawn, advancing square by square to become a queen. On the way she reads the nonsense poem Jabberwocky in mirror writing, meets the Red Queen and White Queen, Tweedledum and Tweedledee, who recite The Walrus and the Carpenter, Humpty Dumpty, who explains the meanings of words, and the gentle White Knight. The book is more structured and melancholy than its predecessor, and introduced words such as chortle and galumph. Its play with logic, language and mirror reversal has fascinated logicians, physicists and philosophers as well as children.
