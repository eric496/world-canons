# Thomas Mann

**1875 – 1955** · German  
Born: Lübeck, Germany · Died: Zurich, Switzerland  
Movements & eras: [Modernism](../movements/modernism-literature.md)  

> The German novelist of Buddenbrooks, Death in Venice and The Magic Mountain, winner of the 1929 Nobel Prize and an exiled opponent of Nazism.

**Links:** [Texts on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Thomas+Mann)

## Biography

Paul Thomas Mann was born in 1875 into a patrician merchant family in Lübeck. After his father's death and the liquidation of the family firm, he moved to Munich, worked briefly in insurance and on a satirical magazine, and published Buddenbrooks (1901), a novel of a merchant family's decline based on his own, which made him famous. Death in Venice followed in 1912. He married Katia Pringsheim, and their children included the writers Klaus and Erika Mann. During the First World War he defended German nationalism, but in the 1920s he became a supporter of the Weimar Republic. The Magic Mountain appeared in 1924, and he received the Nobel Prize in 1929. In 1933 he went into exile, first in Switzerland, then from 1938 in the United States, where he broadcast speeches against Hitler and wrote Joseph and His Brothers and Doctor Faustus. He returned to Switzerland in 1952 and died near Zurich in 1955.

Mann's novels are large, ironic and full of ideas, exploring the conflict between art and life, the bourgeois and the artist, health and disease, and the fate of German culture. He used leitmotifs, recurring phrases associated with characters in the manner of Wagner, and blended realism with myth and philosophical debate. He is regarded as the leading German novelist of the twentieth century.

Buddenbrooks: The Decline of a Family was published in two volumes in 1901, when Mann was twenty-six, and drew closely on the history of his own family of Lübeck grain merchants. The novel follows the Buddenbrooks from 1835, when they celebrate moving into a grand house, to 1877. The robust founder Johann is succeeded by his pious son Jean, then by the dutiful but increasingly exhausted Thomas, who becomes senator and keeps up appearances while his business falters, and finally by Thomas's son Hanno, a sickly, musically gifted boy who dies of typhoid as a teenager. Thomas's sister Tony, cheerful through two failed marriages, provides much of the comedy. Mann depicts the solid bourgeois world of a Hanseatic city in ironic, leisurely detail, while showing how its decline goes hand in hand with a growth of refinement, introspection and artistic feeling, influenced by Schopenhauer and Wagner. A cheap one-volume edition of 1903 became a bestseller, and the Nobel committee cited it specifically when awarding Mann the prize in 1929.

### The Magic Mountain (1924)

[![The Magic Mountain](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/af/1924_Der_Zauberberg_%283%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1924_Der_Zauberberg_%283%29.jpg)

- **Date:** 1924
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Berlin, published by S. Fischer
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Magic%20Mountain%20Thomas%20Mann)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Magic+Mountain+Mann)

The Magic Mountain was begun in 1912, after Mann visited his wife at a sanatorium in Davos, interrupted by the First World War and published in two volumes in 1924. Hans Castorp, an ordinary young engineer from Hamburg, travels to the International Sanatorium Berghof in the Swiss Alps to visit his cousin Joachim, a patient. Found to have a spot on his lung himself, he remains for seven years, in a world where time loses its meaning. He falls in love with the Russian Clavdia Chauchat and is educated by two rival mentors, the Italian humanist Settembrini, who champions reason and progress, and the Jesuit-trained Naphta, who argues for terror and absolutism; later he meets the overwhelming Dutch planter Peeperkorn. In a snowstorm, Hans has a visionary dream about love and death. The outbreak of war in 1914 finally ends his stay, and the novel leaves him on a battlefield. It is a great novel of ideas and a portrait of pre-war European culture, and an ironic version of the Bildungsroman.
