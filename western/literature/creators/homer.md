# Homer

**c. 8th century BC** · Greek  
Born: Ionia (traditionally; birthplace claimed by several cities) · Died: unknown  
Movements & eras: [Greek epic & lyric](../movements/greek-epic-lyric.md)  

> The name the ancient Greeks gave to the poet of the Iliad and the Odyssey, the founding epics of Western literature. Whether a single Homer ever existed is one of the oldest questions in literary scholarship.

## Biography

Nothing reliable is known about Homer's life. Ancient tradition pictured him as a blind singer from the Ionian coast of Asia Minor, and seven cities, among them Smyrna and Chios, claimed to be his birthplace. Modern scholars generally date the two epics to the late eighth or seventh century BC. Since the work of Milman Parry in the 1930s, it has been widely accepted that they grew out of a long tradition of oral composition, in which singers improvised in hexameter using stock phrases and scenes. Whether one poet, two poets or a whole tradition stands behind the poems, and when exactly they were fixed in writing, remains debated; the so-called Homeric Question has never been settled.

Whoever composed them, the Iliad and the Odyssey were the shared foundation of Greek culture: children learned to read from them, philosophers argued with them, and tragedians took their plots from the stories around them. Their formulaic epithets, extended similes and vivid scenes of war, homecoming and grief became the model for epic from Virgil to Milton. Through translation, from Chapman and Pope to modern versions, Homer has never ceased to be read, and the Odyssey in particular supplied the template for countless journeys in later literature, including Joyce's Ulysses.

### Iliad (c. 8th century BC)

- **Date:** c. 8th century BC
- **Type:** Epic poem
- **First published:** Oral composition in Ionia; fixed in writing perhaps 7th–6th century BC
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Iliad%20Homer)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Iliad)

The Iliad is an epic of about 15,700 lines of dactylic hexameter, set during a few weeks in the tenth year of the Greek siege of Troy. It opens with a quarrel between Agamemnon, leader of the Greeks, and their greatest warrior, Achilles, who withdraws from the fighting in anger. Without him the Greeks are driven back to their ships, until the death of his beloved companion Patroclus at the hands of the Trojan prince Hector brings Achilles back in murderous grief. He kills Hector and abuses his corpse, but the poem ends with a scene of reconciliation when the aged king Priam comes to ransom his son's body. Built from formulaic phrases and type scenes inherited from oral tradition, the poem combines brutal battlefield detail with long similes drawn from peaceful life and an extraordinary sympathy for both sides. It was the foundation of Greek education, a model for Virgil and Milton, and is still read in new translations as a profound meditation on war, honour and mortality.

### Odyssey (c. 8th century BC)

- **Date:** c. 8th century BC
- **Type:** Epic poem
- **First published:** Oral composition in Ionia; fixed in writing perhaps 7th–6th century BC
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Odyssey%20Homer)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Odyssey)

The Odyssey, in twenty-four books of hexameter verse, tells of the homecoming of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, after the fall of Troy. It begins with his son Telemachus searching for news of him while suitors devour the household and press his mother Penelope to remarry. Odysseus, held for years by the nymph Calypso, reaches the court of the Phaeacians and there recounts his wanderings: the Cyclops Polyphemus, the witch Circe, the descent to the underworld, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis. Returning disguised as a beggar, he tests his household and, with Telemachus, kills the suitors before being reunited with Penelope. The poem's complex structure, with its flashbacks and stories within stories, and its hero who survives by cunning rather than strength made it the model for countless later narratives. Its episodes have become part of common language, and its influence runs from Virgil and Dante to Joyce's Ulysses and modern novels and films.
