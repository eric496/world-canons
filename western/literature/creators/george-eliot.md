## Biography

Mary Ann Evans was born in 1819 on an estate in Warwickshire where her father was a land agent. Intensely religious as a young woman, she lost her faith through reading biblical criticism, and translated David Friedrich Strauss's Life of Jesus (1846) and Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity. In London she became assistant editor of the radical Westminster Review. In 1854 she began living openly with the critic George Henry Lewes, who was married but unable to divorce, a choice that cut her off from respectable society and from her brother. Encouraged by Lewes, she began writing fiction under the name George Eliot, and Adam Bede (1859) was a great success, followed by The Mill on the Floss, Silas Marner, Romola, Middlemarch (1871–72) and Daniel Deronda. After Lewes's death she married John Cross in 1880 and died that December.

George Eliot brought to the novel a philosopher's intelligence and a deep sympathy for ordinary lives. Her narrators reflect on the moral choices and limited horizons of their characters, linking the smallest acts to wider social change, and her psychological analysis prepared the way for Henry James and the modern novel. Virginia Woolf called Middlemarch one of the few English novels written for grown-up people, and it is regularly voted among the greatest novels in English.

## Key works

### Middlemarch (1871–1872)

- **Date:** 1871–1872
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Edinburgh and London, published in eight parts by William Blackwood and Sons
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Middlemarch%20George%20Eliot%20%28Mary%20Ann%20Evans%29)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Middlemarch+Eliot)

Middlemarch: A Study of Provincial Life was published in eight parts in 1871–72 and is set in a fictional Midlands town between 1829 and 1832, the years leading up to the Great Reform Act. It interweaves several stories. Dorothea Brooke, an ardent young woman longing to do good, marries the elderly scholar Edward Casaubon, whose life's work, a key to all mythologies, proves sterile, and his jealous will tries to prevent her from marrying his young cousin Will Ladislaw. The ambitious new doctor Tertius Lydgate, hoping to reform medicine, is drawn into debt by his marriage to the beautiful, selfish Rosamond Vincy. Rosamond's brother Fred courts Mary Garth, and the banker Bulstrode's hidden past ruins him. Eliot's narrator reflects with wisdom and irony on how lives are shaped by social circumstance and small choices, ending with a celebrated tribute to those who live faithfully a hidden life. Virginia Woolf called it one of the few English novels written for grown-up people, and it is often named the greatest English novel.
