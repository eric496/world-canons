## Biography

Adeline Virginia Stephen was born in London in 1882, the daughter of the critic and editor Sir Leslie Stephen. Educated at home in her father's library, she suffered the deaths of her mother, half-sister and father in close succession, and the first of several severe breakdowns. With her siblings she moved to Bloomsbury, where their friends formed the Bloomsbury Group of writers, artists and thinkers. In 1912 she married the writer Leonard Woolf, and in 1917 they founded the Hogarth Press, which published her own work as well as T. S. Eliot and the first English translations of Freud. Her novels include Mrs Dalloway (1925), To the Lighthouse (1927), Orlando, inspired by her love for Vita Sackville-West, and The Waves. Her essays include A Room of One's Own (1929) and Three Guineas. During the Second World War, fearing another breakdown, she drowned herself in the River Ouse in 1941.

Woolf wanted to record the atoms as they fall upon the mind, and her novels follow the flow of consciousness of several characters, moving freely between present perception and memory, often within a single day. Her lyrical style and experiments with time and point of view made her a central figure of Modernism. Her essays on women and writing, especially the argument that a woman must have money and a room of her own to write fiction, made her a founding figure of feminist criticism.

### Mrs Dalloway (1925)

- **Date:** 1925
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** London, published by the Hogarth Press
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mrs%20Dalloway%20Virginia%20Woolf)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Mrs+Dalloway+Woolf)

Mrs Dalloway was published by the Hogarth Press, which Woolf and her husband ran, in May 1925. Its working title was The Hours. The novel follows a single day in London in June 1923. Clarissa Dalloway, the wife of a Conservative MP, walks through Westminster to buy flowers for her party that evening, and her thoughts move back to her youth at Bourton and her love for Peter Walsh, who unexpectedly visits, and for Sally Seton. Interwoven with her day is that of Septimus Warren Smith, a veteran suffering from shell shock, who hears birds singing in Greek and whose doctors fail him; to avoid being committed, he throws himself from a window. At the party Clarissa hears of his death and feels a strange kinship with him. Woolf moves fluidly between characters' minds, using Big Ben's chimes to mark time. The novel is a landmark of Modernism, and Michael Cunningham's novel The Hours retold it.

### To the Lighthouse (1927)

- **Date:** 1927
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** London, published by the Hogarth Press
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=To%20the%20Lighthouse%20Virginia%20Woolf)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=To+the+Lighthouse+Woolf)

To the Lighthouse was published in May 1927 and draws on Woolf's childhood summers at Talland House in Cornwall and on her parents, Leslie and Julia Stephen. The novel is in three parts. The Window takes place on a single day before the First World War at the Ramsays' holiday house on the Isle of Skye: young James longs to visit the lighthouse, his philosopher father insists the weather will be bad, and Mrs Ramsay, the warm centre of the household, presides over a dinner party, while the painter Lily Briscoe struggles with her picture. The short middle section, Time Passes, describes the empty house over ten years, during which Mrs Ramsay, her daughter Prue and her son Andrew die, the last in the war, events reported in brackets. In The Lighthouse, Mr Ramsay finally sails there with his now adolescent children, while Lily, on the lawn, completes her painting in a moment of vision. The novel's lyrical treatment of time, memory and art makes it Woolf's masterpiece for many readers.
