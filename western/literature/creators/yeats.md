## Biography

William Butler Yeats was born in Dublin in 1865, the son of the painter John Butler Yeats, and grew up between London, Dublin and his mother's home county of Sligo. He studied art, became fascinated by Irish folklore, mysticism and the occult, and in London helped found the Rhymers' Club. In 1889 he met the revolutionary Maud Gonne, whom he loved hopelessly for decades. With Lady Gregory and others he led the Irish Literary Revival and co-founded the Abbey Theatre in Dublin in 1904. After the Easter Rising of 1916 and Gonne's refusal of him, he married Georgie Hyde-Lees in 1917, whose automatic writing fed his visionary system. He bought a tower, Thoor Ballylee, served as a senator of the new Irish Free State and received the Nobel Prize in 1923. He died in France in 1939.

Yeats's early poetry is lyrical, Romantic and steeped in Celtic myth; from around 1910 he developed a harder, more direct and modern voice, addressing politics, ageing, love and history in poems such as Easter, 1916, The Second Coming, Sailing to Byzantium and Among School Children. His late work is among the most powerful written by any poet in old age. He bridged Victorian Symbolism and Modernism and became the central figure of modern Irish literature.

## Key works

### The Second Coming (1919; published 1920)

- **Date:** 1919; published 1920
- **Type:** Poem
- **First published:** The Dial (November 1920); collected in Michael Robartes and the Dancer, 1921
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Second%20Coming%20W.%20B.%20Yeats)

The Second Coming was written in January 1919, in the aftermath of the First World War, the Russian Revolution and the Easter Rising, and first published in The Dial in 1920. In twenty-two lines of loose blank verse, Yeats describes a world in which things fall apart and the centre cannot hold, the falcon cannot hear the falconer, and anarchy is loosed upon the world. The speaker then has a vision of a figure with a lion's body and the head of a man rising from the desert, and asks what rough beast, its hour come round at last, slouches towards Bethlehem to be born. The poem draws on Yeats's cyclical theory of history, later set out in A Vision, in which the two-thousand-year Christian era is ending and giving way to its opposite, symbolised by interlocking gyres. It is one of the most quoted poems of the twentieth century, its phrases borrowed for many book titles, among them Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart.
