## Biography

Nathaniel Hathorne, who later added a w to his name, was born in Salem, Massachusetts, in 1804; one of his ancestors had been a judge in the Salem witch trials. He studied at Bowdoin College alongside Longfellow and the future president Franklin Pierce, then spent a decade in near seclusion in Salem writing tales, published as Twice-Told Tales in 1837. He briefly joined the utopian community of Brook Farm, married the painter Sophia Peabody, and worked at the Boston and Salem custom houses. The Scarlet Letter (1850) made his name and was followed by The House of the Seven Gables and The Blithedale Romance. His friend Pierce appointed him consul in Liverpool, and he later lived in Italy. He died in 1864 while travelling in New Hampshire.

Hawthorne called his novels romances, meaning that they mixed realism with symbol, allegory and the supernatural to probe moral and psychological truth. Ambiguous symbols, such as the scarlet A, the minister's black veil or the birthmark, are at the heart of his tales, and his recurring concerns are hidden guilt, pride and the legacy of the Puritan past. Melville dedicated Moby-Dick to him, and Henry James wrote a study of him. He is a founding figure of American fiction.

## Key works

### The Scarlet Letter (1850)

- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Boston, published by Ticknor, Reed & Fields
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Scarlet%20Letter%20Nathaniel%20Hawthorne)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Scarlet+Letter+Hawthorne)

The Scarlet Letter: A Romance was published in 1850 with a long introductory essay, The Custom-House, in which Hawthorne describes his work at the Salem custom house and claims to have found there the scarlet letter and the papers that inspired the story. Set in Boston in the 1640s, the novel opens with Hester Prynne standing on the scaffold with her infant daughter Pearl, condemned to wear a scarlet A on her breast for adultery. She refuses to name the father. Her long-absent husband, a scholar, arrives in disguise, calls himself Roger Chillingworth and sets out to discover her lover, who is the revered young minister Arthur Dimmesdale. Chillingworth torments Dimmesdale, whose hidden guilt destroys his health, until, after a sermon of unusual power, the minister mounts the scaffold with Hester and Pearl, confesses and dies. Hester, through quiet charity, transforms the meaning of the letter. The novel's symbolism, psychological depth and critique of Puritan intolerance made it one of the first classics of American literature.
