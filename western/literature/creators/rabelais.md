## Biography

François Rabelais was born around 1494 near Chinon in the Loire valley, the son of a lawyer. He became a Franciscan friar and then a Benedictine, learning Greek at a time when the Sorbonne regarded it with suspicion, and corresponded with the great humanist Guillaume Budé. In the late 1520s he left the cloister, studied medicine at Montpellier and became a physician at the hospital in Lyon, where he edited Hippocrates and Galen. There in 1532 he published Pantagruel under the anagram Alcofribas Nasier, followed by Gargantua in 1534 and three further books. Protected by the powerful du Bellay family, he travelled to Rome several times, and the Sorbonne repeatedly condemned his books. He died in Paris in 1553.

Rabelais's books mix humanist learning with popular humour of the most bodily kind: feasts, drinking, lists, coined words, parodies of scholastic argument and satire of monks, lawyers and theologians. Beneath the comedy is an optimistic humanism, expressed in the utopian Abbey of Thélème, whose only rule is do what you will. His exuberant style enriched the French language with hundreds of words. The Russian critic Mikhail Bakhtin made him the centre of his theory of the carnivalesque, and writers from Swift and Sterne to Joyce acknowledged his influence.

## Key works

### Gargantua and Pantagruel (1532–1564)

[![Gargantua and Pantagruel](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f7/Page11-682px-Rabelais_-_Pantagruel%2C_ca_1530.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Page11-682px-Rabelais_-_Pantagruel%2C_ca_1530.jpg)

- **Date:** 1532–1564
- **Type:** Comic novel (five books)
- **First published:** Lyon (first two books) and Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gargantua%20and%20Pantagruel%20Fran%C3%A7ois%20Rabelais)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Gargantua+and+Pantagruel+Rabelais)

Gargantua and Pantagruel is a sequence of five books published from 1532, beginning with Pantagruel and followed by Gargantua (1534), which tells the story of Pantagruel's father, then the Third Book (1546), the Fourth Book (1552) and a posthumous Fifth Book of uncertain authenticity. Loosely based on a popular chapbook about giants, it follows the birth, monstrous appetites and education of Gargantua and his son Pantagruel, Gargantua's war against the ridiculous king Picrochole, the founding of the anti-monastery of Thélème, and the voyages of Pantagruel and his rascally companion Panurge in search of an answer to whether Panurge should marry. Rabelais filled the books with obscene humour, lists, invented words, parodies of legal and theological jargon and serious humanist ideas about education and peace. They were condemned by the Sorbonne but read across Europe, and they added the adjective Rabelaisian to many languages.
