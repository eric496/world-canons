## Biography

Jean-Baptiste Poquelin was born in Paris in 1622, the son of a prosperous upholsterer to the royal household. Educated by the Jesuits, he gave up a secure future to found a theatre company in 1643 with the actress Madeleine Béjart, taking the stage name Molière. After the company's failure in Paris he spent thirteen years touring the provinces, learning his craft from Italian commedia dell'arte. Back in Paris from 1658, he won the patronage of Louis XIV and wrote a stream of comedies, including The School for Wives, Tartuffe, Don Juan, The Misanthrope, The Miser and The Bourgeois Gentleman. His attacks on hypocrisy provoked powerful enemies; Tartuffe was banned for five years. He collapsed while playing the title role of The Imaginary Invalid in 1673 and died a few hours later.

Molière combined the physical comedy of farce with sharp observation of social types, the hypocrite, the miser, the snob, the hypochondriac, the pedant and the jealous husband, and made laughter a means of moral and social criticism. Many of his plays are written in elegant alexandrine couplets. The Comédie-Française, founded in 1680 from his company, is still called the House of Molière, and French is sometimes called the language of Molière. He is among the most performed playwrights in the world.

### The Misanthrope (1666)

- **Date:** 1666
- **Type:** Comedy
- **First published:** Palais-Royal theatre, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Misanthrope%20Moli%C3%A8re%20%28Jean-Baptiste%20Poquelin%29)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Misanthrope+Moli%C3%A8re)

The Misanthrope is often considered Molière's most profound comedy. Alceste refuses all compromise with the politeness and flattery of fashionable society and insists on telling the truth whatever the cost; his friend Philinte argues that one must accept human nature and be tolerant. Yet Alceste loves Célimène, a witty young widow who delights in society, gossip and admirers. He offends a courtier by criticising his sonnet, which leads to a lawsuit, and loses another case that he refuses to fight by the usual means. When Célimène's letters mocking all her suitors are revealed, they abandon her; she is willing to marry Alceste but not to leave the world for a solitary life with him, and he departs alone. Written in alexandrine verse, the play has little action and much conversation, and its ending is far from simple comedy. Rousseau criticised it for mocking a virtuous man, and audiences still debate whether Alceste is admirable or ridiculous.

### Tartuffe (1664 (first version); 1669 (final))

[![Tartuffe](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d2/Tartuffe.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tartuffe.jpg)

<sub>Frontispiece of an early edition of Tartuffe Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tartuffe.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1664 (first version); 1669 (final)
- **Type:** Comedy
- **First published:** Versailles (first version, 1664); Palais-Royal theatre, Paris (1669)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tartuffe%20Moli%C3%A8re%20%28Jean-Baptiste%20Poquelin%29)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Tartuffe+Moli%C3%A8re)

Tartuffe, or the Impostor caused the greatest scandal of Molière's career. A first three-act version was performed for Louis XIV at Versailles in 1664, but the Archbishop of Paris and the devout party at court were outraged, and the play was banned. Molière revised it twice and was finally allowed to stage the five-act version in 1669, when it was a triumph. The wealthy Orgon has taken into his house Tartuffe, whom he believes a holy man, and is so infatuated that he plans to give him his daughter's hand and signs over his property. His wife Elmire exposes Tartuffe by letting Orgon hide under a table while the hypocrite tries to seduce her. Tartuffe then uses the deed and compromising papers to evict the family and denounce Orgon, but a royal officer arrives to arrest the impostor in the name of the all-seeing king. Written in alexandrine couplets, the play is Molière's sharpest attack on religious hypocrisy, and Tartuffe's name became a word for a hypocrite.
