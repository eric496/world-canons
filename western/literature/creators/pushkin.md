## Biography

Alexander Sergeyevich Pushkin was born in Moscow in 1799 into an old noble family; his maternal great-grandfather, Abram Gannibal, had been brought to Russia as an African child and became a general under Peter the Great. Educated at the new lycée at Tsarskoye Selo, he was already a celebrated poet when he graduated. His political verses led to exile in the south of Russia in 1820 and then to confinement on his mother's estate at Mikhailovskoye. Pardoned by Nicholas I in 1826, he remained under the tsar's personal censorship. He married the beautiful Natalya Goncharova in 1831. Rumours of an affair between her and the French officer Georges d'Anthès led Pushkin to challenge him to a duel in January 1837, in which he was mortally wounded.

Pushkin wrote lyric poems, narrative poems, the novel in verse Eugene Onegin, the historical drama Boris Godunov, the Little Tragedies, prose tales such as The Queen of Spades and the historical novel The Captain's Daughter. He fused the Russian vernacular with the literary language and achieved a clarity, lightness and range that made him the foundation on which Gogol, Lermontov, Tolstoy and Dostoevsky built. Many of his works became operas by Glinka, Mussorgsky and Tchaikovsky. In Russia he is regarded as the national poet, beyond comparison.

### Eugene Onegin (1825–1832; complete edition 1833)

[![Eugene Onegin](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ed/Eugene_Onegin_book_edition.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Eugene_Onegin_book_edition.jpg)

- **Date:** 1825–1832; complete edition 1833
- **Type:** Novel in verse
- **First published:** Saint Petersburg (serialised in chapters; complete edition 1833)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Eugene%20Onegin%20Alexander%20Pushkin)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Eugene+Onegin+Pushkin)

Eugene Onegin was published in chapters between 1825 and 1832 and as a complete book in 1833. It is written in a stanza of fourteen lines of iambic tetrameter with an intricate rhyme scheme that Pushkin invented, now called the Onegin stanza. Onegin, a jaded young aristocrat of Saint Petersburg, inherits a country estate, where he befriends the young Romantic poet Lensky, engaged to Olga Larina. Olga's sister Tatyana, a dreamy reader of novels, falls in love with Onegin and writes him a letter declaring it; he coldly lectures her. At Tatyana's name-day party, Onegin flirts with Olga out of boredom, Lensky challenges him to a duel and Onegin kills him. Years later Onegin meets Tatyana again in Saint Petersburg, married to a prince and the queen of society, and falls passionately in love with her, but she, though she still loves him, refuses to betray her husband. With its witty narrator, digressions and panorama of Russian life, Belinsky called it an encyclopaedia of Russian life. Tchaikovsky made it into an opera, and Nabokov produced a famous literal translation.

### The Bronze Horseman (1833; published 1837)

- **Date:** 1833; published 1837
- **Type:** Narrative poem
- **First published:** Saint Petersburg (published posthumously in a censored version, 1837)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Bronze%20Horseman%20Alexander%20Pushkin)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Bronze+Horseman+Pushkin)

The Bronze Horseman: A Petersburg Tale was written in 1833 at Boldino, but Nicholas I, acting as Pushkin's personal censor, demanded changes, and it was published only after the poet's death, in an altered version. It begins with a celebrated prologue in praise of Peter the Great and the city he founded on the marshes of the Neva. The story then follows Evgeny, a poor clerk who dreams of marrying his sweetheart Parasha. During the great flood of November 1824 he sits stranded on a marble lion facing Falconet's equestrian statue of Peter; when the waters recede he finds that Parasha's house and family have been swept away. Driven mad, he wanders the city and, one night, shakes his fist at the statue, which seems to come alive and gallop after him through the streets. His body is later found on a small island. The poem's conflict between the individual and the state, and its mythic vision of Saint Petersburg, made it one of the most influential works in Russian literature, echoed by Gogol, Dostoevsky, Bely and Mandelstam.
