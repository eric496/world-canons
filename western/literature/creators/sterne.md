# Laurence Sterne

**1713 – 1768** · Anglo-Irish  
Born: Clonmel, Ireland · Died: London  
Movements & eras: [Rise of the novel](../movements/rise-of-the-novel.md)  

> A Yorkshire clergyman whose Tristram Shandy, a novel of digressions, black pages and missing chapters, made him a celebrity and a forerunner of modern experimental fiction.

## Biography

Laurence Sterne was born in 1713 at Clonmel in Ireland, the son of an English army officer, and spent his childhood moving between garrisons. He was educated at Jesus College, Cambridge, was ordained, and for over twenty years was a country parson near York, unhappily married and little known. In 1759 he published the first two volumes of The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman, which caused a sensation in London; seven more volumes followed until 1767. Suffering from tuberculosis, he travelled in France and Italy for his health, an experience he turned into A Sentimental Journey through France and Italy (1768). He died in London weeks after it appeared.

Sterne built his comedy on the idea, taken from John Locke, that the mind works by chance associations. His narrator can barely get himself born, wanders into endless digressions and plays with the printed page itself. Combined with a warm, sentimental humour, this made Sterne immensely popular across Europe; Goethe, Diderot and Pushkin admired him. In the twentieth century, writers and critics recognised in Tristram Shandy techniques of modernism and postmodernism, and the Russian formalist Viktor Shklovsky called it the most typical novel in world literature.

## Key works

### Tristram Shandy (1759–1767)

- **Date:** 1759–1767
- **Type:** Novel (nine volumes)
- **First published:** York (vols 1–2) and London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tristram%20Shandy%20Laurence%20Sterne)
- **Links:** [Search Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Tristram+Shandy)

The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman appeared in nine volumes between 1759 and 1767. Its narrator, Tristram, sets out to tell his life and opinions but begins with the moment of his conception, interrupted by his mother's question about winding the clock, and digresses so constantly that he is not born until volume three. Instead the book is filled with his eccentric father Walter, who has theories about names and noses; his uncle Toby, a gentle old soldier obsessed with re-creating the sieges of the War of the Spanish Succession on a bowling green; Toby's servant Corporal Trim; and the parson Yorick. Sterne plays with the printed page: a black page mourns Yorick, a marbled page appears as an emblem, chapters are missing, and diagrams show the wanderings of the plot. Wildly popular and condemned by Samuel Johnson, who predicted it would not last, the novel was later recognised as a precursor of Modernist and postmodern fiction.
