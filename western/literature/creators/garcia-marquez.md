# Gabriel García Márquez

**1927 – 2014** · Colombian  
Born: Aracataca, Colombia · Died: Mexico City  
Movements & eras: [Latin American Boom](../movements/latin-american-boom.md)  

> Colombian novelist and journalist, master of magical realism and author of One Hundred Years of Solitude. Nobel Prize 1982.

## Biography

Gabriel José García Márquez was born in 1927 in Aracataca, a town in Colombia's Caribbean region, and was raised in his early years by his maternal grandparents, whose stories of war and superstition shaped his imagination. He studied law in Bogotá but left to become a journalist, working in Cartagena, Barranquilla and Bogotá and as a correspondent in Europe. His early fiction, including No One Writes to the Colonel, was little noticed. Living in Mexico City, he wrote One Hundred Years of Solitude in about eighteen months; published in Buenos Aires in 1967, it became a phenomenon across the Spanish-speaking world and then internationally. The Autumn of the Patriarch, Chronicle of a Death Foretold and Love in the Time of Cholera followed. He received the Nobel Prize in 1982, remained a political journalist and friend of Fidel Castro, and died in Mexico City in 2014.

García Márquez combined the matter-of-fact narration of his grandmother's tales with the techniques of Faulkner and Kafka, presenting marvels and miracles as ordinary events, a style that became known as magical realism. His fiction explores solitude, power, memory and the cycles of Latin American history. He is the best-known writer of the Latin American Boom and one of the most widely read novelists of the twentieth century.

## Key works

### One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967)

- **Date:** 1967
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Buenos Aires, published by Editorial Sudamericana
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=One%20Hundred%20Years%20of%20Solitude%20Gabriel%20Garc%C3%ADa%20M%C3%A1rquez)

One Hundred Years of Solitude (Cien años de soledad) was written in Mexico City and published in Buenos Aires in May 1967, where its first printing sold out within weeks; it went on to sell tens of millions of copies in many languages. It tells the history of the town of Macondo, founded in a swamp by José Arcadio Buendía and his wife Úrsula Iguarán, and of seven generations of their descendants, whose names, José Arcadio and Aureliano, repeat as their characters and fates do. Colonel Aureliano Buendía fights thirty-two civil wars and loses them all; a plague of insomnia causes the town to forget the names of things; a beautiful girl ascends to heaven while hanging out the laundry; and a banana company brings foreign exploitation and a massacre of striking workers that the authorities deny ever happened. In the end the last Buendía deciphers the manuscripts of the gypsy Melquíades, which foretold the family's whole history, as a wind wipes Macondo away. The novel's magical realism made it the emblem of the Latin American Boom.
