> The anonymous Old English poet who composed Beowulf, the longest surviving epic in Old English. Nothing is known about him.

**Links:** [Texts on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Beowulf+poet)

## Biography

The poet of Beowulf is entirely anonymous. The poem survives in a single manuscript, the Nowell Codex, copied by two scribes around AD 1000 and now in the British Library, where it was damaged in the Cotton Library fire of 1731. When it was composed is much debated: estimates range from the seventh to the early eleventh century, with many scholars favouring the eighth. Whether it is the work of one poet or the written form of a longer oral tradition is also debated. The poet was evidently a Christian, writing in England about pagan Scandinavian heroes of an earlier age, and knew the traditional techniques of Germanic alliterative verse intimately.

The poet's art lies in the alliterative line, the dense compound words and metaphors called kennings, and a structure that balances youth and age, triumph and loss. The poem looks back at a heroic past with admiration and melancholy, aware that its heroes lacked the Christian faith of its audience. Little noticed until the nineteenth century, Beowulf was transformed in the twentieth by J. R. R. Tolkien's 1936 lecture, which argued for it as a work of art rather than merely a historical document, and it now stands at the head of English literature.

- **First published:** Surviving manuscript: Nowell Codex, c. 1000, British Library, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Beowulf%20The%20Beowulf%20poet)

Beowulf is an Old English poem of 3,182 alliterative lines, surviving in a single manuscript copied around the year 1000 and badly damaged in the Cotton Library fire of 1731. It is set in sixth-century Scandinavia. The young Geatish warrior Beowulf sails to Denmark to help King Hrothgar, whose hall Heorot is attacked nightly by the monster Grendel. Beowulf tears off Grendel's arm, then follows Grendel's vengeful mother to her lair beneath a lake and kills her. Fifty years later, as king of the Geats, he fights a dragon that is ravaging his land, killing it with the help of the young Wiglaf but dying of his wounds; the poem ends with his funeral pyre and the lament of his people. Rich in digressions on feuds and old kings, kennings and elegiac reflection, it shows a Christian poet looking back on a pagan heroic world. It was little studied until the nineteenth century and is now the foundational work of English literature, translated by poets including Seamus Heaney.
