## Biography

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born in Geneva in 1712; his mother died days after his birth, and he left the city at sixteen to begin a wandering life. He found a protector in Madame de Warens in Savoy, then went to Paris, where he worked as a music copyist, wrote an opera and contributed to the Encyclopédie. His Discourse on the Sciences and Arts (1750) made him famous with its argument that civilisation corrupts. His epistolary novel Julie, or the New Heloise (1761) was one of the best-selling books of the century, and Emile and The Social Contract followed in 1762. Both were condemned, and he spent years in flight and growing persecution mania, staying for a time in England with David Hume. He wrote his autobiographical Confessions and Reveries of the Solitary Walker in his last years and died near Paris in 1778.

Rousseau's influence on politics and education belongs to philosophy, but his literary impact was just as great. Julie fused a love story with descriptions of Alpine nature and moral reflection, and readers responded with tears and letters. The Confessions, which open by promising to show a man in all the truth of nature, founded modern autobiography. His cult of sincerity, sensibility and the natural world prepared the ground for Romanticism, and writers from Goethe and Wordsworth to Tolstoy acknowledged him as a liberator.

## Key works

### Julie, or the New Heloise (1761)

- **Date:** 1761
- **Type:** Epistolary novel
- **First published:** Amsterdam, published by Marc-Michel Rey
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Julie%2C%20or%20the%20New%20Heloise%20Jean-Jacques%20Rousseau)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Julie%2C+or+the+New+Heloise+Rousseau)

Julie, or the New Heloise takes its subtitle from the medieval lovers Abelard and Heloise. Told in letters, it begins in a village at the foot of the Alps on Lake Geneva, where the young tutor Saint-Preux and his aristocratic pupil Julie d'Étange fall in love and become lovers. Her father forbids the match on grounds of class, and Julie obeys him by marrying the older, rational, atheist Baron de Wolmar. Years later Wolmar invites Saint-Preux to live with them at their estate of Clarens, which Rousseau presents as a model community of virtue and natural order, until Julie dies after rescuing her child from the lake, her last letter confessing that she has never stopped loving Saint-Preux. With its intense feeling, moral reflection and descriptions of mountain scenery, the novel ran through dozens of editions and provoked a flood of letters from readers. It was a major source of the cult of sensibility and of Romantic attitudes to nature and love.
