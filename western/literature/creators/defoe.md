## Biography

Daniel Foe, who later added the aristocratic-sounding De to his name, was born in London around 1660, the son of a Dissenting tallow chandler. Educated at a Dissenting academy, he became a merchant in hosiery, wine and other goods, took part in Monmouth's rebellion of 1685 and went bankrupt more than once. He wrote hundreds of pamphlets on politics, trade and religion; one satire, The Shortest Way with the Dissenters (1702), led to his being pilloried and imprisoned. He then worked as a secret agent and propagandist for the government, notably in Scotland during the negotiations for the Union of 1707, and wrote almost single-handed a periodical, the Review. In 1719 he published Robinson Crusoe, followed by Moll Flanders, A Journal of the Plague Year and Roxana. He died in London in 1731.

Defoe's fiction presents itself as the true account of real people, told in plain, circumstantial prose full of practical detail, accounts and lists. This documentary realism, applied to the lives of ordinary or marginal people, a castaway, a thief, a courtesan, made him one of the founders of the English novel. His interest in trade, work and individual self-reliance has led critics to see his books as expressions of a new economic outlook. Robinson Crusoe has never been out of print and has been imitated so often that castaway stories are called Robinsonades.

## Key works

### Robinson Crusoe (1719)

- **Date:** 1719
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** London, published by William Taylor
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Robinson%20Crusoe%20Daniel%20Defoe)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Robinson+Crusoe+Defoe)

The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe was published in April 1719 as if it were the true autobiography of its hero, with no author's name on the title page. Crusoe, a young man from York, defies his father to go to sea, is captured by pirates, escapes, becomes a plantation owner in Brazil and, on a slave-trading voyage, is shipwrecked on an uninhabited island off the coast of South America. Salvaging tools and provisions from the wreck, he builds a shelter, keeps goats, grows grain, makes pottery and keeps a journal, while gradually finding religious faith. After many years he rescues a native man from cannibals, names him Friday and makes him his servant, and eventually he is rescued by an English ship. Partly inspired by the experiences of the castaway Alexander Selkirk, the novel's realistic detail made it a sensation. It has been read as a myth of economic individualism and, more recently, of colonialism, and it inspired a whole genre of castaway stories.
