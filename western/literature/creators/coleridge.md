## Biography

Samuel Taylor Coleridge was born in 1772 at Ottery St Mary in Devon, the youngest son of a vicar, and educated at Christ's Hospital in London and Jesus College, Cambridge, which he left without a degree after briefly enlisting in the dragoons under a false name. With Robert Southey he planned an ideal community in America that never materialised. In 1797 he settled near Wordsworth in Somerset, and their year of intense collaboration produced Lyrical Ballads, which opened with his Rime of the Ancient Mariner. He studied philosophy in Germany, moved to the Lake District, and struggled for most of his adult life with opium addiction and an unhappy marriage. From 1816 he lived in Highgate in the house of the physician James Gillman, where he became a famous talker and sage. He died there in 1834.

Coleridge's poetic output is small but includes some of the most haunting poems in English: the Ancient Mariner, Christabel, Kubla Khan and the conversation poems such as Frost at Midnight. As a critic he was equally important. His Biographia Literaria (1817) and lectures on Shakespeare introduced German Romantic philosophy to Britain, distinguished imagination from fancy, and gave the phrase the willing suspension of disbelief. He shaped how the nineteenth century thought about poetry, religion and the state.

### The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (1798)

[![The Rime of the Ancient Mariner](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bb/Dore-I_had_done_a_hellish_thing.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dore-I_had_done_a_hellish_thing.jpg)

<sub>Gustave Doré, illustration for The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, 1876 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dore-I_had_done_a_hellish_thing.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1798
- **Type:** Ballad
- **First published:** Bristol, in Lyrical Ballads; revised with marginal glosses in Sibylline Leaves, 1817
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Rime%20of%20the%20Ancient%20Mariner%20Samuel%20Taylor%20Coleridge)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Rime+of+the+Ancient+Mariner+Coleridge)

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere, as it was spelled in its first version, opened Lyrical Ballads in 1798; Coleridge modernised its language in later editions and in 1817 added a prose gloss in the margin, written in the voice of an antiquarian scholar. An old mariner stops a young man on his way to a wedding and holds him with his glittering eye while he tells his story. His ship was driven by storms towards the South Pole, where an albatross appeared and led it out of the ice, and the mariner shot it with his crossbow. The ship is becalmed under a burning sun, the crew die of thirst after a ghostly ship carrying Death and Life-in-Death passes, and the mariner is released from the curse only when he blesses the sea creatures in his heart. He must wander the earth telling his tale. Its vivid images and haunting music made it one of the best-known poems in English, and its phrases, such as an albatross around one's neck, became proverbial. Gustave Doré illustrated it in 1876.

### Kubla Khan (1797; published 1816)

[![Kubla Khan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/74/Kubla_Khan_titlepage.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kubla_Khan_titlepage.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the first edition, 1816 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kubla_Khan_titlepage.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1797; published 1816
- **Type:** Poem (fragment)
- **First published:** London, published by John Murray with Christabel and The Pains of Sleep
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kubla%20Khan%20Samuel%20Taylor%20Coleridge)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Kubla+Khan+Coleridge)

Kubla Khan, or A Vision in a Dream: A Fragment was published in 1816 at Byron's urging, nearly twenty years after its composition. In a preface Coleridge explained that in 1797, in a farmhouse near Porlock, he had fallen asleep after taking an anodyne, a reference to opium, while reading about the Mongol emperor Kublai Khan in Purchas's Pilgrimage, and composed two or three hundred lines in his sleep. On waking he began to write them down, but was interrupted by a person on business from Porlock, and when he returned the vision had vanished. The fifty-four lines describe the pleasure-dome of Xanadu, the sacred river Alph running through caverns measureless to man and an Abyssinian maid with a dulcimer, before turning to the poet who could rebuild the dome in song. Scholars debate how far the preface is literally true. The poem's incantatory music has made it one of the most famous of Romantic poems and a byword for inspiration.
