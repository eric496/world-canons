## Biography

Publius Ovidius Naso was born in 43 BC at Sulmo, east of Rome, into an equestrian family. He was trained for a legal and political career but abandoned it for poetry and quickly became the most fashionable poet of Augustan Rome with his love elegies, the Amores, the Heroides (verse letters from mythical heroines) and the mock-didactic Art of Love. Around AD 8, at the height of his fame and having just completed the Metamorphoses, he was banished by Augustus to Tomis on the Black Sea. The cause, which he called a poem and a mistake, has never been explained. He spent his last years writing poems of complaint and pleas for recall, and died in exile around AD 17.

Ovid's poetry is playful, ironic and endlessly inventive, delighting in rhetoric, paradox and psychological insight. In the Metamorphoses he linked some 250 myths of transformation into a continuous story running from the creation of the world to his own day. Medieval readers allegorised him, and for the Renaissance his works were the main storehouse of classical mythology: Shakespeare, Titian, Bernini and countless others drew on them. His tales of Pygmalion, Narcissus, Daphne and Orpheus remain the versions most people know.

## Key works

### Metamorphoses (AD 8)

- **Type:** Narrative poem
- **First published:** Rome (completed as Ovid went into exile)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Metamorphoses%20Ovid%20%28Publius%20Ovidius%20Naso%29)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Metamorphoses+Ovid)

The Metamorphoses is a poem of fifteen books in hexameter, which Ovid completed around AD 8, just as Augustus banished him to the Black Sea. It strings together roughly 250 myths linked by the theme of change of shape, in a continuous story running from the creation of the cosmos out of chaos through the age of gods and heroes and the Trojan War to the founding of Rome and the transformation of Julius Caesar into a star. Among its stories are Apollo and Daphne, Narcissus and Echo, Pyramus and Thisbe, Phaethon, Arachne, Daedalus and Icarus, Orpheus and Eurydice, and Pygmalion. Ovid moves from one tale to the next with brilliant transitions and tells them with wit, irony, eroticism and psychological insight. For medieval and Renaissance readers it was the main handbook of classical mythology, and its influence on Chaucer, Shakespeare, Titian, Bernini and countless later artists is incalculable.
