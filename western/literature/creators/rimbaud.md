## Biography

Jean Nicolas Arthur Rimbaud was born in 1854 at Charleville in the Ardennes and raised by a strict mother after his father, an army officer, left the family. A brilliant pupil, he was writing accomplished Latin and French verse at fifteen. He ran away to Paris several times during the Franco-Prussian War and the Commune. In 1871 he sent poems, including The Drunken Boat, to Paul Verlaine, who invited him to Paris. Their stormy relationship took them to London and Brussels and ended in 1873 when Verlaine shot Rimbaud in the wrist. That year Rimbaud published A Season in Hell. He then gave up poetry, travelled through Europe, enlisted in and deserted from the Dutch colonial army in Java, and worked as a trader in Aden and Harar, Ethiopia, dealing in coffee and guns. A tumour in his leg forced his return to France, where he died in Marseille in 1891 at thirty-seven.

Rimbaud believed that the poet must become a seer through a long, immense and reasoned derangement of all the senses. His poems move from brilliant traditional verse to the prose poems of A Season in Hell and the fragmentary visions of Illuminations, published by Verlaine in 1886. His rebellion, his free verse and his hallucinatory images made him a hero to the Symbolists, the Surrealists, the Beats and rock musicians, and he remains a byword for youthful genius.

## Key works

### A Season in Hell (1873)

- **Date:** 1873
- **Type:** Prose poem
- **First published:** Brussels, printed by the Alliance typographique (at Rimbaud's expense)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Season%20in%20Hell%20Arthur%20Rimbaud)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=A+Season+in+Hell+Rimbaud)

Une saison en enfer was written in 1873, partly at the family farm at Roche and partly in the aftermath of the shooting in Brussels that ended Rimbaud's relationship with Verlaine. It was printed in Brussels that October at his own expense, though he collected only a few copies and left the rest unpaid at the printer, where they were found in 1901. It is the only work Rimbaud himself published as a book. In a series of prose poems with interspersed verse, a speaker who calls himself damned recounts his revolt against Christianity, Western civilisation and his own bourgeois heritage, his imagined descent into savagery, and his visionary experiments with language in the section Alchemy of the Word. Delirium I, The Foolish Virgin and the Infernal Bridegroom, is often read as an account of his relationship with Verlaine. The work ends with a farewell and the declaration that one must be absolutely modern. Its fusion of confession, prophecy and fragmentary form had an immense influence on Symbolism and Surrealism.
