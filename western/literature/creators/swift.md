## Biography

Jonathan Swift was born in Dublin in 1667 to English parents, his father having died before his birth. He was educated at Trinity College Dublin and became secretary to the statesman Sir William Temple in England, where he met Esther Johnson, the Stella of his later letters. Ordained in the Church of Ireland, he made his name with A Tale of a Tub (1704), a satire on religious excess. In London he became a leading writer for the Tory government of 1710–14 and a friend of Pope, Gay and Arbuthnot in the Scriblerus Club. On the fall of the Tories he became Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral in Dublin, where he spent the rest of his life and became a hero to the Irish for his attacks on English policy, notably in the Drapier's Letters. Gulliver's Travels appeared in 1726. In his last years he suffered severe illness, and he died in 1745.

Swift's satire works through a deadpan persona, an earnest narrator who calmly reports or proposes absurdities, leaving the reader to recognise the horror. His targets were pride, cruelty, corruption, scientific and religious folly and the oppression of Ireland. His prose is plain, exact and devastating. Swift left money to found a hospital for the mentally ill, and his own epitaph, which he composed in Latin, speaks of the savage indignation that tore his heart. Gulliver's Travels has become both a classic of satire and, in shortened form, a children's book.

- **Date:** 1726
- **Type:** Satirical novel
- **First published:** London, published by Benjamin Motte
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gulliver%27s%20Travels%20Jonathan%20Swift)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Gulliver%27s+Travels+Swift)

Travels into Several Remote Nations of the World, by Lemuel Gulliver was published anonymously in 1726, with passages softened by the publisher for fear of prosecution; a corrected edition appeared in 1735. In four voyages the ship's surgeon Gulliver is shipwrecked in Lilliput, where people are six inches tall and wage war over which end to break an egg; abandoned in Brobdingnag, where giants find him a curiosity and the king judges Europeans a pernicious race of odious vermin; visits the flying island of Laputa and its absurd scientists; and finally reaches the land of the Houyhnhnms, wise horses who rule over brutish human creatures called Yahoos. Gulliver returns home unable to bear the company of his own family. Swift satirised politics, science, war and human pride with deadpan precision. Though shortened versions of the first two voyages became children's classics, the full book is one of the darkest and most influential satires in literature.

### A Modest Proposal (1729)

[![A Modest Proposal](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ee/A_Modest_Proposal_1729_Cover.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:A_Modest_Proposal_1729_Cover.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the 1729 pamphlet Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:A_Modest_Proposal_1729_Cover.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1729
- **Type:** Satirical essay
- **First published:** Dublin, published as a pamphlet
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Modest%20Proposal%20Jonathan%20Swift)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=A+Modest+Proposal+Swift)

A Modest Proposal for Preventing the Children of Poor People from Being a Burthen to Their Parents or Country was published anonymously in Dublin in 1729, after years of famine and English economic restrictions on Ireland. In the reasonable, statistical voice of a public-spirited economist, the author observes the misery of beggar women and their children and proposes that poor Irish parents should sell their year-old infants to be eaten by the wealthy, calculating the costs and benefits and suggesting recipes. Only near the end does he dismiss, as impractical, the real remedies, such as taxing absentee landlords and buying Irish goods, that Swift had urged elsewhere. The essay attacks the dehumanising language of political economy, English exploitation and Irish landlords, and it is the most famous example of sustained irony in English. A modest proposal has become a standard phrase for satirical suggestions.
