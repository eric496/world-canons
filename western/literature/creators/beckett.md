# Samuel Beckett

**1906 – 1989** · Irish  
Born: Foxrock, Dublin · Died: Paris  
Movements & eras: [Existentialism & the Absurd](../movements/existentialism-absurd.md), [Modernism](../movements/modernism-literature.md)  

> Irish playwright and novelist, writing in English and French, whose Waiting for Godot is the landmark of the Theatre of the Absurd. Nobel Prize 1969.

## Biography

Samuel Barclay Beckett was born in 1906 in Foxrock, near Dublin, into a Protestant middle-class family. He studied French and Italian at Trinity College Dublin and in 1928 went to Paris as a lecturer, where he became a friend and assistant of James Joyce. He published essays, stories and the novel Murphy (1938), and settled in Paris permanently. During the Second World War he joined the French Resistance and later hid in the south of France. After the war, writing mainly in French, he produced his most important work: the novels Molloy, Malone Dies and The Unnamable, and the play Waiting for Godot, which premiered in Paris in 1953 and became an international sensation. Endgame, Krapp's Last Tape and Happy Days followed. He received the Nobel Prize in 1969 and died in Paris in 1989.

Beckett pared drama and fiction down to their bare essentials: characters waiting, talking, failing and going on in empty spaces, with bleak humour drawn from music hall and silent film. His work deals with time, memory, habit and the difficulty of meaning, and became increasingly minimal in late plays such as Not I. He translated most of his own works between French and English, and he is regarded as one of the most important dramatists of the twentieth century.

## Key works

### Waiting for Godot (1952 (French); premiered 1953)

- **Date:** 1952 (French); premiered 1953
- **Type:** Play
- **First published:** Théâtre de Babylone, Paris (5 January 1953)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Waiting%20for%20Godot%20Samuel%20Beckett)

Waiting for Godot was written in French as En attendant Godot in 1948–49, published in 1952 and premiered at the small Théâtre de Babylone in Paris in January 1953; Beckett's own English translation was first staged in London in 1955. On a country road by a single tree, two tramps, Vladimir and Estragon, wait for a man called Godot, whom they have apparently arranged to meet. To pass the time they talk, quarrel, eat a carrot, consider hanging themselves and play verbal games. They are visited by the tyrannical Pozzo and his slave Lucky, whom he drives on a rope, and who delivers a torrent of garbled philosophy when ordered to think. At the end of each act a boy arrives to say that Godot will not come today but surely tomorrow. The second act repeats the first with variations, and the play ends with the two agreeing to go, and not moving. One critic famously described it as a play in which nothing happens, twice. Its mixture of vaudeville, bleak humour and metaphysical anguish made it the defining work of the Theatre of the Absurd.
