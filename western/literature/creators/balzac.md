## Biography

Honoré Balzac, who added the aristocratic de himself, was born in Tours in 1799 and sent to a harsh boarding school, then trained in law in Paris. Determined to be a writer, he produced potboiler novels under pseudonyms and lost heavily in ventures as a publisher and printer, leaving debts that pursued him all his life. His first success under his own name was The Chouans (1829), and over the next twenty years he wrote at a ferocious pace, often through the night fuelled by coffee. In 1842 he gathered his novels under the title La Comédie humaine. He conducted a long correspondence and affair with the Polish countess Ewelina Hańska, whom he finally married in 1850, a few months before his death in Paris.

Balzac's innovation was to link his novels through recurring characters, so that bankers, journalists, courtesans, criminals and provincial families reappear across books and form a vast fictional society. He described rooms, money, clothes and ambitions in minute detail, convinced that environment shapes people as it shapes animal species. His masterpieces include Eugénie Grandet, Père Goriot, Lost Illusions and Cousin Bette. Marx and Engels said they learned more about French society from him than from the historians, and novelists from Zola to Proust learned from his method.

## Key works

### Père Goriot (1835)

- **Date:** 1835
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Paris, serialised in the Revue de Paris (1834–35), then published by Werdet
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=P%C3%A8re%20Goriot%20Honor%C3%A9%20de%20Balzac)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=P%C3%A8re+Goriot+Balzac)

Le Père Goriot was serialised in 1834–35 and published as a book in 1835. It is set in 1819 in the Maison Vauquer, a dingy boarding house in the Latin Quarter that Balzac describes in famous detail. Among its residents are old Goriot, a retired vermicelli maker who has given his fortune to his two daughters, now married into high society and ashamed of him; Eugène de Rastignac, an ambitious law student from the provinces; and the mysterious Vautrin, who offers Rastignac a criminal shortcut to wealth and is later revealed as an escaped convict. Rastignac becomes the lover of one of Goriot's daughters and witnesses how both daughters exploit their father, who dies alone and destitute, attended only by Rastignac. At the end Rastignac looks down over Paris from the cemetery of Père Lachaise and challenges the city. This was the first novel in which Balzac systematically used recurring characters, and it became a cornerstone of La Comédie humaine. Rastignac's name became a byword for an ambitious young man.
