# T. S. Eliot

**1888 – 1965** · American-British  
Born: St. Louis, Missouri · Died: London  
Movements & eras: [Modernism](../movements/modernism-literature.md)  

> Poet, critic and dramatist whose The Waste Land defined Modernist poetry. He won the Nobel Prize in 1948.

## Biography

Thomas Stearns Eliot was born in 1888 in St Louis, Missouri, into a distinguished New England family. He studied philosophy at Harvard, the Sorbonne and Oxford and in 1914 met Ezra Pound in London, who arranged the publication of The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock in 1915. That year he married Vivienne Haigh-Wood; the marriage was deeply unhappy. He worked as a teacher and for eight years in the foreign department of Lloyds Bank, while writing criticism and editing the journal The Criterion. The Waste Land (1922), edited by Pound, made him the most influential poet of his generation. In 1925 he joined the publisher Faber and Gwyer, later Faber and Faber, where he remained for life. In 1927 he became a British subject and was received into the Church of England. He wrote Four Quartets and verse plays such as Murder in the Cathedral, received the Nobel Prize in 1948 and died in London in 1965.

Eliot's early poems use fragmentation, allusion, multiple voices and the sordid imagery of the modern city to express spiritual emptiness, while his later work, culminating in Four Quartets, is meditative and religious. His essays, such as Tradition and the Individual Talent, shaped literary criticism for decades. Admired as the leading poet of Modernism, he has also been criticised for antisemitic passages in some early poems. His light verse Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats became the musical Cats.

### The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock (1915)

- **Date:** 1915
- **Type:** Poem
- **First published:** Poetry magazine, Chicago (June 1915); collected in Prufrock and Other Observations, London, 1917
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Love%20Song%20of%20J.%20Alfred%20Prufrock%20T.%20S.%20Eliot)

The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock was written mostly in 1910–11, while Eliot was a student, and published in Chicago's Poetry magazine in 1915 after Ezra Pound pressed its editor, Harriet Monroe, to accept it. It became the title poem of Eliot's first collection in 1917. Prufrock, a self-conscious, middle-aged man, invites an unnamed companion to go with him through half-deserted streets to a drawing-room where the women come and go talking of Michelangelo. He circles around an overwhelming question, probably a declaration of love, but is paralysed by self-doubt, measuring out his life with coffee spoons, imagining the mockery of others, and ending with a vision of mermaids who will not sing to him. Its epigraph from Dante, ironic juxtapositions, abrupt shifts and urban imagery, influenced by the French Symbolist Jules Laforgue, marked a radical break with Victorian and Georgian poetry. It is often regarded as the beginning of Modernist poetry in English.

### The Waste Land (1922)

- **Date:** 1922
- **Type:** Poem
- **First published:** The Criterion, London (October 1922) and The Dial, New York (November 1922); book by Boni & Liveright, December 1922
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Waste%20Land%20T.%20S.%20Eliot)

The Waste Land was published in 1922, the same year as Ulysses, first in Eliot's own magazine The Criterion and in the American Dial, and then as a book with Eliot's notes. Much of it was written in 1921 while Eliot was recovering from a breakdown in Margate and Lausanne, and Ezra Pound's drastic editing of the manuscript, which survived and was published in 1971, cut it nearly in half; Eliot dedicated it to Pound as the better craftsman. In five sections, The Burial of the Dead, A Game of Chess, The Fire Sermon, Death by Water and What the Thunder Said, its 434 lines move abruptly between voices, languages and scenes: a crowd flowing over London Bridge, a neurotic couple, a pub conversation, a typist's loveless encounter, and a journey through a dry landscape. It draws on the Grail legend, fertility myth, Dante, Shakespeare, the Upanishads and dozens of other sources. Its fragmentation and allusiveness defined Modernist poetry and influenced generations of writers.
