## Biography

Michel Eyquem de Montaigne was born in 1533 at his family's château near Bordeaux, into a wealthy merchant family recently ennobled. His father had him taught Latin as his first language. He studied law and served for thirteen years as a magistrate in the Bordeaux parlement, where he formed an intense friendship with the writer Étienne de La Boétie, whose early death in 1563 marked him deeply. In 1571 he retired to his tower library to read and write, and in 1580 published the first two books of his Essays. He travelled through Germany and Italy, served two terms as mayor of Bordeaux during the Wars of Religion, and kept revising and enlarging the Essays until his death in 1592.

Montaigne called his pieces essais, attempts or trials, and they range freely over subjects from cannibals and friendship to thumbs, cruelty and death, always returning to himself as the one subject he could know. He wrote in a conversational, digressive French, quoting the ancients constantly while doubting all certainty; his motto was What do I know? His sceptical, tolerant humanism influenced Shakespeare, who borrowed from John Florio's 1603 translation, as well as Bacon, Pascal, Descartes and Emerson. The personal essay as a literary form descends from him.

## Key works

### Essays (1580–1595)

- **Date:** 1580–1595
- **Type:** Essays
- **First published:** Bordeaux (first edition, two books, 1580); Paris (1588; posthumous edition 1595)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Essays%20Michel%20de%20Montaigne)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Essays+Montaigne)

Montaigne published the first two books of his Essais in Bordeaux in 1580, and an enlarged edition with a third book in Paris in 1588; he continued to annotate his own copy until his death, and the posthumous edition of 1595 incorporated many additions. The 107 chapters vary from a page to a hundred pages and range over subjects such as friendship, education, cannibals, cruelty, the power of the imagination, experience and death. Montaigne writes in a candid, conversational and digressive style, constantly quoting ancient authors and testing their ideas against his own experience, habits and contradictions, because, as he says in his preface, he himself is the matter of his book. His scepticism and tolerance, at the height of the French Wars of Religion, were radical. The Essays invented a literary form and profoundly influenced Shakespeare, Bacon, Pascal, Rousseau, Emerson and Nietzsche, and they remain a model for personal writing.
