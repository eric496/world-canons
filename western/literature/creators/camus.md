# Albert Camus

**1913 – 1960** · French (Algerian-born)  
Born: Mondovi (Dréan), French Algeria · Died: Villeblevin, France  
Movements & eras: [Existentialism & the Absurd](../movements/existentialism-absurd.md)  

> The French-Algerian novelist and essayist of The Stranger, The Plague and The Myth of Sisyphus, winner of the 1957 Nobel Prize.

## Biography

Albert Camus was born in 1913 in Mondovi in French Algeria. His father was killed in the First World War, and he grew up in poverty in Algiers with his nearly deaf, illiterate mother. A scholarship took him to the lycée and the University of Algiers, where he studied philosophy, played football and contracted tuberculosis. He worked as a journalist and ran a theatre company. In 1942, in occupied France, he published The Stranger and the essay The Myth of Sisyphus, and he became editor of the Resistance newspaper Combat. The Plague (1947) and the essay The Rebel (1951), which led to a break with Sartre, followed. He was troubled by the Algerian War, condemning both terrorism and colonial repression. He received the Nobel Prize in 1957, aged forty-four, and died in a car accident in 1960.

Camus explored the absurd, the conflict between the human need for meaning and the silent universe, and argued for lucid revolt, solidarity and moderation rather than despair or ideology. He rejected the label existentialist, though he is often grouped with Sartre. His clear, classical style and moral seriousness made him one of the most widely read French writers of the twentieth century.

### The Stranger (1942)

- **Date:** 1942
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Paris, published by Gallimard
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Stranger%20Albert%20Camus)

The Stranger (L'Étranger, also translated as The Outsider) was published by Gallimard in occupied Paris in 1942, the same year as Camus's essay The Myth of Sisyphus. It opens with the flat statement that Mother died today, or maybe yesterday. Meursault, a shipping clerk in Algiers, attends his mother's funeral without showing emotion, begins an affair the next day, and becomes involved in the quarrels of his neighbour Raymond. On a blindingly hot beach, he shoots an Arab man who had fought with Raymond, firing four more shots into the body for no reason he can explain. At his trial the prosecution concentrates on his indifference at the funeral, and he is sentenced to death. In his cell, after angrily rejecting a chaplain's offer of faith, he opens himself to the benign indifference of the universe. Written in short, plain sentences, the novel became a classic of absurdist literature. Kamel Daoud's novel The Meursault Investigation (2013) retold it from the perspective of the victim's brother.

### The Plague (1947)

- **Date:** 1947
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** Paris, published by Gallimard
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Plague%20Albert%20Camus)

The Plague (La Peste) was published in 1947 and was an immediate success. It is presented as a chronicle of an epidemic of bubonic plague in the Algerian coastal city of Oran in the 1940s, narrated by someone who turns out to be Dr Bernard Rieux. Rats die in the streets, then people, and the authorities reluctantly close the city. Cut off from the world, the inhabitants respond in different ways: Rieux simply does his job of treating the sick; the visitor Tarrou organises volunteer sanitary teams; the journalist Rambert tries to escape to his lover in Paris but stays to help; the priest Paneloux preaches that the plague is God's punishment, until the agonising death of a child shakes him; and the petty criminal Cottard thrives on the crisis. After almost a year the plague recedes, but Rieux warns that the bacillus never dies. The novel is widely read as an allegory of the German occupation of France and of the human response to evil and suffering, and it found new readers during the COVID-19 pandemic.
