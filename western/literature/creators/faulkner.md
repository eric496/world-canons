# William Faulkner

**1897 – 1962** · American  
Born: New Albany, Mississippi · Died: Byhalia, Mississippi  
Movements & eras: [Lost Generation & American modernism](../movements/lost-generation.md), [Modernism](../movements/modernism-literature.md)  

> The novelist of the American South, whose fictional Yoknapatawpha County holds The Sound and the Fury, As I Lay Dying and Absalom, Absalom!

## Biography

William Cuthbert Falkner, who later added the u, was born in 1897 in New Albany, Mississippi, and grew up in Oxford, Mississippi, the town where he spent most of his life. He trained with the Royal Air Force in Canada in 1918 but the war ended before he saw action. He attended the University of Mississippi briefly, worked odd jobs, including as postmaster, and was encouraged by Sherwood Anderson to write about his native region. In 1929 he published Sartoris and The Sound and the Fury, beginning his saga of Yoknapatawpha County, followed by As I Lay Dying, Light in August, Absalom, Absalom! and Go Down, Moses. To earn money he wrote screenplays in Hollywood, including for The Big Sleep. His reputation, low in the late 1930s, revived in the 1940s, and he received the Nobel Prize in 1949. He died in 1962.

Faulkner used stream of consciousness, multiple narrators, fractured chronology and long, rhetorical sentences to explore the burden of history, race, family and memory in the South after slavery and the Civil War. His idea that the past is never dead runs through all his work. He influenced writers across the Americas, notably García Márquez and the Latin American Boom.

## Key works

### The Sound and the Fury (1929)

- **Date:** 1929
- **Type:** Novel
- **First published:** New York, published by Jonathan Cape and Harrison Smith
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Sound%20and%20the%20Fury%20William%20Faulkner)

The Sound and the Fury was published in October 1929; its title comes from Macbeth's speech about life as a tale told by an idiot. It tells the story of the Compsons, an old Southern family in decay in Jefferson, Mississippi, in four sections. The first is narrated by Benjy, a thirty-three-year-old man with a severe cognitive disability, whose mind moves without distinction between past and present, on 7 April 1928. The second follows his brother Quentin, a Harvard student, on 2 June 1910, the day of his suicide, obsessed with their sister Caddy's lost virginity. The third is told by the bitter, scheming brother Jason in 1928, and the fourth, in the third person, centres on Dilsey, the family's Black servant, on Easter Sunday 1928, as Caddy's daughter Quentin runs away with Jason's money. The novel's stream of consciousness, fractured chronology and density made it difficult for its first readers, but it is now considered one of the greatest American novels.
