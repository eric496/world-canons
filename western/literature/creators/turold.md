# Turold

**fl. c. 1100** · French (Norman)  
Born: Normandy (probably) · Died:

## Biography

The oldest and finest manuscript of The Song of Roland, copied in England in the twelfth century and now in the Bodleian Library, Oxford, ends with a line saying that here ends the geste which Turoldus declines, an ambiguous verb that may mean composes, recites, transcribes or completes. On this basis the poem is traditionally attributed to a Norman named Turold. Attempts to identify him with real people of the period, such as a bishop of Bayeux or a monk of Fécamp, are speculative. Most scholars date the Oxford version to around 1100, but the poem drew on a much older oral tradition about the defeat of Charlemagne's rearguard at Roncevaux in 778.

Whoever Turold was, the Oxford Roland shows a powerful poetic sensibility: terse, repetitive laisses (stanzas) linked by assonance, sharply drawn scenes, and a stark moral world of loyalty, pride and sacrifice. It is the masterpiece of the chanson de geste, the Old French heroic song, and a founding text of French literature. The figure of Roland passed into Italian romance epic through Boiardo and Ariosto and remained a symbol of heroic loyalty for centuries.

## Key works

### The Song of Roland (c. 1100)

- **Date:** c. 1100
- **Type:** Chanson de geste (Old French epic)
- **First published:** Oldest manuscript: Bodleian Library, Oxford (Digby 23), 12th century
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Song%20of%20Roland%20Turold)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Song+of+Roland+Turold)

The Song of Roland is the oldest major work of French literature, a poem of about 4,000 ten-syllable lines in stanzas linked by assonance. It transforms a minor historical event, the ambush of Charlemagne's rearguard by Basques in the Pyrenees in 778, into a crusading epic of Christians against Saracens. The traitor Ganelon, out of hatred for his stepson Roland, arranges for the rearguard to be attacked at the pass of Roncevaux by an immense Muslim army. Roland, proud and brave, refuses his friend Oliver's pleas to blow his horn, the oliphant, to recall the emperor, and when he finally does, it is too late; he dies facing Spain, offering his glove to God. Charlemagne returns to avenge him, defeats the Saracens and has Ganelon executed. The poem is written in a stark, formal style with repeated scenes and phrases. It shaped ideals of chivalry and became the basis of Italian romance epics by Boiardo and Ariosto.
