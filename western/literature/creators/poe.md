## Biography

Edgar Poe was born in Boston in 1809 to travelling actors; his father deserted the family and his mother died when he was two. He was taken in by John Allan, a Richmond merchant, whose name he added to his own, though he was never formally adopted. He studied briefly at the University of Virginia, enlisted in the army and was expelled from West Point. He published his first poems in 1827 and spent the rest of his life as a struggling magazine editor and critic in Richmond, Philadelphia and New York. In 1836 he married his thirteen-year-old cousin Virginia Clemm, who died of tuberculosis in 1847. The Raven (1845) made him famous but not prosperous. He died in Baltimore in 1849 in mysterious circumstances, having been found delirious in the street.

Poe believed that a poem or tale should produce a single unified effect, and his stories of terror, such as The Fall of the House of Usher, The Tell-Tale Heart and The Cask of Amontillado, are tightly built to do so. In The Murders in the Rue Morgue (1841) he created the first fictional detective, C. Auguste Dupin, and he also pioneered science fiction. Baudelaire translated him and Mallarmé and Valéry revered him, so that Poe's influence on French poetry was greater than in his own country. Through Conan Doyle, Lovecraft and horror film he remains one of the most popular American writers.

### The Fall of the House of Usher (1839)

[![The Fall of the House of Usher](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f7/House-of-Usher-1839.jpg/960px-House-of-Usher-1839.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:House-of-Usher-1839.jpg)

- **Date:** 1839
- **Type:** Short story
- **First published:** Burton's Gentleman's Magazine, Philadelphia (September 1839)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Fall%20of%20the%20House%20of%20Usher%20Edgar%20Allan%20Poe)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Fall+of+the+House+of+Usher+Poe)

The Fall of the House of Usher was published in Burton's Gentleman's Magazine in 1839 and collected in Tales of the Grotesque and Arabesque the following year. The unnamed narrator is summoned to the decaying mansion of his boyhood friend Roderick Usher, which stands above a dark tarn and has a barely visible crack running down its front. Roderick, the last of his line, suffers from a morbid acuteness of the senses and dread, while his twin sister Madeline wastes away from a mysterious illness. When Madeline apparently dies, the two men entomb her in a vault beneath the house. During a storm days later, as the narrator reads aloud to calm Roderick, sounds from the story seem to echo through the house, and Madeline appears at the door, bloodied, having been buried alive; she falls on her brother and both die. The narrator flees as the house splits along its crack and sinks into the tarn. The tale is the classic example of Poe's theory of a single unified effect and a cornerstone of Gothic fiction.

### The Raven (1845)

[![The Raven](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Raven_Manet_C2.jpg/960px-Raven_Manet_C2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Raven_Manet_C2.jpg)

<sub>Édouard Manet, illustration for Mallarmé's translation of The Raven, 1875 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Raven_Manet_C2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1845
- **Type:** Narrative poem
- **First published:** New York, Evening Mirror (29 January 1845)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Raven%20Edgar%20Allan%20Poe)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Raven+Poe)

The Raven was published in the New York Evening Mirror in January 1845 and was immediately reprinted across the United States, making Poe famous. On a bleak December midnight, a lonely scholar reads old books to forget his lost Lenore. A tapping at his window admits a raven, which perches on a bust of Pallas Athena above his door and answers his questions with one word, Nevermore. Amused at first, the narrator grows increasingly frantic as he asks whether there is comfort for his grief and whether he will be reunited with Lenore in heaven, and the poem ends with his soul trapped in the raven's shadow. Poe wrote a famous essay, The Philosophy of Composition (1846), claiming to have constructed the poem methodically to produce a single effect of melancholy, though how seriously he meant it is debated. Its incantatory trochaic rhythm, internal rhymes and refrain have made it one of the most famous American poems. Mallarmé translated it, and Manet and Doré illustrated it.
