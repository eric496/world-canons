## Biography

Aristophanes was born in Athens around 446 BC and had his first play staged in 427 BC, while still very young. He wrote about forty comedies over a career of some forty years, of which eleven survive, among them The Clouds, The Wasps, The Birds, Lysistrata and The Frogs. His work spans the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta and its aftermath. He clashed with the powerful politician Cleon, whom he mocked repeatedly, and in The Clouds he lampooned Socrates as a fraudulent teacher, a portrait Plato later suggested contributed to prejudice against the philosopher. Plato nevertheless gave him a memorable speech about love in the Symposium.

His plays belong to what is called Old Comedy: fantastical plots in which an ordinary citizen makes a private peace with the enemy, founds a city in the clouds or travels to the underworld, combined with personal attacks on living Athenians, choral songs of real lyrical beauty and frank sexual humour. In the parabasis the chorus stepped out to address the audience directly on the poet's behalf. Aristophanes is our main source for what Old Comedy was like, and his mixture of satire and fantasy has influenced writers from Rabelais to modern political satirists.

### Lysistrata (411 BC)

[![Lysistrata](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Lysistrata.jpg/960px-Lysistrata.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lysistrata.jpg)

<sub>Illustration for Lysistrata Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lysistrata.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 411 BC
- **Type:** Comedy
- **First published:** First performed in Athens, probably at the Lenaia festival
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lysistrata%20Aristophanes)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Lysistrata+Aristophanes)

Lysistrata was staged in 411 BC, during the long war between Athens and Sparta and shortly after Athens's disastrous expedition to Sicily. The Athenian woman Lysistrata persuades the women of the warring Greek cities to refuse sex to their husbands until they make peace, while the older women seize the Acropolis and its treasury. A chorus of old men tries to smoke them out and is drenched with water by a chorus of old women. The strike is hard on both sides, with much bawdy comedy as wives slip away and husbands suffer, but in the end the desperate Athenian and Spartan envoys agree to peace, and the play ends in celebration. Behind the obscene farce lies a serious plea for reconciliation. Lysistrata has been one of the most frequently revived Greek comedies in modern times, often staged as an anti-war play.

### The Frogs (405 BC)

[![The Frogs](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Xanthias_Heracles_BM_VaseF233.jpg/960px-Xanthias_Heracles_BM_VaseF233.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xanthias_Heracles_BM_VaseF233.jpg)

<sub>Xanthias and Heracles, South Italian vase, 4th century BC, British Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xanthias_Heracles_BM_VaseF233.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 405 BC
- **Type:** Comedy
- **First published:** First performed at the Lenaia festival, Athens, where it won first prize
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Frogs%20Aristophanes)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=The+Frogs+Aristophanes)

The Frogs was staged in 405 BC, shortly after the deaths of Euripides and Sophocles and in the last desperate year of Athens's war with Sparta. Dionysus, patron of the theatre, laments that there are no good poets left and sets off to the underworld, disguised as Heracles and accompanied by his slave Xanthias, to bring Euripides back. Crossing the lake of the dead, he is mocked by a chorus of croaking frogs. In Hades he finds a contest under way between Aeschylus and Euripides for the throne of tragedy, and he presides over a hilarious debate in which they parody each other's verses and weigh them on a scale. In the end Dionysus chooses Aeschylus as the poet who can save the city. The play is one of the earliest works of literary criticism, a precious source for how Athenians thought about tragedy, and it was so admired that it was given a second performance.
