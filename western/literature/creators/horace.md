## Biography

Quintus Horatius Flaccus was born in 65 BC at Venusia in southern Italy, the son of a freed slave who spent his savings educating him in Rome and Athens. In Athens he joined the republican army of Brutus and fought on the losing side at Philippi in 42 BC. Returning to Rome with his property confiscated, he worked as a treasury clerk and began writing poetry. Virgil introduced him to Maecenas, who became his patron and gave him a farm in the Sabine hills that he celebrated for the rest of his life. He became the leading lyric poet of Augustus's Rome, writing the official hymn for the Secular Games of 17 BC, and died in 8 BC, a few weeks after Maecenas.

Horace's works include the Satires, the Epodes, four books of Odes, two books of Epistles and the verse letter known as the Art of Poetry. He adapted Greek lyric metres to Latin with matchless skill and wrote with urbane irony about friendship, wine, love, the brevity of life and the pleasures of a moderate existence. His Art of Poetry was one of the most influential works of literary criticism ever written, setting the rules of decorum for Renaissance and neoclassical writers. Poets from Petrarch and Ben Jonson to Pope and Auden have imitated him, and many of his phrases became proverbial.

## Key works

### Odes (Carmina) (23 BC (Books 1–3); c. 13 BC (Book 4))

- **Date:** 23 BC (Books 1–3); c. 13 BC (Book 4)
- **Type:** Lyric poetry
- **First published:** Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Odes%20%28Carmina%29%20Horace%20%28Quintus%20Horatius%20Flaccus%29)
- **Links:** [Free text on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/search/?query=Odes+Horace)

Horace published three books of Odes in 23 BC and added a fourth around 13 BC. In them he set out to do in Latin what Sappho, Alcaeus and Pindar had done in Greek, adapting their complex metres with unmatched skill. The poems are addressed to friends, lovers, patrons, gods and the emperor, and range from light invitations to drinking parties and love affairs to grand public odes on Rome's moral renewal under Augustus. Their recurrent theme is the brevity of life and the wisdom of enjoying the present moderately, crystallised in the phrase carpe diem, seize the day, from Ode 1.11. Horace ends Book 3 with the boast that he has built a monument more lasting than bronze. The Odes were imitated by Petrarch, Ronsard, Ben Jonson, Marvell and Pope and translated countless times, and they became the model for the classical ode in European poetry.
