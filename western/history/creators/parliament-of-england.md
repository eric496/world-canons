# Parliament of England

**13th century – 1707** · English  
Born: Westminster · Died: Westminster  
Traditions & eras: [Early-modern political history & documents](../movements/early-modern-documents.md)  

> The English Parliament, whose seventeenth-century struggles with the Stuart kings produced the Petition of Right, the Habeas Corpus Act and the Bill of Rights.

## Biography

The Parliament of England grew in the thirteenth century from royal councils into a body of Lords and Commons whose consent was needed for taxation. In the seventeenth century it clashed repeatedly with the Stuart kings over taxation, religion and the royal prerogative: the conflict led to the Civil War, the execution of Charles I in 1649 and the Restoration of 1660. In 1688 leading politicians invited William of Orange to invade, and James II fled. The Convention Parliament of 1689 offered the throne to William and Mary on terms set out in a Declaration of Right.

Parliamentary lawyers such as Sir Edward Coke and John Selden drew on Magna Carta and the common law to argue for the ancient liberties of the subject. Their statutes and declarations became foundations of constitutional government in Britain and influenced the American colonists, who claimed the same rights as Englishmen. The Parliament of England was merged into that of Great Britain by the Acts of Union of 1707.

### Petition of Right (1628)

- **Date:** 1628
- **Type:** Parliamentary petition / statute
- **Written / held:** Parliamentary Archives, Westminster
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Petition%20of%20Right%20Parliament%20of%20England)

In 1628 Parliament, led by Sir Edward Coke, Sir John Eliot and others, responded to Charles I's forced loans, arbitrary imprisonment, billeting of soldiers on civilians and use of martial law by drafting a petition reasserting the ancient liberties of the subject, citing Magna Carta and earlier statutes. It declared that no one should be compelled to pay any tax without the consent of Parliament and that no free man should be imprisoned without cause shown. The king reluctantly gave his assent in June 1628 in return for subsidies, but soon disregarded it and ruled without Parliament from 1629 to 1640. The petition is one of the foundational documents of English constitutional law and influenced the American Bill of Rights.

### Habeas Corpus Act 1679 (1679)

- **Date:** 1679
- **Type:** Act of Parliament
- **Written / held:** Parliamentary Archives, Westminster
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Habeas%20Corpus%20Act%201679%20Parliament%20of%20England)

The writ of habeas corpus, requiring a jailer to bring a prisoner before a court and justify the detention, had existed in English common law for centuries, but the Crown had found ways to evade it. The Habeas Corpus Act, passed in 1679 under Charles II during the political crisis over the succession, set strict time limits for producing prisoners, imposed penalties on judges and jailers who refused the writ, and forbade sending prisoners overseas to escape the courts' jurisdiction. According to a famous story, it passed the House of Lords only because a fat peer was jokingly counted as ten votes. It became a model for protections against arbitrary detention, and the United States Constitution guarantees the privilege of the writ except in cases of rebellion or invasion.

### Bill of Rights 1689 (1689)

- **Date:** 1689
- **Type:** Act of Parliament
- **Written / held:** Parliamentary Archives, Westminster
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bill%20of%20Rights%201689%20Parliament%20of%20England)

After James II fled in December 1688 and William of Orange took control, the Convention Parliament drew up a Declaration of Right, read to William and his wife Mary before they accepted the crown in February 1689, and enacted it as the Bill of Rights in December. It listed James's offences and declared illegal the suspending of laws, the levying of taxes and keeping of a standing army in peacetime without Parliament's consent, and excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishments. It asserted free elections, freedom of speech in Parliament, the right to petition the monarch and the right of Protestants to bear arms, and it barred Catholics from the throne. It remains part of the British constitution and was a direct model for parts of the American Bill of Rights, though its protections were for a Protestant political nation rather than universal.
