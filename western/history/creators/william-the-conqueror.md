## Biography

William was born around 1028 at Falaise, the illegitimate son of Duke Robert I of Normandy, and became duke as a child. He secured his duchy through years of warfare. Claiming that Edward the Confessor had promised him the English throne, he invaded England in 1066 and defeated King Harold at Hastings, being crowned on Christmas Day. He suppressed English revolts harshly, most notoriously in the 'Harrying of the North', and replaced the English aristocracy with Normans. He died in 1087 at Rouen after being injured on campaign.

At Christmas 1085, at Gloucester, William ordered a survey of the landholdings and resources of his kingdom, compiled with remarkable speed in 1086. The resulting Domesday Book is the most complete record of any European kingdom of its time. William's reign transformed England's language, aristocracy, architecture and church, and remains a turning point in English history; how far it was a clean break with Anglo-Saxon England is still debated.

## Key histories & documents

### Domesday Book (1086)

[![Domesday Book](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Domesday-book-1804x972.jpg/1280px-Domesday-book-1804x972.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Domesday-book-1804x972.jpg)

<sub>Pages of Domesday Book (The National Archives, Kew) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Domesday-book-1804x972.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1086
- **Type:** Survey / public record
- **Written / held:** The National Archives, Kew, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Domesday%20Book%20William%20the%20Conqueror)

At Christmas 1085 William ordered commissioners to survey every shire in England, recording who held each estate before and after the Conquest, how many ploughs, people, mills, woods and animals it contained, and what it was worth. The returns were compiled in 1086 into two volumes, Great Domesday and Little Domesday, covering most of England, though not London, Winchester or the far north. The name Domesday, meaning day of judgement, was given later because its verdicts could not be appealed. It shows the near-total replacement of the English landholding class by Normans. It was used as legal evidence for centuries and is still consulted by historians, geographers and place-name scholars; it is kept at the National Archives in Kew.
