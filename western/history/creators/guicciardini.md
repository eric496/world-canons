## Biography

Francesco Guicciardini was born in Florence in 1483 into an aristocratic family. He trained as a lawyer, served as Florentine ambassador to Spain, and then had a long career in the service of the Medici popes Leo X and Clement VII as governor of papal provinces and military commander. He was a leading adviser in the war that ended in the Sack of Rome in 1527. After the Medici restoration in Florence he served Duke Alessandro, but was sidelined under Cosimo I and retired to his villa at Arcetri, where he wrote his great history before his death in 1540.

Guicciardini's History of Italy covers the years from the French invasion of 1494 to 1534, when the Italian states lost their independence to foreign powers. It treats Italy as a whole and explains events through the calculations and errors of rulers, drawing on documents he had handled. More sceptical than Machiavelli about general rules, he insisted on discretion and judgment in particular cases. His history was widely translated, and he is often regarded as the first great modern historian.

## Key histories & documents

### The History of Italy (1537–1540 (published 1561–64))

- **Date:** 1537–1540 (published 1561–64)
- **Type:** Political history (20 books)
- **Written / held:** Written at Arcetri, near Florence; first printed in Florence, 1561
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20History%20of%20Italy%20Francesco%20Guicciardini)

Guicciardini wrote the Storia d'Italia in retirement in the last years of his life, revising it repeatedly. It covers the forty years from the French invasion of 1494 to the death of Clement VII in 1534, years in which Italy became the battlefield of France, Spain and the empire and its states lost their independence. He had taken part in many of the events as a diplomat, governor and commander, and he drew on official documents. His explanations are psychological and political, based on the ambitions, fears and miscalculations of rulers, and he often notes how events turned out contrary to expectations. The work was published by his nephew two decades after his death, with some passages critical of the papacy removed, and it was quickly translated into Latin, French and English.
