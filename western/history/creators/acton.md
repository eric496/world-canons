# John Dalberg-Acton, 1st Baron Acton

**1834 – 1902** · English  
Born: Naples, Kingdom of the Two Sicilies · Died: Tegernsee, Bavaria  
Traditions & eras: [Professional & scientific history](../movements/scientific-history.md)  

> A liberal Catholic historian and moralist, Lord Acton is remembered for his maxim about power and for planning the Cambridge Modern History.

## Biography

John Emerich Edward Dalberg-Acton was born in Naples in 1834 into an English Catholic family with Bavarian and Italian connections. Barred as a Catholic from Cambridge, he studied in Munich under the church historian Ignaz von Döllinger. He sat in Parliament as a Liberal, became a friend and adviser of Gladstone and was made a baron in 1869. He opposed the declaration of papal infallibility at the First Vatican Council in 1870. In 1895 he became Regius Professor of Modern History at Cambridge, where he planned the Cambridge Modern History. He died at Tegernsee in 1902.

Acton collected a huge library and planned a history of liberty that he never wrote; his writings consist mainly of essays, lectures and letters. He believed that historians should judge the past by firm moral standards. In an 1887 letter to Bishop Mandell Creighton he wrote that power tends to corrupt and absolute power corrupts absolutely. His Cambridge Modern History, planned as an impartial collaborative work, appeared after his death.

## Key histories & documents

### Inaugural Lecture on the Study of History (1895)

- **Date:** 1895
- **Type:** Lecture
- **Written / held:** Delivered at Cambridge, 11 June 1895; published by Macmillan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Inaugural%20Lecture%20on%20the%20Study%20of%20History%20John%20Dalberg-Acton%2C%201st%20Baron%20Acton)

Acton delivered his inaugural lecture as Regius Professor of Modern History at Cambridge in June 1895. He argued that modern history began with the discoveries and the Renaissance around 1500 and that its unifying theme was the progress of liberty, especially liberty of conscience. He praised the critical method of Ranke and the German historians and the opening of archives, but also insisted that historians must uphold the moral law and not debase it by excusing crimes. The lecture, published with extensive notes and quotations, is a classic statement of Victorian liberal historiography. Acton went on to plan the Cambridge Modern History, published after his death.
