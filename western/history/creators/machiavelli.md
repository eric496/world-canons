## Biography

Niccolò Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469 to a family of modest means and good education. After the fall of Savonarola in 1498 he became secretary of the Second Chancery of the republic, and for fourteen years he served on diplomatic missions to France, the papacy and Cesare Borgia, and organised a Florentine militia. When the Medici returned to power in 1512 he was dismissed, imprisoned and tortured on suspicion of conspiracy, then retired to his farm near San Casciano. There he wrote his major works. He was later partly reconciled with the Medici, who commissioned his history of Florence, and he died in 1527.

Machiavelli studied politics as it is practised rather than as it ought to be, drawing lessons from ancient Rome and from what he had witnessed. The Prince, with its advice that a ruler must be ready to act against conventional morality, made his name a byword for cunning, though many readers see in him a republican patriot. His Discourses analysed Livy to explain how republics rise and decline, and his Florentine Histories sought the causes of the city's endless factional strife. He is a founding figure of modern political science and of humanist history.

### The Prince (1513 (published 1532))

[![The Prince](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/14/Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_Il_Principe%2C_1550%2C_title_page.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_Il_Principe%2C_1550%2C_title_page.jpg)

<sub>Title page of a 1550 edition of The Prince Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_Il_Principe%2C_1550%2C_title_page.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1513 (published 1532)
- **Type:** Political treatise
- **Written / held:** Written at Sant'Andrea in Percussina near Florence; first printed in Rome and Florence, 1532
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Prince%20Niccol%C3%B2%20Machiavelli)

Machiavelli wrote The Prince in 1513 in rural exile after the Medici return to Florence, dedicating it eventually to Lorenzo de' Medici in hope of employment. In twenty-six short chapters he classifies principalities, discusses armies, and advises a prince on how to behave, arguing that it is safer to be feared than loved if one cannot be both, and that a ruler must learn to be able not to be good when necessity demands. His examples come from ancient history and from his own experience, especially of Cesare Borgia. The book closes with an appeal to free Italy from the barbarians. Published after his death in 1532, it was placed on the Index of Prohibited Books in 1559. Readers have argued ever since over whether it is a cynical manual of tyranny, a satire, or a clear-eyed description of political reality.

### Discourses on Livy (c. 1517 (published 1531))

[![Discourses on Livy](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/Discorsi_di_Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_firentino%2C_sopra_la_prima_deca_di_Tito_Livio.jpg/960px-Discorsi_di_Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_firentino%2C_sopra_la_prima_deca_di_Tito_Livio.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Discorsi_di_Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_firentino%2C_sopra_la_prima_deca_di_Tito_Livio.jpg)

<sub>Title page of an early edition of the Discourses on Livy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Discorsi_di_Nicolo_Machiavelli%2C_firentino%2C_sopra_la_prima_deca_di_Tito_Livio.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1517 (published 1531)
- **Type:** Political commentary (3 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Florence; first printed 1531
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discourses%20on%20Livy%20Niccol%C3%B2%20Machiavelli)

The Discourses on the First Ten Books of Titus Livy, written around 1513–19, use episodes from Livy as starting points for reflections on how republics are founded, how they expand, and how they decay and can be renewed. Machiavelli argued that Rome's conflicts between nobles and people had made it free and powerful rather than weak, that a citizen militia was superior to mercenaries, and that the Roman religion had served the state well. He praised mixed government on Polybius' model. Less famous than The Prince but longer and more systematic, the Discourses were read by English republicans of the seventeenth century, by Montesquieu and by the American founders, and they show the depth of Machiavelli's engagement with history.

### Florentine Histories (1520–1525)

- **Date:** 1520–1525
- **Type:** History (8 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Florence for Pope Clement VII; first printed 1532
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Florentine%20Histories%20Niccol%C3%B2%20Machiavelli)

In 1520 Cardinal Giulio de' Medici, soon to be Pope Clement VII, commissioned Machiavelli to write a history of Florence. He presented the eight completed books to the pope in 1525. Unlike Bruni, Machiavelli was interested less in the city's external glories than in its internal divisions, which he believed had weakened Florence, in contrast to the productive conflicts of ancient Rome. The narrative covers the barbarian invasions, Guelfs and Ghibellines, the revolt of the Ciompi wool workers of 1378, the rise of the Medici, and the Pazzi conspiracy of 1478, ending with the death of Lorenzo the Magnificent in 1492. Writing for Medici patrons, Machiavelli put some of his sharpest judgments into the mouths of historical characters.
