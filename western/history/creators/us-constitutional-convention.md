## Biography

The Convention met at the Pennsylvania State House, now Independence Hall, from May to September 1787, with George Washington presiding. It was called to revise the Articles of Confederation, under which Congress lacked the power to tax or regulate commerce, but it soon decided to write a new frame of government. Leading delegates included James Madison, whose Virginia Plan set the agenda, Benjamin Franklin, Alexander Hamilton, James Wilson and Gouverneur Morris, who drafted the final text. Rhode Island sent no one.

The delegates worked in secret, and our knowledge of their debates comes largely from Madison's notes. They reached compromises over representation of large and small states and, notoriously, over slavery, counting enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for apportionment and protecting the slave trade until 1808. Thirty-nine delegates signed the finished document on 17 September 1787.

## Key histories & documents

### Constitution of the United States (1787)

- **Date:** 1787
- **Type:** Constitution
- **Written / held:** Signed in Philadelphia; original in the National Archives, Washington, D.C.
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Constitution%20of%20the%20United%20States%20Constitutional%20Convention)

Drafted in secret by the Philadelphia Convention between May and September 1787 and signed on 17 September, the Constitution replaced the Articles of Confederation. Its seven articles create a bicameral Congress, combining equal representation of states in the Senate with representation by population in the House, an executive president chosen by an electoral college, and a federal judiciary, with a system of checks and balances and a division of powers between national and state governments. It included compromises over slavery, including the three-fifths clause and a fugitive slave clause. It came into effect in 1789 after ratification by nine states following fierce debate between Federalists and Anti-Federalists, and it has since been amended twenty-seven times, notably by the Bill of Rights and the post-Civil War amendments that abolished slavery and guaranteed equal protection. Its preamble opens with the words 'We the People'.
