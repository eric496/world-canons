# Anglo-Saxon chroniclers

**9th century – 12th century** · English (anonymous)  
Born: England · Died: England  
Traditions & eras: [Medieval chronicles & charters](../movements/medieval-chronicles.md)  

> The anonymous scribes in several English monasteries who compiled and continued the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle from the reign of Alfred the Great to 1154.

## Biography

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle was begun in Wessex in the late ninth century, probably in the circle of King Alfred the Great, though not necessarily by his direct command. Copies were sent to monasteries around England, where generations of unnamed monks continued them, each adding entries for their own time and region. The surviving manuscripts come from places such as Winchester, Abingdon, Worcester and Peterborough, where the last entries were made in 1154.

Writing year by year in Old English, rather than in Latin, the chroniclers created the first continuous history of a European people in its own language. Their entries range from a single line to vivid narrative and poetry, such as the poem on the battle of Brunanburh. The Chronicle is the essential source for the Viking wars and late Anglo-Saxon England, and a key document for the history of the English language.

- **Written / held:** Compiled in Wessex and continued in several English monasteries; manuscripts in the British Library, Bodleian Library, and Corpus Christi College, Cambridge
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Anglo-Saxon%20Chronicle%20Anglo-Saxon%20chroniclers)

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle is a collection of annals in Old English, first compiled in the 890s in Wessex, probably in the circle of King Alfred, drawing on Bede and earlier records. Copies were distributed to monasteries and continued independently, so the nine surviving manuscripts and fragments differ. Entries range from a single sentence to long narratives of Alfred's wars with the Vikings, the reign of Æthelred the Unready and the Norman Conquest. The Peterborough Chronicle, continued until 1154, gives a bitter account of the anarchy of King Stephen's reign. As the first continuous history of a European people written in its own vernacular, it is a key source both for history and for the development of the English language.
