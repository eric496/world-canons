# Plutarch

**c. AD 46 – after 119** · Greek (Roman citizen)  
Born: Chaeronea, Boeotia, Greece · Died: Chaeronea  
Traditions & eras: [Roman historiography & biography](../movements/roman-historiography.md)  

> A Greek philosopher and priest at Delphi, Plutarch wrote paired biographies of famous Greeks and Romans that became one of the most widely read books in European history.

## Biography

Plutarch was born around AD 46 in Chaeronea in Boeotia, into a prosperous family. He studied philosophy in Athens, travelled to Egypt and Italy, and lectured in Rome, where he made influential friends and became a Roman citizen. Yet he chose to live in his small home town, serving as a magistrate and for many years as one of the priests of Apollo at Delphi. He died some time after 119.

Besides the Parallel Lives he wrote a huge range of essays on ethics, religion, politics and natural science, collected as the Moralia. Plutarch said he wrote lives, not histories: his interest was character, shown as much in small gestures as in great battles. Translated into French by Amyot and into English by North, the Lives were read by Montaigne, supplied Shakespeare with the plots of Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra and Coriolanus, and inspired revolutionaries in America and France with models of republican virtue.

## Key histories & documents

### Parallel Lives (c. AD 100–120)

- **Date:** c. AD 100–120
- **Type:** Paired biographies
- **Written / held:** Written in Chaeronea, Greece
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Parallel%20Lives%20Plutarch)

The Parallel Lives consist of twenty-three surviving pairs of biographies, each linking a Greek and a Roman, such as Alexander and Caesar, Demosthenes and Cicero, or Theseus and Romulus, most followed by a short comparison, plus four single lives. Plutarch explained that he was writing lives, not histories, and that a small action, a phrase or a jest often reveals character better than battles. His aim was moral: to offer examples to imitate and to avoid. Translated into French by Jacques Amyot in 1559 and into English by Thomas North in 1579, the Lives became one of the most influential books of the Renaissance. Shakespeare took the plots of his Roman plays from North's translation, and the American and French revolutionaries drew republican models from it.
