# James Madison

**1751 – 1836** · American  
Born: Port Conway, Colony of Virginia · Died: Montpelier, Virginia  
Traditions & eras: [Age of Revolutions documents](../movements/revolutionary-documents.md)  

> Often called the father of the Constitution, Madison shaped the Philadelphia Convention, co-wrote The Federalist and drafted the Bill of Rights.

## Biography

James Madison was born in 1751 at Port Conway in Virginia into a planter family and studied at the College of New Jersey, now Princeton. He served in the Virginia legislature and in Congress, and was the leading intellectual force at the Constitutional Convention of 1787, where he also kept the fullest record of the debates. With Hamilton and Jay he wrote The Federalist. In the first Congress he drafted the amendments that became the Bill of Rights. He later co-founded the Democratic-Republican Party with Jefferson, served as his Secretary of State, and was President from 1809 to 1817, including during the War of 1812. He died at Montpelier in 1836.

Madison's political thought centred on the problem of faction. He argued that a large republic, with many competing interests, and a system of checks and balances would protect liberty better than direct democracy. Like Jefferson he was an enslaver who expressed misgivings about slavery but did not free the people he owned. He is regarded as the most philosophical of the founders.

## Key histories & documents

### United States Bill of Rights (1789 (ratified 1791))

- **Date:** 1789 (ratified 1791)
- **Type:** Constitutional amendments
- **Written / held:** Proposed by Congress in New York; original enrolled copy in the National Archives, Washington, D.C.
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=United%20States%20Bill%20of%20Rights%20James%20Madison)

Many opponents of the Constitution demanded a bill of rights, and several states ratified only on the understanding that one would follow. In June 1789 James Madison, drawing on the Virginia Declaration of Rights and the proposals of the state conventions, introduced amendments in the first Congress. Congress sent twelve to the states in September 1789, and ten were ratified by December 1791. They protect freedom of religion, speech, press, assembly and petition, the right to keep and bear arms, protection against unreasonable searches, the rights to due process, a speedy and public jury trial and counsel, and freedom from cruel and unusual punishment, and they reserve other powers to the states or the people. Originally they restrained only the federal government; after the Fourteenth Amendment the Supreme Court gradually applied most of them to the states.
