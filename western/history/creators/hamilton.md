## Biography

Alexander Hamilton was born on the Caribbean island of Nevis, in 1755 or 1757, and was orphaned young. Sent to New York to study, he joined the revolutionary cause, became aide-de-camp to George Washington and led an assault at Yorktown. A lawyer in New York, he was a delegate to the Constitutional Convention and the chief advocate of ratification. As Washington's Secretary of the Treasury from 1789 to 1795 he established the national bank and public credit. He was mortally wounded in a duel with Aaron Burr in 1804.

Hamilton argued for a strong national government and a commercial economy, against the agrarian vision of Jefferson and Madison, and their quarrel shaped the first American party system. The Federalist essays he organised with Madison and John Jay remain the most authoritative commentary on the Constitution. Long overshadowed, his reputation revived in the twentieth century and he became a figure of popular culture.

## Key histories & documents

### The Federalist Papers (1787–1788)

<sub>Title page of the first collected edition, volume 1, New York, 1788 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Federalist_%281st_ed%2C_1788%2C_vol_I%2C_title_page%29_-_02.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1787–1788
- **Type:** Political essays (85)
- **Written / held:** Published in New York newspapers; collected edition by J. and A. McLean, New York, 1788
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Federalist%20Papers%20Alexander%20Hamilton)

The Federalist essays appeared in New York newspapers from October 1787 to August 1788 under the pseudonym Publius, to persuade the state to ratify the proposed Constitution. Alexander Hamilton conceived the project and wrote about fifty-one essays, James Madison about twenty-nine and John Jay five; the authorship of a few was long disputed. The essays explain and defend each part of the Constitution. Madison's Federalist No. 10 argues that a large republic can control the effects of faction, and No. 51 that ambition must be made to counteract ambition; Hamilton's No. 78 describes the judiciary as the least dangerous branch. Collected in two volumes in 1788, the essays are still cited by the Supreme Court as evidence of the framers' intentions, and the Anti-Federalist writings that answered them are increasingly studied alongside them.
