## Biography

David Hume was born in Edinburgh in 1711 and studied at the university there. His Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) fell, as he put it, 'dead-born from the press', but his essays made his name. Twice rejected for university chairs because of his religious scepticism, he became librarian of the Advocates' Library in Edinburgh in 1752, which gave him the books he needed to write his History of England. He later served as secretary to the British embassy in Paris, where he was celebrated in the salons, and as an under-secretary of state. He died in Edinburgh in 1776.

Hume's History of England, published in six volumes from 1754 to 1762, ran from Julius Caesar's invasion to the Revolution of 1688. It was attacked at first as too sympathetic to the Stuart kings, but became the standard history for nearly a century and made him rich. It treats religion, commerce and manners as well as politics and favours moderation over party zeal. His philosophy of scepticism and empiricism remains far more influential today.

## Key histories & documents

### The History of England (1754–1762)

- **Date:** 1754–1762
- **Type:** History (6 volumes)
- **Written / held:** Written in Edinburgh; published in London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20History%20of%20England%20David%20Hume)

Hume published his History in reverse order, beginning with two volumes on the Stuarts in 1754 and 1756, followed by the Tudors in 1759 and the earlier period in 1762. The first volume was attacked for its sympathy for Charles I and its scepticism about religious enthusiasm on both sides, but the whole became the most popular history of England until Macaulay. Hume argued that English liberty was not an ancient inheritance, as Whig writers claimed, but a recent and somewhat accidental product of conflict, and he gave attention to manners, commerce, learning and the arts. Its elegant prose made it a standard text for generations. Its title page described Hume simply as 'David Hume, Esq.'
