## Biography

Elizabeth Cady was born in 1815 in Johnstown, New York, the daughter of a judge, from whom she learned about the legal disabilities of women. She married the abolitionist Henry Stanton in 1840, and on their honeymoon in London attended the World Anti-Slavery Convention, where women delegates were excluded. With Lucretia Mott and others she organised the first women's rights convention, at Seneca Falls in 1848. From 1851 she worked in partnership with Susan B. Anthony, and she led national suffrage organisations for decades. She died in New York in 1902.

Stanton was a powerful writer and speaker who argued for women's suffrage, property rights, divorce reform and equality in education. After the Civil War she opposed the Fifteenth Amendment because it enfranchised Black men but not women, sometimes in language now criticised as racist, which split the movement. Her controversial Woman's Bible criticised religious teachings about women. Women in the United States won the vote in 1920, after her death.

## Key histories & documents

### Declaration of Sentiments (1848)

[![Declaration of Sentiments](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/43/Woman%27s_Rights_Convention.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Woman%27s_Rights_Convention.jpg)

<sub>Newspaper notice calling the Woman's Rights Convention at Seneca Falls, July 1848 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Woman%27s_Rights_Convention.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1848
- **Type:** Declaration
- **Written / held:** Adopted at the Seneca Falls Convention, New York, July 1848
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Declaration%20of%20Sentiments%20Elizabeth%20Cady%20Stanton)

The Declaration of Sentiments, drafted mainly by Elizabeth Cady Stanton, was adopted at the first women's rights convention, held at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, on 19–20 July 1848. Deliberately echoing the Declaration of Independence, it declared that all men and women are created equal, and listed the injuries inflicted on women: denial of the vote, of property rights for married women, of education and professions, and of equality in church and law. It was followed by resolutions, of which the demand for the vote was the most controversial and passed only after Frederick Douglass spoke in support. It was signed by sixty-eight women and thirty-two men. The convention launched the organised movement that eventually won women's suffrage.
