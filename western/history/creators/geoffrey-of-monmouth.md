## Biography

Geoffrey was probably born around 1095 in or near Monmouth in Wales, perhaps of Breton descent. From the 1120s to the 1150s he appears as a canon of the collegiate church of St George at Oxford, where he witnessed charters with other scholars. In 1152 he was consecrated bishop of St Asaph in north Wales, though he probably never visited the see. He died around 1155.

His History of the Kings of Britain, finished around 1136, claimed to translate an ancient British book, a claim most scholars treat as a literary device. It traces British kings from the Trojan Brutus to the seventh century and gives the fullest early account of Arthur, Merlin, Lear and Cymbeline. Critics such as William of Newburgh denounced it almost at once as fiction, but it was a medieval bestseller, surviving in over two hundred manuscripts, and it shaped Arthurian romance and English national myth for centuries.

## Key histories & documents

### History of the Kings of Britain (Historia Regum Britanniae) (c. 1136)

[![History of the Kings of Britain (Historia Regum Britanniae)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/81/Vortigern-Dragons.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vortigern-Dragons.jpg)

<sub>Vortigern and Merlin watch the red and white dragons fight, from a 15th-century manuscript of Geoffrey's History Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vortigern-Dragons.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1136
- **Type:** Legendary history
- **Written / held:** Written in Oxford; over 200 medieval manuscripts survive
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=History%20of%20the%20Kings%20of%20Britain%20%28Historia%20Regum%20Britanniae%29%20Geoffrey%20of%20Monmouth)

Geoffrey of Monmouth claimed to have translated a very ancient book in the British language given to him by Walter, archdeacon of Oxford, though most scholars believe this is a fiction covering his own invention combined with material from earlier chronicles and Welsh tradition. The Historia describes the founding of Britain by Brutus, great-grandson of Aeneas, a long line of kings including Lear and Cymbeline, the prophecies of Merlin, and above all the reign of Arthur, who defeats the Saxons and conquers much of Europe before being betrayed by Mordred. Some medieval critics dismissed it as fable, but it became enormously popular and was accepted as history by many for centuries. It provided material for Arthurian romance, for Shakespeare and Spenser, and for royal propaganda.
