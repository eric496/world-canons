## Biography

Edmund Burke was born in Dublin in 1729 to a Protestant father and a Catholic mother, and studied at Trinity College Dublin before going to London to study law. He made his name with a treatise on the sublime and the beautiful, became secretary to the Marquess of Rockingham, and sat in the House of Commons from 1766 to 1794. He supported conciliation with the American colonists, opposed the penal laws against Irish Catholics, and led the long impeachment of Warren Hastings for abuses in India. His opposition to the French Revolution split the Whig party. He died in 1797.

Burke defended inherited institutions, gradual reform and the wisdom embodied in custom and prejudice against abstract plans for remaking society from first principles. Critics from Paine onwards argued that he cared more for the pageantry of monarchy than for the suffering of the poor. He is claimed by conservatives as a founder and studied by liberals as a critic of revolutionary utopianism, and his speeches are classics of English prose.

## Key histories & documents

### Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790)

[![Reflections on the Revolution in France](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3c/BurkeReflections.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:BurkeReflections.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the first edition, London, 1790 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:BurkeReflections.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1790
- **Type:** Political treatise
- **Written / held:** Published in London by J. Dodsley, November 1790
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Reflections%20on%20the%20Revolution%20in%20France%20Edmund%20Burke)

Written as a letter to a young Frenchman and published in November 1790, before the Terror, the Reflections responded to a sermon by the Dissenting minister Richard Price praising the Revolution. Burke argued that the revolutionaries' abstract theories of rights would destroy the institutions, customs and habits of deference that held society together, and predicted that the Revolution would lead to anarchy and military dictatorship. He contrasted it with the English Revolution of 1688, which he presented as a preservation of ancient liberties. His lament for Marie Antoinette and the passing of the age of chivalry became famous. The book sold widely and provoked many replies, including Paine's Rights of Man and Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication of the Rights of Men. It remains central to debates about reform and revolution.
