# Edward Gibbon

**1737 – 1794** · English  
Born: Putney, Surrey, England · Died: London  
Traditions & eras: [Enlightenment history](../movements/enlightenment-history.md)  

> Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire is widely regarded as the greatest work of history in the English language.

## Biography

Edward Gibbon was born in Putney in 1737 into a well-off family. A sickly child, he read voraciously and went to Oxford, which he later scorned; his conversion to Catholicism there led his father to send him to Lausanne, where he returned to Protestantism and absorbed French learning. He served as an officer in the Hampshire militia and, on a visit to Rome in 1764, conceived the idea of writing about the city's decline. He was a member of Parliament from 1774 to 1783 and later settled in Lausanne. He died in London in 1794.

The Decline and Fall, published in six volumes from 1776 to 1789, covers thirteen centuries, from the second-century empire to the fall of Constantinople in 1453. It combines the critical scholarship of the antiquarians with the elegance and irony of the Enlightenment, and its famous fifteenth and sixteenth chapters, which treat early Christianity as a human phenomenon, caused a scandal. Many of his specific explanations have been revised, but his work remains a landmark of historical writing.

## Key histories & documents

### The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire (1776–1789)

- **Date:** 1776–1789
- **Type:** History (6 volumes)
- **Written / held:** Written in London and Lausanne; published in London by Strahan and Cadell
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20History%20of%20the%20Decline%20and%20Fall%20of%20the%20Roman%20Empire%20Edward%20Gibbon)

Gibbon published the first volume in 1776 and the last two in 1788, having finished the work in his garden house at Lausanne in June 1787. Its seventy-one chapters run from the empire at its height in the second century to the fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453, covering the barbarian invasions, the rise of Christianity, Justinian, Muhammad and the Arab conquests, the crusades and the Mongols. Gibbon combined the critical erudition of antiquarian scholars with the irony and broad view of the Enlightenment, and his footnotes are famously witty. He attributed the fall in part to the triumph of barbarism and religion, and chapters 15 and 16, which treated early Christianity's growth and martyrdoms as human phenomena, provoked furious replies. The work has never been out of print and is regarded as one of the summits of historical writing.
