## Biography

Xenophon was born in Athens around 430 BC into a well-off family and became a follower of Socrates. In 401 BC he joined a Greek mercenary army hired by the Persian prince Cyrus the Younger in his bid for the throne. After Cyrus was killed at Cunaxa and the Greek generals were treacherously murdered, Xenophon was among those elected to lead the Ten Thousand on their long retreat to the Black Sea. He later served with Sparta, was exiled from Athens, and lived for many years on an estate near Olympia given him by the Spartans, before moving, probably, to Corinth.

Xenophon wrote in a clear, plain Attic prose that later made him a schoolroom author. Besides the Anabasis and the Hellenica, he wrote Socratic dialogues and memoirs, a fictionalised education of Cyrus the Great, and treatises on hunting, horsemanship and household management. Modern historians value him as an eyewitness while noting his pro-Spartan sympathies and his omissions. The Anabasis in particular inspired later generals and writers, and his portrait of Socrates complements Plato's.

### Anabasis (c. 370 BC)

- **Date:** c. 370 BC
- **Type:** Memoir / military narrative
- **Written / held:** Written in Greece (probably at Scillus, near Olympia)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Anabasis%20Xenophon)

The Anabasis tells how Cyrus the Younger hired Greek mercenaries in 401 BC to seize the Persian throne from his brother, how Cyrus was killed at Cunaxa near Babylon, and how the stranded Greeks, their generals murdered, elected new leaders, among them Xenophon himself, and fought their way north through the mountains of Kurdistan and Armenia to the Black Sea. The moment when the soldiers sight the sea and cry 'Thalatta! Thalatta!' is among the most famous scenes in ancient literature. Xenophon wrote in the third person and published the work under another name, perhaps to make it seem impartial. Its plain style made it a standard schoolbook, and its picture of a democratic army improvising its survival has fascinated soldiers and writers ever since.

### Hellenica (c. 360s–350s BC)

[![Hellenica](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e7/Xenophon_Hellenica.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xenophon_Hellenica.jpg)

<sub>Page of a manuscript or early edition of Xenophon's Hellenica Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xenophon_Hellenica.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 360s–350s BC
- **Type:** Prose history (7 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Greece
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hellenica%20Xenophon)

The Hellenica begins abruptly where Thucydides' history ends, in 411 BC, and carries the story through the end of the Peloponnesian War, the Spartan hegemony and the rise of Thebes, ending with the battle of Mantinea in 362 BC. Xenophon wrote as a participant in some events and with obvious sympathy for Sparta and its king Agesilaus, and he passes over some important developments, such as the founding of the Second Athenian League, in near silence. Even so, it is the only continuous contemporary narrative of the period to survive, and its vivid scenes, such as the trial of the generals after Arginusae, make it indispensable.
