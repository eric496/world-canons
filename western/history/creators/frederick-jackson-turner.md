# Frederick Jackson Turner

**1861 – 1932** · American  
Born: Portage, Wisconsin · Died: Pasadena, California  
Traditions & eras: [Professional & scientific history](../movements/scientific-history.md)  

> An American historian whose 'frontier thesis' argued that the westward-moving frontier had shaped American democracy and character.

## Biography

Frederick Jackson Turner was born in 1861 in Portage, Wisconsin, then a small frontier town, and studied at the University of Wisconsin and at Johns Hopkins, where he trained in the German seminar method. He taught at Wisconsin from 1889 and at Harvard from 1910, and in his later years worked at the Huntington Library in California. He died in Pasadena in 1932.

Turner presented his thesis at the American Historical Association meeting held during the Chicago World's Fair in 1893, just after the census had declared the frontier closed. He argued that the availability of free land, rather than European inheritance, explained American individualism and democracy. The thesis dominated American historical writing for decades. Later historians criticised it for ignoring Native Americans, women, Hispanic and Black Americans, and for treating the West as empty land, but it remains a landmark in the debate about American identity.

## Key histories & documents

### The Significance of the Frontier in American History (1893)

- **Date:** 1893
- **Type:** Lecture / essay
- **Written / held:** Delivered at the American Historical Association meeting, Chicago World's Columbian Exposition, July 1893
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Significance%20of%20the%20Frontier%20in%20American%20History%20Frederick%20Jackson%20Turner)

Turner read his paper to the American Historical Association, meeting in Chicago during the World's Columbian Exposition in July 1893. Starting from the Census Bureau's announcement that there was no longer a continuous frontier line, he argued that the existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession and the advance of American settlement westward explained American development. The frontier, he claimed, had stripped settlers of European habits, promoted individualism, self-reliance and democracy, and created a composite nationality. Initially little noticed, the thesis came to dominate American historical writing. From the 1980s the New Western historians criticised it for treating the West as empty, ignoring Native Americans, Mexicans, women and the environmental and violent costs of expansion, but it remains a reference point.
