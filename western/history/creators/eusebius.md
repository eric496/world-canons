# Eusebius of Caesarea

**c. 260 – 339** · Roman (Greek-speaking)  
Born: probably Caesarea Maritima, Palestine · Died: Caesarea Maritima  
Traditions & eras: [Late antique & church history](../movements/church-history.md)  

> Bishop of Caesarea and adviser to Constantine, Eusebius invented church history and wrote the first chronicle of world history in Christian terms.

<sub>Portrait: Eusebius of Caesarea, miniature in the Rabbula Gospels, 6th century · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Eusebius_of_Caesarea_Rabbula_Gospels_Icon.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Eusebius was born around 260, probably in Caesarea in Palestine, where he studied under the scholar Pamphilus and used the great library founded by Origen. He lived through the Great Persecution of the early fourth century, in which Pamphilus was martyred, and became bishop of Caesarea around 313. He was a leading figure at the Council of Nicaea in 325 and became an admirer and panegyrist of the emperor Constantine. He died in 339.

Eusebius' Ecclesiastical History quoted documents at length, a method unusual in classical history, and preserved many earlier Christian texts that are otherwise lost. His Chronicle laid out the histories of many peoples in parallel columns, giving medieval Europe its framework of world chronology. His Life of Constantine is closer to panegyric than history and its reliability is debated. He is often called the father of church history.

### Ecclesiastical History (c. 313–325)

[![Ecclesiastical History](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Ecclesiastical_History_of_Eusebius_Pamphilus%2C_1842_%28page_7_crop%29.jpg/960px-Ecclesiastical_History_of_Eusebius_Pamphilus%2C_1842_%28page_7_crop%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ecclesiastical_History_of_Eusebius_Pamphilus%2C_1842_%28page_7_crop%29.jpg)

<sub>Page from an 1842 English translation of Eusebius' Ecclesiastical History Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ecclesiastical_History_of_Eusebius_Pamphilus%2C_1842_%28page_7_crop%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 313–325
- **Type:** Church history (10 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Caesarea, Palestine
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ecclesiastical%20History%20Eusebius%20of%20Caesarea)

Eusebius' Ecclesiastical History, which he revised several times before reaching its final form around 324, traces the Church from the time of Christ through the succession of bishops in the great sees, the lives of Christian writers, the struggles against heresy, the sufferings of the Jews and the persecutions of Christians, to the triumph of Constantine. Unlike classical historians, Eusebius quoted his sources at length, so that his work preserves extracts from many early Christian writings that are otherwise lost. It created a new genre, church history, imitated by Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret and, centuries later, Bede. Modern scholars use it with care, as it is shaped by Eusebius' theological views and his celebration of a Christian empire.

### Life of Constantine (c. 337–339)

[![Life of Constantine](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/20/0_Constantinus_I_-_Palazzo_dei_Conservatori_%282%29.JPG/960px-0_Constantinus_I_-_Palazzo_dei_Conservatori_%282%29.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:0_Constantinus_I_-_Palazzo_dei_Conservatori_%282%29.JPG)

<sub>Colossal marble head of Constantine the Great, Capitoline Museums, Rome Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:0_Constantinus_I_-_Palazzo_dei_Conservatori_%282%29.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 337–339
- **Type:** Biography / panegyric (4 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Caesarea, Palestine
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Life%20of%20Constantine%20Eusebius%20of%20Caesarea)

Written after Constantine's death in 337 and left unfinished at Eusebius' own death in 339, the Life of Constantine praises the emperor as God's chosen servant. It reports Constantine's own account, told to Eusebius under oath, of seeing a cross of light in the sky with the words 'by this, conquer' before his victory over Maxentius in 312. It describes his church-building, the Council of Nicaea and his laws, quoting many imperial letters. The authenticity of these documents was once doubted but is now largely accepted, while the work as a whole is read as a panegyric rather than a balanced biography. It shaped the medieval image of the Christian ruler.
