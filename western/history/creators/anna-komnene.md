# Anna Komnene

**1083 – c. 1153** · Byzantine  
Born: Constantinople · Died: Constantinople  
Traditions & eras: [Medieval chronicles & charters](../movements/medieval-chronicles.md)  

> A Byzantine princess and scholar, Anna Komnene wrote the Alexiad, a history of her father's reign and one of the first major histories written by a woman.

## Biography

Anna Komnene was born in Constantinople in 1083, the eldest child of the emperor Alexios I Komnenos and Irene Doukaina. She received an exceptional education in literature, philosophy, medicine and science. Betrothed as a child to the heir to the throne, she lost her prospects when her brother John was born. After her father's death in 1118 she was implicated in a plot against John and spent much of her later life in the convent of Kecharitomene, where she wrote her history. She died around 1153.

The Alexiad, written in learned classicising Greek, describes Alexios' struggles against Normans, Pechenegs, Turks and the knights of the First Crusade, whom Anna viewed with a mixture of fascination and distrust. It is a partisan portrait of her father, but also rich and well informed, drawing on documents and veterans' testimony. It offers a Byzantine view of the crusades and is a key source for the period.

## Key histories & documents

### Alexiad (c. 1148)

- **Date:** c. 1148
- **Type:** Prose history (15 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Constantinople
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Alexiad%20Anna%20Komnene)

Anna Komnene wrote the Alexiad in the 1140s, in retirement in a convent in Constantinople, in a learned Greek modelled on classical historians. Its fifteen books tell the story of the reign of her father Alexios I Komnenos from 1081 to 1118: his rise to power, his wars against the Norman Robert Guiscard and his son Bohemond, against the Pechenegs and the Seljuk Turks, and the arrival of the armies of the First Crusade in 1096–97, whose leaders she describes with a mixture of admiration and suspicion. Anna names her sources, including her own memories and old soldiers' accounts, and repeatedly insists on her impartiality while plainly idealising her father. It is a major source for Byzantine history and the crusades, and a landmark of historical writing by a woman.
