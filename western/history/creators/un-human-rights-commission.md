# UN Commission on Human Rights

**1946 – 1948** · International  
Born: New York and Geneva · Died: Paris (adoption)  
Traditions & eras: [20th-century history & human-rights documents](../movements/twentieth-century-history.md)  

> The United Nations commission, chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, that drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1946–48.

## Biography

The Commission on Human Rights was created by the United Nations in 1946 and chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, the widow of President Franklin D. Roosevelt. Its drafting committee included the Canadian lawyer John Peters Humphrey, who prepared the first draft, the French jurist René Cassin, who reworked it, the Chinese philosopher and diplomat P. C. Chang, and the Lebanese philosopher Charles Malik. Delegates from many countries, including Hansa Mehta of India, who argued for gender-inclusive language, shaped the final text.

The Commission debated for two years in New York and Geneva, with frequent disagreements over economic and social rights, religion and the relation between individual and state. The text was adopted by the General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948, with no votes against but eight abstentions, including the Soviet bloc, South Africa and Saudi Arabia. Eleanor Roosevelt called it a possible international Magna Carta for all people.

## Key histories & documents

### Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)

- **Date:** 1948
- **Type:** International declaration
- **Written / held:** Adopted by the UN General Assembly at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris, 10 December 1948
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Universal%20Declaration%20of%20Human%20Rights%20UN%20Commission%20on%20Human%20Rights)

Drafted over two years by the UN Commission on Human Rights under Eleanor Roosevelt, the Universal Declaration was adopted by the General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948 by forty-eight votes to none, with eight abstentions. Written in the aftermath of the Second World War and the Holocaust, its preamble speaks of the barbarous acts that had outraged the conscience of mankind. Its thirty articles begin by declaring that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights, and include civil and political rights, such as freedom from torture and slavery, freedom of thought and religion and a fair trial, alongside economic, social and cultural rights, such as the rights to work, education and social security. Not itself a binding treaty, it inspired the international covenants of 1966, regional conventions and many national constitutions, and it is among the most translated documents in the world. Debates continue about the universality of its values and the gap between its principles and practice.
