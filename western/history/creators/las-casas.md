# Bartolomé de las Casas

**c. 1484 – 1566** · Spanish  
Born: Seville, Castile · Died: Madrid  
Traditions & eras: [Chronicles of the Americas](../movements/americas-chronicles.md)  

> A colonist turned Dominican friar, Las Casas spent fifty years denouncing the abuse of Indigenous peoples in the Spanish Americas.

## Biography

Bartolomé de las Casas was born in Seville, probably in 1484, and went to Hispaniola in 1502 as a settler, receiving land and Indigenous labourers. He took part in the conquest of Cuba as a chaplain. In 1514 he renounced his holdings and began to campaign against the encomienda system, crossing the Atlantic many times to lobby the crown. He became a Dominican friar in 1523 and was later bishop of Chiapas in Mexico. In 1550–51 he debated the theologian Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda at Valladolid on the justice of the conquest. He died in Madrid in 1566.

Las Casas wrote a massive History of the Indies, apologetic works defending the rationality of Indigenous peoples, and the polemical Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies. His campaigning contributed to the New Laws of 1542, which restricted the encomienda. Early in his career he suggested importing African slaves to relieve Indigenous labourers, a proposal he later bitterly regretted. His writings were used by Spain's enemies to build the 'Black Legend' of Spanish cruelty, and he is now seen as an early advocate of universal human rights, though his figures and rhetoric are debated.

## Key histories & documents

### A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1542 (published 1552))

- **Date:** 1542 (published 1552)
- **Type:** Polemical report
- **Written / held:** Written in Spain for Prince Philip; first printed in Seville, 1552
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Short%20Account%20of%20the%20Destruction%20of%20the%20Indies%20Bartolom%C3%A9%20de%20las%20Casas)

Las Casas wrote the Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias in 1542 for the future Philip II and printed it in Seville in 1552. Region by region, from Hispaniola to Peru, it describes massacres, enslavement, torture and forced labour inflicted on Indigenous peoples by Spanish conquerors and settlers, and it attributes a catastrophic population collapse to their cruelty and greed. Las Casas wrote to persuade the crown to reform colonial policy, and the work contributed to debates that led to the New Laws of 1542. Modern historians accept the reality of widespread atrocity while noting that his figures are often impossible to verify and that epidemic disease caused most deaths. Translated into Dutch, English, French and German, often with lurid engravings by Theodor de Bry, it became a mainstay of anti-Spanish propaganda, the so-called Black Legend.
