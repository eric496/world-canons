# Ammianus Marcellinus

**c. 330 – c. 395** · Roman (Greek-speaking)  
Born: probably Antioch, Syria · Died: probably Rome  
Traditions & eras: [Late antique & church history](../movements/church-history.md)  

> A Greek-speaking soldier from Antioch, Ammianus wrote in Latin the last great history of Rome in the classical tradition.

## Biography

Ammianus Marcellinus was born around 330, probably in Antioch, into a family of some standing. He served as an officer of the imperial guard on the staff of the general Ursicinus in the East and in Gaul, was present at the Persian siege of Amida in 359 and escaped its fall, and took part in the emperor Julian's ill-fated Persian campaign of 363. He later travelled, then settled in Rome, where he wrote his history in the 380s and early 390s.

His Res Gestae continued Tacitus, covering the years from 96 to 378, but only the books from 353 onward survive. Ammianus is an eyewitness for much of what he describes, and he combines vivid narrative with digressions on geography, the Huns and Roman society. A pagan who admired Julian, he writes about Christianity with notable restraint. His account ends with the Roman catastrophe at Adrianople in 378, and Gibbon called him an accurate and faithful guide.

## Key histories & documents

### Res Gestae (History) (c. AD 390)

- **Date:** c. AD 390
- **Type:** Prose history (31 books, 18 survive)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Res%20Gestae%20%28History%29%20Ammianus%20Marcellinus)

Ammianus wrote his history in Rome in the late 380s and early 390s, continuing Tacitus from the reign of Nerva. The first thirteen books are lost; the surviving books 14 to 31 cover the years 353 to 378 in great detail, drawing on his own experience as a staff officer. Highlights include the siege of Amida, which he escaped, the career of the emperor Julian, whom he admired, the arrival of the Huns, and the catastrophic defeat and death of the emperor Valens at Adrianople. He includes digressions on geography, science and the vices of the Roman aristocracy and populace. Writing as a pagan in a Christian empire, he is generally fair-minded. Gibbon relied on him heavily.
