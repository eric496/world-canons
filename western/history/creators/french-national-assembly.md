## Biography

In June 1789 the deputies of the Third Estate at the Estates-General in Versailles declared themselves a National Assembly and swore the Tennis Court Oath not to separate until France had a constitution; many clergy and some nobles joined them. After the storming of the Bastille, the Assembly abolished feudal privileges on the night of 4 August and on 26 August adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, drawing on drafts by Lafayette, who consulted Jefferson, and others. It moved to Paris in October 1789.

Over the next two years the Assembly reorganised France into departments, nationalised church lands, imposed the Civil Constitution of the Clergy and produced the constitution of 1791, establishing a constitutional monarchy. It dissolved itself in September 1791. Its work set the agenda of modern French and European politics, though its compromises soon gave way to more radical revolution.

## Key histories & documents

### Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789)

[![Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen_in_1789.jpg/960px-Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen_in_1789.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen_in_1789.jpg)

<sub>Jean-Jacques-François Le Barbier, painting of the Declaration, c. 1789 (Musée Carnavalet, Paris) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen_in_1789.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1789
- **Type:** Declaration
- **Written / held:** Adopted at Versailles, 26 August 1789; original in the Archives nationales, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Declaration%20of%20the%20Rights%20of%20Man%20and%20of%20the%20Citizen%20French%20National%20Constituent%20Assembly)

The National Constituent Assembly adopted the Declaration on 26 August 1789, after the abolition of feudal privileges, as a preamble to the constitution it was preparing. Its seventeen articles proclaim that men are born and remain free and equal in rights, that the aim of political association is the preservation of the natural rights of liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression, and that sovereignty resides in the nation. It establishes the principles of law as the expression of the general will, equality before the law, presumption of innocence, freedom of opinion, including religious opinion, and of expression, and the separation of powers. Influenced by Enlightenment thought and by American declarations, it did not extend political rights to women or address slavery, omissions challenged at the time by Olympe de Gouges and by abolitionists. It remains part of the French constitution.
