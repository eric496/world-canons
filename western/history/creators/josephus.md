## Biography

Joseph ben Matityahu was born in Jerusalem in AD 37 into a priestly family. When the Jews revolted against Rome in 66 he was given command of Galilee. Besieged at Jotapata in 67, he survived a suicide pact and surrendered to Vespasian, whose rise to emperor he is said to have predicted. Freed when the prophecy came true, he took the family name Flavius and accompanied Titus at the siege and destruction of Jerusalem in 70. He spent the rest of his life in Rome as a pensioner of the Flavian emperors, writing in Greek.

Josephus wrote The Jewish War, the Antiquities of the Jews, a defensive autobiography and the apologetic Against Apion. He is our principal source for the history of Judaea in the late Second Temple period, for figures such as Herod the Great and for the fall of Masada. His works were preserved by Christians, partly because of brief passages about John the Baptist, James and Jesus; the main passage on Jesus, the Testimonium Flavianum, is widely thought to have been altered by later Christian copyists, though how much is debated. Jews long regarded him as a traitor; modern scholars read him as a complex figure caught between two worlds.

### The Jewish War (c. AD 75)

- **Date:** c. AD 75
- **Type:** Prose history (7 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Jewish%20War%20Flavius%20Josephus)

Josephus first wrote an account of the war in Aramaic for the peoples of the East and then produced a Greek version, the one that survives, in Rome in the 70s. After a long introduction covering Jewish history from the Maccabees, it tells how the revolt broke out, how Josephus himself commanded in Galilee and surrendered, and how Titus besieged Jerusalem and destroyed the Temple in AD 70, with horrific scenes of famine and slaughter. It ends with the siege of Masada, where, according to Josephus, the defenders chose mass suicide rather than capture. He blames the disaster on a minority of extremists and treats the Romans as instruments of God's will, an interpretation shaped by his position. The work is the main source for these events and was read for centuries alongside the Bible.

### Antiquities of the Jews (c. AD 93–94)

[![Antiquities of the Jews](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Flavius_Josephus%2C_Antiquitates_Iudaicae%2C_Florence%2C_Plut._66.5.jpg/960px-Flavius_Josephus%2C_Antiquitates_Iudaicae%2C_Florence%2C_Plut._66.5.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flavius_Josephus%2C_Antiquitates_Iudaicae%2C_Florence%2C_Plut._66.5.jpg)

<sub>Latin manuscript of the Antiquities of the Jews, Laurentian Library, Florence, Plut. 66.5 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flavius_Josephus%2C_Antiquitates_Iudaicae%2C_Florence%2C_Plut._66.5.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 93–94
- **Type:** Prose history (20 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Antiquities%20of%20the%20Jews%20Flavius%20Josephus)

In the twenty books of the Antiquities, completed around 93 or 94, Josephus retold the history of the Jews from the creation of the world, following the Hebrew Bible, through the Persian, Hellenistic and Hasmonean periods to the reign of Herod the Great and the Roman governors of Judaea. He wrote to present his people's laws and traditions as ancient and admirable to Greek and Roman readers. For the period after the Bible he is often the only source, preserving decrees and documents. The work contains brief references to John the Baptist and James, the brother of Jesus, and the Testimonium Flavianum on Jesus, generally believed to contain Christian additions. Christians preserved and copied the Antiquities throughout the Middle Ages.
