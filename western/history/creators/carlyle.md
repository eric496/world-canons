# Thomas Carlyle

**1795 – 1881** · Scottish  
Born: Ecclefechan, Dumfriesshire, Scotland · Died: London  
Traditions & eras: [Romantic & national history](../movements/romantic-history.md)  

> A Scottish essayist and historian, Carlyle wrote a thunderous history of the French Revolution and argued that history is made by great men.

## Biography

Thomas Carlyle was born in 1795 in the village of Ecclefechan in Dumfriesshire, the son of a stonemason of strict Calvinist faith. He studied at Edinburgh, abandoned plans for the ministry, and made his name as an interpreter of German literature and philosophy. In 1834 he moved to Chelsea in London with his wife Jane Welsh Carlyle, a brilliant letter-writer. The manuscript of the first volume of his French Revolution was accidentally burned while on loan to John Stuart Mill, and he rewrote it from memory. He became one of the most influential sages of Victorian Britain, and died in London in 1881.

Carlyle's histories, including the French Revolution, Oliver Cromwell's letters and speeches, and a life of Frederick the Great, are written in an idiosyncratic, prophetic style of present tenses, exclamations and apostrophes. He attacked laissez-faire economics and the 'cash nexus', but also came to despise democracy, celebrate strong rulers, and write in defence of slavery, positions that have damaged his reputation. His 'great man' theory of history was widely debated.

### The French Revolution: A History (1837)

- **Date:** 1837
- **Type:** History (3 volumes)
- **Written / held:** Written in Chelsea, London; published by James Fraser
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20French%20Revolution%3A%20A%20History%20Thomas%20Carlyle)

Carlyle's French Revolution, in three volumes titled The Bastille, The Constitution and The Guillotine, covers events from the death of Louis XV in 1774 to Napoleon's 'whiff of grapeshot' in 1795. In March 1835 the only manuscript of the first volume was accidentally burned at the house of John Stuart Mill, to whom he had lent it, and Carlyle rewrote it from memory. Written largely in the present tense, with exclamations, prophecies and addresses to the characters, it presents the Revolution as a fiery judgment on a decayed aristocratic order. It made Carlyle famous, influenced Dickens's A Tale of Two Cities and remains a literary classic, though not a reliable guide to the facts.

### On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History (1841)

- **Date:** 1841
- **Type:** Lectures
- **Written / held:** Delivered in London, 1840; published by James Fraser, 1841
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Heroes%2C%20Hero-Worship%2C%20and%20the%20Heroic%20in%20History%20Thomas%20Carlyle)

In May 1840 Carlyle gave six public lectures in London on the hero as divinity (Odin), prophet (Muhammad), poet (Dante and Shakespeare), priest (Luther and Knox), man of letters (Johnson, Rousseau and Burns) and king (Cromwell and Napoleon). He declared that universal history is at bottom the history of the great men who have worked here. His sympathetic treatment of Muhammad was unusual in Britain at the time. The lectures were enormously popular and gave a name to the 'great man theory' of history, which Herbert Spencer and others criticised for ignoring the social conditions that produce great individuals. Carlyle's later glorification of strong rulers has made the book controversial.
