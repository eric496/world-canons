# Jacob Burckhardt

**1818 – 1897** · Swiss  
Born: Basel, Switzerland · Died: Basel  
Traditions & eras: [Cultural history](../movements/cultural-history.md)  

> A Swiss historian of art and culture, Burckhardt's Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy created the modern idea of the Renaissance.

## Biography

Jacob Burckhardt was born in Basel in 1818, the son of a Protestant pastor. He studied theology, then history and art history in Berlin, where he attended Ranke's lectures. After a period as a journalist and teacher, and travels in Italy that produced a famous guidebook to Italian art, the Cicerone, he became professor at the University of Basel in 1858 and stayed there for the rest of his career, declining Ranke's chair in Berlin. Among his colleagues was the young Nietzsche. He died in Basel in 1897.

Burckhardt turned from political narrative to the study of a whole culture: its state, its individuals, its scholarship, festivals, morality and religion. He saw Renaissance Italy as the birthplace of the modern individual. Many of his claims have been qualified by later scholars, but his book remains the starting point for debate. He was deeply pessimistic about mass politics and the modern state, warning of what he called 'terrible simplifiers'.

## Key histories & documents

### The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860)

- **Date:** 1860
- **Type:** Cultural history
- **Written / held:** Written in Basel; published by Schweighauser, Basel
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Civilization%20of%20the%20Renaissance%20in%20Italy%20Jacob%20Burckhardt)

Die Kultur der Renaissance in Italien, published in 1860, is organised not as a narrative but in six thematic parts: the state as a work of art, the development of the individual, the revival of antiquity, the discovery of the world and of man, society and festivals, and morality and religion. Burckhardt argued that in Italy the medieval veil of faith and illusion first melted away, so that people became conscious of themselves as individuals. He drew on chronicles, letters, poetry and biographies rather than archives of state. The book created the modern image of the Renaissance, and much later scholarship, stressing medieval continuities and the role of northern Europe, has been a dialogue with it. Burckhardt himself called it simply an essay.
