## Biography

Bernal Díaz del Castillo was born in Medina del Campo in Castile around 1496 and sailed to the Americas in 1514. After taking part in two earlier expeditions to the coast of Mexico, he joined Hernán Cortés's expedition of 1519 and fought throughout the campaign that ended with the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521. He later settled in Guatemala as an encomendero and town councillor, and complained repeatedly that his services had been poorly rewarded. He died there in 1584.

In his old age Díaz wrote the True History of the Conquest of New Spain, partly to correct the official account of Cortés's chaplain López de Gómara, which gave all the glory to Cortés. Written in plain, conversational Spanish, it is full of the names, horses and fates of ordinary soldiers and of unforgettable descriptions of Tenochtitlan. Historians treat it as an indispensable but partisan source, written from the conquerors' side decades after the events.

- **Date:** c. 1568 (published 1632)
- **Type:** Eyewitness history
- **Written / held:** Written in Santiago de los Caballeros, Guatemala; first printed in Madrid, 1632
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20True%20History%20of%20the%20Conquest%20of%20New%20Spain%20Bernal%20D%C3%ADaz%20del%20Castillo)

Bernal Díaz began writing his Historia verdadera de la conquista de la Nueva España in the 1550s and finished a version around 1568, revising it until his death; it was first printed in 1632. He wrote to correct accounts that credited the victory to Cortés alone and to claim recognition for the soldiers who fought alongside him. He describes the landing at Veracruz, the alliance with the Tlaxcalans, the first view of Tenochtitlan, which seemed to the soldiers like an enchanted vision, the meetings with Moctezuma, the Night of Sorrows and the final siege of 1521. He gives a central role to Malinche, the Nahua woman who served as interpreter. The work is an invaluable but partisan source, written from the conquerors' side, and is complemented by Indigenous accounts such as those in the Florentine Codex.
