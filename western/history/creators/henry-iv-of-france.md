## Biography

Henry of Bourbon was born at Pau in 1553 and raised a Protestant by his mother, Queen Jeanne of Navarre. He became king of Navarre in 1572 and narrowly survived the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre, which followed his wedding to the Catholic princess Margaret of Valois. He led the Huguenot cause in the Wars of Religion and became heir to the French throne in 1584. On succeeding in 1589 he faced the opposition of the Catholic League, and in 1593 converted to Catholicism, entering Paris the next year. He was assassinated by a Catholic fanatic in 1610.

Henry's Edict of Nantes of 1598 was a pragmatic settlement that allowed limited Protestant worship within a Catholic kingdom. With his minister Sully he restored royal finances and agriculture after decades of war. He was long remembered as 'Good King Henry', and the wish attributed to him that every peasant should have a chicken in the pot on Sundays became proverbial.

## Key histories & documents

### Edict of Nantes (1598)

[![Edict of Nantes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/59/Edit_de_nantes.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Edit_de_nantes.jpg)

<sub>The Edict of Nantes, 1598 (Archives nationales, Paris) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Edit_de_nantes.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1598
- **Type:** Royal edict
- **Written / held:** Signed at Nantes; original in the Archives nationales, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Edict%20of%20Nantes%20Henry%20IV%20of%20France)

Henry IV signed the Edict of Nantes in April 1598, after more than three decades of civil war between Catholics and Protestants. It confirmed Catholicism as the state religion but granted the Huguenots freedom of conscience, the right to worship publicly in specified places, access to public office and the universities, special courts, and, in separate articles, the right to hold around a hundred fortified towns. It was a pragmatic compromise rather than a declaration of religious liberty, and it was resented by many Catholics. Louis XIII removed the Protestants' military privileges after the siege of La Rochelle, and Louis XIV revoked the edict entirely in 1685, driving some 200,000 Huguenots into exile across Europe and America.
