## Biography

Thomas Paine was born in Thetford, Norfolk, in 1737, the son of a Quaker corset-maker. After failing in several trades and as an excise officer, he emigrated to Philadelphia in 1774 with an introduction from Benjamin Franklin. His Common Sense (1776) electrified the colonies, and his American Crisis papers sustained morale during the war. Returning to Europe, he defended the French Revolution in Rights of Man, was charged with seditious libel in Britain, and was elected to the French National Convention, where he opposed the execution of Louis XVI and was imprisoned during the Terror. He died in New York in 1809, poor and largely shunned.

Paine's genius was to turn arguments about natural rights, republican government and the injustice of hereditary rule into vivid, accessible prose. His later Age of Reason attacked organised religion and cost him much of his popularity in America. He also proposed early ideas of social insurance and a fund for every citizen. He is now celebrated as one of the most influential political writers of the revolutionary era.

### Common Sense (1776)

[![Common Sense](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4a/Commonsense.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Commonsense.jpg)

<sub>Title page of Common Sense, Philadelphia, 1776 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Commonsense.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1776
- **Type:** Political pamphlet
- **Written / held:** First printed in Philadelphia by Robert Bell, January 1776
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Common%20Sense%20Thomas%20Paine)

Published anonymously in Philadelphia in January 1776, when most colonists still hoped for reconciliation with Britain, Common Sense argued that it was absurd for an island to rule a continent, that monarchy and hereditary succession were irrational and corrupt, and that the colonies should declare independence and form a republic. Paine wrote in direct, vigorous prose, avoiding Latin quotations and appealing to ordinary readers. It sold in huge numbers, with estimates often given in the tens or hundreds of thousands, a debated figure, and it was read aloud in taverns and camps. It is credited with shifting public opinion towards independence in the months before July 1776.

### Rights of Man (1791–1792)

[![Rights of Man](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/27/PaineRightsOfMan.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PaineRightsOfMan.png)

<sub>Title page of Rights of Man, 1791 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PaineRightsOfMan.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1791–1792
- **Type:** Political treatise (2 parts)
- **Written / held:** Published in London by J. S. Jordan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rights%20of%20Man%20Thomas%20Paine)

Paine published the first part of Rights of Man in March 1791 in answer to Burke's Reflections. He argued that rights derive from nature, not from inherited institutions, that the living cannot be bound by the dead, and that hereditary government is a form of usurpation. In the second part, published in 1792, he proposed a programme of social reform funded by progressive taxation, including support for the poor, old-age pensions and public education. The work sold widely among artisans and working people, and the British government charged Paine with seditious libel; he was convicted in absence and outlawed. It is a classic of democratic radicalism.
