## Biography

Matthew Paris entered the Benedictine abbey of St Albans in 1217 and spent most of his life there. The abbey was a major stop on the road north of London, and visitors, including King Henry III, who knew him personally, brought him news. In 1248 he was sent on a mission to reform a monastery in Norway. He took over the abbey's chronicle from Roger of Wendover around 1236 and continued it until his death in 1259.

His Chronica Majora is a vast history of the world from the Creation, becoming richly detailed for his own time, with copies of documents and outspoken criticism of the king and the papacy. Unusually, Matthew drew and painted many illustrations in the margins, and also made maps, including some of the earliest detailed maps of Britain, and a drawing of an elephant given to Henry III. He is valued both as a historian and as an artist.

## Key histories & documents

### Chronica Majora (c. 1240–1259)

[![Chronica Majora](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4d/Matthew_Paris_Elephant_from_Parker_MS_16_fol_151v.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Matthew_Paris_Elephant_from_Parker_MS_16_fol_151v.jpg)

<sub>Matthew Paris's drawing of the elephant given to Henry III, Chronica Majora (Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, MS 16, f. 151v) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Matthew_Paris_Elephant_from_Parker_MS_16_fol_151v.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1240–1259
- **Type:** Illustrated chronicle
- **Written / held:** Written at St Albans Abbey; autograph manuscripts at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, and the British Library
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chronica%20Majora%20Matthew%20Paris)

The Chronica Majora begins with the Creation, taking over the chronicle of Roger of Wendover, and from 1236 becomes Matthew Paris's own detailed record of events across Europe and the Mediterranean up to 1259: the reign of Henry III, the conflict between the emperor Frederick II and the papacy, the Mongol invasions and the crusades. Matthew copied many documents into it and wrote outspokenly against royal and papal taxation. Unusually for a chronicler, he illustrated the manuscripts himself with tinted drawings in the margins, including battles, coats of arms, an elephant presented to Henry III and the Mongols, and he made itineraries and maps. The autograph volumes survive at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, and in the British Library.
