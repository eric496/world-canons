# Einhard

**c. 775 – 840** · Frankish  
Born: Main valley, East Francia (Germany) · Died: Seligenstadt  
Traditions & eras: [Medieval chronicles & charters](../movements/medieval-chronicles.md)  

> A scholar and courtier of Charlemagne, Einhard wrote the Life of Charlemagne, the first secular biography of the Middle Ages.

## Biography

Einhard was born around 775 into a Frankish landowning family in the Main valley and was educated at the monastery of Fulda. Around 791 he was sent to Charlemagne's court, where he joined the circle of scholars around Alcuin and became a trusted adviser and builder; he may have overseen work on the palace at Aachen. He continued to serve Charlemagne's son Louis the Pious, who granted him estates, and in later life he founded an abbey at Seligenstadt, where he died in 840.

Einhard's Life of Charlemagne, written probably in the 820s, borrows its structure and many phrases from Suetonius' lives of the emperors, an early sign of the Carolingian revival of classical learning. It is a vivid portrait by someone who knew the king personally, though it is also idealised. Einhard also wrote letters and an account of the transfer of relics to his abbey. His church at Steinbach still stands.

## Key histories & documents

### Life of Charlemagne (Vita Karoli Magni) (c. 817–830)

- **Date:** c. 817–830
- **Type:** Biography
- **Written / held:** Written in the Frankish Empire (Aachen or Seligenstadt)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Life%20of%20Charlemagne%20%28Vita%20Karoli%20Magni%29%20Einhard)

Einhard wrote his Life of Charlemagne some years after the emperor's death in 814; its exact date is debated. Following the structure of Suetonius' lives, especially that of Augustus, it describes Charlemagne's wars against the Saxons, Lombards and Avars, his building works and his coronation as emperor in Rome in 800, and then his family, appearance, habits and death. Einhard records that the king tried to learn to write late in life, keeping tablets under his pillow, but without success. It is an idealised portrait, but by someone who knew him well, and it shaped the image of Charlemagne for the rest of the Middle Ages. More than eighty medieval manuscripts survive.
