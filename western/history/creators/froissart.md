# Jean Froissart

**c. 1337 – c. 1405** · Hainuyer (French-speaking)  
Born: Valenciennes, County of Hainaut · Died: probably Chimay, Hainaut  
Traditions & eras: [Medieval chronicles & charters](../movements/medieval-chronicles.md)  

> A poet and courtier from Hainaut, Froissart wrote the great chronicle of chivalry and war in the first half of the Hundred Years' War.

## Biography

Jean Froissart was born around 1337 in Valenciennes in the county of Hainaut. In 1361 he went to England as a secretary and poet to Queen Philippa of Hainaut, wife of Edward III. After her death in 1369 he found patrons in the Low Countries, became a priest, and travelled widely, to Scotland, Italy, the court of Gaston Fébus at Orthez and, in 1395, England once more. He died around 1405, probably at Chimay, where he was a canon.

Froissart's Chronicles cover events from around 1322 to 1400 in France, England, Scotland, the Low Countries and Spain. He gathered material by interviewing knights, heralds and servants and kept revising his text. His interest was in noble deeds and spectacle, from the battles of Crécy and Poitiers to the Peasants' Revolt, and he wrote from the point of view of the aristocracy. Richly illuminated copies were made for princes, and his work remained hugely popular, inspiring Walter Scott and many painters.

## Key histories & documents

### Chronicles (c. 1370–1400)

- **Date:** c. 1370–1400
- **Type:** Chronicle (4 books)
- **Written / held:** Written in the Low Countries, England and France; over 150 manuscripts, many richly illuminated (e.g. the Gruuthuse manuscript, Bibliothèque nationale de France)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chronicles%20Jean%20Froissart)

Froissart's Chronicles, written in French prose in four books, cover events in western Europe from the 1320s to 1400. He began by adapting the chronicle of Jean le Bel and then, by travelling and interviewing participants, extended and repeatedly revised his narrative. The Chronicles contain the classic accounts of the battles of Crécy and Poitiers, the burghers of Calais, the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 and the deposition of Richard II. Froissart's outlook is aristocratic and he is often inaccurate on details, but his vividness made him a favourite of nobles, who commissioned splendidly illuminated copies, and of later readers. Lord Berners translated him into English in the 1520s, and he inspired Walter Scott and countless illustrators.
