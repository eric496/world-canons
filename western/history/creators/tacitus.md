# Publius Cornelius Tacitus

**c. AD 56 – c. 120** · Roman  
Born: probably Gaul or northern Italy · Died: unknown  
Traditions & eras: [Roman historiography & biography](../movements/roman-historiography.md)  

> Senator and historian, Tacitus chronicled the early Roman emperors with a compressed, ironic brilliance and a deep suspicion of tyranny.

<sub>Portrait: Statue of Tacitus outside the Austrian Parliament Building, Vienna (19th century) · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wien-_Parlament-Tacitus.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Tacitus was born around AD 56, probably in Gallia Narbonensis or northern Italy, into a family of equestrian rank. He married the daughter of Gnaeus Julius Agricola, the governor of Britain, and had a distinguished senatorial career under the Flavian emperors, reaching the consulship in 97 and later governing the province of Asia. He was a renowned orator and a friend of Pliny the Younger, who wrote to him about the eruption of Vesuvius. He died some time after 117.

Tacitus wrote two short works, the Agricola and the Germania, and two great histories, the Histories and the Annals, which together covered the empire from the death of Augustus to the death of Domitian. Only parts survive. He promised to write 'without anger or partiality', but his account is shaped by a senator's bitterness at the loss of liberty and the servility of his class. His terse, epigrammatic style and psychological insight made him the favourite historian of early-modern critics of absolutism, and his account of the Germans was later put to nationalist uses he could not have imagined.

### Germania (AD 98)

- **Date:** AD 98
- **Type:** Ethnographic treatise
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome; a manuscript rediscovered at Hersfeld and brought to Rome c. 1455
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Germania%20Publius%20Cornelius%20Tacitus)

In the Germania Tacitus describes the geography, customs, religion and tribes of the Germanic peoples beyond the Roman frontier. Drawing on earlier writers and perhaps on traders and soldiers, he portrays them as fierce, free and unspoiled, implicitly contrasting their simplicity with Roman luxury, though he also notes drunkenness and idleness. The text survived through a single medieval manuscript rediscovered in the fifteenth century, after which German humanists used it to claim a noble ancient ancestry. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it was distorted to support racial nationalism, including by the Nazis, which led the classicist Arnaldo Momigliano to count it among the most dangerous books ever written. Scholars today read it as a Roman text about Roman concerns.

### Agricola (AD 98)

[![Agricola](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Codex-Aesinas-n.8_-linizio-dellAgricola-di-Tacito-carta-52r.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Codex-Aesinas-n.8_-linizio-dellAgricola-di-Tacito-carta-52r.jpg)

<sub>Opening of the Agricola in the Codex Aesinas, 9th–15th century (fol. 52r) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Codex-Aesinas-n.8_-linizio-dellAgricola-di-Tacito-carta-52r.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** AD 98
- **Type:** Biography
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Agricola%20Publius%20Cornelius%20Tacitus)

The Agricola is a short biography of Gnaeus Julius Agricola, the general who governed Britain from about 77 to 84 and led Roman armies into Scotland, defeating the Caledonians at Mons Graupius. Written after the death of Domitian, it praises Agricola as a good man who served honourably under a bad emperor, and it includes a speech put into the mouth of the Caledonian leader Calgacus attacking Roman imperialism with the famous charge that they make a desert and call it peace. It is the most detailed ancient account of Britain and its conquest.

### Histories (c. AD 100–110)

[![Histories](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/59/Tacitus_Historiae_Liber_I._%28Editio_princeps%2C_Venedig_1472%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tacitus_Historiae_Liber_I._%28Editio_princeps%2C_Venedig_1472%29.jpg)

<sub>Tacitus' Histories, book 1, first printed edition, Venice, 1472 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tacitus_Historiae_Liber_I._%28Editio_princeps%2C_Venedig_1472%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 100–110
- **Type:** Prose history (partly preserved)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome; survives in the second Medicean manuscript (Laurentian Library, Florence)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Histories%20Publius%20Cornelius%20Tacitus)

The Histories originally covered the years from 69 to the death of Domitian in 96, but only the first four books and part of the fifth survive, covering 69 and 70. They describe a terrible year in which Galba, Otho, Vitellius and Vespasian successively seized power, with armies marching on Rome and the Capitol burned. Tacitus reveals what he calls the secret of empire: that an emperor could be made elsewhere than at Rome. The surviving part ends with the revolt of the Batavians on the Rhine and the opening of the siege of Jerusalem. It is one of the most gripping narratives of political collapse in ancient literature.

### Annals (c. AD 116–120)

[![Annals](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Tacitus%2C_Annales_XI-XVI%2C_editio_princeps%2C_ca._1471-72%2C_incipit.jpg/960px-Tacitus%2C_Annales_XI-XVI%2C_editio_princeps%2C_ca._1471-72%2C_incipit.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tacitus%2C_Annales_XI-XVI%2C_editio_princeps%2C_ca._1471-72%2C_incipit.jpg)

<sub>First printed edition of Tacitus' Annals, books 11–16, Venice, c. 1471–72 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tacitus%2C_Annales_XI-XVI%2C_editio_princeps%2C_ca._1471-72%2C_incipit.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 116–120
- **Type:** Prose history (16+ books, partly preserved)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome; books 1–6 survive in a single 9th-century manuscript (Laurentian Library, Florence)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Annals%20Publius%20Cornelius%20Tacitus)

The Annals cover the Julio-Claudian emperors from Tiberius to Nero, AD 14 to 68, though the books on Caligula, the early years of Claudius and Nero's last years are lost. Tacitus arranges the narrative year by year in the old republican manner, while showing how real power had passed to the emperor and his household. Famous passages include the treason trials under Tiberius, the death of Germanicus, the revolt of Boudica in Britain, Nero's murder of his mother Agrippina, and the great fire of Rome, after which Nero blamed the Christians, the earliest non-Christian account of their persecution. The surviving books depend on two medieval manuscripts in Florence. Tacitus' irony and moral intensity made the Annals a handbook for critics of tyranny from the Renaissance onward.
