# The barons and King John

**1215 – 1215** · English  
Born: England · Died: England  
Traditions & eras: [Medieval chronicles & charters](../movements/medieval-chronicles.md)  

> The rebel English barons, Archbishop Stephen Langton and King John, whose negotiations at Runnymede in June 1215 produced Magna Carta.

## Biography

King John of England, who reigned from 1199 to 1216, lost Normandy to the French king in 1204 and raised heavy taxes and feudal dues to fund attempts to win it back. After the failure of his campaign of 1214, a group of barons renounced their allegiance and seized London in May 1215. Mediated in part by Stephen Langton, archbishop of Canterbury, the two sides met at Runnymede, beside the Thames, where in mid-June the king agreed to a charter of liberties.

The charter was a peace treaty that failed: within months John had it annulled by Pope Innocent III and civil war resumed. It was reissued in revised forms after his death in 1216 and in 1217 and 1225, and became part of English law. Its authors were concerned chiefly with the rights of free men, the church and the barons, but later generations reinterpreted it as a charter of universal liberties.

## Key histories & documents

### Magna Carta (1215)

- **Date:** 1215
- **Type:** Charter of liberties
- **Written / held:** Four original 1215 exemplifications survive: two in the British Library, one each at Lincoln Castle and Salisbury Cathedral
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Magna%20Carta%20The%20barons%20and%20King%20John)

Magna Carta, the Great Charter, was granted by King John at Runnymede in June 1215 as a peace settlement with rebellious barons. Its sixty-three clauses addressed specific grievances about feudal dues, inheritance, the rights of the church, justice and the royal forests, and set up a council of twenty-five barons to enforce it. Most famously, it promised that no free man would be imprisoned or deprived of his rights except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land, and that justice would not be sold, denied or delayed. The charter was annulled by the pope within weeks, but reissued in revised form in 1216, 1217 and 1225 and became part of English law. Seventeenth-century parliamentarians invoked it against the Stuart kings, and American revolutionaries and later human-rights advocates treated it as a foundation of constitutional liberty, a symbolic meaning far wider than its authors intended.
