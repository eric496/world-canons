# Edward Hyde, 1st Earl of Clarendon

**1609 – 1674** · English  
Born: Dinton, Wiltshire, England · Died: Rouen, France  
Traditions & eras: [Early-modern political history & documents](../movements/early-modern-documents.md)  

> A royalist statesman and Lord Chancellor, Clarendon wrote the History of the Rebellion, the classic contemporary account of the English Civil War.

## Biography

Edward Hyde was born in 1609 in Dinton, Wiltshire, and trained as a lawyer. Elected to Parliament in 1640, he at first criticised Charles I's policies, but became a leading adviser of the king as the conflict deepened. He followed the future Charles II into exile in 1646 and became his chief minister. After the Restoration of 1660 he was made Lord Chancellor and Earl of Clarendon, and his daughter Anne married the king's brother, the future James II. Blamed for the failures of the Dutch war, he was impeached in 1667 and fled to France, where he died in Rouen in 1674.

Clarendon wrote his History in two periods of exile, combining narrative with memoir and vivid character portraits of his contemporaries. It is a royalist and personal account, but it is also judicious and penetrating, and it shaped how the Civil War was understood for two centuries. Its posthumous publication in 1702–04 funded the Clarendon Building at Oxford, and the Clarendon Press is named after him.

## Key histories & documents

### The History of the Rebellion and Civil Wars in England (1646–1674 (published 1702–04))

- **Date:** 1646–1674 (published 1702–04)
- **Type:** Contemporary history
- **Written / held:** Written in Jersey, France and Montpellier; first printed by the Oxford University Press, 1702–04
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20History%20of%20the%20Rebellion%20and%20Civil%20Wars%20in%20England%20Edward%20Hyde%2C%201st%20Earl%20of%20Clarendon)

Clarendon began his History in 1646 while in exile in Jersey, intending it as a confidential account for the king, and resumed it during his second exile after 1667, combining it with his autobiography. It covers the conflict from the accession of Charles I in 1625 to the Restoration of 1660. Clarendon defends the monarchy and the Church of England but criticises the errors of both sides, and his portraits of figures such as Lord Falkland, Hampden and Cromwell are celebrated for their insight and balance. Published by his son in three volumes in 1702–04, at a time of party conflict, it was immediately influential and profitable, its proceeds helping to fund the Clarendon Building in Oxford.
