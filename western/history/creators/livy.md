# Livy (Titus Livius)

**c. 59 BC – AD 17** · Roman  
Born: Patavium (Padua), Italy · Died: Patavium (Padua)  
Traditions & eras: [Roman historiography & biography](../movements/roman-historiography.md)  

> Livy wrote the history of Rome from its foundation in 142 books, giving later ages their picture of early Roman heroes, virtues and legends.

## Biography

Titus Livius was born in Patavium, modern Padua, probably in 59 BC. Unlike most Roman historians he held no political or military office, and he seems to have devoted his life to writing. He lived in Rome under Augustus, with whom he was on friendly terms despite his open admiration for Pompey and the Republic; he is said to have encouraged the future emperor Claudius to write history. He began his great work around 27 BC and worked on it for the rest of his life, returning to Padua, where he died in AD 17.

Livy's history, Ab Urbe Condita, is less a critical investigation than a moral and patriotic narrative. He admits that the early stories are legend, but he uses them to show the virtues that made Rome great and the vices that later undermined it. His prose is rich and dramatic, and his speeches and scenes, from the rape of Lucretia to Hannibal crossing the Alps, became stock subjects for painters and poets. Machiavelli wrote his Discourses as a commentary on Livy, and the work shaped the republican thought of early-modern Europe.

## Key histories & documents

### History of Rome (Ab Urbe Condita) (c. 27 BC – AD 17)

- **Date:** c. 27 BC – AD 17
- **Type:** Prose history (142 books, 35 survive)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome; medieval manuscripts, e.g. the 5th-century Codex Puteaneus (Bibliothèque nationale de France)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=History%20of%20Rome%20%28Ab%20Urbe%20Condita%29%20Livy%20%28Titus%20Livius%29)

Livy spent some forty years writing a history of Rome 'from the founding of the city', running to 142 books and ending with the death of Augustus' stepson Drusus in 9 BC. Only books 1–10 and 21–45 survive, covering the city's legendary origins to 293 BC and the period from Hannibal's war to the conquest of Macedon in 167 BC; the rest are known through summaries. In his preface Livy invites readers to study the lives and morals that made Rome great and the decline that followed. His stories of Romulus and Remus, Horatius at the bridge, Lucretia, Cincinnatus and Hannibal's crossing of the Alps became the common stock of European culture. Renaissance humanists sought lost books eagerly, Petrarch assembled a text, and Machiavelli built his political philosophy on a commentary on the first ten books.
