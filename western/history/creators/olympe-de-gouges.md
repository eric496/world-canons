# Olympe de Gouges

> A playwright and pamphleteer, Olympe de Gouges answered the Declaration of the Rights of Man with a Declaration of the Rights of Woman, and was guillotined during the Terror.

## Biography

Marie Gouze was born in Montauban in 1748 and married young; widowed, she moved to Paris, took the name Olympe de Gouges and became a playwright, writing among other works a play against slavery that was performed at the Comédie-Française in 1789. During the Revolution she wrote dozens of pamphlets and posters on political questions. A supporter of constitutional monarchy and later of the Girondins, she attacked Robespierre and the Jacobins, was arrested in 1793 and guillotined that November.

De Gouges argued that the principles of the Revolution must apply equally to women, demanding equal rights in law, property, marriage and public life, and she also campaigned for the abolition of slavery. Long neglected, she has been rediscovered since the twentieth century as a pioneer of feminism.

## Key histories & documents

### Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen (1791)

[![Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/34/DDFC.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:DDFC.jpg)

<sub>Page from Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman, 1791 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:DDFC.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1791
- **Type:** Pamphlet / declaration
- **Written / held:** Published in Paris, September 1791
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Declaration%20of%20the%20Rights%20of%20Woman%20and%20of%20the%20Female%20Citizen%20Olympe%20de%20Gouges)

In September 1791, as the new constitution was completed, Olympe de Gouges published a pamphlet addressed to Queen Marie Antoinette that rewrote the Declaration of the Rights of Man article by article to apply to women. Its first article declares that woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights. Its tenth article notes that if women have the right to mount the scaffold, they must also have the right to mount the rostrum. She called for equal rights to property and public employment, the right of unmarried mothers to name the fathers of their children, and a new social contract for marriage. The pamphlet had little impact at the time, and its author was guillotined in 1793, but it has become a classic text of feminism.
