# Suetonius (Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus)

**c. AD 69 – after 122** · Roman  
Born: probably Hippo Regius, North Africa (uncertain) · Died: unknown  
Traditions & eras: [Roman historiography & biography](../movements/roman-historiography.md)  

> A scholar and imperial secretary, Suetonius wrote biographies of the first twelve Caesars full of personal detail, gossip and scandal.

## Biography

Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus was born around AD 69 into an equestrian family; an inscription suggests links with Hippo Regius in North Africa. He was a protégé of Pliny the Younger, practised law, and rose to senior posts in the imperial household under Trajan and Hadrian, including responsibility for the libraries and the emperor's correspondence. This gave him access to the imperial archives. According to a later source he was dismissed around 122 for disrespect towards the empress Sabina. Little is known after that.

Suetonius was a prolific antiquarian scholar, but only the Lives of the Caesars and part of his Lives of Illustrious Men survive. His biographies are arranged by topic rather than chronology, covering ancestry, career, appearance, habits and death, and they preserve documents and anecdotes found nowhere else. He was long dismissed as a gossip, but his method shaped biography for centuries: Einhard modelled his life of Charlemagne on him, and his portraits of Caligula and Nero still dominate the popular image of those emperors.

## Key histories & documents

### Lives of the Caesars (c. AD 121)

- **Date:** c. AD 121
- **Type:** Collective biography (12 lives)
- **Written / held:** Written in Rome; oldest surviving manuscript 9th century
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lives%20of%20the%20Caesars%20Suetonius%20%28Gaius%20Suetonius%20Tranquillus%29)

Suetonius wrote the Lives of the Caesars while working in Hadrian's administration, dedicating them to the praetorian prefect Septicius Clarus. The twelve lives run from Julius Caesar to Domitian. Rather than following a strict chronological narrative, Suetonius organises each life by themes: family, career, public works, character, appearance, habits, omens and death. He quotes letters of Augustus and other documents from the imperial archives, along with rumours and gossip that make the book memorable and controversial. Medieval writers such as Einhard used it as a model, and it remains essential for the study of the early empire, and the popular image of Caligula and Nero owes much to it.
