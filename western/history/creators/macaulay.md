# Thomas Babington Macaulay

**1800 – 1859** · British  
Born: Rothley Temple, Leicestershire, England · Died: London  
Traditions & eras: [Romantic & national history](../movements/romantic-history.md)  

> A Whig politician, essayist and poet, Macaulay wrote a History of England that celebrated the Revolution of 1688 and became a Victorian bestseller.

## Biography

Thomas Babington Macaulay was born in 1800 at Rothley Temple in Leicestershire, the son of the abolitionist Zachary Macaulay, and was a famous child prodigy. After Cambridge he made his name with essays in the Edinburgh Review and entered Parliament in 1830, speaking for the Reform Bill. From 1834 to 1838 he served on the Supreme Council of India, where his Minute on Education of 1835 promoted English-language education, a policy long criticised for its contempt for Indian learning. He was later Secretary at War, and was made Baron Macaulay in 1857. He died in London in 1859.

Macaulay's History of England from the Accession of James the Second, published from 1848, sold in huge numbers and was translated into many languages. Written with the pace and colour of a novel, it presents the Glorious Revolution as the foundation of English liberty and prosperity. It is the classic example of what Herbert Butterfield later called the 'Whig interpretation of history', which reads the past as a story of progress towards the present. His Lays of Ancient Rome were widely learned by heart.

## Key histories & documents

### The History of England from the Accession of James the Second (1848–1861)

- **Date:** 1848–1861
- **Type:** History (5 volumes)
- **Written / held:** Written in London; published by Longman
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20History%20of%20England%20from%20the%20Accession%20of%20James%20the%20Second%20Thomas%20Babington%20Macaulay)

Macaulay originally planned to carry his history from 1685 to the early nineteenth century, but he managed only the reigns of James II and William III; the fifth volume, edited by his sister, appeared after his death in 1859. The famous third chapter surveys the state of England in 1685, its population, roads, towns and social classes, an early attempt at social history. The narrative portrays James II as a would-be absolutist and William III as the deliverer of English liberties, and the Revolution of 1688 as the foundation of national greatness. The first two volumes sold tens of thousands of copies in Britain and many more in America. It is the classic example of what Butterfield later criticised as the Whig interpretation of history.
