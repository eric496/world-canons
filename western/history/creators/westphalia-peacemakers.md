# Congress of Westphalia

**1643 – 1648** · International  
Born: Münster and Osnabrück, Westphalia · Died: Münster and Osnabrück  
Traditions & eras: [Early-modern political history & documents](../movements/early-modern-documents.md)  

> The envoys of the emperor, France, Sweden, Spain, the Dutch Republic and the German states who negotiated the Peace of Westphalia in 1643–48.

## Biography

From 1643 diplomats representing the Holy Roman Emperor, the kings of France, Sweden and Spain, the Dutch Republic, and dozens of German princes and cities gathered in the Westphalian towns of Münster and Osnabrück to end the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War. It was among the first great international peace congresses, with several hundred delegates. Leading figures included the imperial envoy Count Maximilian von Trauttmansdorff and the papal nuncio Fabio Chigi, later Pope Alexander VII.

Negotiations dragged on for five years while fighting continued. The resulting treaties, signed in 1648, are often credited with founding a modern system of sovereign states, although historians now argue that this 'Westphalian' interpretation owes more to later theorists than to the treaties themselves.

## Key histories & documents

### Peace of Westphalia (1648)

- **Date:** 1648
- **Type:** Peace treaties
- **Written / held:** Signed at Münster and Osnabrück; originals in national archives, including Vienna, Paris and Stockholm
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Peace%20of%20Westphalia%20Congress%20of%20Westphalia)

The Peace of Westphalia consisted of the treaties signed in 1648 at Osnabrück, between the emperor and Sweden, and at Münster, between the emperor and France, together with the separate Peace of Münster between Spain and the Dutch Republic earlier that year. They ended the Thirty Years' War, which had devastated much of Germany, and the Eighty Years' War, recognising the independence of the Dutch Republic. The treaties confirmed the right of German princes to determine the religion of their territories, now extended to Calvinists, protected religious minorities in some respects, gave territories to France and Sweden, and recognised Swiss independence. Political scientists have often treated Westphalia as the origin of the modern system of sovereign states; many historians argue that this is a later myth, since the treaties strengthened the empire's constitution rather than abolishing it.
