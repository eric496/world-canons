# Duke Ellington

**1899 – 1974** · American  
Born: Washington, D.C. · Died: New York  
Eras & schools: [Jazz](../movements/jazz.md)  

> Bandleader, pianist and composer, Ellington made the jazz orchestra a medium for composition, writing hundreds of pieces for his band over fifty years.

## Biography

Edward Kennedy Ellington was born in Washington, D.C., in 1899 to a middle-class family; his elegant manners earned him the nickname Duke. He moved to New York in 1923 and from 1927 to 1931 led the house band at the Cotton Club in Harlem, whose radio broadcasts made him nationally famous. He kept his orchestra together continuously for nearly half a century, touring the world, and with his collaborator Billy Strayhorn wrote suites, film scores and sacred concerts. His 1956 appearance at the Newport Jazz Festival revived his career. He died in New York in 1974.

Ellington composed with the particular sound of each player in mind, such as the growling trumpet of Bubber Miley or the sweet alto saxophone of Johnny Hodges, creating textures and colours that no one else could reproduce. His works range from three-minute pieces such as Mood Indigo and Ko-Ko to extended suites like Black, Brown and Beige, a tone parallel to the history of African Americans. He disliked the label jazz and preferred to call his work American music. He is widely regarded as the greatest jazz composer.

### Mood Indigo (1930)

- **Date:** 1930
- **Type:** Jazz composition (Duke Ellington and His Orchestra)
- **Premiere / first published:** Recorded in New York, October 1930 (first released as Dreamy Blues)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mood%20Indigo%20Duke%20Ellington)

Mood Indigo, credited to Ellington, Barney Bigard and Irving Mills, was first recorded in October 1930 under the title Dreamy Blues and became one of Ellington's first big hits. Its famous opening chorus places a muted trumpet, a muted trombone and a clarinet in its low register in an unusual voicing, the trombone playing at the top and the clarinet at the bottom; the overtones of the microphones of the time produced what Ellington called a 'mike tone'. Ellington later said he wrote it in fifteen minutes while waiting for dinner. It shows his talent for creating new sonorities from the particular sounds of his players. Lyrics were added later, and it became a jazz standard.

### Black, Brown and Beige (1943)

- **Date:** 1943
- **Type:** Extended suite for jazz orchestra
- **Premiere / first published:** Carnegie Hall, New York, 23 January 1943
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Black%2C%20Brown%20and%20Beige%20Duke%20Ellington)

Ellington premiered Black, Brown and Beige at his first Carnegie Hall concert in 1943, a fundraiser for Russian war relief. Lasting about forty-five minutes, it was described as a tone parallel to the history of African Americans, from Africa and slavery (Black), through emancipation and the contributions of Black soldiers (Brown) to life in Harlem in the 1920s and 1930s (Beige). It includes the spiritual-like melody Come Sunday, played by the alto saxophonist Johnny Hodges. Some critics, both jazz and classical, received it coolly, and Ellington never again recorded it complete, preferring excerpts. It is now recognised as a landmark attempt to create extended composition in jazz.
