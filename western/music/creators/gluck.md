## Biography

Christoph Willibald Gluck was born in 1714 in Erasbach in the Upper Palatinate, the son of a forester, and grew up in Bohemia. He studied in Prague and Milan and for two decades wrote Italian operas for cities across Europe, including London. He settled in Vienna, becoming Kapellmeister of the court theatre. There, with the librettist Ranieri de' Calzabigi and the choreographer Gasparo Angiolini, he produced Orfeo ed Euridice (1762) and Alceste (1767). From 1774 he worked in Paris under the patronage of his former pupil Marie Antoinette, where his operas, including Iphigénie en Tauride, sparked a public rivalry with Niccolò Piccinni. He died in Vienna in 1787.

In the preface to Alceste Gluck explained his aim of making music serve the poetry and drama, without the singers' vanity or needless ornament. His reform operas use simpler plots, continuous orchestral accompaniment of recitative, dramatic choruses and dance, and overtures that prepare the mood of the drama. Berlioz worshipped him, and his ideas influenced Mozart and, through Berlioz and Wagner, the later history of opera.

## Key works

### Orfeo ed Euridice (1762)

[![Orfeo ed Euridice](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/56/Gluck%27s_Orph%C3%A9e_-_title_page_illustration_%28lightened_and_cropped%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gluck%27s_Orph%C3%A9e_-_title_page_illustration_%28lightened_and_cropped%29.jpg)

- **Date:** 1762
- **Type:** Opera (azione teatrale), three acts
- **Premiere / first published:** Burgtheater, Vienna, 5 October 1762
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Orfeo%20ed%20Euridice%20Christoph%20Willibald%20Gluck)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Orfeo+ed+Euridice+Gluck)

Gluck's Orfeo, with a libretto by Ranieri de' Calzabigi, retells the myth of Orpheus and Eurydice in a simple, concentrated form with only three singers and a chorus. The title role was written for the alto castrato Gaetano Guadagni. In place of long da capo arias and showy ornamentation, Gluck used orchestrally accompanied recitative, choruses, dances and simple, expressive airs. The scene in which Orpheus calms the Furies at the gates of Hades and the Dance of the Blessed Spirits are famous. The opera ends happily, as Amor restores Eurydice. Gluck revised it for Paris in 1774, for a high tenor, and in 1859 Berlioz prepared an influential edition for the mezzo-soprano Pauline Viardot. Che farò senza Euridice is one of the most famous arias of the eighteenth century.
