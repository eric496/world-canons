# Guillaume de Machaut

**c. 1300 – 1377** · French

> Poet and composer, Machaut was the dominant figure of the French Ars nova, and his Messe de Nostre Dame is the earliest complete setting of the Mass by a single known composer.

## Biography

Machaut was born around 1300, probably in Champagne, and was educated as a cleric. From about 1323 he served as secretary to John of Luxembourg, King of Bohemia, travelling with him across Europe until John's death at the battle of Crécy in 1346. He was rewarded with a canonry at Reims Cathedral, where he spent much of the rest of his life, while also serving as poet and musician to members of the French royal family and the high nobility. In his later years he supervised lavish manuscript collections of his complete works, an unusual concern with his own legacy. He died in Reims in 1377.

Machaut was as famous a poet as a composer, and his long narrative poems influenced Chaucer. His music includes motets, often isorhythmic, built on repeating patterns of rhythm and pitch, and more than a hundred songs in the courtly fixed forms of ballade, rondeau and virelai, with refined, supple melodies over two or three lower parts. His rondeau Ma fin est mon commencement can be sung forwards and backwards. The Messe de Nostre Dame, probably for the cathedral at Reims, gives the mass a unity unknown before. Because his works were collected so carefully, they survive more fully than those of any other medieval composer.

### Ma fin est mon commencement (c. 1360s)

- **Date:** c. 1360s
- **Type:** Rondeau (three voices)
- **Premiere / first published:** Preserved in Machaut's collected manuscripts
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ma%20fin%20est%20mon%20commencement%20Guillaume%20de%20Machaut)

The title of this rondeau, 'My end is my beginning', describes its construction. The top voice, sung backwards, gives the second voice, and the third voice reaches its midpoint and then repeats itself in reverse, so that the whole piece is a palindrome. Machaut's text explains the puzzle, which was meant as much to be solved by the eye as heard. Despite the ingenuity, the music works as a graceful courtly song in the rondeau form, with its refrain returning between verses. The motto was later embroidered by Mary, Queen of Scots, and T. S. Eliot inverted it in Four Quartets. The piece is a good example of the delight in intellectual design that runs through medieval music.

### Messe de Nostre Dame (c. 1360s)

[![Messe de Nostre Dame](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Guillaume_de_Machaut_-_Kyrie_da_Missa_de_Notre-Dame.jpg/960px-Guillaume_de_Machaut_-_Kyrie_da_Missa_de_Notre-Dame.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Guillaume_de_Machaut_-_Kyrie_da_Missa_de_Notre-Dame.jpg)

<sub>The Kyrie in a fourteenth-century Machaut manuscript Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Guillaume_de_Machaut_-_Kyrie_da_Missa_de_Notre-Dame.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1360s
- **Type:** Polyphonic mass for four voices
- **Premiere / first published:** Probably Reims Cathedral
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Messe%20de%20Nostre%20Dame%20Guillaume%20de%20Machaut)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Messe+de+Nostre+Dame+Machaut)

Machaut's Messe de Nostre Dame sets the six main sung sections of the Mass Ordinary, Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei and the closing Ite missa est, for four voices. It was probably written in the 1360s for Reims Cathedral, where Machaut was a canon, and may have been sung at a Saturday mass of the Virgin for which he and his brother endowed prayers after their deaths. Earlier masses had been assembled from pieces by different composers; this is the first known complete cycle conceived by one author. The Gloria and Credo, with their long texts, move in mostly chordal style, while the other movements are isorhythmic, built on chant melodies laid out in repeating rhythmic patterns in the tenor. The harmony, with its open fifths and sharp dissonances, sounds austere and strange to modern ears. It is the best-known work of fourteenth-century music.
