## Biography

Jean-Philippe Rameau was born in Dijon in 1683, the son of an organist, and spent many years as an organist in provincial cathedrals. His Traité de l'harmonie, published in Paris in 1722, made his name as a theorist. He settled in Paris, published harpsichord music, and only at fifty, in 1733, produced his first opera, Hippolyte et Aricie. Its novelty divided audiences between his supporters and the followers of Lully. Over the next three decades he wrote some thirty stage works, including Les Indes galantes and Castor et Pollux, and became court composer to Louis XV. He died in Paris in 1764.

Rameau's Traité argued that harmony rests on a fundamental bass and on chords built from the natural resonance of sound, ideas that underpin the teaching of harmony to this day. His operas are richer in harmony and orchestral colour than Lully's, with vivid descriptive passages and inventive dances. In the Querelle des Bouffons of the 1750s, Rousseau and other philosophes attacked French opera in favour of Italian comic opera, making Rameau the figurehead of the old style. Debussy later hailed him as a model of French clarity.

## Key works

### Hippolyte et Aricie (1733)

- **Date:** 1733
- **Type:** Tragédie en musique, prologue and five acts
- **Premiere / first published:** Paris Opéra (Académie Royale de Musique), 1 October 1733
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hippolyte%20et%20Aricie%20Jean-Philippe%20Rameau)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Hippolyte+et+Aricie+Rameau)

Rameau was fifty when this opera, with a libretto by the Abbé Simon-Joseph Pellegrin after Racine's Phèdre, was staged in Paris in 1733. It tells of the love of Hippolyte, son of Theseus, and Aricie, threatened by the jealousy of his stepmother Phèdre. While keeping Lully's form of the tragédie en musique, Rameau brought a new richness of harmony and orchestration, with vivid instrumental scenes such as a storm and a hunt. The second trio of the Fates was so difficult that the orchestra could not perform it as written. The work provoked a dispute between the supporters of Rameau, the 'Ramistes', and the defenders of Lully, the 'Lullistes'. It remains a centrepiece of the French Baroque revival.
