# Pérotin

**fl. c. 1200 – c. 1230** · French  
Born: probably France · Died: Paris  
Eras & schools: [Ars antiqua (Notre-Dame school)](../movements/ars-antiqua.md)  

> Pérotin revised Léonin's Magnus liber and composed monumental organum for three and four voices, among the earliest music of such scale in Europe.

## Biography

Pérotin, called Perotinus Magnus by Anonymous IV, worked at Notre-Dame in Paris around 1200. Little is certain about his life; some scholars have linked him with a cathedral official called Petrus, but this is debated. Anonymous IV credits him with improving Léonin's Magnus liber organi by writing shorter discant sections, called clausulae, and with composing organum for four voices, including Viderunt omnes and Sederunt principes. Edicts issued by the Bishop of Paris in 1198 and 1199 calling for such organa at Christmas and on St Stephen's Day help date this music.

Pérotin's four-voice organa build huge blocks of sound above sustained chant notes, with the upper voices locked in bright, dancing rhythmic patterns that interlock and exchange phrases. The effect is hypnotic, and twentieth-century listeners have compared it with minimalism; Steve Reich among others admired it. His clausulae, once words were added to their upper voices, became one of the sources of the motet, the main genre of the thirteenth century. With Léonin he stands at the head of the history of Western composition.

## Key works

### Viderunt omnes (organum quadruplum) (1198)

- **Date:** 1198
- **Type:** Four-voice organum
- **Premiere / first published:** Notre-Dame Cathedral, Paris (Christmas, probably from 1198)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Viderunt%20omnes%20%28organum%20quadruplum%29%20P%C3%A9rotin)

Viderunt omnes is the gradual chant for Christmas Day, and Pérotin's setting for four voices is one of the grandest works of the Notre-Dame school. An edict of Bishop Odo of Sully in 1198 ordered organum in three or four parts for the Christmas season, and this setting is usually linked with it. The lowest voice sings the original chant in enormously long held notes, so that a single syllable may last a minute, while three upper voices weave rhythmic patterns above it in the long-short rhythmic modes, echoing and swapping phrases. The result is a radiant wall of sound suited to the vast new Gothic cathedral then under construction. Only the solo portions of the chant were set in polyphony; the choir sang the rest in plain chant. The work survives in several manuscripts of the Magnus liber and has become one of the most recorded pieces of medieval music.
