# Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

**1756 – 1791** · Austrian  
Born: Salzburg · Died: Vienna  
Eras & schools: [Galant & Classical](../movements/classical-era.md)  

> A child prodigy who became one of the greatest of all composers, Mozart excelled in every genre of his day, from opera and the piano concerto to the symphony and chamber music.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Mozart,_Wolfgang_Amadeus)

## Biography

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was born in Salzburg in 1756, the son of Leopold Mozart, a violinist and composer in the service of the Prince-Archbishop. With his sister Nannerl he was taken on long tours as a child prodigy, performing before the courts of Vienna, Paris and London, and in Italy he wrote operas for Milan while still in his teens. Back in Salzburg he chafed under Archbishop Colloredo, and in 1781 he broke with him and settled in Vienna as a freelance pianist, teacher and composer. He married Constanze Weber in 1782. The 1780s brought his great piano concertos and, with the librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte, The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and Così fan tutte. In 1791 he wrote The Magic Flute for a suburban theatre and began a Requiem, which he left unfinished when he died in December that year, aged thirty-five. Rumours that he was poisoned, by Salieri or anyone else, have no basis.

Mozart wrote more than six hundred works. His music combines melodic grace and perfect proportion with emotional depth and, in the operas, an unrivalled insight into character, so that several people can sing at once and each remain themselves. He raised the piano concerto into a dramatic dialogue and in his last symphonies and quartets reached a new complexity, including Bach-inspired counterpoint. Haydn told Leopold that his son was the greatest composer he knew. Mozart's work became a touchstone of the Classical style.

### Piano Concerto No. 20 in D minor (1785)

- **Date:** 1785
- **Type:** Piano concerto, K. 466
- **Premiere / first published:** Mehlgrube Casino, Vienna, 11 February 1785, with Mozart as soloist
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Piano%20Concerto%20No.%2020%20in%20D%20minor%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Piano+Concerto+No.20+Mozart)

Mozart wrote a series of piano concertos for his subscription concerts in Vienna in the 1780s, and this was the first in a minor key. The orchestra opens with quiet syncopated unease in the strings and menacing rumblings in the bass, before the soloist enters with a new, plaintive theme. The slow movement, a Romance, is serene but has a stormy central section, and the finale returns to D minor before ending in D major. Leopold Mozart heard the premiere and wrote that the concerto was magnificent; Mozart had finished copying the parts only on the day. Beethoven admired the work and wrote cadenzas for it, and in the nineteenth century it was one of the few Mozart concertos that stayed in the repertoire.

### The Marriage of Figaro (Le nozze di Figaro) (1786)

- **Date:** 1786
- **Type:** Opera buffa, four acts, K. 492
- **Premiere / first published:** Burgtheater, Vienna, 1 May 1786
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Marriage%20of%20Figaro%20%28Le%20nozze%20di%20Figaro%29%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Le+nozze+di+Figaro+Mozart)

The Marriage of Figaro was the first of Mozart's three operas with the librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte. It is based on Beaumarchais's play of 1784, which had been banned in Vienna for its mockery of the aristocracy; Da Ponte toned down the politics to win the emperor's approval. Over one day, the servants Figaro and Susanna outwit their master, Count Almaviva, who wants to exercise his feudal claim on Susanna before her wedding, with the help of the neglected Countess and the lovesick page Cherubino. Mozart's ensembles, especially the long finales of acts two and four, follow the comic action and the characters' shifting emotions in continuous music. The Countess's arias and the final scene of forgiveness give the comedy unexpected depth. The opera was more successful in Prague than in Vienna, leading to the commission of Don Giovanni. It is one of the most performed operas in the world.

### Don Giovanni (1787)

- **Date:** 1787
- **Type:** Dramma giocoso, two acts, K. 527
- **Premiere / first published:** Estates Theatre (National Theatre), Prague, 29 October 1787
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Don%20Giovanni%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Don+Giovanni+Mozart)

Commissioned for Prague after the success of Figaro there, Don Giovanni was again written with Da Ponte. It opens with Don Giovanni killing the Commendatore, father of Donna Anna, whom he has tried to seduce, and follows his further conquests and evasions, with his servant Leporello cataloguing his 2,065 lovers in a famous aria. In the final scene the Commendatore's statue, which Giovanni has mockingly invited to dinner, arrives and drags the unrepentant libertine down to hell, announced by the trombones and the dark D minor music of the overture. Mozart blends comedy and tragedy, which Da Ponte's subtitle dramma giocoso reflects. For the Romantics, from E. T. A. Hoffmann to Kierkegaard, it was the opera of operas. The autograph score is in the Bibliothèque nationale de France.

### Eine kleine Nachtmusik (1787)

- **Date:** 1787
- **Type:** Serenade for strings, K. 525
- **Premiere / first published:** Entered in Mozart's catalogue, Vienna, 10 August 1787; published c. 1827
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Eine%20kleine%20Nachtmusik%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Eine+kleine+Nachtmusik+Mozart)

Mozart entered this serenade in his own catalogue of works on 10 August 1787, while he was writing Don Giovanni. The title simply means 'a little serenade'. Why he wrote it is unknown, and it was published only after his death. It is scored for string quartet with double bass or string orchestra and has four movements, a brisk Allegro, a gentle Romanze, a minuet and a lively rondo; Mozart's catalogue lists a second minuet that is now lost. Its perfect balance and unforgettable melodies have made it one of the most familiar pieces in classical music, endlessly used in films and advertising.

### Symphony No. 41 in C major, 'Jupiter' (1788)

- **Date:** 1788
- **Type:** Symphony, K. 551
- **Premiere / first published:** Composed in Vienna, August 1788; first performance date uncertain
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%2041%20in%20C%20major%2C%20%27Jupiter%27%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.41+Mozart)

In the summer of 1788 Mozart wrote his last three symphonies, Nos. 39, 40 and 41, in about six weeks. Whether they were performed in his lifetime is uncertain, though it is likely. The nickname Jupiter was given later, probably by the London impresario Salomon, reflecting the grandeur of the work. Its four movements combine festive C major brilliance with operatic contrasts. The finale is a marvel of contrapuntal skill: its opening four-note motif, a chant formula used in many earlier works, and four other themes are developed in fugal textures, and in the coda all five are combined at once. It is often seen as the culmination of the eighteenth-century symphony.

### The Magic Flute (Die Zauberflöte) (1791)

- **Date:** 1791
- **Type:** Singspiel, two acts, K. 620
- **Premiere / first published:** Theater auf der Wieden, Vienna, 30 September 1791
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Magic%20Flute%20%28Die%20Zauberfl%C3%B6te%29%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Die+Zauberfl%C3%B6te+Mozart)

The Magic Flute was written for Emanuel Schikaneder's popular suburban theatre in Vienna; Schikaneder wrote the libretto and played the bird-catcher Papageno. In its fairy-tale plot, the prince Tamino is sent by the Queen of the Night to rescue her daughter Pamina from the priest Sarastro, only to discover that Sarastro represents wisdom and that the Queen is the villain. Tamino and Pamina undergo trials of silence, fire and water to enter Sarastro's brotherhood, while Papageno finds his Papagena. The work is filled with Masonic symbolism, as both Mozart and Schikaneder were Freemasons. Musically it ranges from folk-like songs to Bachian chorales and the Queen of the Night's astonishing coloratura, rising to high F. A popular success from the start, it was Mozart's last opera, staged ten weeks before his death.

### Requiem in D minor (1791 (unfinished))

- **Date:** 1791 (unfinished)
- **Type:** Requiem mass, K. 626
- **Premiere / first published:** Commissioned anonymously by Count Franz von Walsegg; first performed in Vienna, 2 January 1793 (benefit for Constanze Mozart)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Requiem%20in%20D%20minor%20Wolfgang%20Amadeus%20Mozart)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Requiem+Mozart)

In the summer of 1791 Mozart received an anonymous commission for a requiem. The patron, Count Franz von Walsegg, intended to pass it off as his own composition in memory of his wife. When Mozart died in December, only the opening Introit was complete; for the following movements up to the Offertory he had written the vocal parts and bass line with indications for the orchestra, and the Lacrimosa broke off after eight bars. His widow Constanze, needing the fee, had the work finished, first by Joseph Eybler and then by Mozart's pupil Franz Xaver Süssmayr, who claimed to have composed the Sanctus, Benedictus and Agnus Dei. How much of these sections reflects Mozart's own sketches is debated, and many modern scholars have produced alternative completions. The dark colours of basset horns and trombones and the dramatic Dies irae make it one of the best-loved choral works, surrounded by legends of a mysterious messenger and poisoning.
