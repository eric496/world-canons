## Biography

Robert Schumann was born in Zwickau in 1810, the son of a bookseller and publisher, and grew up steeped in literature. He studied law but turned to music, training as a pianist with Friedrich Wieck in Leipzig until an injury to his right hand ended his hopes of a concert career. In 1834 he founded the Neue Zeitschrift für Musik, writing as the impetuous Florestan and the dreamy Eusebius. After a long legal battle with Wieck he married Wieck's daughter Clara in 1840, the year he wrote some 140 songs. He worked in Leipzig, Dresden and from 1850 as music director in Düsseldorf. He suffered from severe depression; in 1854 he threw himself into the Rhine and was committed at his own request to an asylum at Endenich, where he died in 1856.

Schumann's piano music, including Carnaval, Kinderszenen and Kreisleriana, consists of cycles of short, characterful pieces full of hidden references, codes and literary allusions. His songs, such as Dichterliebe, give the piano a poetic voice of its own. He also wrote four symphonies, a Piano Concerto and chamber music. In his last essay, New Paths, he hailed the young Brahms. His blend of intimacy and fantasy is the essence of German Romanticism.

### Kinderszenen (1838)

[![Kinderszenen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ed/Schumann_-_Kinderszenen%2C_Op15_-_Score_1st_page.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Schumann_-_Kinderszenen%2C_Op15_-_Score_1st_page.jpg)

<sub>First page of the score Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Schumann_-_Kinderszenen%2C_Op15_-_Score_1st_page.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1838
- **Type:** Piano cycle, Op. 15
- **Premiere / first published:** Published Leipzig, 1839
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kinderszenen%20Robert%20Schumann)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Kinderszenen+Schumann)

Schumann wrote Kinderszenen (Scenes from Childhood) in 1838, telling Clara that they were reminiscences of childhood by a grown-up for grown-ups, and not pieces for children. The thirteen miniatures, with titles added afterwards, such as Of Foreign Lands and Peoples, Catch Me, Frightening and Child Falling Asleep, are simple in texture but subtle in harmony and in the links between pieces. The seventh, Träumerei (Dreaming), became one of the most famous piano pieces, and Vladimir Horowitz often played it as an encore. The cycle ends with The Poet Speaks, a quiet reflection. It typifies Schumann's art of poetic character pieces.

### Dichterliebe (1840)

- **Date:** 1840
- **Type:** Song cycle, Op. 48
- **Premiere / first published:** Published Leipzig, 1844
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dichterliebe%20Robert%20Schumann)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Dichterliebe+Schumann)

Schumann wrote Dichterliebe (A Poet's Love) in a single week in May 1840, his 'year of song', when he finally won the legal battle to marry Clara Wieck. It sets sixteen poems from Heinrich Heine's Lyrisches Intermezzo. The cycle moves from the first song, in which love blossoms in the lovely month of May, through dreams, tears and bitter irony, to a final song in which the poet buries his love in a huge coffin. The opening song, which hovers without a firm key and ends on an unresolved chord, is famous. The piano has its own voice, with long postludes that comment on and complete the songs. Dichterliebe is one of the central song cycles of the repertoire.
