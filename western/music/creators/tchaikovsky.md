# Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

**1840 – 1893** · Russian  
Born: Votkinsk, Russia · Died: Saint Petersburg  
Eras & schools: [High Romantic & opera](../movements/high-romantic.md), [Nationalist schools](../movements/nationalist-schools.md)  

> The most popular Russian composer, Tchaikovsky wrote the ballets Swan Lake, The Sleeping Beauty and The Nutcracker, six symphonies and the opera Eugene Onegin.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Tchaikovsky,_Pyotr)

## Biography

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky was born in 1840 in Votkinsk in the Urals, the son of a mining engineer. He trained at the School of Jurisprudence in Saint Petersburg and worked as a civil servant before studying at the new Saint Petersburg Conservatory under Anton Rubinstein. From 1866 he taught at the Moscow Conservatory. His disastrous marriage in 1877 to a former student, entered partly to hide his homosexuality, collapsed within weeks. For thirteen years he received an allowance from the wealthy widow Nadezhda von Meck, with whom he corresponded intensely but never met, which allowed him to devote himself to composition. He became internationally famous, conducting in Europe and opening Carnegie Hall in New York in 1891. He died in Saint Petersburg in 1893, nine days after conducting the premiere of his Sixth Symphony; the official cause was cholera, though other theories have circulated and are disputed.

Tchaikovsky combined Russian melody with Western training in counterpoint and form. His music is direct and emotionally open, rich in long melodies, brilliant orchestration and dance rhythms. His ballets, created for the Imperial Theatres, raised ballet music to symphonic stature. Though members of the Mighty Handful thought him too Westernised, he is now seen as a quintessentially Russian voice, and works such as the First Piano Concerto, the Violin Concerto and the Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Symphonies remain cornerstones of the repertoire.

### Swan Lake (1875–76)

- **Date:** 1875–76
- **Type:** Ballet, four acts, Op. 20
- **Premiere / first published:** Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow, 4 March 1877; Petipa–Ivanov revival Mariinsky Theatre, Saint Petersburg, 1895
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Swan%20Lake%20Pyotr%20Ilyich%20Tchaikovsky)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Swan+Lake+Tchaikovsky)

Tchaikovsky was commissioned to write Swan Lake by the Bolshoi Theatre in Moscow. Prince Siegfried falls in love with Odette, a princess turned into a swan by the sorcerer Rothbart, who can become human only at night. At a ball, Rothbart's daughter Odile, disguised as Odette, tricks Siegfried into swearing love to her, and the lovers die. The first production was poorly received, with weak choreography and staging. After Tchaikovsky's death the choreographers Marius Petipa and Lev Ivanov created a new version in Saint Petersburg in 1895, which is the basis of most later productions, with its white acts for the corps de ballet of swans. Tchaikovsky's symphonic, richly melodic score transformed ballet music, and the work is perhaps the most famous ballet in the world.

### Eugene Onegin (1877–78)

- **Date:** 1877–78
- **Type:** Opera (lyric scenes), three acts, Op. 24
- **Premiere / first published:** Maly Theatre, Moscow, by Conservatory students, 29 March 1879
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Eugene%20Onegin%20Pyotr%20Ilyich%20Tchaikovsky)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Eugene+Onegin+Tchaikovsky)

Tchaikovsky called Eugene Onegin 'lyric scenes' rather than an opera, and it follows Pushkin's verse novel. The young Tatyana falls in love with the worldly Onegin and confesses her love in a long letter, which he coolly rejects. Onegin later kills his friend Lensky in a duel. Years later, he meets Tatyana, now married to a prince, and falls in love with her, but she refuses him. The famous Letter Scene, in which Tatyana writes through the night, was the first part Tchaikovsky composed. Written at the time of his own disastrous marriage, it was premiered by student performers at his request, because he feared the conventions of the Imperial Theatres. It is the most popular Russian opera.

### The Nutcracker (1891–92)

- **Date:** 1891–92
- **Type:** Ballet, two acts, Op. 71
- **Premiere / first published:** Mariinsky Theatre, Saint Petersburg, 18 December 1892
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Nutcracker%20Pyotr%20Ilyich%20Tchaikovsky)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Nutcracker+Tchaikovsky)

The Nutcracker is based on Alexandre Dumas's adaptation of E. T. A. Hoffmann's story The Nutcracker and the Mouse King, and was choreographed by Lev Ivanov from Petipa's scenario. At a Christmas Eve party, young Clara receives a nutcracker that comes to life, battles the Mouse King and takes her to the Land of Sweets, where dances of many lands are performed. For the Sugar Plum Fairy, Tchaikovsky used the celesta, a new instrument he had discovered in Paris. The premiere, in a double bill with his opera Iolanta, was not a great success, but the suite he extracted earlier was popular. From the 1950s, especially George Balanchine's 1954 New York production, the ballet became a Christmas tradition worldwide.

### Symphony No. 6 in B minor, 'Pathétique' (1893)

- **Date:** 1893
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 74
- **Premiere / first published:** Saint Petersburg, 28 October 1893, conducted by the composer
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%206%20in%20B%20minor%2C%20%27Path%C3%A9tique%27%20Pyotr%20Ilyich%20Tchaikovsky)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.6+Tchaikovsky)

Tchaikovsky wrote his last symphony in 1893 and considered it the best thing he had composed. The title Pathétique, roughly 'passionate' or 'emotional' in Russian, was suggested by his brother Modest. It breaks with symphonic tradition by ending in a slow, quiet movement that fades away into darkness, after a march-like third movement whose energetic conclusion often prompts applause. The second movement is a graceful waltz in an irregular 5/4 metre. Tchaikovsky conducted the premiere nine days before his death, and the symphony has been heard as a requiem for himself, an interpretation encouraged by speculation about the circumstances of his death, though he had planned a hidden programme of his own that he never disclosed.
