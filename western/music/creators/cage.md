# John Cage

**1912 – 1992** · American  
Born: Los Angeles · Died: New York  
Eras & schools: [Post-war avant-garde](../movements/postwar-avant-garde.md)  

> Cage's chance operations, prepared piano and the silent piece 4′33″ transformed ideas of what music could be.

## Biography

John Cage was born in Los Angeles in 1912, the son of an inventor. He studied with Henry Cowell and with Schoenberg, who later said he was not a composer but an inventor of genius. In Seattle and then New York he wrote for percussion ensembles and invented the prepared piano, with objects placed between the strings. From the 1940s he was the partner and collaborator of the choreographer Merce Cunningham. Study of Zen Buddhism and the I Ching led him from 1951 to compose by chance operations, as in Music of Changes, and in 1952 to the silent piece 4′33″. He also wrote influential books, notably Silence (1961), was a noted mycologist, and remained a central figure of the American avant-garde until his death in New York in 1992.

Cage aimed to free sounds from the composer's intentions, letting them be themselves. His use of chance, indeterminacy, electronics and everyday sounds influenced Fluxus, performance art, minimalism and later experimental music. He remains among the most debated figures of twentieth-century art.

### Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48)

- **Date:** 1946–48
- **Type:** Cycle for prepared piano
- **Premiere / first published:** Carnegie Recital Hall, New York, 12 January 1949, Maro Ajemian
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sonatas%20and%20Interludes%20John%20Cage)

Cage invented the prepared piano in 1940 in Seattle, when he needed a percussion sound for a dance but had no room for percussion instruments. In Sonatas and Interludes, sixteen sonatas and four interludes, he prescribed precisely where to place screws, bolts, pieces of rubber and plastic between the strings of 45 notes, turning the piano into a delicate percussion orchestra that recalls the Balinese gamelan. Cage had become interested in Indian philosophy, and the cycle attempts to express the nine permanent emotions of Indian aesthetics. It is among his most performed works and won him a Guggenheim fellowship.

### 4′33″ (1952)

- **Date:** 1952
- **Type:** Piece in three movements for any instrument(s)
- **Premiere / first published:** Maverick Concert Hall, Woodstock, New York, 29 August 1952, David Tudor (piano)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=4%E2%80%B233%E2%80%B3%20John%20Cage)

In 4′33″ the performer or performers are instructed not to play their instruments for the duration of the piece, divided into three movements. At the premiere the pianist David Tudor sat at the piano, closed the lid to mark the start of each movement and opened it at the end, while the audience heard the wind, rain on the roof and their own murmurs. Cage was inspired by Zen Buddhism, by Robert Rauschenberg's all-white paintings and by his visit to an anechoic chamber at Harvard, where he heard sounds of his own body and concluded that there is no such thing as silence. The piece is about listening to the sounds of the environment as music. It was mocked and celebrated in equal measure and is one of the most discussed works of the twentieth century.
