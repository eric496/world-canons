## Biography

Byrd was born around 1540 in London and probably studied with Tallis. In 1563 he became organist of Lincoln Cathedral, and in 1572 he joined the Chapel Royal, sharing its organist's post with Tallis. Although he and his family were recusants, repeatedly fined for refusing Anglican services, he kept the favour of Elizabeth I. In the 1590s he moved to Stondon Massey in Essex, near the Catholic household of Sir John Petre, and wrote music for the clandestine masses held there. He died in 1623.

Byrd's output covers almost every genre of his time. His three masses, for three, four and five voices, were printed discreetly without title pages in the 1590s, and his two books of Gradualia provided music for the Catholic church year. For the Anglican rite he wrote the Great Service and many anthems. His keyboard music, collected in My Ladye Nevells Booke and the Fitzwilliam Virginal Book, laid the foundations of an English keyboard school, and his consort songs and music for viols are equally distinguished.

## Key works

### Mass for Five Voices (c. 1594–95)

- **Date:** c. 1594–95
- **Type:** Mass
- **Premiere / first published:** Printed privately in London without title page, c. 1595
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mass%20for%20Five%20Voices%20William%20Byrd)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Mass+for+five+voices+Byrd)

Byrd published three masses, for four, three and five voices, between about 1592 and 1595, each a single slim set of part-books without a title page, which would have reduced the danger of owning them. They were intended for Catholic masses held in private houses, which were illegal in Elizabethan England, such as those at the Petre family's house in Essex. The five-voice mass is the most richly textured, using imitation and expressive word-setting with restraint suited to small groups of singers. Unlike Continental masses, which usually reworked existing music, Byrd's are freely composed. The Agnus Dei, with its plea for peace, is often heard as an expression of the plight of English Catholics. The masses were revived in the twentieth century and are sung in both Catholic and Anglican cathedrals.
