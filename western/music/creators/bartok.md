# Béla Bartók

**1881 – 1945** · Hungarian  
Born: Nagyszentmiklós, Kingdom of Hungary · Died: New York  
Eras & schools: [Second Viennese School & modernism](../movements/second-viennese-school.md), [Nationalist schools](../movements/nationalist-schools.md)  

> Composer, pianist and pioneering folk-music collector, Bartók forged a modern style from Eastern European peasant music.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Bartók,_Béla)

## Biography

Béla Bartók was born in 1881 in Nagyszentmiklós, then in Hungary and now Sânnicolau Mare in Romania. He studied piano and composition at the Budapest Academy of Music, where he later taught piano for nearly three decades. From 1905, with his friend Zoltán Kodály, he travelled through villages in Hungary, Slovakia, Romania and as far as North Africa and Turkey, recording thousands of peasant songs on wax cylinders and transcribing them in meticulous detail. His opera Bluebeard's Castle and the ballet The Miraculous Mandarin date from the 1910s. Opposed to the Nazis and to Hungary's alliance with Germany, he emigrated to the United States in 1940, where he lived in poor health and financial difficulty. The Concerto for Orchestra, commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky, was a success in 1944. He died of leukaemia in New York in 1945.

Bartók distinguished real peasant music from the urban gypsy style that Liszt and Brahms had used as 'Hungarian'. He absorbed its modes, irregular rhythms and ornaments into a style of great energy and rigour, using dissonance, symmetry and night music evoking nocturnal nature sounds. His six string quartets are among the most important since Beethoven's. His teaching pieces, the six books of Mikrokosmos, trace a path from simple exercises to concert works, and his ethnomusicological work remains a model.

### Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta (1936)

- **Date:** 1936
- **Type:** Orchestral work, Sz. 106
- **Premiere / first published:** Basel, 21 January 1937, Basel Chamber Orchestra conducted by Paul Sacher
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Music%20for%20Strings%2C%20Percussion%20and%20Celesta%20B%C3%A9la%20Bart%C3%B3k)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Music+for+Strings+Percussion+and+Celesta+Bart%C3%B3k)

Paul Sacher commissioned this work for the tenth anniversary of the Basel Chamber Orchestra. Bartók divided the strings into two groups placed on either side of the stage, with percussion, piano, harp and celesta in the middle. The first movement is a slow fugue that grows from a single chromatic subject and expands and contracts symmetrically, its climax placed close to the golden section, a proportion some analysts find throughout Bartók's music. The third movement, an eerie Adagio, is a classic example of his night music, with glissandi on timpani and shimmering celesta. Stanley Kubrick used it in The Shining (1980).

### Concerto for Orchestra (1943)

- **Date:** 1943
- **Type:** Orchestral concerto, Sz. 116
- **Premiere / first published:** Symphony Hall, Boston, 1 December 1944, Boston Symphony Orchestra conducted by Serge Koussevitzky
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Concerto%20for%20Orchestra%20B%C3%A9la%20Bart%C3%B3k)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Concerto+for+Orchestra+Bart%C3%B3k)

Bartók, ill and depressed in American exile, received a commission for an orchestral work from Serge Koussevitzky in 1943, which revived him. He explained that the title referred to the way single instruments or groups are treated in a concertante or soloistic manner. Its five movements move from darkness to life. The second, Game of Pairs, presents wind instruments in pairs at different intervals; the fourth, Interrupted Intermezzo, parodies a march theme from Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony, then widely broadcast; and the brilliant finale is based on folk dances. It became one of the most popular orchestral works of the twentieth century.
