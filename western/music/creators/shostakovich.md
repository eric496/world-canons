# Dmitri Shostakovich

**1906 – 1975** · Russian  
Born: Saint Petersburg, Russia · Died: Moscow  
Eras & schools: [Neoclassicism & mid-century tonal modernism](../movements/neoclassicism-music.md)  

> The leading Soviet composer, Shostakovich wrote fifteen symphonies and fifteen string quartets under the shadow of Stalinist censorship.

## Biography

Dmitri Shostakovich was born in Saint Petersburg in 1906 and studied at the Petrograd Conservatory, where his First Symphony (1926) won international attention. His opera Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District was a success until January 1936, when an article in Pravda, reportedly after Stalin attended, condemned it as 'muddle instead of music'. Facing the danger of arrest, he withdrew his Fourth Symphony and in 1937 presented the Fifth, a public rehabilitation. His Seventh Symphony, partly written in besieged Leningrad, became a symbol of resistance. In 1948 he was denounced again for formalism and lost his teaching posts. After Stalin's death he gradually regained position, joining the Communist Party in 1960. He died in Moscow in 1975.

Shostakovich's music combines Mahlerian scale, biting irony, grotesque humour, banal marches and profound tragedy. Whether works such as the Fifth Symphony carry hidden dissent is much debated, especially since the disputed memoir Testimony, published in 1979. His fifteen string quartets, more private in tone, include the Eighth, often heard as autobiographical, and he frequently used his musical monogram DSCH (D, E flat, C, B). He is among the most performed composers of the twentieth century.

### Symphony No. 5 in D minor (1937)

- **Date:** 1937
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 47
- **Premiere / first published:** Leningrad Philharmonic Hall, 21 November 1937, conducted by Yevgeny Mravinsky
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%205%20in%20D%20minor%20Dmitri%20Shostakovich)

Shostakovich wrote his Fifth Symphony in 1937, at the height of Stalin's Great Terror, a year after Pravda's attack on his opera Lady Macbeth. A newspaper article attributed to him called the work a Soviet artist's creative reply to just criticism, though he probably did not choose the phrase. At the premiere the audience gave an ovation of over half an hour, and many listeners wept during the slow movement. The finale ends in a long D major blaze, which can sound triumphal or, as the disputed memoir Testimony claims, a forced rejoicing, like being beaten with a stick and told to celebrate. The debate over its meaning continues. It remains Shostakovich's most popular symphony.

### Symphony No. 7 in C major, 'Leningrad' (1941)

- **Date:** 1941
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 60
- **Premiere / first published:** Kuybyshev (Samara), 5 March 1942; Leningrad, 9 August 1942, during the siege
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%207%20in%20C%20major%2C%20%27Leningrad%27%20Dmitri%20Shostakovich)

Shostakovich began the Seventh Symphony in Leningrad in the summer of 1941, after the German invasion of the Soviet Union, and completed it after being evacuated to Kuybyshev. The first movement contains a long 'invasion' episode in which a banal march theme is repeated over a snare drum, building to a terrifying climax. The score was flown to the West on microfilm, and Toscanini conducted it in New York in 1942. On 9 August 1942 it was performed in besieged Leningrad by the surviving members of the radio orchestra, reinforced by soldiers, and broadcast by loudspeaker towards the German lines. Whether the invasion theme also refers to Stalin's repression has been debated. Later critics dismissed it as propaganda, but it has been regularly revived.

### String Quartet No. 8 in C minor (1960)

- **Date:** 1960
- **Type:** String quartet, Op. 110
- **Premiere / first published:** Glinka Hall, Leningrad, 2 October 1960, Beethoven Quartet
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=String%20Quartet%20No.%208%20in%20C%20minor%20Dmitri%20Shostakovich)

Shostakovich wrote this quartet in three days in July 1960 while in Dresden, a city still in ruins from the Allied bombing of 1945, to work on a film score. Officially it is dedicated to the victims of fascism and war, but in a letter to his friend Isaak Glikman he wrote that he had dedicated it to his own memory. The five movements are played without pause, and nearly every one is based on his musical monogram, D-E flat-C-B (in German D-S-C-H), with quotations from his earlier works, a Jewish theme from his Piano Trio No. 2 and a revolutionary song. It came just after he had been pressured to join the Communist Party. It is his most often performed quartet.
