## Biography

Franz Liszt was born in 1811 at Raiding, in the Hungarian part of the Habsburg empire, where his father worked for the Esterházy family. He studied in Vienna with Czerny and Salieri, then moved to Paris, where Paganini's virtuosity inspired him to transform piano technique. Between 1839 and 1847 he toured Europe from Lisbon to Moscow, provoking the frenzy that Heinrich Heine called Lisztomania. He then settled in Weimar as court music director, championing new music including Wagner's Lohengrin, and writing his symphonic poems. Thwarted in his plan to marry Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein, he moved to Rome and took minor orders in the church in 1865. In later life he divided his time between Rome, Weimar and Budapest, teaching without fee. He died in Bayreuth in 1886, where his daughter Cosima was Wagner's widow.

Liszt's piano works, from the Transcendental Études and Hungarian Rhapsodies to the Sonata in B minor, demanded new technique and helped create the modern piano recital. His symphonic poems, such as Les préludes, were orchestral works inspired by poetry or painting and built through the transformation of themes. His late pieces, spare and harmonically daring, anticipate Debussy, Bartók and even atonality. A generous teacher and promoter of other composers, he was a central figure of the New German School.

### Piano Sonata in B minor (1852–53)

- **Date:** 1852–53
- **Type:** Piano sonata, S. 178
- **Premiere / first published:** Published 1854; dedicated to Robert Schumann; first public performance Berlin, 27 January 1857, by Hans von Bülow
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Piano%20Sonata%20in%20B%20minor%20Franz%20Liszt)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Piano+Sonata+in+B+minor+Liszt)

Liszt completed his only piano sonata in Weimar in 1853 and dedicated it to Robert Schumann, who had dedicated his Fantasy in C to Liszt. Its four main sections, corresponding to the movements of a traditional sonata, are fused into a single continuous movement lasting about thirty minutes, and a handful of themes, such as a descending scale, a hammered motif and a grand chorale, are transformed into new characters throughout. Brahms reportedly fell asleep when Liszt played it to him, and the critic Hanslick condemned it, but Wagner was enthusiastic. Its formal invention influenced later composers, and it is now considered one of the summits of the piano repertoire.

### Les préludes (1848–54)

- **Date:** 1848–54
- **Type:** Symphonic poem, S. 97
- **Premiere / first published:** Weimar Court Theatre, 23 February 1854, conducted by Liszt
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Les%20pr%C3%A9ludes%20Franz%20Liszt)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Les+pr%C3%A9ludes+Liszt)

Les préludes is the third of Liszt's thirteen symphonic poems, one-movement orchestral works inspired by literature or art. It originated as the overture to an unfinished choral work, and Liszt later connected it to a poem by Alphonse de Lamartine, presenting life as a series of preludes to the unknown song of death, a link that scholars think was added after the music was written. A three-note motif is transformed through scenes of love, storm, pastoral peace and a battle-like triumph. The technique of thematic transformation influenced Wagner, Strauss and many others. The work's final fanfare was sadly misused in Nazi newsreels during the Second World War.
