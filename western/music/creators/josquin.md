## Biography

Josquin des Prez was born around 1450, probably in the French-speaking region of Hainaut or Picardy. Documents once thought to trace his early career turned out to belong to another singer, and his biography has been heavily revised by modern research. He served the Sforza family in Milan in the 1480s, sang in the papal chapel in Rome around 1489–94, and in 1503–04 was maestro di cappella to Duke Ercole d'Este in Ferrara, where he wrote the Missa Hercules Dux Ferrariae. He spent his last years as provost of the collegiate church at Condé-sur-l'Escaut, where he died in 1521.

Josquin's music unites technical brilliance with a new attention to the meaning and sound of words. He used pervading imitation, in which all voices take up the same motif in turn, and he paired voices in clear, sonorous dialogue, as in the motet Ave Maria ... virgo serena. His masses range from strict canon to the paraphrase of a chant throughout every voice, as in the Missa Pange lingua. Ottaviano Petrucci, the first printer of polyphonic music, devoted three books to his masses, and Martin Luther said Josquin was master of the notes, which must do as he wished. Some works once credited to him are now judged doubtful, a lively area of scholarly debate.

### Ave Maria ... virgo serena (c. 1485)

- **Date:** c. 1485
- **Type:** Motet (four voices)
- **Premiere / first published:** Printed in Petrucci's Motetti A, Venice, 1502
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ave%20Maria%20...%20virgo%20serena%20Josquin%20des%20Prez)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Ave+Maria+virgo+serena+Josquin+Prez)

This motet, a prayer to the Virgin Mary, was probably written in the 1480s and appeared as the first piece in Petrucci's first book of motets in 1502. It opens with each voice entering in turn with the same phrase, from soprano down to bass, a technique called pervading imitation. Later sections alternate between pairs of voices in dialogue, full textures and a passage in triple time, following the stanzas of the poem, which greets five feasts of Mary's life. It ends with a short plea in chordal style, 'O Mother of God, remember me'. Its clarity, balance and sensitivity to text made it a classic, and it has been called the 'Mona Lisa' of Renaissance music. The dating once proposed as early as the 1470s remains debated.

### Missa L'homme armé super voces musicales (c. 1480s–90s)

- **Date:** c. 1480s–90s
- **Type:** Mass
- **Premiere / first published:** First printed in Petrucci's Misse Josquin, Venice, 1502
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Missa%20L%27homme%20arm%C3%A9%20super%20voces%20musicales%20Josquin%20des%20Prez)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Missa+L%27homme+arm%C3%A9+super+voces+musicales+Josquin+Prez)

The popular song L'homme armé (The armed man) served as the basis for more than forty Renaissance masses, and Josquin wrote two. In the Missa super voces musicales he places the tune in the tenor on a different step of the hexachord (ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la) in each successive movement, so that the melody climbs through the mass. Within this frame he includes mensuration canons, in which one melody is sung simultaneously at different speeds, most famously in the Agnus Dei. The work opened the first printed collection devoted entirely to one composer, Ottaviano Petrucci's Misse Josquin of 1502. It shows how a Renaissance composer could fuse intellectual design with fluent, euphonious music.

### Missa Pange lingua (c. 1515)

- **Date:** c. 1515
- **Type:** Mass
- **Premiere / first published:** Printed posthumously, Nuremberg, 1539
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Missa%20Pange%20lingua%20Josquin%20des%20Prez)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Missa+Pange+lingua+Josquin+Prez)

The Missa Pange lingua is based on the plainchant hymn Pange lingua, sung at the feast of Corpus Christi, and is probably one of Josquin's last masses. Rather than placing the borrowed tune in long notes in the tenor, as older masses did, Josquin paraphrases it freely in all four voices, so that motifs from the hymn pass through the whole texture. The style is spare and transparent, with much writing for pairs of voices, and the Et incarnatus of the Credo moves into hushed chords. The mass circulated in manuscript and was first printed in 1539. It is admired as an example of Josquin's late, concentrated style.
