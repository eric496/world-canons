## Biography

Jean Sibelius was born in 1865 in Hämeenlinna in the Grand Duchy of Finland, then part of the Russian empire, into a Swedish-speaking family. He studied in Helsinki, Berlin and Vienna, and returned home to write works inspired by the Finnish epic Kalevala, beginning with Kullervo (1892). As Russian pressure on Finland increased, works such as Finlandia (1899) became symbols of national resistance, and from 1897 a state pension supported him. In 1904 he moved to Ainola, a house in the forests at Järvenpää. After the Seventh Symphony (1924) and the tone poem Tapiola (1926) he published almost nothing, apparently destroying an Eighth Symphony, and lived three decades of silence before dying in 1957.

Sibelius's symphonies develop themes from small cells into large, organic wholes, with long pedal points, brooding orchestral textures and climaxes evoking the Nordic landscape. He spoke of the profound logic that connects the motifs. Very popular in Britain and the United States in the 1930s, he was later dismissed by modernists, but his treatment of slowly changing textures has influenced composers from spectralists to minimalists.

### Finlandia (1899–1900)

- **Date:** 1899–1900
- **Type:** Tone poem, Op. 26
- **Premiere / first published:** Helsinki, 2 July 1900 (revised version)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Finlandia%20Jean%20Sibelius)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Finlandia+Sibelius)

Finlandia originated in 1899 as the final piece in music for a series of tableaux depicting Finnish history, presented at a 'press celebration' in Helsinki in protest against Russian censorship and restrictions on Finnish autonomy. Sibelius revised it as an independent work in 1900. Its brooding brass opening evokes the struggle of the Finnish people, followed by a stirring Allegro and a hymn-like central melody. Because of its political significance, it was performed under other titles to evade censorship. In 1941 Sibelius arranged the hymn for choir with words by Veikko Antero Koskenniemi, and it is often regarded as Finland's second national anthem.

### Symphony No. 5 in E-flat major (1915; revised 1916 and 1919)

- **Date:** 1915; revised 1916 and 1919
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 82
- **Premiere / first published:** Helsinki, 8 December 1915 (Sibelius's 50th birthday); final version 24 November 1919
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%205%20in%20E-flat%20major%20Jean%20Sibelius)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.5+Sibelius)

Sibelius wrote his Fifth Symphony for celebrations of his fiftieth birthday in 1915 during the First World War, then revised it twice; the final version of 1919 has three movements instead of four. In April 1915 he wrote in his diary of seeing sixteen swans circling above him, one of the greatest experiences of his life. Their motion inspired the great theme of the finale, a swinging, bell-like idea in the horns, which he called the swan hymn. The first movement fuses a slow movement and a scherzo, accelerating continuously; the symphony ends with six widely spaced chords separated by silence. It is the most popular of his symphonies.
