# Orlande de Lassus

**c. 1532 – 1594** · Franco-Flemish  
Born: Mons, Hainaut · Died: Munich  
Eras & schools: [Late Renaissance & Venetian](../movements/late-renaissance-music.md)  

> One of the most prolific and versatile composers of the Renaissance, Lassus wrote more than two thousand works in Latin, French, Italian and German.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Lassus,_Orlande_de)

## Biography

Orlande de Lassus, also known as Orlando di Lasso, was born around 1532 in Mons. According to a story that may be legend, his beautiful voice led to his being kidnapped three times as a boy. He spent his youth in Italy, in the service of the Gonzaga and in Naples and Rome, where he briefly directed the choir of St John Lateran. After time in Antwerp he joined the court of Duke Albrecht V of Bavaria in Munich in 1556, leading its chapel from the 1560s until his death in 1594. He travelled widely and was ennobled by Emperor Maximilian II.

Lassus mastered every genre of his age: masses, motets, Magnificats, Italian madrigals and villanellas, French chansons and German lieder. His style is vivid and dramatic, quick to paint the words in sound, by turns earthy and humorous or intensely penitential, as in the Penitential Psalms and his final cycle, the Lagrime di San Pietro. With Palestrina he represents the summit of late Renaissance polyphony, and his works were printed and reprinted across Europe.

## Key works

### Lagrime di San Pietro (1594)

- **Date:** 1594
- **Type:** Cycle of spiritual madrigals (seven voices)
- **Premiere / first published:** Published in Munich, 1595
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lagrime%20di%20San%20Pietro%20Orlande%20de%20Lassus)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Lagrime+di+San+Pietro+Lassus)

Lassus completed this cycle in May 1594, three weeks before his death, and dedicated it to Pope Clement VIII. It sets twenty stanzas from Luigi Tansillo's poem on Peter's remorse after denying Christ three times, followed by a Latin motet in which Christ speaks from the cross. The seven voices may symbolise the seven sorrows of the Virgin or the seven deadly sins, and the cycle moves systematically through the eight church modes. The music is grave, intensely expressive and harmonically searching, the work of a composer who had suffered from depression in his last years. It is regarded as the culmination of the spiritual madrigal.
