# Giovanni Gabrieli

**c. 1555 – 1612** · Italian  
Born: Venice · Died: Venice  
Eras & schools: [Late Renaissance & Venetian](../movements/late-renaissance-music.md), [Early Baroque & opera](../movements/early-baroque-music.md)  

> Organist at St Mark's in Venice, Gabrieli wrote splendid works for divided choirs and brass that bridged the Renaissance and the Baroque.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Gabrieli,_Giovanni)

## Biography

Giovanni Gabrieli was born in Venice in the mid-1550s and studied with his uncle Andrea Gabrieli, organist at St Mark's. He spent several years in Munich with Lassus, then returned to Venice, becoming organist at St Mark's in 1585 and also at the Scuola Grande di San Rocco. In 1587 he published a collection of his uncle's music together with his own. His fame drew pupils from northern Europe, above all the German Heinrich Schütz. He died in Venice in 1612.

Gabrieli exploited the galleries of St Mark's, placing choirs of voices and instruments in different parts of the basilica for echoes and dialogues, a technique called cori spezzati. His Sacrae symphoniae of 1597 includes the Sonata pian' e forte, among the first pieces to specify both instruments and dynamics. His later works use contrasts of solo and tutti that point towards the Baroque concerto. Through Schütz his style passed into German Protestant music.

## Key works

### Sonata pian' e forte (1597)

- **Date:** 1597
- **Type:** Instrumental canzona for two choirs
- **Premiere / first published:** Published in Sacrae symphoniae, Venice, 1597; St Mark's Basilica
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sonata%20pian%27%20e%20forte%20Giovanni%20Gabrieli)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Sonata+pian+e+forte+Gabrieli)

This short piece from Gabrieli's Sacrae symphoniae of 1597 is scored for two instrumental groups, one led by a cornett and the other by a violin, each with trombones. It was written for the galleries of St Mark's in Venice, where the groups would have answered each other across the space. Its title refers to the dynamic markings piano and forte, which show where one group plays softly alone and where both join loudly. It is among the earliest pieces to call for particular instruments and among the first to mark dynamics, though such practices may have been older. It is a favourite of modern brass ensembles and a landmark in the rise of independent instrumental music.
