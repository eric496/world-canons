## Biography

Johann Sebastian Bach was born in Eisenach in 1685 into a large family of musicians. Orphaned at ten, he was raised by his elder brother in Ohrdruf. After early posts as organist in Arnstadt and Mühlhausen, he served at the court of Weimar (1708–17), where he wrote much of his organ music, and then as Kapellmeister to Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Köthen (1717–23), the period of the Brandenburg Concertos and much chamber music. In 1723 he became Thomaskantor in Leipzig, responsible for the music of the city's main churches, and for some years he wrote a new cantata nearly every week. He had twenty children with two wives, several of whom became important composers. He died in Leipzig in 1750.

Bach mastered every genre of his time except opera and combined the counterpoint of the German tradition with Italian concerto form and French dance styles. His music is often of great intellectual complexity, as in the fugues of The Well-Tempered Clavier and The Art of Fugue, yet also deeply expressive, especially in the Passions and cantatas, which he wrote as acts of Lutheran devotion. In his lifetime he was best known as an organist and his style was thought old-fashioned. Mozart and Beethoven studied his music, and Mendelssohn's 1829 performance of the St Matthew Passion launched a revival that made him a foundation of the Western canon.

### Cello Suites (c. 1717–23)

[![Cello Suites](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/87/Frontespizio_Cello_Suite.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Frontespizio_Cello_Suite.png)

<sub>Title page of Anna Magdalena Bach's manuscript copy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Frontespizio_Cello_Suite.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1717–23
- **Type:** Six suites for unaccompanied cello, BWV 1007–1012
- **Premiere / first published:** Copy by Anna Magdalena Bach, c. 1727–31 (no autograph survives)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Cello%20Suites%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Cello+Suites+Bach)

Bach probably wrote the six suites for unaccompanied cello at Köthen, where the court orchestra had capable string players. Each opens with a prelude followed by a sequence of Baroque dances: allemande, courante, sarabande, a pair of minuets, bourrées or gavottes, and gigue. A single instrument suggests harmony and counterpoint through broken chords and implied voices. The fifth suite requires the top string to be retuned, and the sixth was written for an instrument with five strings. No autograph survives; the main sources are copies by Bach's second wife Anna Magdalena and others. Long regarded as études, the suites were made famous by the Catalan cellist Pablo Casals, who discovered an edition as a teenager and recorded them in 1936–39.

### Brandenburg Concertos (1721 (compiled))

[![Brandenburg Concertos](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Title_page_of_Brandenburg_Concertos.png/1280px-Title_page_of_Brandenburg_Concertos.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Title_page_of_Brandenburg_Concertos.png)

<sub>Title page of Bach's 1721 autograph dedication score Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Title_page_of_Brandenburg_Concertos.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1721 (compiled)
- **Type:** Six concertos, BWV 1046–1051
- **Premiere / first published:** Autograph dedication to Margrave Christian Ludwig of Brandenburg, 24 March 1721; Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Brandenburg%20Concertos%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Brandenburg+Concertos+Bach)

In 1721 Bach sent a beautifully copied set of six concertos to Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg, whom he had met in Berlin, with a dedication in French, perhaps hoping for a post. Most of the music had been written earlier at Weimar and Köthen. Each concerto uses a different combination: No. 2 has a high trumpet, recorder, oboe and violin as soloists; No. 3 is for three groups of strings; No. 5 gives the harpsichord a long solo cadenza, a novelty that points towards the keyboard concerto; No. 6 omits violins altogether. Bach combines the Italian concerto forms of Vivaldi with dense German counterpoint. There is no record that the margrave ever had them played, and the manuscript was rediscovered in the nineteenth century and first published in 1850. They are now among Bach's most popular works.

### The Well-Tempered Clavier (Book I 1722; Book II c. 1742)

[![The Well-Tempered Clavier](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Bach-wtc1-title-ms.jpg/960px-Bach-wtc1-title-ms.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bach-wtc1-title-ms.jpg)

<sub>Autograph title page of Book I, 1722 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bach-wtc1-title-ms.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** Book I 1722; Book II c. 1742
- **Type:** 48 preludes and fugues, BWV 846–893
- **Premiere / first published:** Autograph of Book I (1722), Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin; Book II autograph in the British Library
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Well-Tempered%20Clavier%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Well-Tempered+Clavier+Bach)

Bach compiled the first book of Das wohltemperirte Clavier in 1722 at Köthen, 'for the use and profit of musical youth', and a second book around 1742 in Leipzig. Each book has a prelude and fugue in each of the twenty-four major and minor keys, rising chromatically from C major. Playing in all keys required a tuning system in which all keys sounded acceptable; the term 'well-tempered' probably refers to one of several such tunings rather than modern equal temperament, a point still discussed. The preludes range from simple broken chords, as in the famous C major prelude, to toccatas and dances; the fugues show every variety of contrapuntal technique. The collection circulated in manuscript and was published only in 1801. Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin and generations of pianists studied it, and the conductor Hans von Bülow called it the Old Testament of music.

### St Matthew Passion (1727)

- **Date:** 1727
- **Type:** Passion oratorio, BWV 244
- **Premiere / first published:** St Thomas Church, Leipzig, Good Friday, probably 11 April 1727
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=St%20Matthew%20Passion%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=St+Matthew+Passion+Bach)

The St Matthew Passion was first performed on Good Friday in St Thomas Church, Leipzig, probably in 1727, and revised in 1736. It sets chapters 26 and 27 of Matthew's Gospel, sung by the tenor Evangelist and other soloists, with Jesus' words surrounded by a halo of sustained strings. Between the gospel narrative come reflective arias and choruses to poetry by Picander, and Lutheran chorales sung by the congregation's representative, the choir. The work uses two choirs and two orchestras, and opens with a vast chorus in which a boys' choir sings a chorale above them. Bach's own beautifully written autograph of 1736, with the gospel text in red ink, survives. After Bach's death the Passion was not heard until Felix Mendelssohn conducted it in Berlin on 11 March 1829, a performance that launched the Bach revival.

### Goldberg Variations (1741)

[![Goldberg Variations](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f5/Goldberg-titlepage.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Goldberg-titlepage.png)

<sub>Title page of the first edition, Nuremberg, 1741 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Goldberg-titlepage.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1741
- **Type:** Aria with thirty variations for harpsichord, BWV 988
- **Premiere / first published:** Published by Balthasar Schmid, Nuremberg, 1741
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Goldberg%20Variations%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Goldberg+Variations+Bach)

Bach published this work in 1741 as the fourth part of his Clavier-Übung, an aria with different variations for harpsichord with two manuals. According to his early biographer Forkel, it was written for Count Keyserlingk, who suffered from insomnia, and played by his harpsichordist Johann Gottlieb Goldberg; the story is doubted, as Goldberg was then only about fourteen. The thirty variations are built on the bass line of the opening sarabande. Every third variation is a canon at increasing intervals, from the unison to the ninth, and others are virtuoso showpieces or slow laments; the last is a quodlibet combining folk songs, before the aria returns. Glenn Gould's 1955 recording made the work famous.

### Mass in B minor (1733–1749)

[![Mass in B minor](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/BWV_232_Et_incarnatus.jpg/960px-BWV_232_Et_incarnatus.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:BWV_232_Et_incarnatus.jpg)

<sub>Autograph score, the Et incarnatus est Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:BWV_232_Et_incarnatus.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1733–1749
- **Type:** Missa tota, BWV 232
- **Premiere / first published:** Kyrie and Gloria presented to the Elector of Saxony, Dresden, 1733; first complete performance 1859
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mass%20in%20B%20minor%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Mass+in+B+minor+Bach)

In 1733 Bach presented a Kyrie and Gloria to the new Elector of Saxony in Dresden, seeking a court title. Near the end of his life, around 1748–49, he expanded it into a complete setting of the Latin Mass, writing new music and adapting movements from earlier cantatas, a process called parody. The result has twenty-seven movements for soloists, choir and orchestra, ranging from the archaic counterpoint of the Credo's opening, based on plainchant, to the dancing Gloria and the radiant Sanctus. Why a Lutheran composer completed a full Catholic mass is debated; it may have been a summation of his art, written with no particular performance in mind. It was not performed complete until 1859, and the Swiss publisher Hans Georg Nägeli called it the greatest musical work of all times and peoples.

### The Art of Fugue (c. 1742–1750)

[![The Art of Fugue](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/06/Title_page_of_The_Art_of_Fugue.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Title_page_of_The_Art_of_Fugue.jpg)

<sub>Title page of the first edition, 1751 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Title_page_of_The_Art_of_Fugue.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1742–1750
- **Type:** Collection of fugues and canons, BWV 1080
- **Premiere / first published:** Published posthumously, Leipzig, 1751
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Art%20of%20Fugue%20Johann%20Sebastian%20Bach)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Art+of+Fugue+Bach)

Bach worked on The Art of Fugue in his last decade. It consists of some fourteen fugues, called contrapuncti, and four canons, all derived from a single theme in D minor, presented in increasingly complex forms: in inversion, augmentation, mirror forms that can be played upside down, and multiple subjects. No instrumentation is specified. The final fugue, which introduces the notes B-A-C-H (B flat, A, C, B natural in German naming) as a subject, breaks off. A note by his son Carl Philipp Emanuel says the composer died during its writing, though scholars think it was interrupted earlier. Published in 1751, it sold few copies, but it is now regarded as one of the summits of contrapuntal art.
