## Biography

Fryderyk Chopin was born in 1810 at Żelazowa Wola near Warsaw, to a French father and Polish mother, and grew up in Warsaw, where he studied at the conservatory and was celebrated as a prodigy. He left Poland in 1830, shortly before the November Uprising against Russian rule, and never returned. Settling in Paris in 1831, he preferred playing in aristocratic salons to large concert halls and earned his living mainly by teaching and publishing. From 1838 to 1847 he had a relationship with the novelist George Sand, spending summers at her house at Nohant, where he wrote much of his finest music. Suffering from tuberculosis, he gave his last concerts in Britain in 1848 and died in Paris in 1849.

Chopin transformed the piano's possibilities with singing melodies inspired by Italian opera, rich chromatic harmony, subtle use of the pedal and flexible rubato. His études turn technical exercises into concert music; his ballades and scherzos are large dramatic forms; his mazurkas and polonaises draw on Polish dance and became symbols of national feeling. Schumann greeted him in print with the words, 'Hats off, gentlemen, a genius!' His music remains central to every pianist's repertoire.

### Nocturnes (1827–1846)

- **Date:** 1827–1846
- **Type:** 21 nocturnes for piano
- **Premiere / first published:** Published in Paris, Leipzig and London from 1832
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nocturnes%20Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric%20Chopin)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Nocturnes+Chopin)

The nocturne, a dreamy piano piece with a singing melody over broken-chord accompaniment, was invented by the Irish composer John Field, but Chopin made it his own. His twenty-one nocturnes, published mostly in sets of two or three between 1832 and 1846, with two more after his death, transfer the ornamented lines of Bellini's bel canto opera to the piano, with fluid rubato and rich harmony created by the sustaining pedal. Many have stormy middle sections that contrast with their tranquil outer parts. The Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9 No. 2, is among the most famous of all piano pieces, while late nocturnes such as Op. 48 No. 1 in C minor reach tragic grandeur.

### Études, Op. 10 and Op. 25 (1829–1836)

- **Date:** 1829–1836
- **Type:** 24 concert études for piano
- **Premiere / first published:** Published Paris, Leipzig and London, 1833 and 1837; Op. 10 dedicated to Liszt
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=%C3%89tudes%2C%20Op.%2010%20and%20Op.%2025%20Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric%20Chopin)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=%C3%89tudes+Chopin)

Chopin's two sets of twelve études each focus on a particular technical problem, such as arpeggios, chromatic scales, octaves or the independence of the weak fingers, but they are also concert pieces of great expressive and musical richness. Op. 10, written largely in Warsaw and Vienna before 1832, is dedicated to Liszt, and Op. 25 to Liszt's partner Marie d'Agoult. Among the best known are the 'Revolutionary' Étude in C minor, Op. 10 No. 12, associated with the fall of Warsaw to the Russians in 1831, the lyrical Op. 10 No. 3 in E major, and the 'Winter Wind', Op. 25 No. 11. They established the étude as an artistic genre, followed by Liszt, Debussy, Scriabin and Ligeti.

### Ballade No. 1 in G minor (c. 1831–35)

[![Ballade No. 1 in G minor](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/96/Chopin_Ballade_1.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chopin_Ballade_1.png)

<sub>Opening of the Ballade No. 1 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chopin_Ballade_1.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1831–35
- **Type:** Piano ballade, Op. 23
- **Premiere / first published:** Published 1836; dedicated to Baron Nathaniel de Stockhausen
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ballade%20No.%201%20in%20G%20minor%20Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric%20Chopin)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Ballade+No.1+Chopin)

Chopin's four ballades created a new kind of piano piece: a large, single-movement narrative in which themes are transformed dramatically over the course of the music. The first, begun in Vienna around 1831 and finished in Paris, opens with a mysterious recitative-like introduction before a hesitant waltz-like theme. A lyrical second theme returns, transformed, in a radiant fortissimo, before a virtuosic and tragic coda. The ballades are often linked to the poetry of Adam Mickiewicz, Chopin's compatriot, though Chopin gave no programme. Schumann reported that Chopin told him it was his favourite among his works. It is one of the pinnacles of the Romantic piano repertoire.
