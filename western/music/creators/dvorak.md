# Antonín Dvořák

**1841 – 1904** · Czech  
Born: Nelahozeves, Bohemia · Died: Prague  
Eras & schools: [Nationalist schools](../movements/nationalist-schools.md)  

> Dvořák brought Czech folk spirit into symphonies, concertos and chamber music, and in America wrote the New World Symphony.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Dvořák,_Antonín)

## Biography

Antonín Dvořák was born in 1841 in Nelahozeves near Prague, the son of a butcher and innkeeper, and trained at the Prague organ school. For years he played viola in the orchestra of the Provisional Theatre, under Smetana among others. An Austrian state stipend brought him to the attention of Brahms, who recommended him to his publisher Simrock, and the Slavonic Dances of 1878 made him famous. He was especially popular in England, where he visited nine times. From 1892 to 1895 he directed the National Conservatory of Music in New York, writing the New World Symphony, the American String Quartet and the Cello Concerto. Back in Prague he taught at the conservatory, became its director and wrote operas, including Rusalka. He died in Prague in 1904.

Dvořák's music combines Classical forms, learned partly from Brahms, with Czech and Slavonic dance rhythms and melodies, though he usually invented his own folk-like themes. In America he urged composers to build a national music on African American spirituals and Native American music, which he believed held the seeds of a new style. His nine symphonies, the Cello Concerto, the Dumky Trio and Rusalka are mainstays of the repertory.

Dvořák wrote the New World Symphony in New York in the first months of 1893, while directing the National Conservatory, and its premiere at Carnegie Hall was a triumph. He said he had studied African American and Native American melodies and tried to write in their spirit, though all the themes are his own; how far its idioms are American or Czech has been debated ever since. The slow movement's melody for the cor anglais was later turned into the song Goin' Home by one of his students. The finale recalls themes from all the earlier movements. Neil Armstrong reportedly took a recording to the Moon in 1969. It is one of the most popular symphonies in the world.
