## Biography

Thomas Tallis was born around 1505 and worked at Dover Priory, in London and at Waltham Abbey until its dissolution in 1540. He then sang at Canterbury Cathedral before joining the Chapel Royal around 1543, where he served under Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I and Elizabeth I, adapting his music to each change in the official religion. In 1575 Elizabeth granted him and his younger colleague William Byrd a monopoly on printing music and music paper, and they published the Cantiones sacrae that year. He died in Greenwich in 1585.

Tallis's music moves from the florid Latin antiphons of the pre-Reformation church to the plain, syllabic English anthems required under Edward VI, such as If ye love me, and back to elaborate Latin works under Mary. His two settings of the Lamentations of Jeremiah are deeply expressive, and his tunes for Archbishop Parker's psalter include the melody Ralph Vaughan Williams used in his Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis. Spem in alium, for forty independent voices, is among the most ambitious works of the Renaissance.

## Key works

### Spem in alium (c. 1570)

- **Date:** c. 1570
- **Type:** Motet for forty voices
- **Premiere / first published:** Probably Arundel House or Nonsuch Palace, England; earliest source 1610s
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Spem%20in%20alium%20Thomas%20Tallis)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Spem+in+alium+Tallis)

Spem in alium (I have never put my hope in any other) is written for eight choirs of five voices each, forty separate parts. Its origins are uncertain; a later account suggests it was composed to rival an Italian work for forty voices, perhaps by Alessandro Striggio, who visited London in 1567. It may have been performed at Arundel House in London or at Nonsuch Palace. The music begins with a single voice, and the sound passes around the choirs, which would have stood in a ring, before all forty join in great climaxes, with dramatic pauses before the close. The oldest surviving source was copied for the investiture of Henry as Prince of Wales in 1610 with an English text. It is a landmark of the English Renaissance and has inspired modern works, including Janet Cardiff's sound installation The Forty Part Motet.
