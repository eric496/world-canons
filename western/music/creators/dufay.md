# Guillaume Du Fay

**c. 1397 – 1474** · Franco-Flemish  
Born: probably near Brussels, Hainaut · Died: Cambrai  
Eras & schools: [Franco-Flemish Renaissance](../movements/franco-flemish.md)  

> The leading composer of the early fifteenth century, Du Fay wrote masses, motets and chansons that set the course for Renaissance polyphony.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Du_Fay,_Guillaume)

## Biography

Du Fay was probably born around 1397 near Brussels and grew up as a choirboy at Cambrai Cathedral. In the 1420s he worked in Italy for the Malatesta family and then in the papal chapel, and he spent long periods at the court of Savoy. In 1436 he wrote the motet Nuper rosarum flores for the consecration of Florence Cathedral beneath Brunelleschi's new dome. From 1439 he was based mainly at Cambrai, where he was a canon and a respected figure until his death in 1474, keeping close ties with Italy, Savoy and the Burgundian court.

Du Fay's career spans the change from late medieval to Renaissance music. His early motets still use isorhythm, but he absorbed the warm, full harmony of English composers such as Dunstable and the smooth melodies of Italy. He helped develop the cyclic mass, in which all movements share a borrowed tune, writing masses on the song L'homme armé and on his own chanson Se la face ay pale. His chansons and hymns are melodious and clear in texture. Contemporaries ranked him the foremost composer of his age, and his works shaped the generation of Ockeghem and Josquin.

## Key works

### Nuper rosarum flores (1436)

- **Date:** 1436
- **Type:** Isorhythmic motet
- **Premiere / first published:** Florence Cathedral, consecration, 25 March 1436
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nuper%20rosarum%20flores%20Guillaume%20Du%20Fay)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Nuper+rosarum+flores+Fay)

Pope Eugenius IV consecrated Florence Cathedral, Santa Maria del Fiore, on 25 March 1436, after Brunelleschi had completed his great dome, and Du Fay, then a singer in the papal chapel, wrote this motet for the ceremony. Its text speaks of roses given by the pope to the church. Two lower voices sing a chant melody, Terribilis est locus iste, in canon at the fifth, while two upper voices weave freer lines above. The work falls into four sections whose lengths are in the proportion 6:4:2:3, and a much-discussed theory proposed that these mirror the proportions of the cathedral or of Solomon's Temple; later scholars have questioned the link to the building. Either way, it is a superb example of music written for a specific moment of civic and religious splendour.
