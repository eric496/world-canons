# Louis Armstrong

**1901 – 1971** · American  
Born: New Orleans, Louisiana · Died: New York  
Eras & schools: [Jazz](../movements/jazz.md)  

> Trumpeter and singer, Armstrong turned jazz into a soloist's art, and his Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings are founding classics of the music.

## Biography

Louis Armstrong was born in 1901 in a poor district of New Orleans. Sent to the Colored Waifs' Home for Boys after firing a pistol on New Year's Eve, he learned the cornet in its band. He played on riverboats and in New Orleans clubs, mentored by King Oliver, whom he joined in Chicago in 1922. After a spell with Fletcher Henderson's orchestra in New York, he recorded with his Hot Five and Hot Seven bands in Chicago from 1925 to 1928, among the most influential records in jazz. In the 1930s he became a big-band star and film actor, and from 1947 he toured the world with his All Stars, becoming known as Ambassador Satch. His recordings of Hello, Dolly! and What a Wonderful World were huge popular hits. He died in New York in 1971.

Armstrong's improvisations showed that a jazz solo could tell a coherent, dramatic story, and his swinging rhythm transformed how jazz was played. His warm, gravelly voice and scat singing shaped popular singing, influencing figures from Bing Crosby to Billie Holiday. Some later musicians criticised his genial stage persona, but he spoke out against segregation, notably during the Little Rock school crisis of 1957.

## Key works

### West End Blues (1928)

- **Date:** 1928
- **Type:** Jazz recording (Louis Armstrong and His Hot Five)
- **Premiere / first published:** Recorded for Okeh Records, Chicago, 28 June 1928
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=West%20End%20Blues%20Louis%20Armstrong)

West End Blues was written by King Oliver, Armstrong's mentor, and named after a resort on Lake Pontchartrain in New Orleans. Armstrong recorded it in Chicago in 1928 with his Hot Five, including the pianist Earl Hines. It opens with a dazzling unaccompanied trumpet cadenza, then moves through a slow blues with solos for trombone and clarinet, the latter in call and response with Armstrong's wordless scat singing, and a piano solo by Hines, before Armstrong returns with a long held high note and a descending phrase. The recording showed that jazz improvisation could reach the expressive and structural ambition of composed music. It was inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1974.
