# Ludwig van Beethoven

**1770 – 1827** · German  
Born: Bonn, Electorate of Cologne · Died: Vienna  
Eras & schools: [Galant & Classical](../movements/classical-era.md), [Early Romantic](../movements/early-romantic.md)  

> Beethoven expanded the forms of the Classical style with unprecedented power and personal expression, and continued to compose after he became deaf.

**Links:** [Scores on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Beethoven,_Ludwig_van)

## Biography

Ludwig van Beethoven was born in Bonn in 1770, the grandson and son of court musicians. His father, an alcoholic tenor, pushed him to perform as a prodigy, and by his teens he was playing in the court orchestra and supporting his family. In 1792 he moved to Vienna to study with Haydn and soon made his name as a fiery virtuoso pianist and improviser, supported by aristocratic patrons. Around 1798 he noticed his hearing failing; in the Heiligenstadt Testament of 1802 he wrote of his despair and resolve to go on. The following years produced the Eroica Symphony, the opera Fidelio, the Fifth and Sixth Symphonies and the Emperor Concerto. By 1818 he was totally deaf and communicated through conversation books. His last decade brought the late piano sonatas, the Missa solemnis, the Ninth Symphony and the late string quartets. He died in Vienna in 1827, and tens of thousands attended his funeral.

Beethoven inherited the forms of Haydn and Mozart and pushed them to new extremes of scale, contrast and dramatic struggle, often building whole works from tiny motifs and directing them towards a triumphant conclusion. His late works explored fugue, variation and unusual structures with a depth and strangeness that puzzled contemporaries. He saw himself as an independent artist rather than a servant of patrons, an image that shaped the Romantic idea of the composer as heroic genius. For the next century every composer of symphonies worked in his shadow.

### Piano Sonata No. 14 in C-sharp minor, 'Moonlight' (1801)

- **Date:** 1801
- **Type:** Piano sonata, Op. 27 No. 2
- **Premiere / first published:** Published in Vienna, 1802; dedicated to Countess Giulietta Guicciardi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Piano%20Sonata%20No.%2014%20in%20C-sharp%20minor%2C%20%27Moonlight%27%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Piano+Sonata+No.14+Beethoven)

Beethoven subtitled this sonata 'quasi una fantasia', a sonata in the manner of a fantasy, because it departs from the usual order of movements: it opens not with a fast sonata-form movement but with a slow, quiet Adagio sostenuto in triplets, to be played with the dampers raised. A gentle Allegretto follows, and the sonata ends with a stormy Presto agitato. It was dedicated to his pupil Giulietta Guicciardi, with whom he was briefly in love. The nickname Moonlight comes from the poet Ludwig Rellstab, who after Beethoven's death compared the first movement to moonlight on Lake Lucerne. It is one of the most popular piano works ever written.

### Symphony No. 3 in E-flat major, 'Eroica' (1803–04)

- **Date:** 1803–04
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 55
- **Premiere / first published:** Lobkowitz Palace, Vienna (private), 1804; Theater an der Wien, 7 April 1805 (public)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%203%20in%20E-flat%20major%2C%20%27Eroica%27%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.3+Beethoven)

Beethoven wrote the Third Symphony in 1803–04, soon after the Heiligenstadt crisis over his deafness. He originally intended to dedicate it to Napoleon Bonaparte, whom he admired as a champion of revolutionary ideals, but according to his pupil Ferdinand Ries, when he learned that Napoleon had declared himself emperor, he tore up the title page in anger. A surviving copy of the score has Napoleon's name scratched out so violently that it tore a hole in the paper. It was published as the 'heroic symphony, composed to celebrate the memory of a great man'. Its first movement, almost twice as long as any previous symphonic opening movement, develops its material with violent off-beat chords and clashing dissonances; the second is a funeral march, and the finale is a set of variations on a theme from his ballet Prometheus. It marked a turning point in the history of the symphony.

### Symphony No. 5 in C minor (1804–08)

- **Date:** 1804–08
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 67
- **Premiere / first published:** Theater an der Wien, Vienna, 22 December 1808
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%205%20in%20C%20minor%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.5+Beethoven)

The Fifth Symphony was first performed at a marathon concert in an unheated theatre in Vienna on 22 December 1808, together with the Sixth Symphony, the Fourth Piano Concerto and the Choral Fantasy. Its opening motif of three short notes and a long one dominates the first movement and recurs in different forms throughout. Beethoven's secretary Anton Schindler claimed he called it fate knocking at the door, but Schindler is an unreliable source. The scherzo leads without a break, through a long, eerie transition, into a blazing C major finale, where trombones, piccolo and contrabassoon make their first appearance in a symphony. The Romantic writer E. T. A. Hoffmann wrote a famous review praising its sublime, infinite longing. During the Second World War its opening, which resembles the Morse code for V, was used by the BBC as a symbol of victory.

### Fidelio (1805; final version 1814)

- **Date:** 1805; final version 1814
- **Type:** Opera, two acts, Op. 72
- **Premiere / first published:** Theater an der Wien, Vienna, 20 November 1805; revised version Kärntnertortheater, 23 May 1814
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fidelio%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Fidelio+Beethoven)

Fidelio is based on a French libretto about a true story from the time of the French Revolution. Leonore, disguised as a young man named Fidelio, works as an assistant to the jailer Rocco to free her husband Florestan, a political prisoner held in a dungeon by the tyrant Pizarro. The first version, staged in 1805 just after Napoleon's troops had occupied Vienna, was a failure, and Beethoven revised it in 1806 and again thoroughly in 1814, when it succeeded. He wrote four overtures for it, three called Leonore. The prisoners' chorus, as they come out into the light, and Florestan's dungeon aria are central moments. With its celebration of freedom and marital love, Fidelio has been performed at symbolic occasions, such as the reopening of the Vienna State Opera in 1955.

### Piano Sonata No. 29 in B-flat major, 'Hammerklavier' (1817–18)

- **Date:** 1817–18
- **Type:** Piano sonata, Op. 106
- **Premiere / first published:** Published in Vienna by Artaria, 1819; dedicated to Archduke Rudolph
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Piano%20Sonata%20No.%2029%20in%20B-flat%20major%2C%20%27Hammerklavier%27%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Piano+Sonata+No.29+Beethoven)

The Hammerklavier Sonata, named after the German word for the piano, was written in 1817–18 and dedicated to Beethoven's pupil and patron Archduke Rudolph. Beethoven predicted that pianists would be playing it fifty years later, and his metronome marking for the first movement is so fast that many performers consider it impractical. Its four movements last around forty-five minutes: a heroic opening Allegro, a brief scherzo, a vast and profound slow movement, and, after a hesitant introduction, a gigantic fugue full of trills and leaps that marks the extreme of Beethoven's late style. For much of the nineteenth century it was considered almost unplayable, until Liszt and Clara Schumann championed it.

### Missa solemnis in D major (1819–23)

- **Date:** 1819–23
- **Type:** Mass, Op. 123
- **Premiere / first published:** Saint Petersburg, 7 April 1824 (first complete performance); parts given in Vienna, May 1824
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Missa%20solemnis%20in%20D%20major%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Missa+solemnis+Beethoven)

Beethoven began the Missa solemnis for the enthronement of his patron Archduke Rudolph as Archbishop of Olmütz in March 1820, but finished it three years too late. Its first complete performance took place in Saint Petersburg in 1824, sponsored by Prince Nikolai Galitzin, and three movements were heard in Vienna at the premiere of the Ninth Symphony. Above the Kyrie Beethoven wrote 'From the heart, may it go again to the heart'. He studied old church music and the Latin text closely, and the work is full of personal emphasis, from the fugues of the Gloria and Credo to the violin solo that descends from heaven in the Benedictus and the military sounds that interrupt the plea for peace in the Agnus Dei. He considered it his greatest work.

### Symphony No. 9 in D minor, 'Choral' (1822–24)

- **Date:** 1822–24
- **Type:** Symphony with chorus, Op. 125
- **Premiere / first published:** Theater am Kärntnertor, Vienna, 7 May 1824
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%209%20in%20D%20minor%2C%20%27Choral%27%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.9+Beethoven)

Beethoven had long wanted to set Friedrich Schiller's poem An die Freude, and in the Ninth Symphony, commissioned in part by the Philharmonic Society of London, he did so in a finale for four soloists, chorus and orchestra. The first three movements, a mysterious opening that emerges from empty fifths, a driving scherzo and a serene slow movement, are recalled and rejected at the start of the finale, before the cellos and basses introduce the joy theme. Its variations include a Turkish march and a solemn hymn to the creator above the stars. At the premiere the totally deaf composer stood beside the conductor, and it is said that one of the soloists had to turn him round to see the audience's applause. The joy melody became the anthem of the European Union, and the autograph is listed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register.

### String Quartet No. 14 in C-sharp minor (1826)

- **Date:** 1826
- **Type:** String quartet, Op. 131
- **Premiere / first published:** Published posthumously, Mainz, 1827; first public performance 1835
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=String%20Quartet%20No.%2014%20in%20C-sharp%20minor%20Ludwig%20van%20Beethoven)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=String+Quartet+No.14+Beethoven)

Beethoven's last five string quartets, written in 1825–26 after the Ninth Symphony, explored new forms and expressive extremes that puzzled contemporaries. The C-sharp minor Quartet, Op. 131, has seven movements played continuously, beginning with a slow, mournful fugue that Wagner called the saddest thing ever expressed in notes. Beethoven is said to have regarded it as his greatest quartet, and Schubert asked to hear it on his deathbed, according to a story told by a friend. A theme and variations forms its centre, and the work ends with a furious finale that recalls the opening fugue. The late quartets have been a source of wonder and study for composers ever since, from Wagner to Bartók and Shostakovich.
