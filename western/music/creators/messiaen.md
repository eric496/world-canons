# Olivier Messiaen

**1908 – 1992** · French  
Born: Avignon · Died: Clichy, near Paris  
Eras & schools: [Post-war avant-garde](../movements/postwar-avant-garde.md)  

> A devout Catholic organist, Messiaen built a unique language from birdsong, Indian rhythms and colourful harmony, and taught many of the post-war avant-garde.

## Biography

Olivier Messiaen was born in Avignon in 1908, the son of a literature teacher and the poet Cécile Sauvage, and entered the Paris Conservatoire at eleven. In 1931 he became organist of the church of La Trinité in Paris, a post he held for over sixty years. Captured as a French soldier in 1940, he wrote the Quartet for the End of Time in a German prisoner-of-war camp in Silesia, where it was first performed in January 1941. After his release he taught at the Conservatoire, where his pupils included Boulez, Stockhausen and Xenakis. He married the pianist Yvonne Loriod, for whom he wrote much piano music. He spent years transcribing birdsong across the world, and in his seventies wrote the opera Saint François d'Assise. He died in 1992.

Messiaen's music is an expression of Catholic faith, often ecstatic and celebratory. He devised his modes of limited transposition, used rhythms derived from ancient Greek metres and Indian talas, and experienced harmonies as colours, a form of synaesthesia. His experiment Mode de valeurs et d'intensités (1949) inspired the integral serialism of his students. Works such as the Turangalîla-Symphonie and Catalogue d'oiseaux are among the most original of the century.

### Quartet for the End of Time (Quatuor pour la fin du temps) (1940–41)

- **Date:** 1940–41
- **Type:** Chamber work for clarinet, violin, cello and piano
- **Premiere / first published:** Stalag VIII-A prisoner-of-war camp, Görlitz, Silesia, 15 January 1941
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Quartet%20for%20the%20End%20of%20Time%20%28Quatuor%20pour%20la%20fin%20du%20temps%29%20Olivier%20Messiaen)

Messiaen was captured as a soldier in 1940 and held at Stalag VIII-A at Görlitz, where he found among his fellow prisoners a clarinettist, a violinist and a cellist. He wrote the quartet for them and himself at the piano, and it was performed before an audience of prisoners and guards in January 1941, in the cold, on instruments in poor condition. Its eight movements are inspired by the angel of the Apocalypse in the Book of Revelation, who announces that there shall be time no longer. They include a solo for clarinet, Abyss of the Birds, an energetic dance of fury in unison, and two long, slow songs of praise to the eternity and immortality of Jesus, for cello and violin with piano. Messiaen's account of the premiere, including the number of listeners, has been questioned by historians. It is one of the most performed chamber works of the twentieth century.

### Turangalîla-Symphonie (1946–48)

- **Date:** 1946–48
- **Type:** Symphony for piano, ondes Martenot and orchestra
- **Premiere / first published:** Symphony Hall, Boston, 2 December 1949, Boston Symphony Orchestra conducted by Leonard Bernstein
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Turangal%C3%AEla-Symphonie%20Olivier%20Messiaen)

Serge Koussevitzky commissioned Turangalîla for the Boston Symphony Orchestra, and the young Leonard Bernstein conducted the premiere. The title combines two Sanskrit words, which Messiaen explained as meaning love song, hymn to joy, time, movement, rhythm, life and death. It is the central work of a trilogy on the theme of Tristan and Isolde. Its ten movements use a huge orchestra with prominent piano, played at the premiere by Yvonne Loriod, and the ondes Martenot, an early electronic instrument with a sliding, eerie tone. Recurring themes include the heavy 'statue theme' and a tender 'love theme', and the rhythms are inspired by Indian talas. It is one of the most performed large orchestral works of the post-war era.
