## Biography

Henry Purcell was born in Westminster in 1659 into a family of court musicians and sang as a boy in the Chapel Royal. In 1677 he succeeded Matthew Locke as composer for the King's violins, and in 1679 he became organist of Westminster Abbey, adding a post at the Chapel Royal in 1682. He wrote odes and anthems for the courts of Charles II, James II and William and Mary, including music for Mary's funeral in 1695. In his last years he wrote extensively for the London stage. He died in November 1695, aged only thirty-six, and is buried in Westminster Abbey near the organ.

Purcell had a special gift for setting English words, catching their rhythm and colour, and often built expressive arias over a repeating ground bass, as in Dido's lament. Besides Dido and Aeneas he wrote semi-operas such as King Arthur and The Fairy-Queen, which mix spoken drama with masques, as well as anthems, songs, trio sonatas and fantasias for viols. For two centuries after him no English-born composer matched his reputation, and Britten and Tippett championed his music in the twentieth century.

## Key works

### Dido and Aeneas (c. 1689)

- **Date:** c. 1689
- **Type:** Opera, three acts
- **Premiere / first published:** Josias Priest's girls' school, Chelsea, London (performance documented 1689)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dido%20and%20Aeneas%20Henry%20Purcell)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Dido+and+Aeneas+Purcell)

Dido and Aeneas, with a libretto by Nahum Tate after Virgil's Aeneid, was performed at a girls' boarding school in Chelsea run by the dancing master Josias Priest in 1689, though it may have been written earlier for the court. In under an hour it tells how Aeneas, the Trojan prince, loves Dido, Queen of Carthage, until witches trick him into leaving, and Dido dies of grief. Purcell's setting of English is supple and direct, and the score includes choruses, dances and comic witch scenes. Dido's final lament unfolds over a descending chromatic ground bass repeated eleven times. No score survives from Purcell's lifetime, and the earliest copies date from decades after his death, so some music, including part of the ending of act two, is lost. Today it is one of the most performed Baroque operas.
