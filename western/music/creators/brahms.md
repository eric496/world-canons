## Biography

Johannes Brahms was born in Hamburg in 1833, the son of a double bass player, and earned money as a boy playing piano in dockside taverns. In 1853, touring with the violinist Ede Reményi, he met Joseph Joachim and then Robert and Clara Schumann; Robert proclaimed him the coming genius in a famous article. After Robert's breakdown Brahms helped Clara, and they remained deeply close for life. He settled in Vienna in 1863, where A German Requiem (1868) and the Hungarian Dances brought him fame and financial security. He took more than twenty years to complete his First Symphony (1876), conscious of Beethoven's shadow, then wrote three more in less than a decade. He never married and died in Vienna in 1897.

Brahms was a conservative in form and a master of variation, counterpoint and developing small motifs, yet his music is warm, autumnal and often passionate. He studied early music deeply and edited works by Couperin and others. Critics such as Eduard Hanslick set him up as the opposite of Wagner and Liszt, in what became known as the War of the Romantics. Schoenberg later wrote an essay called Brahms the Progressive, arguing that his methods pointed to modern music.

### A German Requiem (Ein deutsches Requiem) (1865–68)

- **Date:** 1865–68
- **Type:** Choral work, Op. 45
- **Premiere / first published:** Bremen Cathedral, 10 April 1868 (Good Friday); complete seven-movement version Leipzig, 18 February 1869
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20German%20Requiem%20%28Ein%20deutsches%20Requiem%29%20Johannes%20Brahms)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Ein+deutsches+Requiem+Brahms)

Brahms began work on A German Requiem after the death of his mother in 1865, and perhaps with Robert Schumann's death in mind. Instead of the Latin text of the Catholic requiem mass, he selected passages from Luther's German Bible about grief, transience and consolation, opening with 'Blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted'. Six movements were performed in Bremen Cathedral on Good Friday 1868; Brahms then added a fifth movement for soprano solo, 'Ye now have sorrow', and the complete version was heard in 1869. The second movement, 'For all flesh is as grass', is a slow funeral march, and fugues crown several movements. The success of the work made Brahms famous across Europe.

### Violin Concerto in D major (1878)

- **Date:** 1878
- **Type:** Violin concerto, Op. 77
- **Premiere / first published:** Leipzig Gewandhaus, 1 January 1879, with Joseph Joachim as soloist and Brahms conducting
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Violin%20Concerto%20in%20D%20major%20Johannes%20Brahms)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Violin+Concerto+Op.77+Brahms)

Brahms wrote this concerto for his friend Joseph Joachim, one of the leading violinists of the age, who advised him on the solo part and wrote the cadenza still most often played. It was premiered on New Year's Day 1879 in Leipzig. The orchestra plays an important symphonic role, prompting the conductor Hans von Bülow to call it a concerto against the violin rather than for it. The slow movement opens with a long oboe solo, which the violinist Pablo de Sarasate reportedly refused to stand listening to, and the finale is a vigorous rondo with a Hungarian flavour, a tribute to Joachim. With Beethoven's and Mendelssohn's it is one of the great Romantic violin concertos.

### Symphony No. 4 in E minor (1884–85)

- **Date:** 1884–85
- **Type:** Symphony, Op. 98
- **Premiere / first published:** Meiningen, 25 October 1885, conducted by Brahms
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Symphony%20No.%204%20in%20E%20minor%20Johannes%20Brahms)
- **Links:** [Free scores on IMSLP (search)](https://imslp.org/index.php?search=Symphony+No.4+Brahms)

Brahms wrote his Fourth Symphony during two summers at Mürzzuschlag in the Styrian Alps and conducted the premiere with the Meiningen Court Orchestra. The first movement grows from a chain of falling thirds and rising sixths; the slow movement opens in an archaic Phrygian mode; and the third is a boisterous scherzo with a triangle. The finale is a passacaglia, thirty variations and a coda on an eight-bar theme adapted from the closing chorus of Bach's Cantata BWV 150, a rare use of a Baroque form to end a Romantic symphony. Initially some of Brahms's friends found it austere, but it is now seen as one of the summits of the symphonic tradition.
