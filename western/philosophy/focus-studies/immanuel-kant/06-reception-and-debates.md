# Kant Received — Fame, Eclipse, Return, and the Live Debates

*Focus study: Immanuel Kant · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)*

Kant's reception has the shape of his own antinomies: every generation produces the pair of opposed Kants and argues both sides with conviction. He was misunderstood on arrival and famous within five years; completed by heirs he repudiated; buried by the mid-nineteenth century and resurrected by slogan; claimed simultaneously by the philosophers of science and the philosophers of value; and he is currently read, with equal scholarly confidence, as a metaphysician of two realms and as a methodologist of two standpoints. This file tells that story by era, then states the live debates at their current state of play — including the one about his writings on race, where the dossier's rule is the plainest statement of the record and the fairest statement of the dispute.

## The first reception: bafflement, then fame (1781–1790)

The *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) landed badly. Its first substantial review — published in the Göttingen learned gazette in January 1782, written by Christian Garve and cut down, in ways that sharpened it, by the journal's J. G. H. Feder — read Kant as a Berkeleyan idealist for whom the world dissolves into representations. Kant, who thought he had *refuted* that idealism, was stung into the clearest writing of his life: the *Prolegomena* (1783) is the review's unintended gift, and the second edition of the *Critique* (1787) added the Refutation of Idealism and rewrote whole sections against the misreading. The Königsberg mathematician and court chaplain Johann Schulz published in 1784 the *Expositions* of the *Critique* — the one commentary Kant publicly commended — and Reinhold's *Letters* (1786–87, traced in [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)) did the rest. The decade's verdict survives in a phrase of its most distinguished holdout. Moses Mendelssohn, in the Preface to his *Morning Hours* (1785), ruefully confessed that his nervous infirmity had kept him from reading the recent metaphysics of Lambert, Tetens, Platner, "and even the all-crushing Kant" — the epithet (in German, *der alles zermalmende Kant*) stuck, and Mendelssohn, who had not in fact worked through the book, is its reluctant coiner. By the early 1790s Kant was the arbiter of German philosophy: textbooks multiplied, chairs were filled by his readers, and the question was no longer whether the *Critique* was right but what followed from it.

## The succession: German Idealism (1790s–1830s)

What followed, in the eyes of the followers, was completion. The sequence runs Reinhold's *Elementary Philosophy* (a single first principle beneath Kant's results), Fichte's *Wissenschaftslehre* (the thing in itself deleted, the I as sole ground), Schelling's philosophy of nature and identity, and Hegel's absolute idealism — each system presenting itself as Kant with the residual dualisms removed. Kant's own response to the process is part of the record: the 1799 public disowning of Fichte, and, more quietly telling, his judgment on the movement's sharpest internal critic. Salomon Maimon's *Essay on Transcendental Philosophy* (1790) argued that the *Critique* never bridges the gap between the given and the concepts meant to order it; Kant wrote to Herz that none of his critics had understood him and the main question as well as Maimon — and held his position anyway. After Kant's death in 1804 the succession became an era: by the 1820s "Kantian" named a stage one passed through on the way to Hegel, and the master's reservations were filed as an old man's failure to recognize his own offspring. Schopenhauer's counter-verdict — that the professor-philosophers had buried the real Kant under their systems — was available throughout (see [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)) and mostly ignored.

## Eclipse and the neo-Kantian return (1830s–1914)

The systems collapsed with their makers: after Hegel's death in 1831, German academic philosophy fragmented into schools, polemics, and finally the materialism controversy of the 1850s, in which Kant figured as a superseded episode — a chapter in the prehistory of science, not a resource for it. The resurrection, when it came, came from several directions at once: Helmholtz's 1855 Königsberg lecture on human vision, enlisting Kant's doctrine of space for the new physiology of perception; Eduard Zeller's 1862 call for an epistemological turn; Friedrich Albert Lange's *History of Materialism* (1866), which read Kant as materialism's true answer; and, as the movement's slogan, Otto Liebmann's *Kant und die Epigonen* (1865), whose chapters on the Idealist "descendants" each close with the refrain that one must go *back to Kant*. Scholarly fuel was supplied by the Fischer–Trendelenburg dispute (1865–71) over the "neglected alternative" — whether the Transcendental Aesthetic really excludes space and time from things in themselves — the quarrel from which serious modern Kant commentary descends.

The return institutionalized itself as neo-Kantianism, dominant in German universities from the 1870s to the First World War, in two great houses. The **Marburg school** — Hermann Cohen, whose *Kant's Theory of Experience* (1871) founded the line, then Paul Natorp and Ernst Cassirer — read the first *Critique* as the logic of mathematical natural science: the "given" of sensation dissolves into thought's own constructions, and philosophy becomes the theory of science's presuppositions (Cassirer's later philosophy of symbolic forms widened the same method to all of culture). The **Southwest, or Baden, school** — Wilhelm Windelband and Heinrich Rickert, at Freiburg and Heidelberg — read Kant as the philosopher of *validity* and value: philosophy's real subject is not how cognition happens but what *holds*, and Windelband's distinction between the law-seeking (nomothetic) natural sciences and the event-describing (idiographic) historical sciences made Kant the patron of the cultural sciences. The two schools agreed on little except the starting point, and neither aimed at restoration: Windelband's maxim — to understand Kant means to go beyond him — could stand over both doors. The movement's symbolic end came at Davos in 1929, where Cassirer faced Heidegger, whose *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* (1929) conscripted Kant as a prophet of human finitude: a reading so violent its violence became the point at issue between the old Kantianism and the century to come.

## Strawson, Wood, and the modern scholarly Kant (1945– )

The Anglophone postwar scene began in near-neglect — Kant read in creaking translations, or as a cautionary tale about German obscurity — and turned on Strawson's *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), whose severing of the *Critique*'s arguments from its idealism is traced in [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md). Jonathan Bennett's *Kant's Analytic* (1966) and *Kant's Dialectic* (1974) worked the same reconstructionist seam. Then came the scholars' correction, and with it the field's modern shape. Henry Allison's *Kant's Transcendental Idealism* (1983) mounted the systematic defense of the two-aspect reading and made idealism respectable in analytic company again; Paul Guyer's *Kant and the Claims of Knowledge* (1987) prosecuted the *Critique*'s central arguments with a historian's command of the texts and found several of them wanting — between Allison's defense and Guyer's prosecution much of the contemporary literature positions itself. The institutional keystone is the Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant (1992– , with Guyer and Allen W. Wood as general editors): reliable English texts of everything, including the lectures, the notes, and the correspondence, which quietly changed what a claim about "Kant's view" has to be based on. Wood's *Kant's Ethical Thought* (1999) completed the parallel recovery in ethics, presenting Kant's moral philosophy as a living position rather than a foil for utilitarians. The field today divides — amicably enough, and with traffic between — into reconstructionists, who test the arguments, and contextualists, who resituate them in the eighteenth century; both would be unrecognizable to the neo-Kantians, which is perhaps the surest sign the return succeeded.

## Live debates

**(a) Two worlds or two aspects?** The founding document is F. H. Jacobi's complaint of 1787: without the presupposition of the thing in itself, he wrote, "I could not enter into the system, and with that presupposition I could not remain within it." The *two-world* reading takes Kant's language at face value: appearances and things in themselves are two classes of entity, related by a real affection the theory simultaneously needs and forbids itself to describe. Its strengths are the Aesthetic's apparently ontological conclusions — things in themselves are not in space or time — and Kant's talk of the ground of appearance; its cost is the Jacobi problem generalized, a doubled ontology whose connecting relation (causation) is officially inapplicable across the divide. The *two-aspect* reading (Allison's "epistemic conditions" version is the standard) holds that "appearance" and "thing in itself" name one reality considered two ways: as it is relative to the conditions under which we can cognize it, and as it is in abstraction from those conditions. Its strengths are Kant's empirical realism, his insistence that the same things that appear are the things in themselves, and the dissolution of the affection problem into a distinction of standpoints; its cost is the suspicion that the idealism becomes trivial — a remark about our descriptions rather than a doctrine about reality. Between and beside the poles sit positions like Rae Langton's *Kantian humility* (things in themselves have intrinsic natures we are genuinely ignorant of — metaphysical in content, deflationary in epistemic ambition) and Karl Ameriks's "moderate" Kant. No consensus exists; the dispute still organizes editions, translations, and first lectures on the *Critique*, and readers should treat any confident one-line summary of transcendental idealism as a party statement.

**(b) What does the Transcendental Deduction prove?** The *Critique*'s legal centerpiece — Kant's *quid juris* argument that the categories legitimately apply to objects of experience — is contested at every joint. Dieter Henrich's structural analysis reads the B-Deduction as proceeding in *two steps in one*: §20 establishing that the categories govern anything given in one sensible intuition in general, §26 completing the proof only for intuition in *our* forms, space and time — which relocates the argumentative weight onto the Aesthetic and its idealism. Guyer's prosecution holds that even so completed, the deduction secures at most that we must *judge* experience categorially, not that objects conform — Barry Stroud's general challenge to transcendental arguments (they bridge "we must think it so" and "it is so" only by smuggling) applied to the original. The Kantian reply, recorded in the library entry, is that within transcendental idealism the gap cannot open: objects of experience just are appearances under the categories, so a condition of experiencing objects is a condition of the objects experienced. Whether that reply rescues the deduction or merely shows what it costs (the whole of idealism, as its premise rather than its prize) is the debate. A further front concerns the A-Deduction's "subjective" side — the threefold synthesis — long discarded as empirical psychology and increasingly defended as constitutive analysis conducted in the vocabulary available in 1781.

**(c) Non-conceptual content.** Contemporary philosophy of perception gave an old Kantian tension a new name. Gareth Evans's term (1982) asks whether a representation can have objective content — be *of* something, correctly or incorrectly — without deploying concepts. Kant's texts pull both ways, which is why both parties claim him. For conceptualism: "intuitions without concepts are blind" (A51/B75), and the Deduction's insistence that all combination is an act of the understanding; John McDowell's *Mind and World* (1994) develops this Kant into the thesis that experience itself, as openness to the world, already exercises conceptual capacities. For non-conceptualism: Kant credits animals with perceptions while denying them cognition through concepts, and the Aesthetic's space is a representation that precedes and structures any concept of it; Robert Hanna has defended a robust Kantian non-conceptualism on these texts, and Lucy Allais has argued a middle way for space specifically. What rides on it is not a footnote: whether Kant can be conscripted into current debates about perception and animal minds, and how to read the faculty architecture — two independent stems of knowledge, or one capacity in two employments.

**(d) Kant's writings on race and the critical philosophy.** The record first, without softening. Kant lectured on physical geography for some forty years and published three essays on race — "Of the Different Races of Human Beings" (1775), "Determination of the Concept of a Human Race" (1785), and "On the Use of Teleological Principles in Philosophy" (1788) — which develop one of the era's most systematic theories: a single human species differentiated by climate into four hereditary races, whose inherited "germs" and predispositions fix capacities that his texts rank, with Europeans at the top and the rest described in terms of lesser talent, industry, and receptivity to culture. The *Observations* of 1764 already contains a footnote endorsing the claim that Africans are naturally inferior in mental capacity. These are published essays and decades of lectures, not private marginalia, and no honest dossier calls them incidental.

The scholarly debate concerns what the record means, and it runs on three tracks, all live. *Scope:* Charles Mills ("Kant's Untermenschen," 2005) and Robert Bernasconi argue that the race theory is entangled with the critical philosophy's central categories — that Kant is a founder of the modern concept of race and that his moral universalism coexisted with, and was in practice restricted by, a graded conception of personhood. Others reply that the entanglement is asserted more than demonstrated: the moral philosophy's principles are universal in form and, applied consistently, condemn the hierarchy — the prejudice, on this view, is Kant failing his own philosophy, in company with nearly every European thinker of his century. *Change:* Pauline Kleingeld ("Kant's Second Thoughts on Race," 2007) argues that the Kant of the 1790s abandoned the hierarchy: *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) denounces the conquest-disguised-as-visiting of the colonial powers, the *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797) treats non-European peoples as bearers of rights, and the ranking claims vanish from the late published work. Critics of the thesis answer that silence is not retraction — Kant issued no recantation, and lecture material long recycled the old anthropology. *Consequence:* Lucy Allais ("Kant's Racism," 2016) holds both that the racism is real and that it contradicts Kant's own stated principles — which is precisely why a Kantian can and must repudiate it — while other scholars doubt the contradiction is so cleanly quarantined from the system's own anthropology of human development. The dossier delivers no verdict among these. What it insists on is the sequence: the record is not in dispute; its relation to the philosophy is.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **The clockwork man.** The story that Königsberg's citizens set their watches by Kant's afternoon walk — and that the walk was missed, in all those years, only for *Émile* — comes from later biographical tradition, polished above all by Heine's satirical portrait for French readers; it is literature about Kant, not documentation of him. The early biographies show something less mechanical and more interesting: the regular routine was a *late-life* regimen, adopted by a man managing frail health, and the younger Kant was a celebrated lecturer, a sought-after dinner guest, and a wit in company. Even his never leaving Königsberg, the legend's other half, exaggerates a small range of real travel into a cell. The regimen was real; the automaton is a caricature that survives because it flatters the reader — the philosophy looks stranger, and safer, if a machine wrote it.
- **"Kant refuted metaphysics."** He refuted one pretension of it: knowledge of the supersensible — God, the soul, the world-whole — by pure theoretical reason. The *Critique* names itself a propaedeutic to a metaphysics "that will be able to come forward as science," calls metaphysics properly reformed the inventory of everything we possess through pure reason, and was followed by Kant's own *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* (1786) and *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797). His successors in the Idealist succession understood him as a *founder* who had not finished the building, not as its demolition contractor; the "refutation" reading mistakes the *Critique*'s border police for an abolition of the country.
- **The thing in itself as a hidden second world.** The cartoon version — a ghostly duplicate realm behind the visible one, populated by noumena we might almost peek at — is licensed by neither party in the genuine scholarly dispute ((a) above). Kant's noumenon in the negative sense is a *limit-concept*: it marks, from inside cognition, the thought that things are not exhausted by the way they appear to us. To furnish that beyond as a second, shadowy world with properties is exactly the reification the Transcendental Dialectic diagnoses as reason's characteristic illusion. The live question is how to state the distinction without the cartoon — as two realms, as two aspects, as humble ignorance of intrinsic natures — not whether the cartoon is true. It is not.
- **The categorical imperative as the Golden Rule in Latin dress.** Kant anticipated the conflation and rejected it in a footnote of the *Groundwork* (4:430): the rule "do as you would be done by" is, he says, merely derivative from his principle, and a defective derivative — it is indexed to what you happen to want (the masochist and the indifferent pass it trivially), it cannot ground duties toward oneself, and the convicted criminal could invoke it against the judge who sentences him as the judge would not wish to be sentenced. The categorical imperative is different machinery altogether: it tests not the symmetry of your preferences but the *maxim* of your action for consistency under universalization — whether the practice your act presupposes survives everyone acting likewise, and whether any person is used merely as a means. A test of maxims, administered by reason to itself, is neither the Golden Rule nor a rulebook; confusing them dissolves precisely the autonomy the doctrine exists to state.
