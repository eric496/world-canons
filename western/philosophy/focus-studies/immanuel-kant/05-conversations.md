# Kant in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: Immanuel Kant · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)*

The legend gives Kant no interlocutors: one man, one town, one unbroken routine, thinking alone. The record gives something different — a philosophy built in sustained argument. Kant was formed inside a school system he had to escape from within, provoked into his mature problem by Hume, morally re-educated by Rousseau, and forced to work out his solution in letters during a decade of near-silence in print. He was then launched, by other people's publicity, into the loudest public quarrel of his decade, and spent his last years watching heirs claim his name for systems he disowned. Since his death the conversation has not stopped; it has only changed participants. This file traces it in three directions; the reception history is in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## Backward: the inheritance, and the breaks

### Wolff and Baumgarten: the school he escaped by completing

Kant's first philosophy was the system of Christian Wolff, the great organizer of [Leibniz](../../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md)'s inheritance into a complete demonstrative science of everything reason might claim: rational cosmology, psychology, and theology arranged under a general ontology. Kant's teacher Martin Knutzen was a Wolffian (and the man who also put Newton in front of him), and Kant's earliest work argues *inside* the school's disputes. The school's textbook form was Alexander Gottlieb Baumgarten's *Metaphysica* (1739), from which Kant lectured for some four decades, filling his copy with the marginal notes now printed as his *Reflexionen*; Baumgarten also founded aesthetics as a named discipline, a word Kant borrowed and turned to a different use.

The critical philosophy is best read as this system's self-examination rather than its rejection from outside. The *Critique of Pure Reason* keeps the Wolffian table of contents — the special metaphysics of world, soul, and God becomes the Transcendental Dialectic's itinerary — while denying the demonstrations that were supposed to fill it. And Kant never sneered at the school he displaced: the second edition's Preface calls Wolff "the greatest of all dogmatic philosophers," praising his spirit of thoroughness. Kant's complaint is precise: Wolff built as if reason's instrument needed no prior inspection. Critique is that inspection — the schoolmaster's own standards of rigor, applied to the schoolmaster.

### Hume: the awakening, as Kant chose to tell it

The most famous sentence in Kant's intellectual biography is his own retrospective composition. In the Preface to the *Prolegomena* (1783) — a book written to sell the *Critique* to a public that had misunderstood it — Kant declares that "the remembrance of David Hume was the very thing that many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber" and redirected his researches. The dossier presents this as what it is: Kant's own framing, written a dozen years and more after the turn it describes, and not a lab notebook. What exactly Kant read of [Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md), and when, is genuinely debated: the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* was available in German from 1755, while the *Treatise* had no full German translation until 1790–92, after the critical system existed. The stimulus may also have been mediated by the German debate around Hume rather than by the texts alone.

The substance is clear regardless of the conduit. Kant took from Hume exactly one problem and made it philosophy's master question: the concept of cause claims a *necessary* connection that no experience delivers and no analysis of the concept contains — so how is any synthetic a priori judgment possible? Hume's genius, on Kant's telling, was to stop halfway: he asked the question about causality and never generalized it. The *Critique* is the generalization, and its answer relocates necessity from a connection between things to a condition the mind imposes on anything it can experience. In the practical philosophy Hume figures again, as a rejected foundation: moral distinctions resting on sentiment cannot, for Kant, ground obligation — feeling varies, duty does not.

### Rousseau: the re-education of a scholar

The second awakening was moral, and it came first in time. Kant read [Rousseau](../../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md) intensively in the early 1760s — *Émile* and the *Social Contract* both appeared in 1762 — and his private *Remarks*, written in the margins of his own *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* (1764–65), record the conversion: "I am myself by inclination a seeker after truth… There was a time when I believed that this alone could constitute the honor of mankind, and I despised the common people, who know nothing. **Rousseau set me right.**" The blinding prejudice vanished, he writes; he learned to honor human beings. In the same notes Kant pairs Rousseau with Newton: as Newton first saw order and simplicity in the motions of the heavens, Rousseau first discerned, beneath the forms civilization imposes, the hidden nature of man.

Two hedges belong to the record here. The beloved story that Kant once missed his afternoon walk, absorbed in *Émile*, comes from later biographical tradition, not contemporary documentation; and his early biographers' report that the only picture in his otherwise bare rooms was a portrait of Rousseau is just that — a report, repeated because it is so exactly right in spirit. The doctrinal descent is firmer ground, though scholars debate its depth: that worth is located not in rank, wealth, or learning but in a moral capacity every human being shares is the seed of Kant's dignity doctrine, and the general will stands visibly behind his idea of a legislation every rational agent gives itself — transformed, as Kant transforms everything he takes, from a political device into the structure of autonomy itself.

## Sideways: the silent decade and the public quarrel

### Herz and Lambert: the Critique composed by letter

Kant's Inaugural Dissertation of 1770 separated sensibility from understanding and then stopped, unsure how pure concepts could relate to objects. The working-out happened in correspondence. **Marcus Herz** — Kant's former student, by then a Berlin physician in Mendelssohn's orbit, who had served as respondent at the Dissertation's defense — received on February 21, 1772 the most consequential letter Kant ever wrote. It states the missing piece as a question: "On what ground rests the relation of that in us which we call representation to the object?" Intellectual representations neither copy objects (we are not passive to them) nor create them (we are not God); so what entitles them to agree with objects at all? The letter promises a *Critique of Pure Reason* within three months. It took nine years.

**Johann Heinrich Lambert**, the Swiss polymath with whom Kant had corresponded since 1765, pressed the Dissertation from the other side. In his letter of October 1770 he objected that time cannot be a merely subjective form: change is real, and real change requires real time. Kant took the objection seriously, drafted toward an answer, and never answered it in print; Lambert died in 1777, and the Transcendental Aesthetic must serve as the indirect reply. The "silent decade" itself wants one qualification: Kant lectured constantly and published occasionally (the first of the race essays dates to 1775, a fact [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) returns to). The silence was about the book, not about philosophy.

### Hamann: the neighbor who answered with language

Johann Georg Hamann, Kant's fellow Königsberger and counter-Enlightenment gadfly, drafted in 1784 a *Metacritique of the Purism of Reason* — the first statement of the objection that has pursued Kant ever since: that reason's supposed purity ignores its embodiment in language, which is not reason's tool but its organ and criterion. Published only in 1800, the *Metacritique* changed little in Kant's lifetime; as the seed of every later linguistic objection to transcendental philosophy, it has outlasted most of what was printed against him.

### Jacobi, Mendelssohn, and the orientation essay

The Pantheism controversy made Kant a public combatant. Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi's *On the Doctrine of Spinoza* (1785) detonated the era's central anxiety: reason, Jacobi argued, pursued with perfect consistency ends in Spinozism — fatalism, atheism, what he would later name nihilism — and can be escaped only by a leap of faith. Moses Mendelssohn, defending his dead friend Lessing's reputation and rational religion in *Morning Hours* (1785), died in January 1786 with the quarrel unresolved. Mendelssohn was no stranger: he had beaten Kant for the Berlin Academy's prize in 1763 (Kant's entry took second place), and his courteous, puzzled reply to the Dissertation — pleaded on grounds of his nervous health — is among the correspondence's most human documents.

Pressed by allies of both camps, Kant intervened with "What Does It Mean to Orient Oneself in Thinking?" (October 1786, in the *Berlinische Monatsschrift*). It refuses both parties in a single gesture. Against Jacobi: no leap beyond reason can count as philosophy, however sincere. Against Mendelssohn: speculative reason cannot *demonstrate* God, and pretending otherwise discredits the cause. Instead, reason has a legitimate *need* — in its practical use it cannot orient itself without postulating a supreme being — and assent grounded in that need is *rational faith*: held firmly, claimed as no knowledge. The essay is the second *Critique*'s doctrine of the postulates in embryo, and Kant's one direct entry into the controversy that, as the next file shows, made his reputation.

## Forward: the heirs, wanted and unwanted

**Reinhold: the popularizer.** Karl Leonhard Reinhold's *Letters on the Kantian Philosophy*, serialized in Wieland's *Teutsche Merkur* in 1786–87, accomplished what the *Critique* itself had not: they presented Kant in accessible prose as the resolution of the age's defining quarrel — the very faith-versus-reason dispute of the Pantheism controversy — and made him famous. Reinhold's appointment at Jena in 1787 turned that university into the Kantian capital, the seedbed from which Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel all grew. Then Reinhold began the succession game: his *Elementary Philosophy* (from 1789) undertook to supply the single first principle Kant had allegedly failed to state. Kant was grateful for the publicity and endorsed none of the completions. The pattern was set in the first instance.

**[Fichte](../../eras/04-19th-century/johann-gottlieb-fichte.md): the heir disowned.** Fichte arrived in Königsberg in 1791 an unknown; his *Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation* (1792), published anonymously, was widely taken to be Kant's own new book — a misattribution Kant publicly corrected, praising the real author and making his name overnight. Fichte's *Wissenschaftslehre* (1794) then claimed to be nothing but the Kantian philosophy finally consistent with itself: the thing in itself dropped, the self-positing I installed as the single principle from which all else is derived. It was Kant's system, Fichte insisted, in spirit if not in letter. In August 1799 Kant replied in public, in terms without precedent in his polite career: he declared the *Wissenschaftslehre* a totally indefensible system and refused the paternity. The episode founded the durable pattern of Kantian succession — the master disowning the heir who claimed to complete him — and the next century repeated it twice more.

**[Schopenhauer](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md): the heir by hostile takeover.** Schopenhauer declared himself Kant's true successor with Plato as his only other master, and his dossier in this repo — [the Schopenhauer focus study](../arthur-schopenhauer/README.md) — is the natural next read after this one, not least because the two dossiers argue with each other. His judgment was that Kant's distinction between appearance and thing in itself was the master's greatest merit, and the long "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy" appended to *The World as Will and Representation* (1819; expanded 1844) is homage and annexation at once: the Aesthetic and the demolition of rational theology are praised; of the twelve categories only causality survives; the ethics of duty is dismissed as theology in disguise; and the identification of the thing in itself with *will* is presented as the one step Kant saw and stopped short of. That this is Kant completed is Schopenhauer's claim, not a finding — the dossier records it as his.

**[Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md): the verdict and the Aufhebung.** Hegel's Kant is double, deliberately. Kant is the pivot of modern philosophy: the *Lectures on the History of Philosophy* treat him as the point where thought takes its stand on subjectivity, and Hegel called the insight of the transcendental unity of apperception one of the deepest in the *Critique*. And Kant is a stage to be overcome: the thing in itself is, for Hegel, the emptiest abstraction — a beyond manufactured by thought and then declared off-limits to it; the categories remain fixed in a borrowed table; the moral law condemns itself to empty formalism. Hegel's own system presents itself not as Kant refuted but as Kant *aufgehoben* — canceled and preserved in one motion, the critical insight driven through the limit Kant had forbidden it to cross. Whether that is completion or repudiation under a courteous name is the founding dispute of post-Kantian philosophy, and it is still the frame within which the Idealist succession is taught.

**Strawson: the hinge of the Anglophone revival.** P. F. Strawson's *The Bounds of Sense* (1966) performed a severing operation that defined English-language Kant study for a generation: on one side, a living core of arguments about the necessary structure of experience — objectivity, reidentification, the unity of consciousness — worth reconstructing in Strawson's own austere idiom; on the other, transcendental idealism, dismissed as the doctrine of "the imaginary subject of transcendental psychology." The book made Kant arguable-with in Oxford and everywhere Oxford's habits reached. The historians' counter-attack — Allison, Guyer, and the scholarship traced in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) — restored the idealism as integral rather than ornamental; but the argumentative posture Strawson installed, reconstruction first, remains one of the two native styles of Kant scholarship. (Neither Strawson nor Korsgaard, below, has a library entry in this repo; both appear here in their roles as readers.)

**Rawls and Korsgaard: the living Kantianisms.** [John Rawls](../../eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md) made the most consequential Kantian move of the twentieth century in *A Theory of Justice* (1971): its §40 offers "the Kantian interpretation of justice as fairness," reading the original position as a procedural version of autonomy — principles are just when they would be chosen by free and equal rational beings under conditions that screen out heteronomy. Rawls's later "Kantian constructivism" (1980) generalized the method: moral principles are not discovered in an independent order but *constructed* by a procedure that models what reasonable agents could will. Christine Korsgaard's *The Sources of Normativity* (1996) works the other end of the same inheritance: the normative question — what justifies morality's claims on us? — is answered from within agency itself, and her constitutivism holds that the categorical imperative is the constitutive standard of action, the rule an agent must follow simply to count as acting at all. Together they mark the current state of the forward conversation: Kant treated not as a doctrine to be believed but as a method to be run — construct, from the standpoint of free and equal agents, the principles that standpoint commits you to.

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Kant in eight of its nine threads — the widest presence of any figure in the index, and a fair map of why the forward conversation keeps returning to him:

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — the categories and forms make experience possible: knowledge secure within phenomena, impossible of things in themselves.
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — the Paralogisms: a substantial, simple, identical soul cannot be inferred from the "I think," which is a formal unity of experience, not an object of knowledge.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — the speculative proofs fail on their own terms; God and immortality return as postulates of practical reason, and religion within reason is morality recognized as divine command.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — the categorical imperative: act only on maxims you can will as universal law, and treat humanity always also as an end.
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — right as external freedom under universal law; perpetual peace through republican constitutions, a federation of free states, and cosmopolitan hospitality.
- [§6 Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time) — the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations: the unity of apperception gives personal identity its form without yielding a knowable soul.
- [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) — Newtonian science is possible because space, time, and the categories are a priori conditions any experience must obey.
- [§9 History & Progress](../../themes.md#9-history--progress--does-history-have-a-direction-and-can-we-know-it) — nature's hidden plan works through unsocial sociability toward a cosmopolitan condition: history read as progress toward right.

The index does not place him in §7 (Language & Meaning) — the one absence, and broadly an accurate one: Kant scattered remarks on signs and concepts across the Logic and Anthropology but built no philosophy of language. It is the thread Hamann, above, tried to open against him; the index records, by omission, that in Kant's own work the opening was never taken up.
