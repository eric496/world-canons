# Focus Study: Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

## The figure in brief

Immanuel Kant spent all seventy-nine years of his life in and around Königsberg, East Prussia — born there in 1724 to a harness-maker's Pietist household, schooled there, a poorly paid lecturer there for fifteen years, a professor there from 1770, and dead there in 1804 without ever having left the province. The life was so quiet that legend has had to invent its drama. The thought was not quiet at all. After a decade of near-silence in print, Kant published the *Critique of Pure Reason* in 1781 and rebuilt philosophy's foundations: knowledge begins with experience but is structured by the mind's own a priori forms — space, time, the categories — so we can know phenomena with genuine necessity while things in themselves lie beyond knowledge altogether. He was to "deny knowledge in order to make room for faith": freedom, God, and immortality, unreachable by theory, return as postulates of practical reason. The practical philosophy is equally radical: morality is not obedience to an external law but the law a rational will gives itself, tested by universalizability and by the dignity of humanity as an end in itself. Between 1781 and 1790 the *Groundwork*, the second and third *Critiques*, and the *Prolegomena* re-founded epistemology, ethics, aesthetics, and the philosophy of religion in a single decade; late in life the system collided with Prussian censorship and held its ground on a point of wording. Nearly everything written after him — German idealism above all, but also modern deontology, liberal international theory, and analytic philosophy's abiding questions about the a priori — is written in his wake or against him.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

Three reasons, beyond the library's portrait. First, *position*: Kant is the hinge the whole library turns on. The learning path's rule — never meet Kant before Hume, never meet Hegel, Nietzsche, or Rawls without Kant — reflects the actual structure of the tradition: he answers Hume and Leibniz at once, and everyone after him, friend or enemy, starts from the ground he cleared. Second, *method*: Kant teaches a transferable discipline, the critique — before asking what we know, asking what makes such knowledge possible and where its jurisdiction ends — and the *Critique of Pure Reason* is the best training there is in holding a long argument's architecture in view while its parts interlock. Third, *the debates are live*: two-world versus two-aspect readings of transcendental idealism, the Deduction's exact reach, the empty-formalism charge against the categorical imperative, and the tension between his universalist ethics and his period writings on race and sex are all unsettled questions in current scholarship, argued on texts — not monuments to be admired from a distance.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Kant in **Stage 3** (Early Modern to Kant) as a *core* figure, in a strict sequence — "What Is Enlightenment?", the *Groundwork* whole, the *Prolegomena*, then *Critique of Pure Reason* selections only — at the end of the stage, and this dossier keeps that order and deepens it rather than replacing it. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out an **8-week plan of roughly 40 hours of scheduled study (about 47 hours all-in once the daily card queue is counted)**: the *Groundwork* and the two existing library guides worked in full, the *Critique* selections read with a second guided pass at the Deduction and the Aesthetic, the *Critique of Practical Reason* taken up properly rather than as an optional extra, and a closing pass through the *Critique of Judgment*'s bridge and the late public writings. It runs at the learning path's weekly rhythm: primary reading first, reconstruction before commentary, the daily FSRS queue fed by the dossier's cards. It plugs into Stage 3 at the Kant block; the hours sit on top of the Stage 3 budget, as focus-study hours always do, and the plan document shows the arithmetic week by week.
