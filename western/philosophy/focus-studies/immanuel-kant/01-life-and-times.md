# Life and Times: Königsberg, Pietism, and the Critical Turn

Kant's life is the standing counterexample to the idea that a philosopher's biography explains the philosophy. Outwardly almost nothing happens: one city, one university, no marriage, no journeys worth the name, and a working routine of legendary sameness. Inwardly, across the same decades, European philosophy was dismantled and rebuilt. The sources are good but particular: no diary, and the intimate record rests chiefly on three biographies published in the year of his death by men who knew him — Ludwig Ernst Borowski, a former student; Reinhold Bernhard Jachmann, his amanuensis; and Ehregott Andreas Christoph Wasianski, who helped care for him at the end — supplemented by Kant's extensive correspondence. Where this account repeats a story that comes from literature rather than from the record — above all the famous story of the neighbors and their watches — it says so. The dossier's rule is the archive's rule: hedged where the record is thin.

## A harness-maker's son in Pietist Königsberg

Immanuel Kant was born on April 22, 1724, in Königsberg, the capital of East Prussia — a Hanseatic trading city on the Pregel River, garrisoned, Lutheran, and about as far from the centers of European fashion as a university town could be. He was baptized Emanuel, a spelling he later changed to Immanuel in the belief that it matched the Hebrew original. He was the fourth of nine children of Johann Georg Kant, a master harness- and saddle-maker of modest means, and Anna Regina, née Reuter. Both parents were Pietists: adherents of the movement within Lutheranism that stressed inward devotion, conscience, and moral self-examination over doctrine and ceremony.

Pietism formed the household and then the school. Through the family friend Franz Albert Schulz — a leading Pietist theologian, pastor, and from 1733 director of the Collegium Fridericianum, the Pietist school in Königsberg — Kant entered the Fridericianum in 1732, aged eight, and stayed until 1740. The regime was exacting: long days dominated by Latin and religious exercises, with constant self-scrutiny as a duty. Kant's mature verdict on it was genuinely double. He permanently left Pietist practice behind him, and in later conversation he recalled the school's devotional rigor with distaste; but the Pietist valuation of intention over outward conformity, and its insistence that morality is a matter of the inner disposition rather than of results, left marks that scholars have plausibly traced in his ethics. His mother — by the early biographers' account the parent he felt closest to, and the one whose intelligence he praised — died in 1737, when he was thirteen. His father died in 1746. Whatever else the Fridericianum gave him, it gave him a first-rate Latin education and entry to the university.

## The university and the tutor years

Kant matriculated at the University of Königsberg — the Albertina — in 1740, at sixteen. His teacher and patron was Martin Knutzen, a Wolffian philosopher and mathematician who introduced him both to the Leibnizian-Wolffian system that structured German academic philosophy and to Newton's physics, and lent him books from his own library. The curriculum ranged widely — logic, metaphysics, mathematics, physics, geography, theology — and Kant's first publication, *Thoughts on the True Estimation of Living Forces*, drafted while he was still a student, intervened confidently in a live dispute about the measure of force left over from the Leibniz–Descartes quarrel. It is the work of a young Wolffian with Newtonian sympathies and no doubt at all that the two could be reconciled by sufficient care.

On leaving the university around 1748, Kant spent roughly six years as a *Hauslehrer* — a live-in private tutor — in a succession of households in the East Prussian countryside, at Judtschen, at Groß-Arnsdorf, and at Rautenberg. These are his only sustained periods of residence away from Königsberg, and by later testimony they polished him: the future lecturer remembered as witty and sociable in company acquired his ease in these years. In 1754 he returned to Königsberg to qualify as a teacher, and in 1755 the qualification came in a rush: a dissertation *On Fire* for the master's degree in the spring, and in September the habilitation dissertation, *A New Elucidation of the First Principles of Metaphysical Cognition*, defended for the right to lecture. He became a *Privatdozent* — an unsalaried lecturer, paid directly by the students who enrolled.

## Fifteen years a Privatdozent (1755–1770)

The fifteen years of the *Privatdozent* period were poor, busy, and intellectually omnivorous. Kant lectured, several hours a day, on logic, metaphysics, moral philosophy, mathematics, and physics, and — his own additions to the local curriculum, and among his most popular courses — on physical geography from 1756 and later on anthropology. He wrote steadily: on earthquakes (spurred by the Lisbon disaster of 1755), on winds, on the rotation of the earth, on optimism, on Swedenborg, on the diseases of the head. Two books of these years stand out for this dossier's purposes (see [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)): the *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens* (1755), with its nebular hypothesis of the solar system's origin, published anonymously in the same year he began lecturing; and *The Only Possible Argument in Support of a Demonstration of the Existence of God* (1763), which already contains his enduring objection to the ontological proof — that existence is not a real predicate. The same years show him competing successfully at the center of German academic philosophy: in the Berlin Academy's prize competition for 1763, on whether metaphysical truths admit proofs as distinct as geometry's, his *Inquiry Concerning the Distinctness of the Principles of Natural Theology and Morality* took second prize behind Moses Mendelssohn — one essay placing the unsalaried Königsberg lecturer second in the most visible contest in the German republic of letters — and its methodological moral, that philosophy should imitate Newton's method rather than borrow mathematics', became a fixed point of his development.

The decade also made him, locally, a literary figure. His *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* (1764), a graceful, essayistic book in a style he never repeated, found the general readership his technical work had not. In 1766 he took his first salaried post, a modest one: sub-librarian at the Royal Castle Library. Chairs elsewhere found him — he declined prospective posts at Erlangen and Jena in 1769 rather than leave Königsberg for the wrong chair — and the chair he wanted came at last in 1770, when he was appointed professor of logic and metaphysics at his own university. The *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer* (1766), his strange, satirical engagement with the Swedish visionary Emanuel Swedenborg, closes the period: a book that mocks metaphysics for exactly the overreach his own academic training embodied, and a signpost toward the discipline of limits he was about to impose on it.

## The Dissertation and the silent decade (1770–1781)

Kant marked his professorship with an inaugural dissertation, defended on August 21, 1770: *On the Form and Principles of the Sensible and the Intelligible World*. It draws the distinction on which everything later depends — between **sensibility**, the receptive faculty by which objects are given under the subjective forms of space and time, and the **understanding**, which thinks objects as they are. But the Dissertation still supposed that the understanding could know the intelligible world directly, and Kant saw the problem with his own construction quickly. In the most famous letter of the decade, to Marcus Herz on February 21, 1772, he stated the question he could not yet answer: on what ground rests the relation of a representation to its object — how can concepts formed a priori latch onto things at all? Answering that question took him the rest of the decade, and the answer is the *Critique of Pure Reason*.

The decade is conventionally called silent, and in print it nearly was: no book appeared between 1770 and 1781, and the scattered small pieces — a review, an announcement, the essay *On the Different Races of Man* (1775) — would fill a slim pamphlet. The silence was compositional, not idle: Kant was teaching full-time (anthropology lectures began in the winter of 1772–73), corresponding, and working through successive drafts of the critical system. Nor was it obscurity: in 1778 he declined the chair at Halle — more prestigious and better paid than his own — choosing Königsberg and the unfinished work over advancement. What forced the pace of the problem, by Kant's own later account, was Hume. In the preface to the *Prolegomena* (1783) he wrote that the remembrance of David Hume had, many years before, interrupted his "dogmatic slumber" and given his researches in speculative philosophy a new direction: Hume's demonstration that the concept of cause and effect cannot be derived from experience showed that the metaphysics Kant had been taught simply assumed the very thing that needed proving. The confession was written a decade after the fact and its exact dating is still discussed; that Hume's problem is the *Critique*'s ignition is not.

## The great decade (1781–1790)

The *Critique of Pure Reason* appeared in the spring of 1781, published by Johann Friedrich Hartknoch in Riga — a large, difficult book that its author had expected to make him famous and that was instead, initially, misunderstood or ignored. The decisive blow was the first major review, published at Göttingen in January 1782 (written by Christian Garve and heavily edited by J. G. H. Feder), which read Kant as one more idealist in Berkeley's line — precisely the position the book had been built to avoid. Kant's response shaped the next ten years. First came repair: the *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* (1783), a shorter, clearer restatement written for exactly the reader the *Critique* had lost; and a substantially revised second edition of the *Critique* itself (1787), with a new preface containing the Copernican analogy, a rewritten Transcendental Deduction, a recast chapter on the paralogisms, and the new Refutation of Idealism aimed at the Berkeley charge.

Around the repairs, the system spread across every domain. The essay "An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?" (December 1784) — *sapere aude*, dare to know, emergence from self-incurred immaturity — became his most widely read short piece. The *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) laid the foundation of the practical philosophy: the good will, the categorical imperative in its formulations, autonomy. The *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* (1786) applied the critical principles to matter and motion. The *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) systematized the ethics and installed freedom, God, and immortality as postulates of practical reason. The *Critique of Judgment* (1790) completed the critical trilogy with the beautiful, the sublime, and teleological judgment, and announced itself as the bridge between the systems of nature and freedom. These were also the years of settled personal life: in 1783 Kant bought the house on Prinzessinstraße that remained his home, kept a famous dinner table at which students remembered the lecturer as a sparkling conversationalist, and enjoyed the close friendship of the English merchant Joseph Green. The strictly ordered daily routine of the later years — rising before five, lectures, writing, the afternoon walk, bed at ten — dates from this maturity, not from his youth. The fame, when it finally came, arrived through a publicist: Karl Leonhard Reinhold's *Letters on the Kantian Philosophy* (1786–87), serialized in Wieland's *Teutsche Merkur*, presented the critical philosophy as the settlement that Germany's quarrels over reason and faith had been waiting for, and students began making the journey to Königsberg. The unsalaried lecturer of 1755 ended the decade as the most consequential philosopher in Germany.

## The censorship clash (1792–1798), and the pledge

The system's last domain brought it into collision with the state. Frederick the Great, under whom Kant had published freely, died in 1786; his successor, Frederick William II, governed through the pietist minister Johann Christoph Wöllner, whose Religious Edict of 1788 tightened supervision of doctrine. Kant's long-planned work on religion began appearing as journal pieces. The first, "On Radical Evil in Human Nature," passed the Berlin censor in 1792 — the philosophy censor, G. F. Hillmer, is reported to have approved it on the ground that only deep-thinking scholars read Kant. The second piece was refused by the theological censor as a work of biblical theology; the journal's editor, Johann Erich Biester, appealed twice, and lost. Kant then used a route the law left open: he submitted the complete work for the imprimatur of the *philosophical* faculty of the University of Jena — philosophy faculties, not theological ones, were competent to license a philosophical book — and published *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* at Königsberg in 1793, with a second edition in 1794.

The crown answered in October 1794 with a cabinet order, signed by the king and drafted in Wöllner's circle, accusing Kant of misusing his philosophy "to distort and disparage many of the cardinal and basic teachings of the Holy Scriptures and of Christianity," and demanding both a conscientious account of himself and an undertaking not to offend again. Kant's reply, dated October 12, 1794, defended his writings at length — he had treated religion as a philosopher, from philosophical texts, never disparaging Scripture — and then made the pledge on which everything turned: he solemnly declared, **"as Your Majesty's most loyal subject,"** that he would thereafter refrain altogether from public discourse on religion, natural or revealed, in lectures or in writings. He kept the pledge scrupulously; when the Göttingen theologian C. F. Stäudlin later offered him a route to publish on religion beyond Prussian reach, he declined, regarding the offer as an evasion of his word.

Frederick William II died on November 16, 1797. Kant then published, in 1798, *The Conflict of the Faculties*, whose preface prints the royal order, his reply, and his justification for resuming: the pledge, on his reasoning, had been made not simply *by* a subject but *as* "His Majesty's most loyal subject" — a qualification he states in the preface he had chosen with deliberation, precisely so as not to renounce his freedom of judgment in perpetuity — and the obligation it created ran to that king personally, expiring with him. The reasoning has been argued about ever since, on evidence Kant himself published. His defenders note that he honored the letter under pressure, at real cost, when easier evasions were available to him, and that reading promises as owed to persons is no lawyer's trick. His critics, then and since, have found the construction casuistical: a reservation planted in a solemn declaration, by the philosopher of universalizable maxims, looks like exactly the kind of mental reservation his ethics condemns in the lying promise. Kant scholarship has not settled the question, and this dossier presents it as Kant left it — a genuine dispute about the man, conducted over his own documents.

## Decline and death (1796–1804)

Kant gave his last lectures in the summer of 1796, closing forty-one years of teaching, and published his remaining books in quick succession: the *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797), *The Conflict of the Faculties* and the *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View* (1798) — the latter worked up from the lecture course he had given for some twenty-five years. He continued to write in private: the loose, unfinished manuscript now known as the *Opus postumum*, notes toward a transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics, which occupied him nearly to the end and which he never prepared for publication.

From about 1800 the decline was visible and then steep: failing eyesight, failing memory, the loss of the routine that had organized his days. Friends and former students managed the household; Wasianski, who lived nearby, took an increasing part in his care and is the chief source for the final months. Kant died in Königsberg on February 12, 1804, two months short of his eightieth birthday. His reported last words — *"Es ist gut,"* "It is good" — come from Wasianski's account of the last evening, and are reported words, handed down by a devoted witness rather than documented ones; the dossier prints them as such. He was buried in the professors' vault at Königsberg Cathedral, after a funeral the whole city attended.

Two legends deserve their explicit labels, since both will outlive any correction. The neighbors who set their watches by Kant's afternoon walk — and in the fuller telling, the single day the walk was missed because he could not put down Rousseau's *Émile* — enter the literature through Heinrich Heine's essay on German religion and philosophy (1834), written thirty years after Kant's death for French readers, and are literary portraiture, not testimony; no contemporary source records the *Émile* exception. That the underlying life was indeed one of exceptional regularity is agreed by the friends who wrote about him in 1804. The legend exaggerates a truth; it remains a legend.
