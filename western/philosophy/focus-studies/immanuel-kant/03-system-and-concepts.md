# Kant: The System and Its Concepts

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Immanuel Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) · Study guides: [*Critique of Pure Reason*](../../study/kant-critique.md) · [*Groundwork*](../../study/kant-groundwork.md)*

## The system in one view

Kant's critical philosophy is one building in three wings, and the architecture is the argument. The first *Critique* (1781/1787) asks what we can *know*, and answers: the structure of experience, all of it — but only experience, never what lies beyond it. The *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) asks what we ought to *do*, and finds that the very reason barred from speculative knowledge legislates a moral law with unconditional authority, and is entitled — on practical grounds alone — to affirm freedom, God, and immortality, the three things theory had to leave undecided. The *Critique of Judgment* (1790) mediates between the other two through purposiveness, beauty, and organic life. Underneath all three runs one question, posed in the first *Critique*'s Introduction: **how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?** — how can we know things both genuinely informative and strictly necessary, when neither unpacking concepts nor consulting experience supplies both features at once? Everything in this file is part of Kant's single, ramifying answer.

## The Copernican turn

The system's founding gesture is a reversal of direction, announced in the Preface to the second edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (Bxv–xviii). Metaphysics had always assumed that "all our cognition must conform to the objects"; on that assumption, Kant argues, no a priori knowledge of objects is intelligible, since what we bring to objects cannot be learned from them. He proposes to try the opposite hypothesis: "the objects must conform to our cognition" — as Copernicus explained the heavens by making the observer revolve instead. On Kant's version, we can know a priori of things "only what we ourselves put into them" — not their content, which is given from outside us, but their *form*: the structural conditions anything must satisfy to be an object of experience for beings constituted as we are.

Two guards against misreading belong with the gesture. First, it concerns the form of experience, not a claim that the mind invents the world: Kant is an **empirical realist**, and within experience objects are public, law-governed, and independent of what anyone thinks. Second, the reversal is a *hypothesis the Critique tests*, not a premise it assumes. The B Preface's sentence of intent belongs here: Kant "had to *deny knowledge* in order to make room for *faith*" (Bxxx) — theory's road to God, freedom, and immortality is closed so that practical reason's road to them can be legitimate. That partition is the system's master key.

## Two stems of cognition: sensibility and understanding

Human cognition, Kant holds, has two stems, "which perhaps spring from a common, but to us unknown, root" (A15/B29). **Sensibility** is the receptive faculty: through it objects are *given* to us, in **intuition** — an immediate, singular representation of an object present to the mind. **Understanding** is the spontaneous faculty: through it objects are *thought*, by means of **concepts** — general, mediate representations under which many things can fall. Neither stem can do the other's work, and neither can swap functions: "The understanding is not capable of intuiting anything, and the senses are not capable of thinking anything" (A51/B75). Hence the book's most quoted sentence: "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (A51/B75).

The division of labor generates the first *Critique*'s structure. The **Transcendental Aesthetic** studies sensibility's a priori contribution. The **Transcendental Logic** studies the understanding: its **Analytic** sets out the concepts and principles that make experience possible, and its **Dialectic** exposes the illusion that arises when reason — a third power, seeking the unconditioned totality behind all conditioned cognition — operates those concepts beyond experience. Sensibility comes first, because it fixes the arena inside which everything else must operate.

## Space and time: the forms of intuition

The Aesthetic's claim is that sensibility contributes two a priori **forms of intuition**. **Space** is the form of *outer* sense: nothing can be represented as outside us, or alongside other things, except in space (A22–26/B37–41). **Time** is the form of *inner* sense: we represent our own states only as successive in time (A30–33/B46–49) — and since all representations are states of the mind, time is mediately the form of all appearances, outer as well as inner.

Kant's arguments (Argument 1 in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)) run the same pattern for both. Space is not an empirical concept abstracted from outer experience, because representing things as outside me or side by side already presupposes it. It is a necessary representation: I can think space empty of objects, but I cannot represent outer objects except in it. And space is not a discursive concept at all but a single, infinite *intuition*: particular spaces are limitations carved out of the one space, not instances collected under a general term. The **transcendental exposition** draws the payoff: only if space is an a priori form of sensibility can geometry be synthetic a priori knowledge, necessary and yet informative, holding of everything that can appear to us (A26–27/B42–43).

The doctrinal conclusion is **transcendental idealism**, held in both of its halves at once. Space and time are **transcendentally ideal**: forms of our cognition, not determinations of things as they are in themselves. And they are **empirically real**: within experience, the spatiotemporal world is entirely objective — public, measurable, and law-governed, not a private show. The two halves are one claim, and most misreadings of Kant come from dropping one of them.

## The categories and the two Deductions

What the Aesthetic does for sensibility, the Analytic does for the understanding. If experience is to be experience *of objects* — enduring substances in causal order, not a rhapsody of perceptions — the given manifold must be unified under rules that cannot be abstracted from the experience they make possible. The understanding's a priori concepts are the **categories**: pure concepts prescribing the forms any unification of a manifold into an object must take.

Kant claims to find them completely and systematically, rather than collecting them rhapsodically as Aristotle had. The **Metaphysical Deduction** (A66–83/B91–116) takes as its "clue" the table of the logical functions of judgment: the same function of the understanding that gives unity to representations in a judgment also gives unity to a manifold in intuition. Twelve forms of judgment yield twelve categories (A80/B106), in four groups of three: **quantity** (unity, plurality, totality), **quality** (reality, negation, limitation), **relation** (substance and accident, cause and effect, community), and **modality** (possibility, existence, necessity). Whether the derivation is a genuine discovery or a borrowing dressed as one — and whether the list is complete — has been disputed since the book appeared.

Finding the categories is only half the problem; vindicating their use is the other. "Deduction" is a legal term before a logical one: a *quid juris* question — by what right do we apply these concepts to objects? — against the *quid facti* question of how we came by them, which Locke answered empirically and Hume, on Kant's reading, so as to destroy the concepts' claim to necessity. The **Transcendental Deduction** answers that the categories are legitimately used because they are conditions of the possibility of experience itself: nothing could be an object *for a self-conscious mind* except as unified under them. Its core (Argument 2) turns on the **original unity of apperception** — "The *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations" (B131–132) — a unity that is no passive fact but an achievement of **synthesis**, combination of a manifold according to rules whose rule-forms are the categories.

There are two Deductions, and the flag matters. Kant rewrote the chapter wholesale for 1787, and the **A Deduction** (A95–130) and the **B Deduction** (§§15–27, B129–169) are different arguments in structure and emphasis, not two printings of one. A proceeds through a threefold synthesis — apprehension in intuition, reproduction in imagination, recognition in a concept (A98–110) — with a psychological flavor critics seized on. B is more austere: first (§§16–20) the manifold in any intuition is subject to the categories insofar as it is to be unified in one consciousness, then (§§24–26) the gap to *our* spatiotemporal intuition is closed through the "figurative synthesis" of imagination acting on sensibility. Teaching reads B as primary, with A as the famous alternative; the scholarship still argues about which, if either, fully succeeds, and how B's two steps fit. What is not in dispute is the conclusion's built-in limit: objective validity for objects of possible experience — and beyond that boundary, forms of thought without knowledge.

## The Schematism: how categories meet appearances

Between the Analytic's two halves sits a short, dense chapter answering an obvious problem. The categories are pure concepts — nothing sensible is in them — while appearances are through and through sensible. How can an appearance be *subsumed* under a concept so unlike it? Subsumption needs a mediator homogeneous with both sides, and Kant's candidate is **time**: a **transcendental schema** is a determination of time according to a category's rule (A137–147/B176–187). The schema of substance is the *permanence* of the real in time; of causality, *succession* of the manifold insofar as it is subject to a rule; of necessity, *existence at all times*. Schemata are not images — no picture could depict causality — but procedures of the productive imagination for giving a concept sensible significance. With them in hand, the Analytic of Principles states the system's empirical constitution: the Analogies of Experience (permanence of substance; rule-governed succession — the Second Analogy is Argument 3; thoroughgoing interaction) and, new in B and aimed at Cartesian skepticism, the **Refutation of Idealism** (B274–279, Argument 4): consciousness of my existence as determined in time presupposes something permanent perceived *outside* me, so inner experience is possible only through outer.

## Phenomena and noumena — and the interpretive debate this dossier does not settle

The Aesthetic's conclusion and the Deduction's limit together yield the system's central distinction. We know **phenomena** — things as they appear under our forms of intuition and categories. A **thing in itself** is a thing considered as it is independently of those conditions. The corresponding technical term, **noumenon**, Kant handles with care: in the *negative* sense it is a thing insofar as it is not an object of our sensible intuition — a limit-concept (*Grenzbegriff*), whose function is "to curb the pretensions of sensibility" (A255/B310–311); in the *positive* sense it would be an object for an intellectual intuition we do not possess, and of such an object we can form no determinate concept at all.

How even that careful statement should be read is the central interpretive dispute in Kant scholarship, flagged here, in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), and in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) without being settled. On the **two-world (two-object) reading**, phenomena and things in themselves are two distinct realms: appearances, in space and time and knowable, and a separate unknowable reality behind them. On the **two-aspect (one-world) reading**, there is one world of things, considered two ways: as they appear to beings with our forms of cognition, and as they are in abstraction from those forms. Each reading buys its clarity at a price the other names. The two-world reading must explain how things in themselves can *affect* us — "affection" being causal, and causality valid only within experience — without violating the system's own boundary; critics have pressed exactly this point since Kant's earliest readers. The two-aspect reading must explain the many passages where Kant speaks as if appearances and things in themselves were numerically distinct, and say what work the distinction does if it is merely methodological. The dispute is a live fault line through the whole secondary literature; a reader of the *Critique* should know which reading a commentator assumes.

## The Dialectic: paralogisms, antinomies, and the Ideal

The understanding, bounded, yields knowledge. **Reason** (*Vernunft*), the faculty that seeks the *unconditioned* — the completion of every series of conditions — overruns the boundary by its own logic, and the Transcendental Dialectic is the anatomy of the resulting illusion. Reason forms three **ideas**, totalities no experience can contain: the **soul** (the unconditioned unity of the thinking subject), the **world** (the unconditioned totality of appearances), and **God** (the unconditioned ground of all possibility).

The **Paralogisms** (A341–405; recast at B399–432) dismantle rational psychology. From the *I think* — the formal unity of apperception the Deduction had established — the rational psychologist infers a soul that is a substance, simple, numerically identical over time. Each inference, Kant argues, trades on an equivocation: the "I" that must accompany representations is the *logical subject* of thought, never given as an object among objects; from the form of self-consciousness nothing follows about the constitution of a soul-thing. Theory can settle nothing about the soul's substantiality, simplicity, or survival — a negative result that leaves the practical question standing where it was.

The **Antinomies** (A426–567/B454–595) are the Dialectic's centerpiece, and the brief for this file requires all four laid out. When reason treats the world — the totality of appearances — as a completed object given in itself, it can prove, with equal apparent rigor, both sides of four contradictions, one for each way the conditioned series of appearances can be totalized:

1. **First Antinomy (the world's magnitude).** *Thesis:* the world has a beginning in time and is enclosed within limits in space. *Antithesis:* the world has no beginning and no limits in space, but is infinite in regard to both time and space.
2. **Second Antinomy (division).** *Thesis:* every composite substance in the world consists of simple parts, and nothing exists anywhere except the simple or what is composed of simples. *Antithesis:* no composite thing in the world consists of simple parts, and nowhere in the world does anything simple exist.
3. **Third Antinomy (causality).** *Thesis:* causality in accordance with laws of nature is not the only causality from which the appearances of the world can all be derived; to explain them it is also necessary to assume a causality through freedom. *Antithesis:* there is no freedom, but everything in the world takes place solely in accordance with laws of nature.
4. **Fourth Antinomy (necessary being).** *Thesis:* there belongs to the world something that, either as its part or as its cause, is an absolutely necessary being. *Antithesis:* an absolutely necessary being exists nowhere — neither in the world nor outside the world as its cause.

Kant's resolutions split the four into two pairs. The first two, the **mathematical** antinomies, concern magnitudes homogeneous in kind; both sides turn out **false**, because both presuppose that the world as a whole is a determinate object, whereas the world exists only in the empirical regress of our representations, never given complete — the series of conditions is indefinite, neither finished nor endless. The last two, the **dynamical** antinomies, concern the connection of existences, where the condition need not be homogeneous with the conditioned; here both sides can be **true** from different standpoints — the antithesis true of appearances, the thesis possibly true of things in themselves. The Third Antinomy's resolution is the one the system banks on: it does not prove we are free; it proves that universal natural determinism and freedom are not in formal contradiction once standpoints are distinguished, and hands the question to practical reason. The whole collision also serves Kant as an indirect proof of transcendental idealism (Argument 5): on the assumption that appearances are things in themselves the contradiction is unavoidable; on his distinction it disappears.

The **Ideal of Pure Reason** (A567–642/B595–670) completes the demolition on theology's side. Reason forms the idea of God as the *ens realissimum*, the most real being, containing all reality. Speculative theology attempts three proofs of this being's existence, and Kant shows there can be only three (A590/B618): the **ontological** (from the mere concept of the most real being), the **cosmological** (from anything contingent, to a necessary being), and the **physico-theological** (from nature's order, to an intelligent author). They fail in a dependency chain: the physico-theological establishes at most an architect of nature, not a creator, and must borrow the cosmological to reach a necessary being; the cosmological in turn relies on the ontological, since it can identify its necessary being with the *ens realissimum* only by the ontological argument's move. And the ontological argument fails at its pivot: **existence is not a real predicate** — not a determination that could be added to a concept to enlarge it (A598/B626). "A hundred actual thalers do not contain the least bit more than a hundred possible ones" (A599/B627): to say a thing exists is not to add a property to its concept but to *posit* the object corresponding to it, so no concept, God's included, contains its own instantiation (Argument 6). The net result is the partition stated at the outset: speculative reason can neither prove nor disprove God, freedom, or immortality — though the ideas retain a **regulative** use, directing inquiry toward systematic unity without licensing any constitutive claim.

## The practical system: the moral law and autonomy

The practical philosophy begins from a different given: not experience, but the moral law as it presents itself in ordinary moral consciousness. The *Groundwork* (1785) opens by isolating the one unconditional good: "It is impossible to think of anything at all in the world, or indeed even beyond it, that could be considered good without limitation except a **good will**" (Ak. 4:393). Talents, temperaments, and even happiness are good only conditionally — a bad will can turn every one of them to harm. A good will is good not by what it accomplishes but by its willing: by acting not merely *in conformity with* duty but *from* duty, on a **maxim** — the subjective principle on which an agent acts.

Imperatives divide accordingly. A **hypothetical imperative** commands an action as a means to some desired end and binds only on condition of wanting the end. A **categorical imperative** commands the action as necessary in itself, without reference to any end, and binds unconditionally. There is, Kant argues, only one such imperative, expressed in several **formulas** — not rival tests but one law regarded from different sides:

- **Universal law:** "Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (4:402; restated, with the law-of-nature variant, at 4:421). The test runs a maxim through universalization: if the universalized maxim cannot even be *conceived* without contradiction — the false promise of Argument 7, which universalized destroys the practice of promising it exploits — or cannot be *willed* without contradiction, the maxim is contrary to duty.
- **Humanity:** "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means" (4:429). Argument 8 reconstructs its grounding.
- **Autonomy:** the idea of "the will of every rational being as a will giving universal law" (4:431) — the will is subject to the law because it is the law's author.
- **Kingdom of ends:** act on maxims fit for membership in a "possible kingdom of ends" (4:433) — a systematic union of rational beings under common laws they give themselves, each at once legislating and subject, each treated always as an end. In that kingdom everything has either a **price**, for which an equivalent can be substituted, or a **dignity**, exalted above all price (4:434–435).

**Autonomy** is the system's practical center: a will is autonomous when the law it obeys issues from its own reason and nothing else. The opposite, **heteronomy**, is every morality that takes the law from an object outside reason — inclination, happiness, divine command, moral feeling. Autonomy is not inventing values to suit oneself: the law reason gives itself is a law *any* rational being gives itself, which is what its universality means. And autonomy connects the practical philosophy to the first *Critique*'s open question, via the *Groundwork*'s two-standpoints doctrine (Argument 9): freedom is the *ratio essendi* of the moral law, the moral law the *ratio cognoscendi* of freedom (5:4n) — we know our freedom only through the law, but the law holds only because we are free.

## Fact of reason, postulates, primacy, and the highest good

How that connection is secured changed between 1785 and 1788, and the shift is a known scholarly crux this dossier flags rather than smooths over (Argument 9). The *Groundwork*'s third section attempts a **deduction** of the moral law: a proof, from the standpoint-structure of rational agency, that we are free and therefore bound. Kant himself locates a circle in the attempt (4:450): we take ourselves as free in order to think ourselves under moral laws, and under moral laws because we have ascribed freedom to ourselves. The *Critique of Practical Reason* reverses the direction of proof. The moral law is now given as a **fact of reason** (5:31): "Consciousness of this fundamental law may be called a fact of reason, because one cannot reason it out from antecedent data of reason" — it forces itself on us, needs and admits no deduction; freedom, undemonstrable by theory, is instead *inferred* as the law's necessary condition, its reality established "by an apodictic law of practical reason" (5:47). Whether this reversal is a candid abandonment of a failed deduction or the maturation of the *Groundwork*'s own argument — Karl Ameriks influentially read it as a "great reversal" Kant never acknowledged — remains genuinely disputed.

Granted the moral law's authority, practical reason generates its own metaphysics — under strict controls. Reason directs us to promote the **highest good** (*summum bonum*): virtue, the supreme good, together with happiness proportioned to it as its completed object (5:110–114). Two obstacles follow: complete virtue — holiness of will — is unattainable by a finite being at any moment, yet the law demands it; and nature distributes happiness without the least regard to worthiness. Pure practical reason therefore **postulates** — assumes, as a necessary condition of an object it is commanded to pursue, without thereby acquiring theoretical knowledge — three things theory had left undecided: **freedom** (inferred from the law itself), **immortality** (endless duration for unending progress toward holiness, 5:122), and **God** (a moral author of nature who can guarantee happiness in accord with worthiness, 5:124–125) — a postulate being "a theoretical proposition, though one not demonstrable as such, insofar as it is attached inseparably to an a priori unconditionally valid practical law" (5:122). Argument 10 reconstructs the God-postulate.

The doctrine licensing this traffic is the **primacy of practical reason** (5:119–121): where speculative reason cannot decide a question and practical reason has a necessary interest in it, speculative reason must accept the propositions practical reason supplies — not as knowledge, but as faith on practical grounds — provided they contradict nothing speculation has established. Stated already in the first *Critique*'s Canon (A795–831/B823–859), it lets Kant answer his third question — *What may I hope?* (A805/B833) — while the first *Critique*'s negative results stand untouched.

## Radical evil

One doctrine of the practical philosophy cuts against its own optimism, developed late: *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* (1793), whose first part argues that the human being is "by nature evil" — not in the sense of a beastly nature beneath morality, but in a precise, technical one. Human beings have a **propensity to evil** (*Hang zum Bösen*): a subjective ground, itself adopted by freedom, for the possibility of deviating from the moral law. It shows itself in three grades (6:29–30): **frailty** (adopting good maxims but failing to follow them), **impurity** (needing incentives besides the law to do what the law requires), and **depravity** (reversing the moral order of incentives — subordinating the moral law, in one's supreme maxim, to self-love). Because the propensity corrupts the ground of *all* maxims — the root (Latin *radix*) — Kant calls it **radical evil**: "a radical innate evil in human nature (not any the less brought upon us by ourselves)" (6:32).

Every qualifier there is load-bearing, and the strain among them is the doctrine's acknowledged difficulty. The propensity is *innate* — presupposed in every human being, prior to every observable deed — yet it must be **imputable**, brought upon us by ourselves, since otherwise it would be a natural mechanism and not evil at all: it must consist "in maxims of the power of choice contrary to the law," adopted by a free agent (6:32). And its ground is, by Kant's own insistence, **inscrutable**: the human being "holds within himself a first ground (to us inscrutable) for the adoption of good or evil maxims" (6:21), and the propensity's rational origin "remains inscrutable to us" (6:43). Argument 11 sets out the resulting paradox — a universal, innate corruption that must nevertheless be each agent's own free deed — and why Kant thought flagging its inscrutability was honesty rather than failure.

## Judgment: purposiveness, beauty, and the sublime

The third *Critique* (1790) completes the system by examining the faculty that mediates between reason's other two employments: **judgment** (*Urteilskraft*), the faculty "for thinking the particular as contained under the universal" (5:179). Where judgment is *determinative*, the universal is given and the particular subsumed under it; where it is **reflective**, only the particular is given, and the universal must be found for it. Reflective judgment operates under its own a priori principle: the **purposiveness of nature** — the presupposition, necessary for inquiry though constitutive of no object, that nature's empirical diversity is ordered as if an understanding had arranged it for our faculties. The principle is regulative: it guides the search for empirical laws and genera, and supports both aesthetic and teleological judgment — the two places where we must judge nature as purposive without being entitled to claim it *is* so by design.

A **judgment of taste** — that something is beautiful — is the aesthetic case, analyzed in four **moments** (§§1–22). As to *quality*, the pleasure is **disinterested**: independent of the object's existence and of any desire it gratifies (§§1–5). As to *quantity*, the judgment claims **universal validity without a concept**: I demand everyone's agreement, though no rule compels it (§§6–9). The ground of the demand is the **free play** of imagination and understanding — the object's form sets the faculties into harmonious activity, imagination exhibiting and understanding unifying without determination by any definite concept, and the pleasure is in that animation itself (§9, 5:217–218). As to *relation*, the beautiful exhibits **purposiveness without a purpose**: its form is judged as if designed for our faculties, while no definite end is represented or ascribed (§§10–17, esp. §15). As to *modality*, the pleasure is felt as **necessary**, on the presupposition of a *common sense* (*sensus communis*) shared by all judges (§§18–22).

The **sublime** (§§23–29) is the beautiful's counterweight. Where beauty is bounded form and calm pleasure, the sublime is provoked by the formless and apparently **contrapurposive** — what defeats the imagination — and its pleasure is negative: attraction and repulsion in vibration, admiration rather than delight. The **mathematically sublime** (§§25–27) arises when imagination fails to comprehend an absolutely great magnitude — the starry sky, a vast range — and the failure itself reveals reason's idea of infinity, beside which everything sensible is small. The **dynamically sublime** (§28) arises when nature is represented as a *might* — storms, volcanoes, the ocean in uproar — that could annihilate us physically but over which, judged from safety, we discover a superiority of another order: our moral vocation, against which physical power counts for nothing. Strictly, sublimity is not in the object but only in the mind of the one who judges (§23) — nature involuntarily schooling us in our supersensible calling, on the warrant of the moral feeling it presupposes in every judge (§29).

The *Critique of Teleological Judgment* extends the same logic to organisms. A living thing must be judged as a **natural end**: a whole whose parts are reciprocally cause and effect of one another, self-organizing as no machine is — a watch's parts do not produce one another, a tree's do (§§64–66; the principle at §66, 5:376–377). Mechanism can never make this intelligible to us; yet teleology remains a maxim of *reflective* judgment only, not a constitutive claim that nature was designed.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Kant's own usage, with the textbook senses that mislead flagged where they exist.

- **Sensibility:** the receptive faculty through which objects are given to us in intuition (A19/B33). Not emotional "sensitivity" — Kant's term covers the whole givenness of objects, and sensibility contributes a priori forms (space and time) of its own.
- **Understanding (*Verstand*):** the spontaneous faculty of concepts and rules, through which what sensibility gives is thought (A51/B75). It cannot intuit or supply its own content; its pure concepts are the categories.
- **Intuition (*Anschauung*):** an immediate, singular representation through which an object is given (A320/B377). Human intuition is sensible only — we have no intellectual intuition — which is why concepts without intuition are empty.
- **Category:** a pure concept of the understanding — an a priori rule for unifying a manifold of intuition into experience of an object (A80/B106). Not a pigeonhole into which things are sorted, but a function of synthesis.
- **Apperception:** the self-consciousness that must be able to accompany all my representations (B131–132). Its "original unity" is the Deduction's supreme principle: transcendental (a condition of experience), not empirical (a fact about a mind's stream of thought).
- **Schema:** the mediating representation — a determination of time — that lets a pure concept apply to appearances (A137/B176). A procedure of the imagination, not an image: permanence for substance, rule-governed succession for causality.
- **Phenomenon:** a thing as it appears under the conditions of human cognition — the only kind of object we know. Not an illusion: phenomena are empirically real, public, and law-governed.
- **Noumenon / thing in itself:** a thing considered in abstraction from our cognitive conditions (negative sense, A255/B310–311). A limit-concept marking where knowledge stops; whether it names a separate realm or a second way of considering the same things is the unresolved two-world/two-aspect dispute.
- **Transcendental idealism:** Kant's doctrine that space, time, and the categories apply to things as they appear to us, not to things in themselves — paired, always, with empirical realism. Not the claim that the world is a dream; Kant added the Refutation of Idealism to combat that reading.
- **Idea (of reason):** a concept of the unconditioned totality of a series of conditions — soul, world, God (A321/B378). Ideas have a legitimate regulative use, guiding inquiry toward unity; their constitutive use, as if they named knowable objects, is the Dialectic's illusion.
- **Maxim:** the subjective principle on which an agent actually acts — "in circumstances C, I will do A, for end E" (Groundwork, 4:421n). The categorical imperative tests maxims, not act-types in the abstract — which is why their honest description matters.
- **Categorical imperative:** the single supreme principle of morality: act only on maxims fit to be universal law (4:421) — one law expressed in the formulas of universal law, humanity, autonomy, and the kingdom of ends. Not the Golden Rule, and not a list of rules.
- **Autonomy:** the will's property of giving the moral law to itself, bound by nothing outside its own reason (4:431–433). Not doing as one pleases: the law reason legislates is the same for every rational being. Its opposite, **heteronomy** — determination by inclination, happiness, command, or feeling — is the principle of every failed moral system.
- **Postulate (of practical reason):** a theoretical proposition, indemonstrable as such, assented to because it is inseparably attached to an unconditionally valid practical law (5:122). Freedom, immortality, and God are postulated — objects of rational faith, not knowledge.
- **Highest good (*summum bonum*):** the complete object of practical reason: virtue together with happiness in exact proportion to it (5:110–114). Supreme (virtue) versus complete (virtue crowned with deserved happiness) is a distinction Kant draws and critics exploit.
- **Purposiveness (*Zweckmäßigkeit*):** the character of being ordered as if by a concept toward an end (5:220). In aesthetics it appears "without a purpose"; in teleology, as a regulative maxim for judging organisms. Never, in either use, a knowledge-claim that something was designed.

## Known internal tensions

The template asks for the strains to be named, since they drive the scholarship (see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)). Five recur, and the dossier does not pretend any of them is settled. **(1) Affection by the unknowable.** The system says things in themselves affect our sensibility, and also that causality is valid only within experience. Jacobi's famous verdict — that without the presupposition of the thing in itself he could not enter the system, and with it he could not remain in it — states a problem the two-aspect reading relocates but does not obviously remove. **(2) The freedom the system needs and the freedom it can certify.** The Third Antinomy makes freedom thinkable; *Groundwork* III attempts to certify it and finds a circle; the second *Critique* takes the moral law as a fact and infers freedom from it. Whether the "fact of reason" is a solution, a re-description, or a retreat into the dogmatism critique exists to prevent is the practical philosophy's standing dispute (Argument 9). **(3) Happiness inside an anti-eudaemonist ethics.** The moral law must determine the will without regard to happiness — yet the highest good requires happiness proportioned to virtue, guaranteed by a postulated God. Critics have charged that the second *Critique*'s Dialectic re-admits through the back door the heteronomy its Analytic expelled; defenders reply that happiness enters as the law's object, never as the will's incentive. **(4) Universal evil, freely chosen.** Radical evil must be innate and universal to explain the moral data, and freely self-incurred to be imputable; Kant flags the ground as inscrutable rather than resolving the conjunction, and every reading of the *Religion* is partly a decision about how much paradox to accept (Argument 11). **(5) The deduction that comes in two versions.** A system advertised as a single edifice contains, at its load-bearing joint, two different Transcendental Deductions, a Metaphysical Deduction whose table may be borrowed from the schools, and a Schematism Kant's earliest readers already found obscure.
