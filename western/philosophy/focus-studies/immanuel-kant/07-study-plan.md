# Study Plan — Kant in Eight Weeks: The Critical System Whole

## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Kant at the end of Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant), as the stage's closing core figure (●), in a deliberately bounded sequence: "What is Enlightenment?", the *Groundwork* whole, the *Prolegomena* in selections, the *Critique of Pure Reason* in selections only, and the *Critique of Practical Reason* deferred as an optional later pass. That treatment is enough to know what the nineteenth century is answering. This focus study replaces and deepens it: **the first *Critique*'s selections worked at the guide's full depth, the practical philosophy completed** — the second *Critique*'s core and the *Religion*'s doctrine of radical evil, which the path leaves untouched — **and the system's two short completions**, the *Critique of Judgment*'s aesthetics and *Perpetual Peace* whole. Run it in place of the learning path's Stage 3 Kant block. Two placement rules are not negotiable: **Hume comes first** (Kant's own testimony is that Hume interrupted his dogmatic slumber; the Second Analogy is unintelligible except as Hume's answer), and **the Stage 3 gate essay is written in Week 8**, while the causality material is warm — this plan is built so that its first capstone prompt *is* the gate preparation.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈40 hours of scheduled study across eight weeks (~5–6 hrs/week of reading, dossier work, and media), plus the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) queue (~10 minutes a day ≈ 7 hrs across the eight weeks) — ≈47 hours all-in.** One compression to be candid about: the repo's [*Critique* guide](../../study/kant-critique.md) budgets its selections at four to five weeks on the path's lighter rhythm; this plan runs them in three (Weeks 2–4), at the dossier's denser weekly hours and with the Deduction held to the outline grasp the guide itself sanctions for a first pass. The weeks bought back are spent where the path is thinnest — Kant's practical philosophy beyond the *Groundwork*, and the third *Critique*.

**Reading texts.** The *Critique of Pure Reason* in **Guyer and Wood's Cambridge translation** — the learning path's ruling; learn the A/B pagination (1781/1787) in Week 2 and cite by it thereafter. The *Groundwork* in **Gregor's translation as revised by Timmermann, with Korsgaard's introduction (Cambridge Texts)** — the path's first choice (Wood's Yale version is the sanctioned alternative; Ellington's Hackett is older but serviceable). The *Prolegomena* in **Hatfield's Cambridge edition**. The *Critique of Practical Reason* in **Gregor's translation (in *Practical Philosophy*, Cambridge)** or Pluhar's (Hackett). The *Religion* in **Wood and di Giovanni's Cambridge volume, *Religion and Rational Theology***. The *Critique of Judgment* in **Guyer and Matthews (Cambridge)**. *Perpetual Peace* in **Reiss (ed.) and Nisbet (trans.), *Kant's Political Writings* (Cambridge)**. Citations to the shorter works are by Akademie volume and page (4:421, 5:31, 6:21), which every named edition prints. **Not Kemp Smith, not Meiklejohn, not an unlabeled Gutenberg *Critique*** — the path's bans, repeated because they are the classic false economy.

**The order, and why.** Week 1 orients with Kant's own map: the *Prolegomena* (1783) is his shorter, clearer restatement of the first *Critique*, written after the 1781 edition was mistaken for Berkeleyan idealism — a reader who has worked it meets the *Critique* knowing where the road goes. Weeks 2–4 then take the *Critique* selections in the book's own order (Aesthetic → Analytic → Dialectic), with the repo's guide as session companion throughout. The *Groundwork* waits until Week 5 deliberately: its Section 3 problem — how freedom and nature cohere — is the Third Antinomy's problem, and lands with full force only after Week 4. Weeks 6–7 complete the system outward (practical reason's postulates, radical evil, taste and the sublime, perpetual peace), and Week 8 consolidates and writes.

## Week 1 — Orientation: the map before the territory (~5 hrs)

**Primary.** Two short texts, in the learning path's order. First, **"An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?" (1784)** — a few pages, and the dossier's keynote: enlightenment as emergence from self-incurred immaturity, *sapere aude*, and the distinction between the public and private uses of reason. Then the ***Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* whole** (Hatfield's Cambridge edition): the Preface — where Hume "interrupted my dogmatic slumber" (4:260) — and the three parts, in which Kant re-derives the first *Critique*'s conclusions by the regressive, "analytic" route: from the given fact of mathematics and pure natural science back to their conditions. It is the only major Kant text designed to be read quickly; read it at speed, for the map. Finally, the **IEP's "Immanuel Kant" article (Tim Jankowiak)**, before anything harder — the learning path's explicit rule for this figure (IEP before, for Kant as for Spinoza: the two places beginners most need a plain-language map first).

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) (the silent decade, the Königsberg routine, the 1781 detonation), [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) (where each text you will read sits in the system), and [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) in full — the architecture and the figure glossary. Read 03 now: knowing that *a priori*, *transcendental*, and *Idea* are load-bearing terms with exact jobs is what makes Week 2 survivable.

**Media.** *Philosophize This!* episodes #056 ("Kant pt. 1 — An Introduction to the Introduction," April 2015, ~26 min) and #057 ("Kant pt. 2 — The Actual Introduction") as *preview* before the *Prolegomena*: West stages the rationalist–empiricist deadlock and Hume's demolition briskly. Popularizer's compression — orientation only, never evidence; the learning path's rigor caveat for this show applies with special force to Kant. Then, listen-after: Peter Millican's [*General Philosophy*](https://podcasts.ox.ac.uk/series/general-philosophy) (Oxford Podcasts), part **2.7, "Overview: Kant and Modern Science"** (~17 min) — the one Kant-specific stretch of the Oxford first-year course, presenting Kant as Hume's answerer at the close of its historical survey — and part **3.1, "Hume's Argument Concerning Induction,"** the problem your Week 3 will watch Kant solve. Note the honest gap, recorded here so nobody goes looking: **Adamson's *History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps* has not reached Kant** — the series' latest Western episodes (ep. 502, on Bayle and the Dutch Republic) still sit in the seventeenth century, and at its pace Kant is years away. When it arrives it will become this week's natural spine; until then, the relay above is the verified state of the field.

**Checkpoints (book closed; short answers given, full treatments in the dossier files named).**
1. *What did Hume wake Kant from, and what exactly was the waking problem?* From "dogmatic slumber" — uncritical confidence in metaphysics' concepts. The problem: Hume had shown the concept of cause contains no necessity discoverable by reason or experience, yet science and metaphysics both trade in necessary connections. Kant's generalization: how are synthetic a priori judgments possible at all? *Prolegomena*, Preface and §1; [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).
2. *Distinguish the Prolegomena's method from the Critique's.* The *Prolegomena* proceeds analytically (regressively): it assumes mathematics and pure natural science are actual and asks how they are possible. The *Critique* proceeds synthetically (progressively): it builds from the faculties' elements upward. Same conclusions, opposite directions — which is why the short book is the map and the long one the territory. (Same file.)
3. *State the public/private use of reason distinction, with Kant's own example type.* Public use: the reason one exercises as a scholar before the reading world — must be free. Private use: the reason exercised in a civil post or office — may be restricted without hindering enlightenment (the officer obeys, the cleric teaches the creed; both may argue publicly). Freedom is located in the scholar's address to the public, not in disobedience. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md).
4. *In one sentence: what does "transcendental" mean in Kant's usage?* Cognition is transcendental when it concerns not objects but our mode of cognition of objects insofar as it is possible a priori (A11–12/B25) — inquiry into the conditions of possible experience, not into things beyond it. If this wobbles, Week 2 will hurt — fix it now. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), glossary.

**Reconstructions.** None yet — the first blank-page reconstruction comes with Week 2's Aesthetic argument. This week's retrieval work is the checkpoint set plus restating the *Prolegomena*'s route (fact of science → conditions → the bounds) from memory at week's end.

**Cards.** Import the first 4 cards of [cards.tsv](cards.tsv) (synthetic a priori, the Copernican turn, enlightenment, transcendental idealism and empirical realism) when the week's reading is done — never before.

## Week 2 — The Aesthetic and the Analytic of Concepts (~6 hrs)

**Primary.** The *Critique*, in Guyer and Wood: **both Prefaces and the Introduction whole** (the tribunal of reason; the Copernican analogy, Bxvi; "deny knowledge to make room for faith," Bxxx; the driving question of the synthetic a priori), then the **Transcendental Aesthetic whole** (A19/B33–A49/B73: space and time as a priori forms of intuition; transcendental idealism stated), then the **Analytic of Concepts' opening**: the clue to the categories and the Metaphysical Deduction (A64/B89–A126/B169 in selection — read for the list of twelve categories and its claimed completeness, not for the derivation's every step). **Session companion: the repo's [study guide to the *Critique*](../../study/kant-critique.md), used as designed** — its section-by-section walkthrough in Session B, its reconstructions as the check on yours, its skip list as law. This plan does not duplicate the guide; it schedules it.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) (sensibility and understanding, the two stems; phenomena and noumena, with the two-aspect/two-world dispute flagged) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "Space is an a priori intuition — the geometry argument."

**Media.** Dan Robinson's Oxford lecture series *Kant's Critique of Pure Reason* (Oxford Podcasts, eight lectures given in 2011, ~43 min each) — lectures 1–4 across the week, as preview or review per session: 1, "Just what is Kant's 'project'?"; 2, "The broader philosophical context"; 3 (on space, time, and the Aesthetic's revolution); 4, "How are a priori synthetic judgements possible?" Robinson is a demanding lecturer and assumes the text; Session B only, after your own first attempt at each stretch.

**Checkpoints.** This week's retrieval set is the guide's own: **work the [guide's checkpoint questions](../../study/kant-critique.md) 1–8 book-closed** (A/B citation; the Copernican analogy; the analytic/synthetic and a priori/a posteriori grid; the driving question; two arguments for space's apriority; "transcendentally ideal, empirically real"; the two readings of the phenomena/noumena distinction; the categories and the Metaphysical Deduction's claim) and check yourself against its printed answers — they are the full set, and this plan assigns rather than restates them. Two plan-specific additions:
1. *Why does the Aesthetic, not the Analytic, come first — what depends on the order?* Because the categories' deduction will claim validity only for objects *given* in intuition, and the Aesthetic fixes what givenness means for us: spatiotemporal, receptive, finite. Read the Analytic first and its limit ("concepts without intuition are empty") has no content. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).
2. *State the sentence at A51/B75 and gloss both halves in plain English.* "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind." Concepts alone yield no knowledge of any object (empty); un-conceptualized sensation is not yet experience of anything (blind). It is also a Stage 3 exit checkpoint — own it in your own words, with Descartes and Hume as the one-sided foils. Guide checkpoint material; [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

**Reconstructions.** The Aesthetic's argument for space, written blank-page by week's end (the guide's reconstruction (a) is your comparison text — compare *after* writing). It enters the +2d/+1wk/+3wk rhythm (cadence table below).

**Cards.** +4 (the Aesthetic argument, the two stems of cognition, the categories, phenomena and noumena).

## Week 3 — The Deduction and the Analogies: Kant against Hume (~6 hrs)

**Primary.** The *Critique*'s argumentative summit, at the guide's prescribed depth. The **B Transcendental Deduction (§§15–27) in outline**: synthesis, the original unity of apperception ("The *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations," B131), the object as that in the concept of which a manifold is united, and the second step closing the categories over everything given in space and time. The guide's warning is this plan's rule: **do not skip it, do not grind on it** — an outline grasp you can reconstruct book-closed is a successful first pass; the buffer-week second pass exists for the rest. The **Schematism in brief** (the problem: pure concepts, sensible intuitions, and time as mediator; the schema of causality as rule-governed succession). Then the **Analytic of Principles in outline, with two full stops**: the **Analogies of Experience** — permanence (First), **succession according to the law of cause and effect (Second Analogy, A189/B232ff.: the house and the boat — objective succession distinguished from the subjective order of perceptions only under a rule)**, interaction (Third) — and the **Refutation of Idealism (B274–279)**, Kant's B-edition answer to the skeptic about the outer world.

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "The Transcendental Deduction: the categories are valid for all objects of experience," "The Second Analogy: causality as the condition of experiencing events," "The Refutation of Idealism: inner experience presupposes outer experience." The guide remains session companion: its reading-plan weeks 3–4 are this week's source text.

**Media.** Robinson lectures 5–7 as review: 5, "Idealisms and their refutations"; 6, "Concepts, judgement and the Transcendental Deduction of the Categories"; 7, "The 'Self' and the Synthetic Unity of Apperception." Hear them after the primary sessions — Robinson's Deduction is a second, sterner exposition, best used to test your outline rather than to form it.

**Checkpoints.** The guide's questions again: **work [guide checkpoints](../../study/kant-critique.md) 9–11 book-closed** (the "I think" and its role; the Deduction's conclusion with its built-in limit; the Schematism's problem and mediator), checked against its answers. Plan-specific additions, with answers:
1. *Reconstruct the Second Analogy in four steps, and name its exact anti-Humean claim.* (1) Time itself cannot be perceived, so objective temporal order cannot be read off impressions. (2) Perceiving a house, my apprehension's order is reversible; perceiving a boat drift downstream, it is not — yet both orders are, as perceptions, equally successive. (3) The difference is that the event's order is conceived as *necessary under a rule*: the state that follows is determined by the state that precedes according to the concept of cause. (4) Therefore causality is not a habit formed on experience but a condition of there being experience of events at all. The anti-Humean claim: the necessity Hume could not find *in* the impressions belongs to the rule under which alone impressions constitute an objective succession. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 3.
2. *Press Stroud's objection to the Deduction, and give the Kantian reply.* The argument may show only that we must *experience* the world as categorially structured, not that objects conform. The reply collapses the gap from inside the system: objects of experience just are appearances structured by our forms and categories, so a condition of experiencing them is a condition of the objects as experienced. It works only given transcendental idealism — which is this week's real lesson. Guide reconstruction (b); [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).
3. *What does the Refutation of Idealism add that the Aesthetic did not already give?* A *proof-shaped* argument, aimed at Descartes' starting point: consciousness of my own existence as determined in time presupposes perception of something permanent outside me — so inner experience is possible only through outer experience. The Aesthetic asserted empirical realism; the Refutation argues for it against the problematic idealist on his own ground. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 4.

**Reconstructions.** The Deduction in outline and the Second Analogy, both blank-page by week's end; the Aesthetic argument gets its +1-week touch in Session C.

**Cards.** +5 (the Deduction, the Second Analogy, the Refutation of Idealism, the schema, the Analogies as time-determination).

## Week 4 — The Dialectic: reason overreaching (~5 hrs)

**Primary.** The *Critique*'s negative payoff, in the guide's selection. The **Paralogisms**: the opening and the first paralogism in full, the rest in summary, B version primary — rational psychology's slide from the "I" as logical subject of thought to the soul as simple, persisting substance. The **Antinomies**: the **First** (the world's limits in time and space) and the **Third** (freedom against universal natural causality) in full with their resolutions; the Second and Fourth in summary. The **Ideal of Pure Reason in summary** — the critique of the proofs of God's existence, whose upshot (existence is not a real predicate; no theoretical proof *or* disproof) the Canon, which you *do* read, presupposes. Close with the **Canon of Pure Reason (A795/B823ff.)**: the three questions — What can I know? What ought I to do? What may I hope? — and the highest good's first appearance.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) for the Paralogisms, and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "The antinomies as an indirect argument for transcendental idealism" — and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) for the two-standpoints afterlife of the Third Antinomy's resolution.

**Media.** Robinson lecture 8, "The discipline of reason: The paralogisms and Antinomies of Pure Reason," as review of the whole Dialectic arc. Then *Philosophize This!* #063 ("Kant pt. 8 — Limits of Knowledge," July 2015) as the week's closing review: West's compressed version of exactly this payoff — knowledge bounded, faith's room made — checked against the Canon you have just read.

**Checkpoints.** The guide's set concludes: **work [guide checkpoints](../../study/kant-critique.md) 12–15 book-closed** (the Paralogisms' slide; the First Antinomy and its resolution, contrasted with the Third's; what the Third Antinomy does and does not establish; the three questions and the Canon's answer to hope), checked against its answers. One plan-specific addition:
1. *Why do the mathematical and dynamical antinomies resolve in opposite forms — and why does the difference matter for everything after this week?* The mathematical pair (First, Second) treat the world-whole as an object in space and time; since no such totality is ever given, both sides are *false*. The dynamical pair (Third, Fourth) admit a standpoint outside the series of appearances; both sides can be *true*, the antithesis of appearances, the thesis of things in themselves. It matters because the Third Antinomy's "both true" is the exact logical space the *Groundwork*'s Section 3 and the second *Critique* will occupy: freedom not proved, but never again theoretically vetoed. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5.

**Reconstructions.** The Third Antinomy (thesis, antithesis, resolution — and its limit) blank-page by week's end; the Deduction gets its +1-week touch, the Aesthetic its +3-week touch.

**Cards.** +4 (the Paralogisms, the Third Antinomy, the Ideas of reason, existence is not a real predicate). Deck note: with this week the first *Critique* is carded whole — theory's half of the system is now in the queue.

## Week 5 — The moral law: the Groundwork whole (~5 hrs)

**Primary.** The ***Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* whole** (Gregor/Timmermann): §1, the good will, acting *from* duty against merely *in accordance with* it (the honest shopkeeper), and the first propositions; §2, the taxonomy of imperatives, the categorical imperative in its formulations — universal law (4:421), humanity as end in itself (4:429), autonomy and the kingdom of ends (4:433–441) — worked through Kant's four examples (suicide, the lying promise, neglected talents, beneficence); §3, the deduction's attempt: freedom, the two standpoints, and how a categorical imperative is possible. **Session companion: the repo's [*Groundwork* guide](../../study/kant-groundwork.md)**, whose checkpoints this week assigns as it did the *Critique* guide's — read its section treatments in Session B, and hold its closing bridge ("How to approach the *Critique of Pure Reason*") up against the book you have now read in the opposite order: you are one of the few readers who meet the bridge from the far side.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) (maxim, autonomy, dignity) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "The universal-law test: the false promise and the contradiction in conception," "The humanity formula: rational nature as an end in itself."

**Media.** BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*: **"Kant's Categorical Imperative" (first broadcast 21 September 2017; Melvyn Bragg with Alison Hills, David Oderberg, and John Callanan; ~49 min)** as *review* after §2 — a scholarly panel working the formulations and the hard cases, including the panel's own divisions over rigorism. *Philosophize This!* #058 ("Kant pt. 3 — Deontology vs Consequentialism") as *preview* before the primary sessions, and #059 ("Kant pt. 4 — Categorical Trolley Cars") as review — West's trolley framing is a genuinely useful stress test, provided you notice where it stops being Kant's question (Kant tests maxims, not outcomes). Optional walk-time across Weeks 5–6: Gregory Sadler's *Sadler's Lectures* podcast playlist on the *Groundwork* (16 episodes, short core-concept format) — the learning path's one-Sadler-set-per-figure rule makes this the dossier's single Sadler assignment.

**Checkpoints (book closed; answers given — this week's text has no repo guide checkpoints of its own beyond the companion's, so the plan prints its set).**
1. *What is the good will, and why is it the only thing good without qualification?* A will that acts from duty — from respect for the moral law, not from inclination or expected effect. Everything else (talents, temperament, happiness itself) can serve a bad will or corrupt; the good will's worth is intrinsic and undiminished by what it fails to accomplish. *Groundwork* §1 (4:393–394); [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).
2. *Run the universal-law test on the lying promise, stating both contradiction types.* Maxim: "When I need money, I will borrow and promise repayment I do not intend." Universalized, the practice of promising collapses — no one would accept such promises — so the maxim cannot even be *conceived* as a universal law without contradiction (contradiction in conception; contrast the contradiction in will, as with neglected talents, where the end can be conceived universally but not willed by a rational being who may need others' help). §2 (4:422); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).
3. *State the humanity formulation and what "end in itself" excludes and requires.* "Act so that you use humanity, in your own person or another's, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (4:429). It excludes deception and coercion — uses that bypass the other's rational consent — and requires that the other could in principle share the end of your action. Its ground: rational nature is the only thing with dignity (unconditional, incomparable worth) rather than price. (Same file.)
4. *What does §3 add that §2 does not attempt, and does Kant think it succeeds?* §2 *exhibits* the moral law; §3 must *deduce* it — show how a categorical imperative is possible for beings who are also natural. The route runs through freedom and the two standpoints (as appearance, I am determined; as thing in itself, I must count myself free, and a free will and a will under moral laws are one). Kant concedes a "kind of circle" and answers it with the standpoints distinction; whether the deduction succeeds is a standing scholarly dispute — and the second *Critique*'s retreat to the "fact of reason" (Week 6) is Kant's own later verdict that a different grounding was needed. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

**Reconstructions.** The categorical imperative (both contradiction types worked on the lying promise) blank-page by week's end; the Third Antinomy gets its +1-week touch — notice, as you write both in the same week, that they are one argument's two halves.

**Cards.** +6 (the universal-law test, the humanity formulation, the good will, maxim, hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives, autonomy vs. heteronomy).

## Week 6 — Practical reason completed: the second Critique and radical evil (~6 hrs)

**Primary.** Two texts. The ***Critique of Practical Reason* in selections**: the Preface and the **Analytic's core** — the moral law as the *fact of reason* (5:31: consciousness of the moral law, given as a "fact," underivable from anything prior), the theorems on incentives, and **respect** (*Achtung*) as the law's effect on feeling; then the **Dialectic's practical payload**: the highest good (virtue and happiness in proportion), and the **postulates** — immortality (5:122) and God (5:124–132) — with the **primacy of practical reason** (5:119–121) as the system's keystone: where theory must be silent, practice may rationally *believe*. Then the ***Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason*, Book One** (1793): the human **propensity to evil** in its three grades (frailty, impurity, wickedness), and **radical evil** proper — the freely adopted supreme maxim that subordinates the moral law to self-love, evil at the root of all maxims yet wholly self-incurred and therefore imputable (6:21–53).

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "Autonomy and the fact of reason: the Groundwork III deduction and its replacement," "God postulated: the highest-good argument," "Radical evil: the propensity to evil and its inscrutability" — and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) on the long argument over whether the postulates are philosophy or theology in disguise.

**Media.** *Philosophize This!* #062 ("Kant pt. 7 — Suicide," June 2015) as review: duties to oneself are where radical evil and the *Groundwork*'s first example meet, and West's tour of the Augustine–Aquinas–Hume–Kant positions is a fair map of the terrain — check his Kant against §2's suicide example and Book One's grades of the propensity.

**Checkpoints.**
1. *What is the "fact of reason," and what does its introduction concede about the Groundwork?* The consciousness of the moral law's bindingness, which cannot be derived from any prior datum of reason but forces itself on us as synthetic a priori and practical (5:31). It concedes that *Groundwork* §3's deduction — deriving the law from freedom — could not be carried through on theory's terms: the second *Critique* reverses the direction, taking the law as given and freedom as inferred from it. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).
2. *Reconstruct the postulate argument for God, keeping its modality exact.* (1) Practical reason commands pursuit of the highest good: virtue together with happiness proportioned to it. (2) Ought implies can: the highest good must be possible. (3) Nature, indifferent to virtue, cannot guarantee the proportion; only a moral author of nature could. (4) Therefore the existence of God is *postulated* — rationally believed as a condition of morality's object, never known. The modality is the doctrine: faith (Fürwahrhalten on practical grounds), fenced off from the theoretical knowledge the first *Critique*'s Ideal destroyed. (Same file; the Canon, Week 4, is its seed.)
3. *Distinguish radical evil from ordinary vice and from mere frailty — and say why "radical" does not excuse.* Frailty is weakness in executing a good maxim; vice, wrong maxims in particular cases. Radical evil is upstream of both: the corruption of the *supreme* maxim — the standing policy of ranking self-love's incentives above the moral law when they conflict — which is why it infects every particular maxim's ground. It does not excuse because the propensity, though called innate, is itself represented as freely adopted: an intelligible deed, imputable to its author. *Religion* I (6:29–43); [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "Radical evil."
4. *Set Spinoza's answer beside Kant's on the same phenomenon.* Spinoza: what men call evil is a mode of thinking, a privation relative to a model (E4 Preface) — no one does wrong *as* wrong; all is necessity and inadequate ideas. Kant: evil is real, chosen, and imputable precisely because the agent could rank the law first — freedom is the condition of blame, not its victim. Hold both reconstructions side by side; capstone prompt 2 lives in this gap. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md).

**Reconstructions.** The postulates argument and radical evil, both blank-page by week's end; the categorical imperative gets its +1-week touch; the Second Analogy its +3-week touch.

**Cards.** +6 (the two-standpoints/*Groundwork* III argument, the postulates, radical evil, the fact of reason, the kingdom of ends, the highest good).

## Week 7 — Judgment and peace: the system's two completions (~4.5 hrs)

**Primary.** Two shorter works, both whole at the assigned depth. The ***Critique of Judgment* selections**: the **Analytic of the Beautiful** (§§1–22, core: the judgment of taste as disinterested (§2), the **free play** of imagination and understanding (§9), **purposiveness without a purpose** (§11), the claim to universal assent without a concept (§§8, 20–22)) and the **sublime** (§§23–29 in selection: the mathematical sublime of magnitude, the dynamical sublime of nature's might, and reason's supersensible vocation disclosed in feeling's failure). Then ***Perpetual Peace* (1795) whole** — it is short, and it is the political completion of the moral philosophy: the six preliminary articles, the three **definitive articles** (every state's constitution republican; a federation of free states; cosmopolitan right limited to universal hospitality, 8:356ff.), and the First Supplement's unsettling guarantee — nature itself, through war's costs and commerce's interest, drives peoples toward what reason commands and inclination resists.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) (reflective judgment, purposiveness) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "*Perpetual Peace*: the definitive articles"; [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) for the contract tradition Kant is revising from inside.

**Media.** *Philosophize This!* #060 ("Kant pt. 5 — The Sublime") as review of the week's first text, and #061 ("Kant pt. 6 — What is Enlightenment?") as the deliberate spiral return: re-hear West's treatment of Week 1's opening essay with the whole system now behind you, and notice what the essay's confidence costs by 1795's light.

**Checkpoints.**
1. *What is "purposiveness without a purpose," and why does taste need it?* The beautiful object's form is judged as if designed for our cognitive faculties — imagination and understanding in free, harmonious play — while no determinate purpose or concept is (or may be) represented: the pleasure is in the form's fit to the mind, not in the object's use or perfection (§§10–11). Taste needs it because the judgment claims universal validity without a concept to enforce it; the free play, identical in all judging subjects, is the only ground that claim could have. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).
2. *Distinguish the beautiful from the sublime in one sentence each.* The beautiful: form's bounded harmony, pleasure in free play, the mind at rest in contemplation. The sublime: formlessness or overwhelming magnitude/might in which imagination fails and the failure *pleases*, because it reveals reason's supersensible ideas (the infinite; our moral vocation) as greater than anything sensible (§§23–28). (Same file.)
3. *State the three definitive articles of Perpetual Peace, and the problem the First Supplement solves.* Republican constitutions in every state; a federation of free states; cosmopolitan right as universal hospitality. The problem: the articles state what *ought* to be, but states, like the individuals of the *Groundwork*, act from inclination — the Supplement's "guarantee" is nature's mechanism (war's escalating cost, the spirit of commerce) bending self-interest toward the juridical condition reason demands, so the moral politician is not asked to wait for moralized men. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "*Perpetual Peace*."

**Reconstructions.** *Perpetual Peace*'s definitive articles reconstructed; the postulates get their +1-week touch; the categorical imperative its +3-week touch. After this week, every argument on the cadence table has had its three spaced sessions inside the dossier or is explicitly handed to Stage 3's Session C rotation.

**Cards.** +3 (*Perpetual Peace*, the beautiful, the sublime) — deck effectively complete at 32; the final 2 arrive with Week 8's synthesis.

## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 8, book closed for the first draft. **Prompt 1 is built to serve as the Stage 3 gate essay**: the gate asks what justifies our expectation that the future will resemble the past, with at least two replies to Hume, one of them Kant's — prompt 1 *is* that question worked from the dossier's materials, and a strong draft here is the gate draft, needing only the second reply (a Humean naturalist or a Popperian) widened to full length. **Prompt 2 is cross-dossier eligible**: it stages Kant against the Spinoza dossier's capstone on freedom without free will, and the two essays can be written as a pair.

1. **Kant against Hume on causality. (Gate-eligible.)** Hume: the idea of necessary connection arises from custom, and no reasoning justifies expecting unobserved cases to follow observed ones. Kant's Second Analogy: distinguish perceiving a house from perceiving an event, and show why the distinction already presupposes the causal rule Hume sought in the impressions. Then judge the exchange: does making causality a condition of experience *answer* Hume, or change the subject — and what, on Kant's account, are we *not* entitled to conclude about nature itself? (Stage 3 gate essay: expand with a second reply to stand alone.)
2. **Two freedoms, no free will in common. (Cross-dossier eligible.)** Spinoza denies uncaused choice outright and rebuilds freedom as understood necessity; Kant denies that theory can know freedom and rebuilds it as practical reason's postulate. Reconstruct both, then judge: is Kant's two-standpoints freedom genuinely more free than Spinoza's intellectual love of necessity — or does Kant purchase imputability (blame, radical evil, the ought) at the price of locating freedom where nothing can be known? Argue one verdict against the best statement of the other.
3. **The Deduction on trial.** Stroud's charge: transcendental arguments show at most how we must *think*, not how things must be. Take the B Deduction's outline: state where the charge bites, give the Kantian reply from inside transcendental idealism, and assess the cost — if the reply works only by assuming the idealism, has the argument persuaded anyone who did not already accept it? Compare Strawson's austere salvage (objectivity without the idealist machinery) as the third position.
4. **Kant's moral religion: philosophy, or theology with the proofs removed?** The first *Critique* destroys the theoretical proofs of God; the Canon and the second *Critique* reinstall God as a postulate; the *Religion* recasts church faith as moral faith and names evil radical. Reconstruct the sequence and judge its coherence: is the highest good a rational need or a wish reason has dignified — and does Kant's own rigorism about self-deception leave him a way to tell the difference?
5. **The categorical imperative goes to war.** *Perpetual Peace* applies the moral philosophy to states: republican constitutions, federation, hospitality. Reconstruct the definitive articles from the *Groundwork*'s principles (where exactly is the derivation?), then press the First Supplement: if nature *guarantees* peace through self-interest, is the outcome still a moral achievement — and is Kant here more Spinozist (right channeled by power and mechanism) than his ethics allows him to admit?

## Mastery checklist — you are done when you can…

- □ Explain *synthetic a priori* and A51/B75 ("thoughts without content are empty…") in plain English, with Descartes and Hume as the foils — the Stage 3 exit checkpoint, verbatim.
- □ Reconstruct the Aesthetic's argument for space, and state the objection from non-Euclidean geometry with the Kantian retreat — and say what the retreat costs.
- □ Give the B Deduction's outline from the "I think" (B131) to the categories' bounded validity, and state Stroud's objection and the idealist reply without blurring them.
- □ Reconstruct the Second Analogy (house and boat) and say exactly what it claims against Hume — and what it does not claim about things in themselves.
- □ Distinguish the mathematical from the dynamical antinomies' resolutions, and state what the Third Antinomy establishes about freedom (thinkability) and refuses to establish (reality).
- □ State the categorical imperative in two formulations, run both on the lying promise with the correct contradiction type, and contrast the result with Mill.
- □ Say what *Groundwork* §3 attempts, where its circle threatens, and how the "fact of reason" changes the grounding strategy in the second *Critique*.
- □ Reconstruct the postulate argument for God with its modality exact — rational faith as a condition of the highest good, never knowledge.
- □ Define radical evil against frailty and vice, and explain why its being a freely adopted supreme maxim makes it imputable rather than excusable.
- □ Distinguish the beautiful from the sublime by their mechanisms (free play; imagination's failure disclosing reason), each in one or two sentences.
- □ State *Perpetual Peace*'s three definitive articles and the First Supplement's guarantee — and the tension between them.
- □ Recommend, for a new reader, the correct first Kant text and edition — and talk them out of opening the *Critique* unguided, or in Kemp Smith.
