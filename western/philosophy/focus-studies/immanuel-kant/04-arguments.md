# Kant: The Arguments

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Immanuel Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guides: [*Critique of Pure Reason*](../../study/kant-critique.md) · [*Groundwork*](../../study/kant-groundwork.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — Kant's own where he gave one, the literature's where he didn't, labeled as such. Where scholars dispute what the argument even is, the dispute is presented as a dispute. Citation follows the scholarly conventions: the *Critique of Pure Reason* by **A/B** page (1781/1787); all other works by **Ak.** volume and page of the Akademie edition.

**Argument 1. Space is an a priori intuition — the geometry argument**

*What it proves:* that space is not a property of things in themselves, nor a concept drawn from experience, but the a priori form of outer sense — so that geometry is synthetic a priori knowledge and transcendental idealism holds for space (and, in parallel, for time).

1. To represent things as *outside* me, or as alongside one another in different places, I must already represent them in space; representing spatial relations presupposes the representation of space rather than yielding it. Space is therefore not an empirical concept abstracted from outer experiences (A23/B38).
2. Space is a necessary representation: I can represent space as empty of objects, but I can never represent that there is no space, and no outer object can be represented except in it (A24/B39).
3. Space is not a general concept under which many spaces fall, as instances under a kind; it is essentially one — the various spaces are parts of the single all-embracing space, introduced by limitation, and it is represented as an infinite given magnitude (A24–25/B39–40).
4. Therefore space is an original *intuition*, given a priori, not a concept: a **pure intuition** that underlies all outer perception (A25/B40).
5. Geometry consists of propositions about space that are synthetic — they genuinely extend our knowledge — yet a priori, holding with strict necessity and universality of everything that can be presented in space; such propositions can be explained only if space is the subjective form under which alone objects can be given to us (transcendental exposition, A26–27/B42–43).
Therefore, space is the a priori form of outer sense: empirically real, transcendentally ideal — and the same form of argument, applied to time (A30–33/B46–49), grounds arithmetic and the possibility of representing change.

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic: the metaphysical and transcendental expositions of space (A22–27/B37–43) and of time (A30–33/B46–49).

**Objection.** Premises 3 and 5 lean on the Euclidean geometry of Kant's century as unique and necessary. Non-Euclidean geometries show that alternative spatial structures are consistently thinkable, and general relativity treats the geometry of physical space as an empirical matter settled by physics, not legislation by the mind. On the objection, Kant mistook the geometry he was taught for the form of intuition itself, and the necessity premise 5 claims has been empirically revoked.

**Reply.** The literature's defense distinguishes the *form of intuition* — spatiality as such, being given in a single ordered manifold — from the determinate *metric* geometry Kant took to articulate it. Particular Euclidean theorems may fall to physics while the core survives: outer experience still requires some a priori spatial form, contributed by sensibility rather than read off the world. Defenders concede the reply narrows the conclusion; whether the retreat saves the argument or concedes its center — Kant thought Euclidean geometry specifically was the synthetic a priori science — remains a live dispute.

**Argument 2. The Transcendental Deduction: the categories are valid for all objects of experience**

*What it proves:* that the categories — the understanding's pure concepts — legitimately apply to everything that can be an object of experience for us, because they are conditions of the possibility of experience itself; and that they apply *only* there.

1. A manifold of representations is *mine* — belongs to one experience — only if it can be taken up into a single self-consciousness: "The *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations," for a representation that could not in principle be accompanied would be nothing for me (B131–132, §16).
2. Representations are unified in one consciousness only through **synthesis** — combination according to rules — and combination is an act of spontaneity of the understanding, never given through the senses (§15, B129–130).
3. The analytic unity of apperception presupposes a synthetic unity: the manifold must actually be combined into one, and the concepts expressing this unity of combination a priori are the categories; an **object** "is that in the concept of which the manifold of a given intuition is united" (A104–105; B137, §17).
4. Judgment is the form in which a manifold is brought under this unity, and the same functions that unify representations in judgment unify the manifold in intuition (§19, B140–142) — which is why the categories, read off the forms of judgment, are exactly the inventory of the rules of synthesis.
5. The manifold given to us is given in space and time; but space and time are themselves represented as unities whose manifold must likewise be combined — so the synthesis apperception requires extends over everything given in our forms of intuition (§§24–26: the figurative synthesis, B151–152; all sensible intuitions stand under the categories, B161–165).
Therefore, the categories are objectively valid for all objects of possible experience: nothing can be an object for us except under them. And because the argument runs entirely through our forms of intuition, it establishes nothing about things in themselves (§§21, 27, B146, B165–166).

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Deduction, B edition, §§15–27 (B129–169); the parallel A edition argument at A95–130 (threefold synthesis, A98–110). The "object" formulation quoted in premise 3 is from the A Deduction (A104–105); the B Deduction's corresponding claim is at B137.

**Objection.** Barry Stroud's challenge to transcendental arguments generally bites hardest here: the deduction may show only that we must *experience* the world as categorially structured — that the categories are unavoidable for us — without showing that any object must conform to them. Between "I cannot help combining my representations under the categories" and "everything that can be an object for me is so combined" a gap remains, which only a further bridge principle could close. A second objection, as old as the book, targets premise 5: critics charge that §§24–26 quietly assume the applicability to intuition that the first step had not yet earned.

**Reply.** Kant's reply is internal to transcendental idealism: an "object of experience" just *is* an appearance unified under the conditions of our cognition, so a condition necessary for any manifold to be an object for a self-conscious mind is thereby a condition those objects, as experienced, satisfy. The gap Stroud opens is the gap between appearances and things in themselves, and the deduction claims validity only on the near side of it — which is why the reply works only given the idealism, and why later philosophers who borrow the strategy without it owe the bridge Stroud demands. On the second objection, the literature remains divided over whether B's two steps succeed; the dossier records the argument in its strongest standard form without declaring that dispute closed.

**Argument 3. The Second Analogy: causality as the condition of experiencing events**

*What it proves:* against Hume, that the concept of cause and effect is no habit derived from experience but an a priori condition of there being objective events to experience at all — hence that every event presupposes something from which it follows according to a rule.

1. All apprehension is successive: my perceptions follow one another in time whether what I perceive is simultaneous or successive. Looking over a house, my perceptions of roof, window, and door succeed one another — yet the parts of the house coexist; the succession is in my apprehension only (A190/B235).
2. To *experience* an event — an objective succession, something happening — I must therefore distinguish the order of my perceptions from the order in the object; but time itself cannot be perceived, so the distinction cannot be read off the perceptions themselves.
3. In the case of an event, the order of perceptions is **irreversible** and bound down: watching a ship move downstream, I must perceive it first higher up, then lower down, and cannot reverse the sequence — whereas the house's parts I can take in either order (A192/B237).
4. What binds the order down — what makes it an order *in the object* rather than in my survey — can only be a rule by which the succeeding state is referred to a preceding state as its condition: the concept of a cause, from which the effect follows not merely after it but *because of* it, according to a rule.
Therefore, objective succession is possible only under the presupposition of causality: "everything that happens (begins to be) presupposes something which it follows in accordance with a rule" (A189/B232) — the category of cause is a condition of the possibility of experiencing events, and Hume's problem is answered by relocating necessity from observed constant conjunction to the structure of experience itself.

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Pure Reason*, Second Analogy (A189–211/B232–256); the house at A190/B235, the ship at A192/B237; Kant's own summary of the principle at A189/B232.

**Objection.** The classic objection, raised by Arthur Lovejoy in "On Kant's Reply to Hume" (1906) and renewed since, charges a non sequitur. At most, the argument shows that perceiving an *event* requires taking my perceptions as rule-governed — that objective succession needs *some* ground determining the order. It does not follow that this ground is a *cause* in Kant's loaded sense, nor that every event has a cause regularly connected with it; and the universal causal principle the Analogy announces outruns what the house-and-ship contrast can deliver. Critics add a circularity worry: I can identify the ship's motion as an event only if I already take its order to be objective — what the argument was meant to establish.

**Reply.** Kant's reply, as developed by the literature (Allison, Watkins), is that the objection underrates what "objective order" requires. In the ship's case the order of apprehension is *constrained* to one sequence, and the only available explanation of that constraint — time being imperceptible, the perceptions themselves neutral — is that the states perceived are connected by a rule of succession in the object; that rule-governed connection just is causality in the schematic sense the Analytic needs. The argument claims no more than the *category's* applicability: which events cause which, under what empirical laws, is for experience to say. Whether it delivers universal causality, or only an objective order Kant then names causality, remains contested.

**Argument 4. The Refutation of Idealism: inner experience presupposes outer experience**

*What it proves:* that the existence of objects in space outside me is not a doubtful inference from my inner states but a condition of the very consciousness of those states in time — reversing Descartes' order of certainty. New in the B edition (1787) and aimed at the "problematic idealism" that grants the *I am* while doubting the external world.

1. I am conscious of my own existence as *determined in time*: my inner states stand before me in a determinate temporal order — this memory before that perception, this thought after that one (B275).
2. All determination of time — all fixing of succession and duration — presupposes something **permanent** in perception, against which change can be registered; without a permanent, no temporal order can be determined (B275).
3. This permanent cannot be anything in me: my representations, the material of inner sense, are in constant flux and offer nothing abiding, and the "I" of apperception is a form, not a perception of something permanent; nor can the permanent be a mere representation *of* an outer thing — the argument requires something perceived, not imagined (B276–278).
4. Therefore the permanent must be a thing outside me, actually perceived: "the determination of my existence in time is possible only by means of the existence of actual things that I perceive outside me" (B276).
Therefore, consciousness of my own existence in time "is at the same time an immediate consciousness of the existence of other things outside me" (B276) — inner experience is possible only through outer experience, and the idealist's starting point refutes his doubt. Kant remarks in the B Preface that it "remains a scandal of philosophy and universal human reason" that the existence of things outside us should have had to be taken merely on faith (Bxxxix n.).

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Pure Reason*, Refutation of Idealism (B274–279), inserted among the Postulates of Empirical Thought; the "scandal" remark in the footnote at Bxxxix–xli.

**Objection.** The vulnerable joint, identified since Kant's contemporaries, is the move from premise 2 to premise 3's conclusion. Determinate temporal order may require only a permanent *framework* — a stable order of representations — not an actually existing spatial thing. The slide from "something permanent in perception" to "a permanent thing *outside me*" has struck many critics as assuming what Descartes doubted. Paul Guyer's influential treatment concludes that the argument, on its own stated premises, does not succeed; the Refutation is barely two pages long, and each premise has attracted repair attempts.

**Reply.** Kant's own reply is the regress in premise 3: a merely *represented* permanence would itself need temporal determination, restarting the problem; and inner sense offers no candidate — its contents are successive by definition, and the self is not an object of perception at all. The literature's defenses (Allison's is the most developed) stress that the argument runs from *time-determination*, a stronger, technical notion than order among representations: determining my existence in time requires measuring change against something whose existence is not itself a sequence of my states. Whether that closes the gap, or concedes the argument needs support from the First Analogy's doctrine of permanence, is a live question.

**Argument 5. The antinomies as an indirect argument for transcendental idealism**

*What it proves:* not any cosmology, but a diagnosis with a doctrinal payoff — that the contradictions reason generates about the world as a whole arise only on the assumption that appearances are things in themselves, so that the distinction between them is confirmed by the contradiction's dissolution.

1. Reason, seeking the unconditioned totality of the series of conditions, generates four antinomies: apparently rigorous proofs, on both sides, that the world is finite and that it is infinite in time and space; that composites consist of simples and that nothing simple exists; that there is a causality through freedom and that there is none; that a necessary being belongs to the world and that none exists (A426–461/B454–489; laid out in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)).
2. The contradiction is unavoidable so long as one assumes **transcendental realism**: that the world — the sum of appearances — is a thing in itself, a completed totality given independently of the conditions under which it can be given to us. On that assumption, the world must be either finite or infinite, either composed of simples or not — and both alternatives collapse.
3. Assume instead **transcendental idealism**: appearances are not things in themselves; the world exists only in the empirical regress of representations, never as a given totality. Then the mathematical antinomies' two sides are jointly *false* (the world is neither finite nor infinite, because it is not a whole that could bear either predicate), and the dynamical antinomies' two sides can be jointly *true* from different standpoints (A501–532/B529–560; A532–558/B560–586).
Therefore, the conflict and its resolution constitute an indirect proof of transcendental idealism: the contradiction's arising under the opposite assumption, and disappearing under this one, confirms the phenomena / things-in-themselves distinction in the very place reason most needed it (the "critical decision" of the cosmological conflict, A497–507/B525–535; Kant returns to the point in the *Prolegomena*, §§50–53).

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Pure Reason*, Antinomy of Pure Reason (A426–567/B454–595): the conflicts at A426–461/B454–489; the critical decision at A497–515/B525–543; the resolution of the mathematical antinomies at A529–532/B557–560 and of the dynamical at A532–567/B560–595.

**Objection.** The argument is only as strong as the proofs it dissolves, and the proofs have never lacked critics. Strawson treated the Dialectic as the *Critique*'s expendable metaphysical residue, and the thesis- and antithesis-proofs rest on assumptions — about completed infinities, empty time and empty space — that a critic can reject severally, without conceding that the contradiction indicts transcendental realism as such. If the proofs fail as proofs, the "conflict of reason with itself" is an artifact of particular bad arguments, and the indirect confirmation of idealism evaporates.

**Reply.** Kant's reply is that the objection mislocates the argument's strength. The antinomies are not eight free-standing demonstrations whose failure would leave the doctrine untouched; they exhibit what reason, by its own legitimate demand for the unconditioned, *will* generate whenever it takes appearances for things in themselves — the proofs' very speciousness is the phenomenon to be explained, and transcendental realism has no explanation of why reason falls into precisely these collisions, in precisely these four places (corresponding to the categories by which a series of conditions can be totalized). The literature remains split on the proofs' quality — the First Antinomy's are generally reckoned strongest — but the diagnostic argument survives most readings of them; and the indirect proof was, for Kant, corroboration of an idealism established independently in the Aesthetic and Analytic, not its sole support.

**Argument 6. "Existence is not a real predicate": the demolition of the ontological argument**

*What it proves:* that no existence-claim can be extracted from a concept — hence that the ontological argument, which purports to derive God's existence from the concept of the most real being, fails at its pivot, and with it the cosmological and physico-theological arguments that secretly depend on it.

1. A **real (determining) predicate** is a concept of something that can be *added* to the concept of a thing so as to enlarge it; a merely *logical* predicate can be anything whatever, since logic abstracts from all content (A598/B626).
2. "Being" is not a real predicate in this sense: when I say "God is," I attach no new determination to the concept of God — I *posit* the subject itself, with all its predicates, as an object corresponding to my concept (A598–599/B626–627).
3. The test case is the hundred thalers: "A hundred actual thalers do not contain the least bit more than a hundred possible ones" — for if the actual contained more, my concept would not express the whole object. The actual differs from the possible not in content but in that the object is posited absolutely, with all its determinations (A599/B627).
4. Every existential proposition is therefore synthetic: existence can never be found by analyzing a concept, but only by the concept's connection with a perception — or, in God's case, by nothing available to speculative reason (A598/B626; A601/B629).
5. The ontological argument requires exactly the forbidden move: it treats necessary existence as one perfection contained in the concept of the *ens realissimum*, so that denying God's existence would be self-contradictory; but by 1–4, existence is not a determination any concept can contain (A592–602/B620–630).
Therefore, the ontological argument fails; and since the cosmological must identify its necessary being with the most real being by the ontological argument's own inference, and the physico-theological borrows the cosmological, all three speculative proofs fall together (A603–630/B631–658).

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Pure Reason*, the Ideal of Pure Reason, section on the impossibility of an ontological proof (A592–602/B620–630); the three-proof taxonomy at A590/B618; the reductions of the cosmological and physico-theological proofs at A603–630/B631–658.

**Objection.** The most consequential objection is that the argument refutes only the proof Kant had in view — the Cartesian version, in which existence figures as one perfection among others. Later modal versions (Malcolm's, Plantinga's) agree that existence is no ordinary predicate and proceed differently: through *necessary* existence as a modal status, arguing that a maximally great being is possible and that maximal greatness includes existence in every possible world. Whether Kant's dictum touches such arguments is genuinely contested: on one reading it does not — "necessary existence" is a claim about the concept's instantiation across worlds, not a determining predicate; on another, the modal machinery re-performs the positing Kant diagnosed, since the premise that such a being is *possible* already carries the existential weight. A second objection (from Fregean logicians) is that Kant conflates grammatical with metaphysical predication — though the modern logic of quantification, treating existence as a second-order property of concepts, is often said to vindicate his conclusion by other means.

**Reply.** Kant's reply is built into premise 3 and is stronger than the slogan: his point is not about the word "is" but about the relation of concept to object. Any concept, however complete, determines only how an object *would be* if given; its instantiation is never a further fact about the concept's content, so no analysis of content — modal or otherwise — can supply it. Defenders of the modal arguments reply that they argue from possibility rather than content, which is precisely where the dispute now sits: whether the possibility premise is innocent, or assumes the possible instantiation of a concept whose possibility is the whole question. The dossier presents the exchange as open; what Kant established beyond serious dispute is the narrower result — that the textbook ontological argument, as Descartes and the schools stated it, treats existence as a determination and fails for that reason.

**Argument 7. The universal-law test: the false promise and the contradiction in conception**

*What it proves:* that the categorical imperative works as a test of maxims — that at least one central class of immoral maxims, the lying promise, disqualifies itself by its own logic, without appeal to consequences, sentiments, or authority.

1. A **maxim** is the subjective principle on which an agent acts — the rule the agent actually follows: "in circumstances like these, I will do this, for this end." Morality's question about any action is a question about its maxim.
2. The supreme principle of morality — the only form a law of a free will can take once every material end is set aside — is the universal-law formula: "Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (*Groundwork*, Ak. 4:402; restated at 4:421).
3. Take the test case: a man in financial difficulty borrows money on a promise to repay, knowing he will not — maxim: "when I believe myself to be in need of money, I will borrow money and promise to repay it, although I know I never will" (4:402, 4:422).
4. Universalize it: suppose everyone in need made such promises as a universal law. Then promises of repayment in need would no longer be believed; the practice of promising, on which the maxim's success depends, would cease to exist, and with it the very action proposed: "the promise and the end one might have in it would themselves become impossible" (4:422).
5. The universalized maxim therefore contains a **contradiction in conception**: the agent cannot even *think* it as a universal law without the action it prescribes destroying itself — the maxim is parasitic on an exception to the practice it requires.
Therefore, the lying-promise maxim is contrary to duty, and its wrongness is exhibited by reason alone: not that universal false promising would have bad effects, but that the maxim cannot be willed as universal law without incoherence — the agent wills both the practice (as his means) and its abolition (as his law).

**Textual basis.** *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, Section I (the formula first stated, Ak. 4:402) and Section II (the formula restated at 4:421; the false-promise example treated at 4:422, the second of Kant's four examples). Kant distinguishes contradictions in conception (perfect duties) from contradictions in the will (imperfect duties) at 4:424.

**Objection.** Hegel's charge of empty formalism remains the standard objection: the test condemns or acquits depending on how the maxim is *described*, and description is elastic. A maxim specified with enough ingenuity — "borrow falsely only from banks, on Tuesdays" — universalizes without destroying any practice, since almost no one falls under it; and innocent maxims described unluckily can be made to fail. If the verdicts are artifacts of description, the test yields no determinate duties and smuggles its conclusions in through the description it is fed. A related objection asks why contradiction is the criterion: the universalized lying promise is self-defeating, but self-defeat is a failure of the agent's *purpose* — the territory of hypothetical imperatives.

**Reply.** The literature's reply (Korsgaard's "practical contradiction" reading is the most influential) begins by conceding that the test governs *honestly described* maxims — the maxim on which the agent actually acts, not a formula tailored to escape it — and argues this is a condition of the test's application, not a cheat, since a maxim is by definition the agent's real principle. On that reading the criterion is exactly premise 5's structure: universalized, the maxim defeats the agent's own end — the false promiser wills to get money *by means of* a promise's being believed, and wills a world in which such promises are not believed; the incoherence is in the willing, not in bad consequences. Kant's texts add that the formulas are one law seen from several sides (4:436), so the humanity formula (Argument 8) catches what description-games slip past this one. Whether the combined defense fully answers Hegel remains a live question, and this dossier leaves it live.

**Argument 8. The humanity formula: rational nature as an end in itself**

*What it proves:* that the categorical imperative has objective content and not merely form — that rational nature itself is an end in itself, of absolute worth, which every maxim must respect in every person it touches.

1. The grounds of all practical law must lie in something whose existence is an end in itself — of absolute, not merely conditional, worth; for ends adopted from inclination have only the worth the inclination gives them, and a law resting on them could only be hypothetical (*Groundwork*, Ak. 4:428).
2. A human being necessarily represents his own existence in this way — as an end in itself; this is the subjective principle on which every rational agent acts (4:429).
3. But every *other* rational being represents his existence in the same way, on the same rational ground; the representation is no private preference but rests on a feature — rational nature — that is the same in all (4:429).
4. What holds on rational ground for oneself holds objectively: rational nature is an end in itself not merely for the agent whose nature it is, but as an objective principle from which the supreme practical law can be derived.
Therefore: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means" (4:429). Treating a person *as* a means is ordinary moral life — one uses the driver's services; treating them *merely* as a means bypasses their rational agency — deception and coercion being Kant's paradigms, since the deceived or coerced person cannot in principle consent to the maxim on which they are used (4:429–430).

**Textual basis.** *Groundwork*, Section II, Ak. 4:428–429 (the derivation of the humanity formula), with its application to the four examples at 4:429–430 (suicide, false promise, neglected talents, indifference to others).

**Objection.** The derivation's hinge — the step from premise 2 to premise 4 — is the most criticized passage in the *Groundwork*. From the fact that I *necessarily represent* my own existence as an end in itself, it does not obviously follow that rational nature *is* an end in itself objectively; the step can look like an inference from a psychological necessity of self-conception to a metaphysical value-fact — the kind of slide the first *Critique* taught us to suspect. A second objection presses determinacy: whether a given practice — employment, taxation, triage — treats persons *merely* as means depends on a standard of possible consent Kant does not specify, and interpreters divide over whether the test is actual consent, rational consent, or the possibility of sharing the agent's end.

**Reply.** The literature's best reply (Wood, Korsgaard) reconstructs the argument as a regress on the conditions of value: anything valued conditionally is valued by a rational will that confers worth by taking it as an end; unless the worth-conferring source — rational nature itself — has unconditional worth, the regress never grounds out and no categorical law is possible. On this reading premise 2 is not psychology but a claim about the structure of valuing, and premise 4 states that the ground I find in my own case is a *rational* ground, whose authority cannot coherently be restricted to my case alone. On determinacy: the formula operates jointly with the universal-law test and Kant's worked examples — deception and coercion fail because the victim *cannot* consent to the maxim (the false promisee cannot share an end concealed from him) — and this possible-consent standard, while needing judgment in application, is neither empty nor a mere prohibition of use. The dispute over premise 4 persists, flagged here without a verdict.

**Argument 9. Autonomy and the fact of reason: the Groundwork III deduction and its replacement**

*What it proves — or, in its second form, declines to prove:* that the moral law binds us because we are free, and that our freedom is certain — though the route to that certainty is the practical philosophy's most famous crux.

1. **The *Groundwork* III attempt (1785).** A rational being has two standpoints from which to regard itself: as appearance, belonging to the sensible world and standing wholly under laws of nature; and as thing in itself, belonging to an intelligible world and standing under laws grounded in reason alone (4:452).
2. As a member of the intelligible world, the agent must regard its will as free — independent of determination by sensible causes — and freedom thus supplies the standpoint from which the moral law, the intelligible world's law, applies to the agent (4:453–454).
3. Therefore the categorical imperative is a synthetic a priori practical proposition connecting the standpoints: I *ought* because, qua intelligible, I *can* — the law binds the sensible self because it legislates for the intelligible one (4:454–455).
4. **The admitted circle.** Kant himself flags the suspicion: "we take ourselves as free in the order of efficient causes in order to think ourselves under moral laws in the order of ends, and we afterwards think ourselves as subject to these laws because we have ascribed freedom of the will to ourselves" (4:450). The two-standpoints doctrine of premises 1–3 is the intended way out — whether it succeeds is disputed below.
5. **The second *Critique*'s reversal (1788).** No deduction of the moral law is offered or, Kant now says, possible: the moral law is given as a **fact of reason** — "consciousness of this fundamental law may be called a fact of reason, because one cannot reason it out from antecedent data of reason... but instead it forces itself upon us" (5:31) — and freedom, whose reality theory could not establish, is *inferred* from the law: the moral law is the *ratio cognoscendi* of freedom, freedom the *ratio essendi* of the moral law (5:4n), the law itself serving as freedom's "credential" (5:47–48).

**Textual basis.** *Groundwork*, Section III (Ak. 4:446–463): the circle at 4:450, the two standpoints at 4:452, the synthesis at 4:454–455. *Critique of Practical Reason*: the Preface's ratio essendi / ratio cognoscendi footnote (5:4n), the fact of reason (5:29–31, esp. 5:31), and the law as freedom's credential (5:47–48).

**Objection.** The crux is notorious, and the dossier names it rather than adjudicating it. One line of objection, developed most influentially by Karl Ameriks, holds that *Groundwork* III's deduction simply fails — the two-standpoints move shows at most that we must *regard* ourselves as free, not that we are — and that the "fact of reason" is an unacknowledged retreat: Kant abandons the deduction his system needs and substitutes the assertion that the moral law's authority is simply given, which a skeptic can read as dogmatism, the very thing the critical method exists to prevent. A second objection cuts the other way: if the fact of reason suffices in 1788, the machinery of 1785 was an error Kant should have owned, and the silence about the change is itself the scandal.

**Reply.** Kant's reply, on the most developed reading in the literature (Allison's "reciprocity thesis"), is that the reversal corrects the *order of justification* rather than abandoning the doctrine: the *Groundwork* already holds that freedom and the moral law reciprocally imply each other, and the second *Critique* makes explicit that of the two, the moral law is the one of which we are immediately certain, so justification must start from it. On this reading the "fact" is no empirical fact or intuition but the law's presence in every act of practical deliberation — the datum the deduction was always trying to secure. The literature remains genuinely divided between "great reversal" (Ameriks) and maturation (Allison); the dossier's recommendation is to learn the two texts as the two positions Kant himself successively occupied, since the dispute about them *is* the current state of the question.

**Argument 10. God postulated: the highest-good argument**

*What it proves:* not that God exists — theory has closed that road — but that a rational agent committed to morality is warranted, indeed required, to *assume* God's existence (and immortality) as conditions of the highest good's possibility: a postulate of practical reason, an article of rational faith.

1. The moral law commands unconditionally; among its commands is the promotion of the **highest good** — virtue together with happiness in exact proportion to virtue — as the necessary object of a will determinable by the law (*Critique of Practical Reason*, 5:110–114).
2. A law cannot rationally command pursuit of an impossible object: ought implies can, so the highest good must be possible if the command to promote it is to stand (5:114, 5:143–144).
3. Its first element, complete virtue — holiness of will — exceeds any finite being's achievement at any moment; it can be approached only in an endless progress, requiring an endlessly enduring existence of the same rational being: hence the postulate of **immortality** (5:122).
4. Its second element — happiness proportioned to virtue — is not in our power at all: we are not authors of nature, whose mechanism distributes happiness without regard to worthiness. The connection can be guaranteed only by a supreme cause of nature acting by intelligence and will in accordance with morality: a moral author of the world — **God** (5:124–125).
5. These assumptions are **postulates** in Kant's defined sense: theoretical propositions, indemonstrable as such, assumed only because "attached inseparably to an a priori unconditionally valid practical law" (5:122); and the **primacy of practical reason** (5:119–121) entitles speculative reason to accept them on practical grounds, since they contradict nothing speculation has established.
Therefore, it is "morally necessary to assume the existence of God" (5:125) — necessary for a rational agent's *believing*, not demonstrated for knowledge; the first *Critique*'s partition — knowledge denied, faith made room for — is executed.

**Textual basis.** *Critique of Practical Reason*, Dialectic (5:107–148): the highest good at 5:110–114; the primacy of practical reason at 5:119–121; the definition of a postulate and the postulate of immortality at 5:122; the postulate of God at 5:124–126. The doctrine is anticipated in the first *Critique*'s Canon (A795–831/B823–859, esp. A805/B833).

**Objection.** The standing objection is wishful thinking: a *need* to believe the universe is morally governed is not evidence that it is. An internal version, pressed since Schiller, is sharper: the highest good reintroduces happiness — as an element of morality's necessary object — into a system whose purity consisted in excluding happiness from the will's determination, so the Dialectic seems to undo the Analytic's work. A third objection targets premise 4: even granting happiness must be proportioned to virtue, why a single omnipotent moral author — why not an impersonal moral order, or human progress itself, as guarantor?

**Reply.** Kant's reply is a discipline of status: the postulates claim **no theoretical warrant whatsoever**. The argument does not run "we need God, therefore God exists" but "we are commanded to promote an end; we cannot coherently will an end we hold to be impossible; therefore, in our practical capacity, we must hold the end's conditions true" — a conclusion inside the practical standpoint, protected from the wishful-thinking charge precisely by the first *Critique*'s refusal to let it count as knowledge. On the internal objection, the reply distinguishes the will's *incentive* (the law alone, always) from its *object* (the highest good, in which happiness belongs because reason puts it there — as what virtue deserves, not what motivates). On premise 4: happiness-in-proportion connects *nature* with a ground outside its mechanism, which only an intelligent author of nature as a whole could effect; human progress cannot reach the dead or proportion anything exactly. Whether the practical warrant is a genuine third thing between knowledge and wish — or wishful thinking with a transcendental license — is the dispute the literature continues.

**Argument 11. Radical evil: the propensity to evil and its inscrutability**

*What it proves — and what it deliberately declines to explain:* that the universal human proneness to subordinate the moral law to self-love is neither a sensible compulsion nor an accident of upbringing, but a freely adopted, imputable ground of maxims — whose ultimate ground Kant declares, on principle, inscrutable.

1. Distinguish the *predisposition* to good in human nature (animality, humanity, personality — the last being susceptibility to respect for the law) from the **propensity to evil**: "the subjective ground of the possibility of an inclination," presupposed in the agent prior to every deed (*Religion*, Ak. 6:28–29).
2. Experience — history's catalogue of cruelty in the state of nature and of polished selfishness in the civilized one — testifies that the propensity is universal: the human being is "by nature evil," even "the best" included (6:32–33).
3. The propensity's grades ascend from **frailty** (the good maxim adopted but too weak in performance) through **impurity** (the law needing foreign incentives to be followed) to **depravity**: the reversal of the ethical order of incentives, in which self-love and its inclinations are made the condition of compliance with the moral law — the law obeyed, but second (6:29–30).
4. This propensity is **radical**: it corrupts the ground of *all* maxims, the supreme subjective ground of the adoption of maxims themselves — hence "a radical innate evil in human nature" (6:32).
5. Yet it must be **imputable**: evil that flowed from sensibility or nature would be mechanism, not evil; so the propensity must itself consist in maxims of the power of choice — it is "brought upon us by ourselves," a free, intelligible deed, not a datum of our animal constitution (6:32, 6:35).
6. But then the demand for its ground regresses without end: the supreme ground of the adoption of our maxims, being itself freely adopted, would require a further maxim as its ground, and so on — "the rational origin of this perversion of our will... remains inscrutable to us" (6:43); the first ground of the adoption of good or evil maxims is "to us inscrutable" (6:21).
Therefore, human evil is a universal, innate, self-incurred propensity to invert the order of incentives in the supreme maxim — one that morality must presuppose, freedom must own, and whose first ground reason cannot in principle exhibit.

**Textual basis.** *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason*, Part One (Ak. 6:18–53): the inscrutable first ground at 6:21; propensity defined and graded at 6:28–30; universality and the "radical innate evil" at 6:32–35; the origin declared inscrutable at 6:43.

**Objection.** The conjunction of premises 4 and 5 is the paradox: if the propensity is universal and innate — presupposed in every human being before every deed — it looks exactly like a natural determination, and imputing it to freedom is a formality; if it is each agent's free deed, its universality is a miracle of coincidence, since free deeds might have gone either way. Kant seems to require both horns at once. Critics add that the retreat to inscrutability (premise 6) is suspiciously convenient: the one point where the system most needs an explanation — why a rational being with the law present to it would subordinate that law — is where explanation is declared impossible by design.

**Reply.** Kant's own reply is that the inscrutability is not a gap in this doctrine but a theorem of the system: any explanation of a free adoption would have to cite a determining ground, and a determined adoption would not be free — so a *rational origin* of the propensity, explaining it by reasons or causes, is something reason should not expect to find, for evil any more than for good; the ground of the good supreme maxim's adoption is inscrutable in exactly the same way, and no one counts that a defect of the doctrine of virtue. The deed's location outside time — an intelligible act, of which the empirical life is the appearance — lets Kant hold universality (the deed belongs to the species' intelligible character) and imputability (it is the agent's own act) together, at the admitted price of our inability to say how a timeless act can be *the agent's*. The literature's versions (Allison's incorporation thesis: incentives determine the will only as incorporated into a maxim, so the inversion of incentives is itself the supreme maxim's content) clarify the structure without removing the paradox's core — leaving the reader to decide whether Kant's inscrutability flag is depth or surrender.

**Argument 12. Perpetual peace: right, republicanism, and nature's guarantee**

*What it proves:* that perpetual peace is not a pious wish but a duty of right with institutional conditions — and, by a characteristic Kantian turn, that nature's own mechanism works toward those conditions whether human beings will them morally or not.

1. States, like individuals in the state of nature, exist alongside one another in a condition of lawless freedom that is itself a condition of war — not only actual hostilities but the standing threat of them; reason therefore requires exit from this condition into a lawful one, as a matter of **right**, not of philanthropy (*Toward Perpetual Peace*, Ak. 8:354–356; the preliminary articles at 8:343–348).
2. The **first definitive article**: the civil constitution of every state shall be **republican** (8:349–353) — founded on the members' freedom, their dependence on common legislation, and their legal equality, with executive separated from legislative power. Under it, war requires the consent of citizens who would bear its costs themselves — the fighting, the taxes, the rebuilding — whereas a despot, for whom war costs nothing of his own, can decide for it for the most trivial reasons, as if it were a mere pleasure party (8:351).
3. The **second definitive article**: the right of nations shall be based on a **federation of free states** (8:354–356) — not a world state, which Kant rejects (a universal monarchy would decay into soulless despotism and then anarchy), but a league of peace: an expanding federation of republics renouncing war among themselves.
4. The **third definitive article**: cosmopolitan right shall be limited to the conditions of **universal hospitality** (8:357–360) — the right of a stranger not to be treated with hostility on arriving in another's land, grounded in the original common possession of the earth's spherical surface, on which human beings cannot disperse infinitely and must tolerate one another's company.
5. The **guarantee**: what assures this is not utopian is "no less a power than the great artist nature" — **nature's mechanism** works toward peace through the very inclinations that seem to prevent it: war drives peoples into states and then into lawful relations; the spirit of commerce, incompatible with war, draws states into mediation; even a nation of devils, if only they have understanding, could solve the problem of a just constitution, because a well-ordered constitution makes private inclinations check one another (First Supplement, 8:360–368, esp. 8:360–362, 8:366).
Therefore, perpetual peace is achievable and obligatory in a single structure: right specifies its conditions (republican constitutions, a federation, cosmopolitan hospitality), and nature — through war's pressures and commerce — bends history toward them; perpetual peace "is not an empty idea, but a task that, gradually solved, comes steadily closer to its goal" (8:368).

**Textual basis.** *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795), Ak. 8:341–386: preliminary articles 8:343–348; definitive articles 8:349–360; the First Supplement, "On the guarantee of perpetual peace," 8:360–368; the Appendix, 8:370–386. Kant's remark that democracy in the strict sense is "necessarily a despotism" (8:352) — republicanism being distinguished from it by the separation of powers — is essential context for premise 2.

**Objection.** Two standard objections pull in opposite directions. From the moral side: the guarantee makes morality otiose — if nature secures peace through mechanism, even from devils, the dutiful work of right is decoration on a process that runs by itself, and the "nation of devils" passage reads as an abandonment of the moral project for institutional engineering. From the political side: the federation is too weak for its assigned work — a voluntary league without coercive power over members leaves the exit from the state of nature incomplete by Kant's own logic, since he insists individuals must be *coerced* into the civil condition; the refusal of a world state looks like an unprincipled stopping point.

**Reply.** Kant's reply to the moral objection is the argument's two-track structure: nature's guarantee is a supplement for *hope*, not a substitute for duty — the definitive articles bind regardless of the mechanism, and the guarantee answers only the despairing question whether obeying them is futile. That a good constitution harnesses inclinations is a feature of right's design, since right concerns external actions and their lawful compatibility, not inner worth: the devils passage is about *right*, where legality suffices. To the political objection, the literature's best reply notes that the federation is designed to *grow* by the attraction of a successful republican center (8:356), and that rejecting the world state is itself a conclusion of right: a coercive world authority would be answerable to no law above it — the despotism worry is internal to the theory, not an exception to it. Whether a league without a sovereign can bear the weight Kant puts on it remains the live question in the essay's reception.
