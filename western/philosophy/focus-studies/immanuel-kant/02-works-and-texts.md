# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

Kant's bibliography has a shape no other major philosopher's has: twenty-six years of competent, varied, Wolffian-era publication; a decade of near-silence; ten years in which the system was published essentially whole; and a sheaf of unfinished notes at the end. The corpus is therefore read in this dossier in an order that respects both chronology and pedagogy: the pre-critical landmarks that show the problems forming, the Dissertation that first draws the great distinction, then the critical works in their publication order — the *Critique of Pure Reason* flanked by the *Prolegomena* that unlocks it, the *Groundwork* and the two further Critiques, and the late public books — closing with the *Opus postumum*, labeled for what it is. Citation first, because with Kant citation is not pedantry but navigation.

## A note on citation before the works

Kant is never cited by the page of a modern paperback. Two systems coexist. The ***Critique of Pure Reason*** is cited by the pagination of its original editions: **A** for the first edition of 1781, **B** for the second of 1787 — A51/B75 ("thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind") is a passage standing in both; a single letter (the Refutation of Idealism, B274–279) marks a passage in one only; Roman numerals pick out the prefaces, as in Bxxx, the "deny knowledge to make room for faith" sentence. Every other work is cited by the **Akademie-Ausgabe** — *Kants gesammelte Schriften*, the standard critical edition issued by the Prussian Academy of Sciences (Berlin, 1900– ) — as **Ak. volume:page**: the categorical imperative's universal-law formula is at Ak. 4:421, in volume 4, which holds the *Groundwork* and the *Prolegomena*; the *Critique of Practical Reason* and the *Critique of Judgment* are Ak. 5; *Religion* and the *Metaphysics of Morals* Ak. 6; the *Conflict of the Faculties* and the *Anthropology* Ak. 7; *Toward Perpetual Peace* and the short political essays Ak. 8; the *Opus postumum* fills Ak. 21–22. The Akademie text is what the Cambridge translations below translate and print in their margins, so either system takes you to the same line. This dossier's study plan cites the reading in these terms throughout.

## The pre-critical landmarks

### 1. Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens (1755)

Kant's first book, published anonymously in Königsberg by Johann Friedrich Petersen in the year he began lecturing: an attempt to explain the constitution and mechanical origin of the whole universe "in accordance with Newtonian principles." Its famous content is the **nebular hypothesis** — the solar system condensing, under attraction and repulsion, from a rotating cloud of dispersed matter — extended outward into a system of galaxies like our own Milky Way, island universes scattered through space. By the standard account, the book was stillborn commercially: Petersen went bankrupt shortly after printing and the stock was seized by creditors, so the work was scarcely distributed and Kant was complaining in print by 1763 that Lambert had arrived at a similar cosmology (1761) without having seen it; Laplace reached the hypothesis independently in 1796, and "Kant–Laplace theory" is the name posterity settled on. **Difficulty:** low to moderate; the physics is dated, the prose is the young Kant at his most expansive. **Place in the development:** the pre-critical natural philosopher at full confidence — a universe intelligible through mechanism alone — and a permanent part of his scientific self-image. The dossier reads it in selection, early, for that picture and for the theological frame Kant still hangs on it.

### 2. The Only Possible Argument in Support of a Demonstration of the Existence of God (1763)

The most substantial of the pre-critical books, and the one the critical philosophy never disowned. Kant canvasses the available proofs of God's existence and argues that exactly one argument could work: from the internal possibility of all things, which presupposes something actual in which that possibility is grounded. The demolition along the way matters more than the construction. Against the Cartesian ontological argument he insists that **existence (being) is not a real predicate** — a hundred real thalers contain not a coin more than a hundred possible ones, a thought the first *Critique* will restate nearly unchanged (A598/B626). **Difficulty:** moderate; scholastic furniture, but the arguments are clean. **Place in the development:** the clearest standing proof that the "critical turn" was a turn taken over years, not a conversion in a night — by 1763 the negative program (what metaphysics cannot do) is already drafted.

### 3. Dreams of a Spirit-Seer, Elucidated by Dreams of Metaphysics (1766)

Kant's strangest book, also published anonymously: an examination, half satire and half something more uneasy, of Emanuel Swedenborg's *Arcana Coelestia* and its commerce with the spirit world. Kant had written to Swedenborg and bought the expensive volumes; the book he produced mocks the visionary's claims in detail and then turns the mockery on academic metaphysics itself, whose spirit-seeing, he suggests, differs from Swedenborg's mainly in being less honest about it. Its closing gesture — metaphysics reimagined as a science of the *limits* of human reason — is the critical project in embryo. **Difficulty:** low as prose, high as tone; the irony has misled readers in both directions. **Place in the development:** read it as the dossier does, as the moment the young professor publicly laughed at his own discipline — and kept the joke's moral.

### 4. On the Form and Principles of the Sensible and the Intelligible World — the Inaugural Dissertation (1770)

The Latin dissertation with which Kant took up the chair of logic and metaphysics, and the hinge of his development. For the first time the two stems of cognition are pulled apart in print: **sensibility**, whose objects are given under the a priori forms of space and time, now argued to be subjective conditions of our cognition rather than features of things in themselves; and the **understanding**, which deals with things as they are. The Dissertation's space and time doctrine is recognizably the first *Critique*'s; its complacency is not — it still allows the understanding a direct "real use," cognition of the intelligible world, and Kant's letter to Herz of February 1772 (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)) records his discovery that he had no account of how such cognition could connect to its objects. **Difficulty:** moderate — short, dense, terminologically transitional. **Place in the development:** indispensable as the "before" photograph: the silent decade begins the moment this book's central assumption fails.

## The critical works

### 5. Critique of Pure Reason (A 1781; B 1787)

The masterwork, and the dossier's center of gravity: what reason can know, what it contributes to its own knowing, and where knowledge ends. Its architecture is the system's table of contents — the Transcendental Aesthetic (space and time as forms of intuition), the Analytic (the categories and the Transcendental Deduction of their legitimacy), the Dialectic (the paralogisms, antinomies, and the critique of rational theology, where reason overreaches), and the Doctrine of Method. The library's [guide to the *Critique*](../../study/kant-critique.md) prescribes this dossier's reading — selections only on a first pass, B preferred where the editions diverge, the Deduction in outline — and this file endorses that ruling rather than restating it.

**What changed between A and B** is a required piece of knowledge, not a bibliographical nicety. The 1787 edition adds an entirely new **Preface** (the Copernican analogy; "deny knowledge to make room for faith"); rewrites the **Transcendental Deduction** wholesale — the A version's threefold synthesis is replaced by the B version's argument from the unity of apperception, and the two are different arguments, not two printings of one; adds the **Refutation of Idealism** (B274–279) against the Berkeley reading; and recasts the **Paralogisms** in shorter, different form. Long stretches — most of the Aesthetic, the Antinomies — stand unchanged in both. Kant's motives were mixed and are debated: genuine rethinking of the Deduction's strategy, certainly; but also a direct answer to the first reviewers, and readers who call B simply "the mature text" flatten a real scholarly dispute about which Deduction Kant should be held to. **Difficulty:** the highest in the corpus; the learning path's verdict — selections only, sequence never inverted — is this dossier's law.

### 6. Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics (1783)

Kant's own repair job, written in the wake of the Garve–Feder review: a short book that asks whether metaphysics is possible at all, and answers by walking, in Kant's words, analytically — regressively, from given sciences (mathematics, pure natural science) back to their conditions — where the *Critique* had proceeded synthetically. Its preface contains the "dogmatic slumber" confession about Hume; its Appendix answers the Göttingen reviewers and demands that critics first state the system they attack. **Difficulty:** moderate — genuinely the easiest door into the theoretical philosophy, which is not the same as easy. **Place in the dossier:** read, per the learning path's sequence, after the *Groundwork* and *before* the *Critique* selections; a reader who has worked through it meets the big book knowing where the road goes.

### 7. Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785)

Three short sections and the most argued-over hundred pages in modern ethics: from common moral cognition to the supreme principle of morality (Section I — the good will, duty, the categorical imperative's first formulations); from popular moral philosophy to the metaphysics of morals (Section II — the imperative's formulas in full: universal law, humanity as end in itself, autonomy, the kingdom of ends); and the attempted transition to a critique of practical reason (Section III — freedom, and the circle Kant frankly acknowledges). **Difficulty:** moderate in I–II, high in III. The dossier works it with the library's [section-by-section guide](../../study/kant-groundwork.md), which carries the reconstructions, the checkpoints, and the worked examples; its own placement in the sequence — before the *Prolegomena* — is the learning path's, since the ethics gives a first-time reader Kant's voice at its clearest before the *Critique*'s machinery.

### 8. Critique of Practical Reason (1788)

The system's second pillar: the moral law as a "fact of reason" for which no deduction from anything prior is possible or needed; the primacy of practical over theoretical reason where their interests conflict; and the Dialectic's postulates — freedom, immortality, God — as conditions of the highest good, happiness proportioned to virtue. Its relation to *Groundwork* III is a standing interpretive dispute: Kant appears to drop that section's attempted deduction of the moral law from freedom and to reverse the order of grounding (the moral law now reveals freedom rather than freedom grounding the law), and whether this is retreat, refinement, or mere change of exposition is argued in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). **Difficulty:** high in the Analytic's opening chapters, lower thereafter. **Place in the dossier:** read properly — the learning path lists it as a later-pass extra; this dossier promotes it, because the Canon of the first *Critique* is unintelligible in full without it.

### 9. Critique of Judgment (1790)

The third Critique, in two halves: the critique of aesthetic judgment — the beautiful as purposiveness without a purpose, pleasure claiming universal assent without a concept; the sublime; genius and fine art — and the critique of teleological judgment, on organisms as natural purposes that mechanism seems unable to exhaust. Kant presents the book as the bridge between the domain of nature (the first Critique) and the domain of freedom (the second), through judgment's own a priori principle of nature's purposiveness for our cognition. **Difficulty:** high, but differently — less architectonic density, more conceptual invention per page. **Place in the dossier:** selections (the Analytic of the Beautiful entire, the sublime in outline, the teleology in selection) in the plan's closing weeks.

### 10. Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason (1793; 2nd ed. 1794)

Four "pieces" on the religion the critical philosophy leaves standing: radical evil — a propensity to evil in human nature that Kant insists is self-incurred and imputable; the archetype of moral perfection and the fight of the good against the evil principle; the ethical community, an invisible church, as the social form of the moral life; and true versus counterfeit service of God. Revealed religion is treated throughout as the historical vehicle and outer court of a rational moral faith — the treatment that brought down the censorship conflict narrated in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), to which this entry defers for the Jena imprimatur and the 1794 pledge. **Difficulty:** moderate; the biblical material is handled throughout by reinterpretation, which the reader must watch Kant doing. **Place in the dossier:** selections centered on the first two pieces.

### 11. Toward Perpetual Peace (1795)

Kant's most public late essay, written in the deliberately ironic form of a peace treaty: preliminary articles (no standing armies in the long run, no interference, no secret reservations for future war), definitive articles (republican constitutions, a federation of free states, cosmopolitan right limited to universal hospitality), and appendices quarreling politely with the "practical men" who find it utopian. **Difficulty:** low — short, direct, and written for the republic of letters rather than the seminar. **Place in the development:** the historical-political application of the practical philosophy, and an ancestor of liberal international theory; read whole.

### 12. The Metaphysics of Morals (1797)

The system applied: Kant's mature doctrine of right and of virtue, published in two parts — the *Metaphysical Foundations of the Doctrine of Right* (January 1797) and the *Metaphysical Foundations of the Doctrine of Virtue* (August 1797). The *Doctrine of Right* grounds property, contract, punishment, and the republican state in the universal principle of right; the *Doctrine of Virtue* treats duties to oneself and to others as ends that are also duties, including the notorious casuistical questions. Readers meet here both the system's reach and its most-contested particular verdicts (on lying, on punishment, on resistance to authority), each of which must be distinguished from the framework that generated it. **Difficulty:** moderate to high — drier than the *Groundwork*, far more concrete. **Place in the dossier:** selections, paired with *Perpetual Peace* as the late public Kant.

### 13. The Conflict of the Faculties (1798) and Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View (1798)

Two closing volumes, read together. *The Conflict of the Faculties* assembles three essays on the university's order — philosophy, the "lower" faculty, free to follow reason; theology, law, and medicine, the "higher" faculties, bound to state-sanctioned texts — and its **Preface** is a primary document of this biography: it prints the 1794 cabinet order, Kant's reply, and his reasoning that the pledge had lapsed with the king (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). The dossier reads the preface and the first conflict entire. The *Anthropology* is the published form of the lectures Kant had given since 1772–73: what the human being, as a free actor, makes of himself — faculties, character, peoples — in a register of observation rather than system. It is the most approachable book Kant ever wrote, and it requires one honest flag: it carries period judgments about nations, races, and the sexes that sit in open tension with the universalism of his ethics, a tension the scholarship has not dissolved and the reader should not have dissolved for them. **Difficulty:** low. **Place in the dossier:** the last primary reading — Kant in his public and his everyday voice.

### 14. Opus postumum (c. 1796–1803) — Nachlass, not a book

The final item must carry its warning in its heading. What goes by this name is a bundle of loose sheets from Kant's last working years: drafts, repeatedly restarted, toward a "transition from the metaphysical foundations of natural science to physics," together with late reflections on God, the world, and the human being as a self-positing subject. Kant never finished, ordered, or published it; its very convoluted state is the document. It reached print posthumously in fragments (1882–84), was edited by Erich Adickes, and occupies volumes 21–22 of the Akademie edition. Whether its late turns — some readers find the system straining toward the idealism Fichte was building from it — represent Kant's last philosophy or the wreckage of an unfinished bridge has been debated since its publication and is not settled. **Use:** named here so the corpus is complete on the page; the dossier assigns none of it. It is Nachlass: evidence about Kant's final questions, admissible with the caution due to papers an author left in a drawer.

## Editions and translations

**The critical text** is the Akademie-Ausgabe (above): the reference instrument behind every modern translation, not a reading copy. The dossier's working texts are the Cambridge editions, following the learning path's rulings wherever the path has ruled.

For the ***Critique of Pure Reason***, the default is **Paul Guyer and Allen W. Wood's translation (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, 1998)** — the text the learning path names and the standard teaching edition: terminologically consistent, fully annotated, and printing both editions' pagination in the margins, which the A/B citation system requires. **Norman Kemp Smith (1929)** is the translation two generations of Anglophone readers learned Kant from, and it remains **serviceable with caveats** for readers who already own it or want a comparison text: its terminology is dated by modern scholarly lights (occasional renderings have misled beginners), its single blended text makes tracking which edition one is reading less clean than Guyer and Wood's, and the learning path rules it out as a *first* read — a ruling this dossier keeps. **Meiklejohn (1855)** and unlabeled public-domain reprints lacking A/B margins are to be avoided: cheapness here costs orientation.

For the ***Prolegomena***, use **Gary Hatfield's Cambridge Texts edition (2003; revised edition 2004)** — the learning path's selections are keyed to it, and its apparatus usefully includes the first reviews of the *Critique* that Kant wrote the book to answer. For the ***Groundwork***, use **Mary Gregor's translation as revised by Jens Timmermann, with Christine Korsgaard's introduction (Cambridge Texts, 2nd edition, 2012)** — the learning path's named default; Allen Wood's Yale edition (2002) is its named alternative, and Ellington's older Hackett version is serviceable.

For the remaining works, stay inside the Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant in translation: the *Critique of Practical Reason* and the *Metaphysics of Morals* in *Practical Philosophy* (Gregor, 1996), which also supplies *Toward Perpetual Peace*; *Perpetual Peace* is equally well read in *Political Writings* (trans. H. B. Nisbet, ed. Hans Reiss, 2nd ed., 1991); the *Critique of Judgment* in the volume edited by Guyer and translated by Guyer and Eric Matthews (2000); *Religion* in *Religion and Rational Theology* (trans. George di Giovanni, 1996); the *Anthropology* in *Anthropology, History, and Education* (trans. Robert B. Louden, 2007); the *Opus postumum*, for anyone who goes, in the Cambridge volume translated by Eckart Förster and Michael Rosen (1993). The pre-critical works are available in the Cambridge volumes *Theoretical Philosophy, 1755–1770* and *Natural Science*. One discipline across the whole dossier, the same the learning path imposes: **pick one translator per work and do not alternate mid-reading** — Kant's terminology is load-bearing, and every translator's system of equivalents differs enough from every other's to confuse a reader who switches.
