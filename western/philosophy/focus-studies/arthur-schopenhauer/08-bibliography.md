# Bibliography — Schopenhauer, Annotated

Tiered by use, in the order the [study plan](07-study-plan.md) needs them. Prices are deliberately not quoted except where a format choice is the point; availability notes reflect what could be verified at writing.

## 1. Primary texts and editions

**The World as Will and Representation — E. F. J. Payne's translation (Dover, two volumes).** The plan's default reading text. Payne (1958, from the Hübscher text) is complete for both volumes, terminologically consistent (Will capitalized, *Vorstellung* as "representation" throughout), and cheap in the Dover reprint — the rare case where the inexpensive edition is also a defensible one. Its apparatus is thin; that is what the Cambridge edition is for.

**The World as Will and Representation — Cambridge edition: Vol. I, trans. Judith Norman and Alistair Welchman, ed. Christopher Janaway (2010); Vol. II, same team (2018).** The current scholarly standard in English: fuller introductions, notes that track the German and the Kantian background, and updated renderings. If you buy one volume in hardback for a lifetime, buy Cambridge Vol. I; if you read in Payne, borrow the Cambridge introduction and notes for Books 2 and 4. The plan's section (§) references work identically in both.

**On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason — Payne's translation.** Read the 1847 second edition (the mature text, with the anti-Hegel polemics intact), which is what Payne translates. Short, dense, and — uniquely for a dissertation — the system's foundation stone rather than a juvenilium. Do not substitute summaries: Week 1's taxonomy is load-bearing for everything after.

**Parerga and Paralipomena — the selections problem, stated plainly.** The complete *Parerga* exists in the Cambridge edition (Vol. I trans. Sabine Roehr and Christopher Janaway, 2014; Vol. II trans. Adrian Del Caro and Janaway, 2015) — use it if the essays catch you and you want them whole. For the plan's Week 1 on-ramp, **R. J. Hollingdale's Penguin *Essays and Aphorisms*** is the right selection: it has "On the Suffering of the World" and the *Aphorisms on the Wisdom of Life*, in a translation that keeps the prose's snap. What it omits (the long scholarly essays) the plan does not need.

**The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics — Payne's translation** (a Cambridge edition, translated by Janaway, also exists; the Payne remains serviceable). Contains both prize essays: *On the Freedom of the Will* (1839, the one that won) and *On the Basis of Morality* (1840, the one the Danish Academy declined to crown although it was the only entry — his preface about the affair is itself a document). Week 5 reads the conclusions and the compassion chapters, not the polemical frame.

## 2. First secondary books, in this order

**Bryan Magee, *The Philosophy of Schopenhauer* (Oxford University Press, 1983; revised and expanded 1997).** The standard first book, and it is not close. Magee — a Wagnerian and a broadcaster as well as a philosopher — gives the clearest systematic exposition in English, an honest critical chapter on the system's problem points, and the best account of Schopenhauer's influence on the arts. Read it alongside Weeks 2–4 or immediately after; reading it *before* the primary text would violate the plan's reconstruction-before-commentary rule.

**Christopher Janaway, *Self and World in Schopenhauer's Philosophy* (Oxford University Press, 1989).** The analytic deepening: self-consciousness, the body, and the epistemology of the Will, argued at professional standard but readably. This is the book that arms you for the thing-in-itself objection in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). After Magee, before the Companion.

**David E. Cartwright, *Schopenhauer: A Biography* (Cambridge University Press, 2010).** The scholarly life — documentary, unsparing about the Marquet lawsuit, the mother, and the treatment of women, and the necessary corrective to every anecdotal version (including, where they conflict, this dossier's [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), which defers to it). Cartwright's *Historical Dictionary of Schopenhauer's Philosophy* is the useful shelf companion for names, works, and German terms while you read.

**Rüdiger Safranski, *Schopenhauer and the Wild Years of Philosophy* (German 1987; English translation by Ewald Osers).** The readable life: Schopenhauer placed inside the post-Kantian generation he despised, told with a novelist's pacing and a scholar's sources. Read for pleasure after Week 6, or instead of Cartwright if biography is seasoning for you rather than substance — but know that the two are doing different jobs.

## 3. Advanced and specialist

**Christopher Janaway (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Schopenhauer* (Cambridge University Press, 1999).** The map of the modern field in one volume: essays on the Fourfold Root, the metaphysics, aesthetics, ethics, and the Indian influences, by the scholars whose names recur throughout [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). The natural next step after the dossier.

**Robert Wicks's work** — start with his SEP article (below) and *Schopenhauer* (Blackwell, 2008), a compact critical study strong on the aesthetics and the system's unity; Wicks is also the name to follow on Schopenhauer and the visual arts.

**Sandra Shapshay, *Reconstructing Schopenhauer's Ethics: Hope, Compassion, and Animal Welfare* (Oxford University Press, 2019).** The live fight over Week 4: Shapshay argues the compassion ethics can be reconstructed on grounds thinner than the full metaphysics of the one Will — the "compassion-as-unity" debate in its best current form. Read against Janaway's more metaphysical presentation; the dossier's capstone prompt 2 is this dispute.

## 4. Reference

**[Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Arthur Schopenhauer," by Robert Wicks](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schopenhauer/)**. The dossier's court of appeal: full system survey with the scholarly disputes flagged and a bibliography maintained to professional standard. Per the learning path's timing rule, read it in full *after* your own reconstruction (Week 6), in sections before that only for orientation.

**[Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Schopenhauer, Arthur"](https://iep.utm.edu/schopenh/)**. The gentler on-ramp (life, system, pessimism, well organized) — the learning path's "IEP before" slot for this figure. The IEP also carries a specialist article, "Arthur Schopenhauer: Logic and Dialectic" (Jens Lemanski, 2023), for anyone whose Week 1 appetite for the *Fourfold Root* survives the week.

**[PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/)** — the Schopenhauer category under 19th-Century Philosophy is the discovery layer for current journal literature (the *Schopenhauer Jahrbuch* and the journal *Schopenhauerian Studies* carry the specialist conversation); use it when a debate in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) makes you want the primary scholarship rather than a summary of it.

## 5. Media (all items verified before assignment)

**[Philosophize This!](https://www.philosophizethis.org/) (Stephen West), episodes #098 and #099** — the two-part Schopenhauer series (2017): pt. 1, metaphysics (Kant's correction, the will to life); pt. 2, "Ethics" (~23 min). Transcripts: https://www.philosophizethis.org/transcript/episode-98-transcript and https://www.philosophizethis.org/transcript/episode-99-transcript . Assigned in Weeks 1 and 4 as preview and review respectively. West is a popularizer — the plan's standing caveat applies: orientation and consolidation, never evidence. (A later episode, #237, pairs Schopenhauer's and Nietzsche's critiques of Stoicism; a pleasant Week 6 optional, not assigned.)

**BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*: ["Schopenhauer"](http://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00nfrrz)** — broadcast 29 October 2009, ~43 minutes; Melvyn Bragg with A. C. Grayling, Beatrice Han-Pile, and Christopher Janaway. The scholarly counterweight to West: three specialists, one of them Janaway, compressing the system into a single sitting. Assigned as Week 2 review. Episode record and audio via the BBC *In Our Time* archive and podcast mirrors (a full transcript mirror is at https://www.tapesearch.com/episode/schopenhauer/CuqqdCkW8mZxz6UJVbbKDM ).

**[*The Great Philosophers*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Philosophers) (BBC television, 1987), episode 10: Bryan Magee with Frederick Copleston on Schopenhauer** (~45 min). The long-form discussion format the learning path recommends as a discussion substitute — Copleston, whose own history of philosophy treated Schopenhauer at length, pressed patiently by Magee. Assigned as Week 3 viewing; select episodes of the series circulate on YouTube (see the survey at https://www.openculture.com/2012/05/bryan_magees_in-depth_uncut_tv_conversations_with_famous_philosophers_1978-87.html ), and the dialogue appears in revised form in the series' companion book. Note the attribution, often gotten wrong: the Schopenhauer conversation is from this 1987 series; *Men of Ideas* (1978) hosted contemporary philosophers on contemporary topics and has no Schopenhauer episode.

**One absence, recorded so nobody goes looking:** Peter Adamson's [*History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*](https://historyofphilosophy.net/) — the learning path's audio spine for the earlier stages — proceeds chronologically and, per the repo's round-6 verification, had reached only the early modern period (Bayle) at last check. It has no Schopenhauer episodes yet. When it arrives at the nineteenth century it will become this dossier's natural spine; until then, the relay above is the verified state of the field.
