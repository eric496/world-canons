# Arthur Schopenhauer — Works and Texts

Schopenhauer is an unusually manageable author: one system, stated essentially complete by 1819, and a forty-year afterlife of supplementation rather than revision. There is no "early" and "late" Schopenhauer in the way there is an early and late Wittgenstein. What follows is the complete working corpus in the order this dossier reads it, with the edition guidance the study plan actually uses. The learning path's edition rulings do not single Schopenhauer out, so where the path is silent this file rules, and says so.

## 1. On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason (1813; 2nd edition 1847)

The doctoral dissertation, written at Rudolstadt in the summer of 1813, and the foundation stone: before the system can claim that the world is Will, it fixes what can count as an explanation *within* the world. Schopenhauer argues that the principle of sufficient reason — nothing is without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise — is not one principle but four, irreducible to one another: the ground of **becoming** (causality, for physical objects), of **knowing** (the justification of judgments), of **being** (the a priori relations of space and time, hence mathematics), and of **acting** (motivation, causality seen from within). Confusing the four roots is, he holds, the master-error of philosophy after Kant — including Kant's own slide from the categories to the thing-in-itself. Read it first because Volume I of the masterwork presupposes it on page one and refuses to explain itself. It is short, dry in patches, and far easier than its reputation. **Textual caution:** the 1847 second edition is substantially revised and expanded, and it is the 1847 text that represents the mature system; the 1813 original is a document for specialists. Read the work in E. F. J. Payne's translation (Open Court) or in the Cambridge edition volume *On the Fourfold Root and Other Writings* — either is reliable; Payne's is the cheaper route.

## 2. On Vision and Colors (1816)

A short treatise with a famous quarrel inside it. Against Newton's optics — color as a property of light, decomposable into rays — Schopenhauer argues physiologically: color is the activity of the retina itself, the qualitative division of its response, so that color pairs are complementary because they are fractions of the eye's total activity. The theory was developed in conversation with Goethe's *Theory of Colors*, and the book is dedicated to Goethe's cause against the Newtonians; but Schopenhauer's explanation is his own, not Goethe's, and he said so plainly. Goethe, who had expected an ally and a disciple in that order, responded with lasting coolness. Read the treatise now for three reasons: it is Schopenhauer's only sustained piece of natural science; it displays the method (start from what is given in experience, not from abstractions) in miniature; and its central claim — that perception is the organism's construction, not a copy — is the Fourfold Root's epistemology at work in the body. It can be read quickly and may be skimmed on a first pass without damage to the system; the study plan schedules it short. Available in Payne's translation as a standalone volume (Berg, 1994, edited by David E. Cartwright) — a separate purchase from item 1.

## 3. The World as Will and Representation, Volume I (first published December 1818, title page dated 1819; 2nd edition 1844; 3rd edition 1859)

The masterwork, and the center of the dossier. Four books, one argument. **Book 1**: the world as representation — experience as structured by space, time, and causality under the principle of sufficient reason, with a sustained critique of both realism and the post-Kantian idealisms. **Book 2**: the world as Will — the body's double knowledge, the generalization to all nature, the grades of the Will's objectification (his adaptation of Plato's Ideas), and nature as the Will at war with itself. **Book 3**: art — aesthetic contemplation as will-less knowing, the hierarchy of the arts by grade of objectification, the genius, and music as a direct copy of the Will itself. **Book 4**: ethics and salvation — affirmation and denial of the will-to-live, suffering as existence's structure, justice and compassion, asceticism, and the famous closing pages in which what remains after denial can only be called "nothing" from where we stand. The long **Appendix**, "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy," is not optional: it is the clearest statement anywhere of what Schopenhauer takes from Kant and what he throws away. **Which volume I?** The 1819 first edition already contains the whole argument; the 1844 second edition (the basis of all modern translations) adds the second volume of supplements without significantly rewriting the first. Do not read Volume II first, and do not mistake it for an afterthought — but the system is Volume I.

**Translations — this dossier's ruling.** Two complete modern options exist. E. F. J. **Payne's** translation (1958; widely reprinted, including a Dover two-volume set) was the standard English Schopenhauer for half a century: vigorous, readable, occasionally free with terminology. The **Cambridge edition** — *The World as Will and Representation*, translated by Judith Norman and Alistair Welchman, edited by Christopher Janaway (Volume 1, 2010; Volume 2, 2018) — is now the scholarly text: terminologically consistent across the whole Cambridge Schopenhauer series, with proper introductions and notes. **Recommendation: buy and read the Cambridge Volume 1** if you are acquiring the book for this dossier; if Payne is already on your shelf, read Payne without anxiety — the arguments survive both. Avoid the old Haldane and Kemp translation (1883–86) except as a historical document: it is dated in language and was made before modern Schopenhauer scholarship. And avoid, firmly, the abridged one-volume selections that excerpt Volume I and discard the Appendix or whole books: the system *is* the whole structure, and a selection that hides it teaches a mood instead of a philosophy.

## 4. On the Will in Nature (1836; 2nd edition 1854)

A short, combative corroboration: having claimed in 1819 that the inner nature of the world is Will, Schopenhauer reviews the empirical sciences of his day — physiology, comparative anatomy, plant life, physical astronomy, even animal magnetism and the linguistics of his time — and argues that, read without prejudice, they keep pointing at the same conclusion: nature as striving without purpose. Modern readers will find some of the science dated and a few of the corroborations (he is generous to any report that agrees with him) credulous; the book's value now is methodological and rhetorical. It shows what Schopenhauer thought confirmation of a metaphysics could look like, and it contains some of his sharpest statements of the doctrine of the body's primacy. The study plan treats it as a one-week corroborative read after Volume I, in the older English version (Mme Karl Hillebrand's 1889 translation, published with the *Fourfold Root* as *Two Essays*, is in the public domain) or in selection — the one place in the dossier where a dated translation is acceptable, because the plan reads it for the argument's shape rather than the letter.

## 5. The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics (1841)

Two prize essays, two institutional verdicts, one book. ***On the Freedom of the Will*** (crowned by the Royal Norwegian Society, 1839) draws the system's distinction between empirical necessity and the intelligible character: actions follow from character plus motive with strict necessity, and freedom survives, if at all, not in what we do but in what we are. ***On the Basis of Morality*** (refused by the Royal Danish Society, 1840, though the only entry) is the constructive ethics: a demolition of Kant's categorical imperative as crypto-theology, and the positive doctrine that compassion (*Mitleid*) — the immediate participation in another's suffering — is the sole genuine incentive of moral worth, from which justice and loving-kindness are derived. Read after Volume I, Book 4, these essays give the ethics in its most argumentative, least mystical form, and the preface — Schopenhauer's reply to Copenhagen — is independently famous. **Text:** read the Cambridge edition (*On the Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics*, translated and edited by Christopher Janaway, 2009); Payne's older versions of the two essays are serviceable alternatives.

## 6. The World as Will and Representation, Volume II (1844)

Not a sequel but a supplement: fifty chapters keyed to Volume I's four books, written in a looser, richer, more essayistic register, and for many readers the most enjoyable Schopenhauer there is. The dossier does not assign all of it; it assigns the chapters the system cannot be understood without, in the study plan's order — among them the supplements on death and the indestructibility of our inner nature (ch. 41), the metaphysics of sexual love (ch. 44), and the great closing survey "On the Vanity and Suffering of Life" (ch. 46), where the pessimism receives its fullest statement. Other chapters — on madness, on the primacy of the will in self-consciousness, on the objectification grades revisited — are marked in the plan as high-value extensions. **Text:** Cambridge Volume 2 (2018), or Payne's Volume II; keep the translation consistent with your Volume I. A caution worth stating: because Volume II is quotable, it is often looted for aphorisms; read the assigned chapters whole, in their keyed order, or the supplements degenerate into the wisdom-literature Schopenhauer despised being mistaken for.

## 7. Parerga and Paralipomena (1851; 2nd edition 1862, posthumous)

"By-works and things left out": two volumes of essays and aphorisms on everything the system touched but could not house — the wisdom of life, honor, fame, reading and scholarship, noise, the ages of life, religion dialogued against itself, and, in the second volume, the notorious essay "On Women," whose hostility the dossier neither conceals nor endorses and whose criticism is treated in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). *Parerga* made him famous, and it is still where most readers begin; the dossier ends with it deliberately, because the essays read differently — deeper and more ironic — once the system they ornament is known. Within the two volumes, the *Aphorisms on the Wisdom of Life* (Volume I) are the summit: Schopenhauer's practical eudemonology, written in full knowledge that his metaphysics says happiness is only ever negative. **Texts:** the complete English *Parerga* exists in Payne's two-volume translation (Oxford) and in the Cambridge edition (*Parerga and Paralipomena*, Volumes 1 and 2, 2014–15). For a first taste before committing, R. J. Hollingdale's Penguin selection, *Essays and Aphorisms* (1970), draws on *Parerga* only — it is the best short Schopenhauer in English and the study plan's week-one warm-up — but it is a selection, and the dossier's final week reads beyond it.

## 8. The manuscript remains — a caution, not a reading assignment

Schopenhauer left extensive notebooks, drafts, and annotated copies, published after his death as the *Manuscript Remains* (edited by Arthur Hübscher; English edition translated by Payne). They include the youthful *Eis heauton* ("for himself") notes and the working papers behind every book. Two rules govern their use in this library. First, they are **working papers, not doctrine**: a notebook entry shows what Schopenhauer was trying out, and no claim in the dossier rests on the remains where a published text says otherwise. Second, the famous literary remains cuts both ways — the Gracián translation he completed in the 1820s, published posthumously, is genuine work; the various "books" sometimes extracted from the notebooks by editors are editorial constructions and are labeled as such wherever the dossier mentions them. The remains matter for scholars and for the biography (the ledger's *obit anus* is there); a first full study does not need them, and the study plan assigns none.

| Order | Work | Role in the dossier |
|---|---|---|
| warm-up | *Parerga* selections (Hollingdale, *Essays and Aphorisms*) | the voice, before the system |
