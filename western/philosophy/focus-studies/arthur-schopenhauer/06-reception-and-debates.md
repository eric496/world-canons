# Schopenhauer: Reception and the Live Debates

*Focus study: Arthur Schopenhauer · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md)*

No major philosopher's reception has a stranger shape: thirty years of silence, then a decade of conquest, then forty years as the most-read philosopher in Europe, then academic eclipse, then a slow scholarly rebuilding that is still under way. The debates below are live ones, stated as debates — who holds what, and on what evidence — because Schopenhauer studies today is not a settled field but a set of well-defined disagreements.

## Act I: the silence, 1818–1850

*The World as Will and Representation* appeared from Brockhaus at the end of 1818, title page dated 1819, and vanished. Reviews were few, brief, and mostly baffled or hostile; by Schopenhauer's own later account, much of the edition was eventually pulped as waste paper. The exceptions prove the depth of the silence: the writer Jean Paul praised the book warmly and early, and almost nobody listened. The Berlin lectures of 1820, scheduled against Hegel's, drew a handful of students and were abandoned — the story is told in the [library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md). *On the Will in Nature* (1836), his bid to show the sciences confirming his metaphysics, sank with barely a review.

The 1841 *Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics* contains the era's most revealing reception document: a natural experiment in being judged by the establishment. Its first essay, *On the Freedom of the Will*, won the prize of the Royal Norwegian Society in Trondheim. Its second, *On the Basis of Morality*, was the *sole submission* to the Royal Danish Society's prize competition in Copenhagen — and the prize was withheld, the Academy pointedly regretting the essay's disrespect toward eminent contemporary philosophers. Schopenhauer published it anyway, with a preface flaying the judges. A philosophy whose ethics had literally been refereed by Europe's academies and failed on grounds of manners could hardly complain it had not been read; it could, and did, complain about the referees. Meanwhile he lived in Frankfurt on the income of his capital, revised nothing in the system, and added the file of newspaper clippings that reported his nonexistence.

## Act II: the breakthrough of the 1850s

*Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851) — essays and aphorisms in a prose style anyone could read — was the Trojan horse. The spark came from England: John Oxenford's "Iconoclasm in German Philosophy," in the *Westminster Review* of April 1853, presented Schopenhauer to British readers as the great unheard voice of German thought, and German journals, embarrassed into attention by the foreign praise, began to follow. Schopenhauer tracked the turn himself, clipping the reviews that now arrived from everywhere.

The "apostles" organized the conquest (see [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)): Frauenstädt's *Letters on Schopenhauer's Philosophy* (1854) gave the doctrine a systematic public form, Lindner promoted it in the Berlin press, and pilgrims began appearing at the Englischer Hof in Frankfurt, where the philosopher dined daily and held forth. By his death in 1860 the obscure author of 1819 was a European celebrity — the fame arriving, as he sourly observed, just in time for him to watch others enjoy it.

## Act III: the late-century dominance, 1860–1900

For four decades Schopenhauer was arguably the most widely read philosopher in Europe, and his influence ran less through philosophy departments than through the culture at large: Wagnerism carried him into music (the Wagner conversation is traced in [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)); the young Nietzsche preached him before revolting; novelists from Tolstoy to the young Thomas Mann read him as a contemporary.

The period's defining academic episode was the **pessimism controversy** (*Pessimismusstreit*). Eduard von Hartmann's *Philosophy of the Unconscious* (1869) — a bestseller through many editions — fused Schopenhauer's Will with Hegel's Idea into an "unconscious" world-ground and drew the pessimistic conclusion with systematic optimism about its rigor. The orthodox Schopenhauerians saw a betrayal dressed as a completion; Hartmann's defenders saw the master finally made consistent. Around the fight grew a literature: Agnes Taubert's *Der Pessimismus und seine Gegner* (1873), the first systematic defense against the critics, and in English James Sully's *Pessimism: A History and a Criticism* (1877). The questions the controversy sharpened — does the metaphysics actually entail the verdict that existence is bad? can suffering and satisfaction even be summed on one scale? — are still the live questions of debate (b) below. In the same decades the Sanskrit scholar Paul Deussen, a devoted disciple, founded the Schopenhauer Society (1911, just into the next act) and its yearbook — institutionalizing the flame even as the universities let it drop.

## Act IV: the eclipse, c. 1900–1945

Professional philosophy moved elsewhere: neo-Kantianism defined the German seminar, then phenomenology and logical positivism redefined rigor, and in each dispensation Schopenhauer figured as a brilliant amateur — a *writer* — whose metaphysics belonged to a superseded age. Pessimism, after 1914 and again after 1933, read to many as a period mood rather than an argument. The readership did not vanish; it emigrated. The interwar Schopenhauer lives in literature and in private study — Mann, Hesse, the young Wittgenstein's generation — while the academy largely cited him as a precursor (of Nietzsche, of Freud) rather than as a philosopher to be answered. The eclipse was therefore disciplinary, not total: he stopped being an interlocutor and became an influence, which is a different and quieter kind of survival.

## Act V: the scholarly reconstruction, 1945 to the present

The rebuilding began in English. Bryan Magee's *The Philosophy of Schopenhauer* (1983; revised 1997) argued, with a convert's energy and a scholar's patience, that the system had been misjudged by people who had not read it — and a generation of Anglophone work followed: Christopher Janaway's *Self and World in Schopenhauer's Philosophy* (1989), his editorship of the *Cambridge Companion to Schopenhauer* (1999), and *Beyond Selflessness* (2007) on the ethics; David E. Cartwright's biography (2010), the standard life in English. The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Schopenhauer, under Janaway's general editorship, has been replacing E. F. J. Payne's mid-century translations as the scholarly standard text in English. Current research fronts include the ethics of compassion read against contemporary moral psychology, the aesthetics of music, the philosophy of animals, and a newly careful comparative literature on his Indian sources. The verdict of the reconstruction is not that the system stands — the debates below are unresolved — but that it is a system again: argued with, premise by premise, rather than admired or deplored as an atmosphere.

## The live debates

### (a) Is the system consistent about its own center?

The founding problem: Schopenhauer's Kantian premises say the thing-in-itself cannot be known, yet his system *names* it — Will. His defense in Vol. I is the body's double knowledge: in willing, I am not observing an object but being the thing itself. But in Vol. II he qualifies hard: what we know in inner awareness is still known *under the form of time*, hence is only the most immediate *manifestation* of the thing-in-itself, not the thing-in-itself stripped of all appearance. Critics from the first reviewers onward have called this having it both ways. Defenders — the dominant line in recent scholarship, Janaway's work prominent among it — read the qualification as the doctrine itself: "Will" names the thing-in-itself *relative to its closest appearance*, and the extension from my willing to the inner nature of all things is an argued analogy, not a deduction. Whether the analogy can bear the weight of the whole system remains the field's central open question.

### (b) Does the pessimism follow?

Grant the metaphysics: existence is one restless Will, individuated into competing centers of wanting. Does the value verdict — that it would be better not to be — follow? Critics press an is/ought worry: the badness of suffering, and the asymmetry Schopenhauer builds on (satisfaction is merely negative, the silencing of a pain, while pain is positive and felt; *WWR* I, §58), are premises about value smuggled in as facts. His counter-argument is comparative and structural: this is, he argues in Vol. II, the *worst of all possible worlds* — a world arranged to be slightly worse could not sustain itself at all — inverting Leibniz with the optimism of the pessimist's joke that is not a joke. Contemporary discussion divides between those who find the hedonic asymmetry an empirical claim that fails (lives contain positive goods not reducible to relief) and those who defend the argument in its structural form (willing as such guarantees frustration, whatever the local goods). The debate is live in the current literature on pessimism as a philosophical (not literary) position.

### (c) Compassion against its critics

*On the Basis of Morality* grounds all genuine morality in *Mitleid* — the immediate participation in another's suffering. The Kantian critique is as old as the book: a contingent feeling cannot ground the universality and necessity morality claims; what I happen to feel is weather, not law. The Nietzschean critique cuts deeper: compassion multiplies suffering (now two suffer), preserves what should perish, and is the ascetic ideal in its most seductive mask. Defenders answer on two fronts: empirically, moral psychology's evidence that fellow-feeling, not rule-cognition, is what actually moves agents — Schopenhauer as a premature researcher of moral motivation; and philosophically, with the metaphysical backing Nietzsche lacked: if individuation is illusion, compassion is not a sentiment but a *perception* — seeing the world as it is. Whether that metaphysical backing rescues the ethics or merely relocates the problem (debate (a)) is exactly where the current discussion sits; it is also the point at which his ethics connects to present-day care ethics and animal ethics.

### (d) How deep, and how accurate, was the Indian reading?

The reception question of [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) has a scholarly edge: Schopenhauer read the Upanishads in a Latin translation of a Persian translation, and Buddhism largely through early, uneven European scholarship. Indologists and comparative philosophers divide. One line holds that the convergence is genuine and philosophically creditable — he grasped, through bad translations, real structural parallels (suffering, impermanence, the unreality of the separate self) decades before European Indology matured. The other line holds that he projected his finished system onto hospitable texts: his "Buddhism" affirms an inner essence (the Will) that Buddhist doctrine exists to deny, and his Vedanta is a selection — *maya* and *tat tvam asi* in, the ritual and theistic bulk of the traditions out. Recent comparative work has grown more precise on both sides; the honest summary is that his Indian philosophy is a creative appropriation whose accuracy varies by topic, profound on suffering and individuation, unreliable as reportage.

### (e) "On Women," and the biography problem

The facts first, without varnish. The essay "On Women" (*Parerga*, Vol. II, 1851) argues that women are intellectually childlike, unsuited to art and philosophy — he calls them "the unaesthetic sex" — fitted by nature for obedience and the nursing of children, and it endorses polygamy as the arrangement honest about male nature. It is not a youthful indiscretion; it is a considered piece of his most popular book, and no honest dossier can treat it as a footnote.

The scholarly question is what it connects to. His considered philosophy of sex is the chapter "The Metaphysics of Sexual Love" (*WWR*, Vol. II, ch. 44): sexual love is the Will-to-live's stratagem, the species' will overriding the individual's deluded sense of personal choice — a doctrine presented here as his position, and detachable in principle from any view about women's minds. On the connection, scholarship divides much as it does over other philosophers' prejudices: one side argues the misogyny is biographical sediment (the war with his mother, the failed courtships, the seamstress Caroline Marquet, whom he injured on a Berlin staircase in 1821 and paid an annuity for the rest of her life — his notebooks record, at her death in 1852, the Latin jingle "obit anus, abit onus," the old woman dies, the burden departs) that his own principles — compassion grounded in seeing through individuation, extended explicitly to all sufferers — actually condemn. The other side notes that the system does contain a metaphysics of the sexes, in which woman figures as the sex through whom the species-will works most directly, and that the essay draws on it. What no current scholarship defends is the essay's claims themselves. The dossier's stance is the archive's: print it in the works list, mark it in the debates, and let the arguments about entrenchment be had in the open.

### (f) One system, or a philosophy that grew?

Schopenhauer insisted his masterwork expressed "a single thought," fully present in 1819, and that everything after was commentary — he never revised the system's foundations. Developmentalists answer that the corpus tells a different story: the ethics of compassion receives its systematic ground only in 1840; Vol. II (1844) doubles the masterwork with material (the sexual metaphysics, the fuller Buddhism, the primacy of the will) that shifts its emphases; and the *Aphorisms on the Wisdom of Life* in *Parerga* teach a prudent, skillful pursuit of happiness that sits in visible tension with the denial of the Will — a tension he himself flags, presenting the aphorisms as written from the lower, "immanent" standpoint the higher standpoint renounces. Unitarians reply that the standpoints doctrine was always there and the additions are explicitation. The question matters practically (it decides what a reader may treat as "the" philosophy) and it is not settled: the fixed-early system and the quiet revisions are both in the texts.

## The standard misconceptions, in full form

**"He preached suicide."** He argued the opposite, at length (*WWR* I, §69). Suicide, on his analysis, is a *strong affirmation* of the Will: the suicide wills life and rejects only its sufferings, destroying one phenomenon while the Will itself — the only thing he thinks worth escaping — persists unharmed. The denial of the Will he praises aims at the willing, not at the body. The grim reputation reverses his doctrine.

**"The pessimism is a temperament."** It is a deduction, offered as valid whoever performs it: if willing is the essence and willing is structurally unsatisfiable, suffering is the form of existence, for the cheerful as for the gloomy. Schopenhauer himself took documented pleasure in music, theater, travel, good dinners, and his late fame; the pendulum between pain and boredom is a claim about the structure of desire, not a report on his digestion. Refuting the man leaves the argument untouched.

**"He copied Buddhism."** The chronology forbids it (see debate (d)): the system was complete in outline by 1818, before his serious Buddhist reading, and he said — and scholars broadly agree — that the Indian texts confirmed an independently reached position. What he took was corroboration and vocabulary; what he made of it is European metaphysics, for better and worse.

**"The Will is a cosmic person — a god by another name."** He is explicit that "Will" is a *denominatio a potiori* — a thing named from its best-known instance (*WWR* I, §22). The Will has no purposes, no foresight, no self-awareness; it does not want the world the way a person wants lunch. Reading intention into it reinstates exactly the theology the system exists to refuse.

**"He lived as a misanthropic hermit."** The Frankfurt record shows a man of rigid routine — the noon walk, the flute, the successive poodles named Atman — who nonetheless dined daily in company at the Englischer Hof, corresponded voluminously, received disciples and pilgrims through the 1850s, followed his reviews with a scrapbooker's devotion, and enjoyed being famous. The hermit of legend is the embittered Berlin lecturer of the 1820s projected across forty years. He was difficult, litigious, and frequently contemptuous; he was not a recluse, and the philosophy of compassion was written by a man who liked an audience — a tension his readers have always had to hold alongside the doctrine, exactly as with the essay discussed in debate (e).
