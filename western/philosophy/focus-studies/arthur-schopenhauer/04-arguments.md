# Schopenhauer: The Arguments

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Arthur Schopenhauer](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — Schopenhauer's own where he gave one, the literature's where he didn't, labeled as such. Where scholars dispute what the argument even is, the dispute is presented as a dispute. *WWR* = *The World as Will and Representation*; citations by volume, book, and section follow the standard section numbering.

---

**Argument 1. The fourfold root: explanation comes in exactly four kinds**

1. The principle of sufficient reason — nothing is without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise — is applied very differently in different domains: we demand a *cause* for a physical change, a *ground* for a judgment's truth, a *determination* of position for a spatial or temporal relation, and a *motive* for an action.
2. These demands are not interchangeable: a motive is not a cause in the physical sense, a logical ground is not a temporal determination, and no one of the four does the work of the others.
3. Each form correlates with a distinct class of objects (physical objects, concepts/judgments, space-time determinations, actions) and a distinct cognitive faculty (understanding, reason, pure sensibility, self-consciousness).
4. The principle is known a priori in each form, but only as it governs its own class; applied beyond that class — above all, applied to the world as a whole or to the thing-in-itself — it produces pseudo-questions.
Therefore, there are exactly four roots of the principle of sufficient reason, and every legitimate explanation is an explanation under one of them; nothing about the world's inner nature can be extracted from any of them.

**Textual basis.** *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason* (1813; 2nd ed. 1847), throughout; presupposed at *WWR* I, Book 1, §§14–16.

**Objection.** The taxonomy looks stipulated rather than derived. Why should the classes of objects be exactly four, when the classification is itself drawn from the faculties it is meant to justify? Critics from his day to ours have also doubted the sharp separation of the roots — mathematical determination and causality, for instance, interpenetrate in actual physics.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own reply is that the fourfold division is not deduced from an abstract principle but read off the differences in what we actually accept as satisfying an explanation in each domain — the test is whether a "why" question goes silent when answered in that form. The literature's more sympathetic version adds that even if the taxonomy is revisable at the edges, its critical conclusion survives: the principle governs relations among phenomena, and no form of it licenses a cause of the world or a reason for the thing-in-itself. That negative use — against rationalist cosmology and theology — is the argument's real work in the system.

**Argument 2. The world as representation, and the correction of Kant**

1. Every object of knowledge is an object *for a subject*: object and subject are correlative, and neither is intelligible as existing without the other.
2. All objects are given under the a priori forms of space, time, and causality; we know nothing of objects except as structured by these forms.
3. Therefore nothing we know is the world as it is in itself; the known world is representation (appearance), and a world of objects without any subject is a contradiction in terms.
4. Kant nevertheless treated the thing-in-itself as that which *causes* our sensations and affects our sensibility.
5. But causality is, by Kant's own doctrine, a form valid only within experience; applying it to the relation between thing-in-itself and representation transgresses the critical limit.
6. The other eleven categories are, inspection shows, either reducible to causality or empty forms abstracted from it.
Therefore, Kant's framework survives only in a pruned form: the world is representation under space, time, and causality alone — and the thing-in-itself, if it is to be known at all, must be reached by some route other than causal inference.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Book 1 (esp. §1 and §§4–7); the critique of Kant in the Appendix to Volume I, "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy."

**Objection.** Premise 5's charge — that Kant smuggled causality across the border — is one of the oldest in Kant scholarship and is contested: many Kant scholars hold that Kant's "affection" by the thing-in-itself is not a causal claim in the empirical sense, and that Schopenhauer's pruning of the categories rests on a forced reading of the Metaphysical Deduction. Premise 1 also invites the realist's standing reply: correlation between known objects and a knower does not show that *being an object* depends on a subject.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own reply to the realist is Berkeleyan in form: "no object without subject" is, he argues, analytic once we see that objecthood means *being represented*; the supposedly independent object is always smuggled back in as a represented one. On Kant, his defense is textual: Kant does repeatedly speak of the thing-in-itself as the ground of appearance in ways hard to square with his own restriction, and the "two-aspect" readings that acquit Kant of double affection postdate the criticism. The literature remains divided on whether the Appendix is a correction or a brilliant misreading — the dossier presents it as the latter-day consensus presents it: a live dispute, with Schopenhauer's version at minimum a coherent radicalization of Kant's premises.

**Argument 3. The body's double knowledge: the Will as thing-in-itself**

1. Every phenomenon is known from outside, as representation under the principle of sufficient reason — with one exception: my own body.
2. My body is known in two ways: from outside, as one physical object among others, and immediately, from within, as willing — striving, desiring, acting.
3. An act of will and the corresponding bodily movement are not two events linked by causality but one event known in two ways: the movement, perceived from outside, *is* the act of will itself.
4. What is thus known immediately, without the mediation of representation, is not subject to the forms of space and individuation in the way outer knowledge is; it is the phenomenon's own inner nature.
5. There is no reason to treat my body as uniquely double-aspected: every phenomenon has an inside known to itself not at all (in lower nature) or dimly, of which outer appearance is the mere exterior. Nature is to be interpreted by analogy with the one case where inside and outside are both given.
Therefore, the inner nature of all phenomena — the thing-in-itself — is Will: one blind striving, objectifying itself in every force and organism, and known by each of us, in his own case, from within.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Book 2 (esp. §18 on the twofold knowledge of the body, and §§17–19); *WWR* II, ch. 17 ("On Man's Need for Metaphysics") and ch. 19; crucially qualified in *WWR* II, ch. 18, "On the Possibility of Knowing the Thing-in-Itself."

**Objection.** Everything about this argument is fought over, and the dispute over what it even claims must be stated first. On the **strong reading**, Schopenhauer identifies the thing-in-itself with Will, full stop. On the **qualified reading**, pressed by much modern scholarship, he does no such thing: in Volume II he concedes that even our inner knowledge of willing remains conditioned by the form of time, so that "will" names the thing-in-itself's *most immediate manifestation* — the closest appearance, not the thing-in-itself as it is apart from all manifestation. Critics of the strong reading add the two classic charges: the argument violates the Kantian limits Schopenhauer himself accepts (Argument 2), and premise 5's generalization — from one case of inner willing to the inner nature of stones and gravity — is an analogical leap with no controlling evidence, mistaking the familiar for the metaphysically privileged.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer anticipated both charges. To the Kantian charge he replies that this knowledge is not representation at all but an immediate identity of knower and known — an exception *internal* to the system, since the body is the one place where the subject is itself an object. The qualified reading is his own: Volume II's chapter 18 openly asks how far the identification reaches and refines it, which defenders cite as honesty rather than retreat — the word "will" is admitted to be a name taken from the clearest instance (*denominatio a potiori*), so the doctrine is that the thing-in-itself *shows itself most directly as will*, not that our everyday volitions are the inner essence of things. On the leap, his reply is methodological: metaphysics has exactly one datum of this kind, and explanation must go as far as the one key turns; a philosophy that refuses the analogy leaves the inner nature of nature simply blank — which he regarded not as caution but as failure.

**Argument 4. Individuation: one Will, many phenomena, self-divided**

1. Plurality — many distinct individuals existing simultaneously and successively — is possible only in space and time, the *principium individuationis*.
2. Space and time are forms of representation only; they do not belong to the thing-in-itself.
3. The thing-in-itself — Will — is therefore not plural: it is one and indivisible, wholly present in every phenomenon, as a single nature is whole in each of its instances.
4. Appearances, however, are individuated, embodied, and mutually exclusive: each individual will affirms itself by consuming, displacing, and resisting others.
5. What appears at the phenomenal level as conflict among individuals is therefore, at the metaphysical level, the one Will in conflict with itself — "feasting on itself," since outside itself there is nothing for it to will against.
Therefore, individuation is appearance and unity is reality; the war of each against all is the single Will's self-division, and egoism rests on taking an appearance for the truth.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Books 2 and 4 (esp. §23 on the Will's wholeness in each phenomenon and §§61–66 on egoism and its overcoming).

**Objection.** The monism does expository work the evidence doesn't fund: even granting (what critics of Argument 3 contest) that my inside is will, nothing shows the inside of *everything* is the *same* will — unity is inferred from the absence of space and time, but "not individuated by our forms" does not entail "one" rather than "many in some way we cannot conceive," or neither one nor many. Further, the system's ethics seems to need real individuals: if separateness is illusion, the compassion of Argument 7 and the denial of Argument 9 happen to no one in particular.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own reply is that one/many are themselves categories drawn from the phenomenal order; the Will is called "one" *negatively* — it is outside the conditions of plurality — not as one item counted against others. Defenders in the literature develop this as a claim about the inapplicability of numerical predicates beyond representation, while conceding the second difficulty as the system's deepest strain: Schopenhauer needs the phenomenal individual to be real enough to suffer, choose asceticism, and be saved, yet unreal enough for metaphysics — and he manages the tension by treating individuation as fully real *within* the only order in which ethics and salvation are transacted. Whether that is resolution or elegant equivocation remains genuinely open in the scholarship.

**Argument 5. Striving and suffering: the pessimism argument**

1. All willing arises from need, lack, and deficiency; to lack is to suffer.
2. Satisfaction of a desire is merely the removal of that lack — a negative state, the absence of a pain — and is by nature brief.
3. As soon as one want is silenced, another takes its place; and if none does, the result is not contentment but boredom, which is itself a suffering and shows that existence, unprodded by want, has no positive worth of its own.
4. Pleasure is therefore never a positive quantity added to life; it is always a temporary zero between pains.
5. This structure belongs to willing as such, not to particular social arrangements: reform can redistribute suffering and relieve particular evils, but it cannot alter the fact that a being who wills is a being who lacks.
Therefore, suffering is the positive and necessary element of life, pleasure the merely negative; existence as willing is essentially suffering, and life "swings like a pendulum backward and forward between pain and boredom."

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Book 4, §57 (the pendulum passage) and §§56–58 generally; *WWR* II, ch. 46, "On the Vanity and Suffering of Life"; *Parerga* II, "On the Vanity of Existence."

**Objection.** The argument turns on premise 4, and critics deny that it was ever established: that satisfaction *ends* a desire does not show it is merely negative — eating when hungry, finishing a work, hearing the resolution of a chord are experienced as positive goods, and Schopenhauer's "negative" verdict seems to be imposed by definition rather than found in experience. A second line, running from Nietzsche onward, attacks the whole accounting: the argument treats the *amount* of pleasure and pain as the measure of life's value, but a life's worth may lie in what its striving achieves or expresses, suffering included — pessimism confuses a hedonic audit with a verdict on existence.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own reply to the first objection is phenomenological and stubborn: attend closely, he says, and every "positive" pleasure decomposes into the silencing of a prior want plus the brief interval before the next; the supposed positive glow is contrast with the pain it ends — which is why satisfaction is always, in his word, disappointing relative to the desire that promised it. To the second objection he has a partial reply that later defenders develop: the argument is conditional — *if* value is assessed by the subject's felt state across a life, the books do not balance — and he is entitled to it because his opponents, the optimists, themselves argue on hedonic and providential grounds. Whether pessimism follows for those who reject the hedonic standard entirely is, in the current literature, the standing question about him.

**Argument 6. The worst of all possible worlds (against Leibniz)**

1. Leibnizian optimism holds that this is the best of all possible worlds, created by an omnipotent, omniscient, benevolent God.
2. Consider instead whether the world could be *worse* while still being possible.
3. A world is possible, in the relevant sense, only if it can continue to exist: a world arranged slightly worse than ours — its evils slightly increased past a threshold — would not be a sadder world but no world at all, collapsing under its own accumulated misery into non-existence.
4. Our world, by contrast, demonstrably continues; and at every point its evils sit close to the maximum compatible with continued existence (a small worsening of earthquakes, diseases, climates, or tempers would already be unsurvivable).
5. Therefore our world is arranged as badly as it possibly could be while still existing.
Therefore, this is not the best but the *worst* of all possible worlds — and optimism, in Schopenhauer's verdict, is not merely false but "a bitter mockery of the unspeakable suffering of mankind."

**Textual basis.** *WWR* II, ch. 46, "On the Vanity and Suffering of Life"; the "bitter mockery" verdict is in *WWR* I, §59.

**Objection.** The argument is widely judged the weakest of the system's set pieces. Premise 3 trades on an equivocation in "possible": from "a worse world would not long persist" it does not follow that every world worse than ours is impossible — it might exist wretchedly and briefly, and brief existence is still existence; at most the argument shows ours is near the worst *sustainable* arrangement. Critics also note that premise 4's empirical claim — that evils are fine-tuned near a survivability ceiling — is asserted rather than shown, and suspiciously unfalsifiable.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer offered it as an *ad hominem* counter-thrust inside the theodicy debate, not as a free-standing proof, and its defenders read it that way: against an opponent who defines possible worlds by what a perfect God could actualize *and sustain* as an ordered creation, the inversion has real dialectical bite — it turns Leibniz's modal machinery against his conclusion. Read as an independent demonstration it is usually conceded to fail on the equivocation; read as a burlesque of theodicy's method — drawing a cheerful metaphysical conclusion from an armchair survey of the world's contents — it makes his methodological point, that optimism and pessimism should be settled by looking at existence, not by deduction from divine attributes.

**Argument 7. Compassion as the sole basis of morality (against Kant)**

1. An action has genuine moral worth only if its incentive is neither egoistic (aiming at one's own well-being) nor malicious (aiming at another's harm).
2. Kant's candidate incentive — action from duty, from reverence for the moral law the agent gives himself — is either motivationally inert or covertly egoistic: insofar as it moves us, it trades on anticipated self-approval, fear of self-contempt, or hope of reward, and its structure of universalized self-legislation is egoism generalizing itself.
3. Theological morality locates the incentive in obedience to a stronger power and hope of payment — egoism again, with an omnipotent paymaster.
4. There remains one incentive that is neither: *Mitleid*, compassion — the immediate, involuntary participation in another's suffering, in which the agent wills the other's well-being directly, as his own.
5. Compassion is possible, metaphysically, because individuation is appearance: in compassion the agent experiences, obscurely, the truth that the sufferer is the same Will as himself — *tat tvam asi*, "this art thou."
Therefore, compassion is the sole genuine basis of morality; from it spring its two cardinal virtues, justice (compassion restraining us from harm) and loving-kindness (compassion moving us to help); and any ethics of duty, command, or divine law is morality's counterfeit.

**Textual basis.** *On the Basis of Morality* (1840), esp. §§6–8 (the critique of Kant) and §§16–22 (the positive foundation); anticipated at *WWR* I, Book 4, §§66–67.

**Objection.** Two objections dominate. First, Kant's defenders reply that premise 2 caricatures him: duty as Kant conceives it is not a disguised bargain, and the charge that autonomous self-legislation is egoism confuses the *form* of universal law with self-interest — a law I must will for everyone, myself merely included, is precisely not egoism. Second, compassion looks too variable to found morality on: it is partial (stronger for the near, the similar, the vividly present), fatigable, and silent about what to do when compassions conflict — duties of justice, impartial institutions, and obligations to the distant seem to require exactly the universalizing machinery Schopenhauer dismisses.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own reply to the first is diagnostic: strip Kantian duty of every consequence for the agent — no self-respect gained, no self-contempt feared, no God watching — and ask whether the bare concept of law still *moves* anyone; he holds that what survives the stripping is either compassion or nothing. On the second, his reply concedes ground deliberately: he is not offering a decision procedure for institutions but a theory of moral *worth* and its metaphysical root — where compassion is absent, "justice" enforced by rules is prudence wearing morality's clothes, however socially necessary prudence may be. The literature's verdict is split in an instructive way: his critique is often judged to fail against Kant read carefully, while the positive theory — morality as rooted in an affective identification with others that metaphysics then explains — is treated as a live and increasingly respected position, standing comparison with Hume's sympathy and modern care ethics.

**Argument 8. Aesthetic release, and the special case of music**

1. Ordinary cognition serves the will: it perceives objects only in their relations (causes, uses, threats) under the principle of sufficient reason.
2. In aesthetic contemplation, perception tears itself free from this service: attention rests wholly on the object, the perceiver forgets his individuality and its wants, and he becomes the pure subject of knowing, perceiving not the individual thing but the (Platonic) Idea embodied in it.
3. What is thus perceived is no longer an object of desire or aversion; hence aesthetic contemplation is, for its duration, a release from willing and its suffering — knowledge's "Sabbath from the penal servitude of willing."
4. The several arts present the Ideas at successive grades of the Will's objectification, from architecture (gravity, rigidity) to tragedy (the Idea of humanity, showing the Will at war with itself).
5. Music, however, presents no Ideas at all: melody and harmony do not copy phenomena — Schopenhauer finds in the deep bass the grades of unorganized nature, in the inner voices the organic world, in melody the conscious striving of man — yet music moves us independently of any represented content.
Therefore, music is not a copy of the Ideas but "a copy of the Will itself," the one art that expresses the inner nature of the world directly — which explains its unique depth and immediacy among the arts.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Book 3 (pure subject of knowing, §34; genius, §36; the Sabbath passage, §38; the arts' hierarchy, §§43–51; music, §52); parallels *WWR* II, ch. 39, "On the Metaphysics of Music."

**Objection.** Premise 2 faces the epistemological objection pressed against all of Book 3: if the Ideas are objects of a perception that has shed the forms of representation, how is such perception knowledge at all — and if the pure subject has lost individuality and memory, who reports the experience afterward? Premise 5 invites the music-specific objection: the claim that music copies the Will is unverifiable metaphor, and modern musicology notes that Schopenhauer's mapping of voices to metaphysical grades is a just-so story fitted to the Western tonal practice he happened to know, not a theory of music as such.

**Reply.** To the first, Schopenhauer's reply is that aesthetic experience is self-certifying in the way pain is: the state is known by being had, and its mark is precisely the silence of the will, available to anyone who has stood before a landscape or a painting and noticed the quiet. Philosophy's role is not to prove the state possible but to say what it is. On music, he concedes more than critics often notice: the parallels between music and world are, he says, analogies that *illustrate* a relation whose ground is metaphysical — music could not copy the Will in the way a picture copies a scene, and the doctrine's content is finally negative and experiential: music speaks of the essence without the phenomena, which is why we understand it immediately yet cannot translate it. Detached from the metaphysics by later hands (Wagner's above all), the argument survived as the most influential account of musical meaning of the nineteenth century — a reception the dossier tracks in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

**Argument 9. Denial of the will: salvation, and why suicide is not denial**

1. Complete and constant knowledge of the whole — of the Will's unity, of individuation as appearance, and of the suffering essential to all willing — does not merely inform an individual; it transforms his relation to willing itself.
2. Such knowledge becomes a "quieter of the will": motives, which move us only while we take our individual existence as ultimately real, progressively lose their power over one who sees through individuation.
3. The result is the will's turning against itself in this phenomenon: renunciation of the will-to-live — voluntary chastity, poverty, fasting, endurance of insult without retaliation — the ascetic holiness that the great religions, on Schopenhauer's reading, all enshrine at their core.
4. Suicide is the contrary phenomenon: the suicide wills life itself and is dissatisfied only with its *conditions*; he destroys his individual phenomenon in order to escape its sufferings, while affirming the Will without reserve.
5. Death in any case destroys only a phenomenon; the Will, being outside time, is untouched by the death of any individual.
Therefore, the only "salvation" the system admits is the denial of the will-to-live, achieved not by self-destruction (which is affirmation in disguise) but by knowledge ripening into renunciation — after which what remains, to those still willing, appears as nothing, and to the one delivered, is peace.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Book 4: denial and its phenomenology, §68; suicide, §69; the concluding "nothing," §71.

**Objection.** The argument exhibits the system's celebrated internal strain: at the phenomenal level every act is necessary (Argument 10), individuation is illusion (Argument 4), and the Will is blind striving with no purposes — so *who* performs this turning, and how can sheer *knowledge*, which the system elsewhere demotes to the will's tool, acquire the power to abolish its master? Critics add a theological objection from the other side: the ascetic ideal here is borrowed prestige — Buddhism and Christian mysticism are read through the system until they agree with it, and the agreement is then cited as independent confirmation.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own reply to the first objection is the system's one admitted point of phenomenal freedom: the Will as thing-in-itself is free — outside necessity — and the denial is precisely the phenomenon of that freedom breaking into the phenomenal order, the sole place where the two orders touch; he calls the transition, borrowing the theologians' word without their doctrine, an effect of "grace." He therefore presents the paradox not as a solvable puzzle but as the exact spot where philosophy hands over to the experience it can only indicate — the literature divides between those who accept this as principled mysticism and those who judge it an admission of inconsistency. On the religions, his defense is that the reading is corrective, not circular: strip the ascetic traditions of their mythical dress, he argues, and the denial of the will is what remains standing in all of them — an empirical convergence his system explains and theirs, he thought, only allegorize.

**Argument 10. Freedom and character: *operari sequitur esse***

1. Every human action follows with strict necessity from two factors: the agent's character (fixed) and the motives present to his intellect (given).
2. The intellect's role is only to *present* motives; it does not create the character on which they act, and deliberation merely makes the conflict of motives surveyable — the outcome is determined by which motive is strongest *for this character*.
3. Hence there is no *liberum arbitrium indifferentiae*, no freedom of indifference by which the same person, in the same circumstances and with the same character, could do either of two opposed acts. A man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills.
4. Yet responsibility is real, and conscience testifies to it: we hold ourselves responsible not ultimately for our deeds but for *what we are* — the deeds are only the evidence.
5. What we are — the intelligible character — lies outside time and the principle of sufficient reason, and is therefore not itself necessitated: it is the will's own free, groundless act, of which the empirical life is the necessary unfolding.
Therefore, freedom is not found in *doing* (operari), which is necessary, but in *being* (esse): *operari sequitur esse* — doing follows being — and moral responsibility attaches to the timeless character that phenomena merely reveal.

**Textual basis.** *On the Freedom of the Will* (1839), the prize essay crowned by the Royal Norwegian Society of Sciences; *WWR* I, Book 4, §§53–55; *WWR* II, ch. 19.

**Objection.** Relocating freedom into a timeless character looks to many critics like saving the word by surrendering the thing: a character I never chose, at no time, under no conditions, that unfailingly determines every act of my life, is indistinguishable in its consequences from plain determinism, and calling it "free" because it is uncaused by anything *else* mistakes absence of external constraint for agency. Kant's own version of the intelligible/empirical distinction, from which Schopenhauer borrows, faces the same charge — importing it imports the problem.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer accepts most of the description and denies it is a defect: freedom of indifference, he argues, is not an ideal we fail to reach but an incoherent fiction — an act uncaused by character and motive would be a chance event, no more *mine* than a seizure. What moral consciousness actually demands, on his analysis, is that my acts be *mine*, expressive of my nature rather than imposed from outside; and this the doctrine secures completely. His defenders add that the relocation explains the specific phenomenology of guilt better than its rivals: remorse fastens not on the isolated act but on the discovery of what one is ("that I could do *that*"), and shame at one's character is unintelligible on a purely libertarian account. The essay's contemporary verdict stands as a curiosity of the record: the Norwegian society crowned it, while its companion piece on morality was refused by the Danish society — the only entry — for, among other things, its treatment of Hegel.

**Argument 11. Eternal justice: the world as the Will's self-torment**

1. Temporal (human) justice is retribution in time: an evil deed is balanced by a later suffering inflicted on the doer, and its rationality presupposes that doer and sufferer are the same person across time.
2. But time, like all individuation, belongs to appearance only; metaphysically, the one Will is the inner nature of both the tormentor and the tormented.
3. The murderer and his victim, the oppressor and the oppressed, are therefore — in themselves, though not in appearance — one being: the Will, which, individuated and blinded, fails to recognize itself and inflicts on itself the suffering it undergoes.
4. Every suffering in the world is thus exactly balanced by guilt in the world, not by causal bookkeeping but by identity: the total suffering is the one Will's self-laceration, "the world itself is the tribunal of the world."
5. No one, therefore, suffers undeservedly in the metaphysical ledger: not because each victim committed a personal crime, but because existence as an individuated will *is* the guilt — existence itself is the offense whose penalty is existence's suffering.
Therefore, justice is not a future event to be awaited from gods or history; it is eternal and already accomplished in the structure of being: the Will pays, in the suffering of its phenomena, the full price of its own affirmation.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* I, Book 4, §§63–64.

**Objection.** This is the argument at which even sympathetic readers most often balk. Morally, it appears to sanctify atrocity: telling the tortured child that tormentor and tormented are metaphysically one seems less like justice than like its abolition — if no one is really distinct from anyone, desert has nothing to attach to, and premise 5's "existence itself is the guilt" converts a metaphysical slogan into a verdict on every victim. The doctrine also sits awkwardly beside Argument 7: compassion depends on taking the other's suffering seriously as *his*, which eternal justice seems to undercut by reassigning it to the cosmic self.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's own framing anticipates the moral charge by sharply separating orders: eternal justice is a *metaphysical* doctrine about what suffering ultimately is, never a phenomenal rule — at the level of appearance, individuals are real, rights bind absolutely, and the criminal is to be restrained and judged exactly as if no metaphysics existed; indeed state punishment is justified in his political remarks on precisely phenomenal grounds (deterrence, never retribution). Defenders in the literature read the doctrine as a theodicy inverted — instead of justifying God by the world's suffering, it justifies *no one*: there is no external judge and no cosmic excuse, only a single self-harming reality whose horror is the final argument for denying the will rather than affirming it. Whether the two-level defense is coherent — whether a truth about the in-itself can be quarantined from the conduct it seems to describe — is one of the standing questions in the interpretation of Book 4.

**Argument 12. The primacy of the will over the intellect**

1. In human self-consciousness, what is primary and unceasing is willing — desiring, striving, craving, averting — while knowing is intermittent, effortful, and fatigable: the intellect tires and sleeps; the will, in heartbeat, appetite, and dream-anxiety, does not.
2. The intellect presents objects and motives, but the decisions that follow express the character of the will, not the conclusions of reasoning: interest bends attention, memory, and judgment (we see arguments against our desires as weak, and their flaws as decisive), and men routinely act against their professed maxims while never acting against their wants.
3. Developmentally and comparatively, intellect is proportioned to need: animals have exactly as much as their mode of life requires, and in man the intellect appears as an instrument added for a complex will's navigation — in origin, a tool, like claw or trunk.
4. Where the two conflict, the will wins and conscripts the intellect to rationalize the victory after the fact; knowledge can guide willing among means but cannot supply, revise, or abolish its ends.
Therefore, the will is the master and the intellect its servant — "the will is the strong blind man who carries on his shoulders the lame man who can see" — and a philosophy that begins from the intellect mistakes the tool for the agent.

**Textual basis.** *WWR* II, ch. 19, "On the Primacy of the Will in Self-Consciousness" (the source of the blind-man image, Schopenhauer's own); *WWR* I, Book 2, §19; *Parerga* II, "On the Primacy of the Will" materials in the psychological chapters.

**Objection.** As an empirical argument it is built from introspection and anecdote, and modern psychology complicates its clean hierarchy: cognition demonstrably reshapes desire (therapy, extinction learning, deliberative preference change), and the "tool" metaphor smuggles in the purposiveness it is meant to establish — functions in nature are read backward from effects. Skeptics also note that the argument, if fully sound, saws off its own branch: a philosophy produced by an intellect that is the will's rationalizing servant has no special claim to be believed, Schopenhauer's included.

**Reply.** Schopenhauer's reply to the last point is built into the system: philosophy becomes possible exactly where the intellect temporarily exceeds its service — in genius and in pure contemplation (Argument 8) — and he presents his own doctrine as the record of that anomaly, not as an exemption from human nature generally. On the empirical side, later defenders argue the hierarchy was never meant as a law without exceptions: the claim is about *default and direction of fit* — intellect proposes within ends it does not set, and its corrections of desire are themselves typically driven by stronger or longer-sighted desires. That Freud could acknowledge Schopenhauer as a precursor of the unconscious, and that motivated reasoning is now a laboratory commonplace, has given this argument the warmest afterlife of any in the volume: it is the piece of the system most often judged, even by the metaphysics' opponents, to have seen something true first.
