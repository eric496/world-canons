# Arthur Schopenhauer — Life and Times

## A merchant's son in Danzig (1788–1805)

Arthur Schopenhauer was born on February 22, 1788, in Danzig (Gdańsk), then a free city under Polish sovereignty and one of the great trading ports of the Baltic. His father, Heinrich Floris Schopenhauer (1747–1805), was a wealthy merchant of Dutch descent — cultivated, anglophile, a reader of Voltaire, and determined that his son would succeed him in business. By the standard family account, he chose the name *Arthur* precisely because it is written the same in German, English, and French: a small piece of branding for a future international merchant. His mother, Johanna (1766–1838, née Trosiener), was some twenty years her husband's junior, lively and literary in a household where those qualities had limited scope.

Politics redirected the family before temperament could. When Prussia annexed Danzig in 1793, Heinrich Floris — who valued the free city's status — moved the family to Hamburg, where the firm prospered. Arthur's education was designed for trade: languages over Latin, experience over school. In 1797 he spent months in Le Havre with the family of a business correspondent, the Grégoire de Blésimaire, acquiring the fluent French he kept for life. In 1803–04 his parents took him on a long tour of England, France, Switzerland, and Austria; he was left for a stretch at a school in Wimbledon while they traveled on. The travel diary he kept is the first surviving evidence of the philosopher: alongside the sights, the fifteen-year-old records workhouses, poverty, executions, and misery with a fixity that later readers have seen as the pessimism already forming. That reading may be hindsight; what the diary plainly shows is a boy who looked at suffering longer than he looked at anything else.

## The father's death and the broken apprenticeship (1805–1809)

In 1804 Schopenhauer confirmed, entering a formal apprenticeship with a Hamburg merchant house — by his own later account he chose the counting-house partly to accompany his parents on one more journey, understanding too late what he had signed. On April 20, 1805, Heinrich Floris fell from a loft window of the family's warehouse into the canal below and died. The standard account, held by contemporaries and by his biographers since, is that it was suicide: he had been depressed and anxious about the firm's fortunes in the Napoleonic disruption of trade, and the fall from that window is difficult to read as accident. The evidence is circumstantial rather than documentary, and the dossier states it as the tradition does — apparent suicide — but the fact that shaped the son's life is not in dispute: at seventeen, Arthur was bound to a career he hated, and the man who had bound him was dead.

His mother settled her affairs with unexpected speed. Johanna sold the Hamburg business interests, and in 1806 moved to Weimar — then the literary capital of Germany — where her salon and her novels (she became one of the most widely read German novelists of her generation) made her famous. Arthur remained in Hamburg, miserable at his desk, until 1807, when Johanna and his mentor Fernow arranged his release. He made up a classical education in roughly two years: at Gotha (where a satirical poem about his teachers forced a discreet withdrawal) and then in Weimar under private tuition, mastering Latin and Greek at an age when most students were finishing them. The pattern of the whole life is already visible: solitary, ferociously industrious, incapable of institutional tact.

## Göttingen, Berlin, and the dissertation (1809–1814)

In 1809, having reached his majority and drawn his share of the inheritance — a share he thereafter managed with a caution that made him financially independent for life, one of the very few major philosophers who never needed an academic salary — he enrolled at Göttingen, first in medicine and natural science, then in philosophy. There the skeptic Gottlob Ernst Schulze, author of *Aenesidemus*, gave him the advice he always credited as decisive: confine yourself first to Plato and Kant; touch no one else, least of all the moderns, until those two are mastered. Schopenhauer obeyed, and never really disobeyed: his philosophy is a lifelong negotiation with exactly those two inheritances.

In 1811 he moved to Berlin, then at the height of its post-Kantian ferment. He attended Fichte's lectures — the surviving notebooks begin in attention and curdle into contempt; Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre struck him as word-spinning that had emptied Kant of meaning. He attended Schleiermacher's lectures as well, with the same independence of judgment. He studied philology under Friedrich August Wolf and filled notebooks with reading. When war with France threatened Berlin in the spring of 1813, Schopenhauer — who was not going to volunteer for anyone's national cause — withdrew to Weimar and then to Rudolstadt, where, in a guesthouse over the summer of 1813, he wrote his doctoral dissertation, *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason*. The University of Jena awarded the doctorate in absentia in October 1813. The dissertation already contains the system's foundation: every explanation in the world of representation takes one of four irreducible forms of ground and consequent.

The winter of 1813–14 in Weimar was brilliant and catastrophic. In his mother's salon he met Goethe, and the two worked together on the theory of color — the one living German whose recognition Schopenhauer craved, and who valued the young man's mind. And there the orientalist Friedrich Majer introduced him to Indian religion. Schopenhauer began reading the *Oupnek'hat* — the Upanishads in Anquetil Duperron's Latin version, itself translated from a Persian rendering — and read it, by his own practice, every night for the rest of his life. He would rank the Upanishads beside Plato and Kant as his sources.

In May 1814 mother and son quarreled finally. The immediate causes were mutual exasperation, much of it aimed at her companion Gerstenbergk, a younger civil servant whose place in her household Arthur treated as an insult to his father's memory. Beneath the friction lay twenty-six years of temperamental incompatibility, and Johanna's letter ending the relationship is famously cold and precise about it. They never met or spoke again. She died in Jena in 1838; there is no record that the news altered his routine. Biographers have mined the rupture for explanations of everything from the misogyny of the late essays to the metaphysics of the Will; the dossier notes the temptation and resists single-cause biography. What can be said is that from 1814 Schopenhauer was, by his own choice, without family — his sister Adele, with whom he corresponded, died in 1849 — and that he organized the rest of his life as a structure for one activity: the work.

## Dresden and the masterwork (1814–1819)

He settled in Dresden, a quiet capital with good libraries and no university to annoy him, and wrote at speed. *On Vision and Colors* appeared in 1816 — his physiological theory of color, offered as science's correction to Newton's optics and developed in dialogue with Goethe's *Theory of Colors*. Goethe had expected an ally; what he got was a disciple with his own theory (color as the activity of the retina, not a property of light), and the relationship cooled into distant politeness — a pattern (the master who fails to recognize the successor) that Schopenhauer's life repeats with almost comic reliability. Then, in four years of concentrated work, he composed the book that contains his entire system: *The World as Will and Representation* was finished in 1818 and published by Brockhaus in December of that year, its title page dated 1819, with a confidence in its preface — he suggested the reader read it twice, and implied the age would eventually catch up — that the market immediately punished. The book sold so badly that, by his own later account, much of the printing was eventually remaindered as waste paper, a fact he knew and nursed. In 1819 he traveled in Italy — Venice, Florence, Rome, Naples — in the first flush of having said everything; the journey was cut short by bank troubles in Danzig touching the remainder of his funds, and he returned to secure them. (Biographical research also records a daughter born in Italy in 1819 who died in infancy; the documentation is thin and the dossier passes it on as such.)

## Berlin: the challenge to Hegel, and the Marquet affair (1820–1831)

In 1820 Schopenhauer qualified as a Privatdozent at the University of Berlin, delivering a trial lecture before a faculty that included Hegel. Then came the legendary gesture: he announced his lectures for the same hour as Hegel's own. Whether the motive was contempt, strategy, or both, the result is certain — students chose Hegel in crowds; Schopenhauer's room emptied, and after a single semester he effectively stopped, though he kept the lectureship on the books and re-announced courses, undelivered, for years. He never taught again, and he never forgave the academic world, whose philosophy — above all Hegel, "that clumsy charlatan" in his private vocabulary — he treated for forty years as a corrupt trade practiced by men who lived *from* philosophy rather than *for* truth. The bitterness is real, but a fair account adds that Schopenhauer did almost nothing a career requires: no school, no journal alliances, no concessions of manner. The neglect of his book and the fiasco of his lectureship belong together, and both belong partly to him.

The Berlin years also produced the episode his detractors have always reached for. In August 1821, in the lodging house where he lived, he came upon the seamstress Caroline Luise Marquet — a neighbor in her forties — talking with others on the landing outside his door; he had complained of the noise before. Words were exchanged, and Marquet fell or was thrown down the stairs. She sued, claiming injuries that prevented her working; Schopenhauer maintained she had thrown herself down theatrically and was simulating the after-effects. The courts did not believe him, or at least did not acquit him: in 1826, after years of proceedings, he was ordered to pay her an annuity of 15 thalers quarterly for as long as her incapacity lasted. He paid it for over two decades, protesting in his account books the whole time — and when she died in 1852 he recorded in his ledger, with a satisfaction he made no attempt to hide, *obit anus, abit onus*: the old woman dies, the burden departs. The dossier's judgment is the record's: the altercation happened; who fell how, and how real the injuries were, remain genuinely disputed; the court's verdict and the payments do not. It is the ugliest documented episode of the life and it is not improved or worsened by embroidery.

Through the 1820s he persisted without a public: further Italian travel (1822–23), a return to Berlin in 1825, a translation of Gracián's *Oráculo manual* completed (and published only after his death), a short Latin expansion of the color theory (1830), and revisions of nothing, because nothing of his was in print long enough to revise. In 1831 cholera reached Berlin. Schopenhauer fled — first to Frankfurt am Main, then to Mannheim for a year, before returning to Frankfurt for good in 1833. Hegel, who stayed, died that November in the same epidemic; the death was officially ascribed to cholera, though later writers have questioned the diagnosis. Schopenhauer did not record any softening at the news.

## Frankfurt: obscurity, system-building, and the two prizes (1833–1850)

From 1833 he lived in Frankfurt permanently, in rented rooms (longest at Schöne Aussicht 17, overlooking the Main), and never left the city again except for short excursions. The famous routine assembled itself: rise early, cold water, black coffee, three hours of writing, the flute (he played Rossini daily and well), lunch at the Englischer Hof, an afternoon of reading, a fast two-hour walk in all weather, the theater or a concert in the evening, light supper reading, and bed — a timetable described by visitors with the fidelity usually reserved for Kant's Königsberg walks, and like Kant's, partly performance and partly simply true. He lived on the interest of his capital, investing with the same suspicious care that had tripled his effective share of the inheritance, and he kept his accounts to the pfennig. The solitude was real but not absolute: a small circle of the faithful — his "apostles," above all Julius Frauenstädt, and correspondents like Johann August Becker and David Asher — read him, argued with him, and kept the system's name alive in print while its author waited.

The work of these decades is consolidation and proof. *On the Will in Nature* (1836) marshaled the empirical sciences as corroboration of the metaphysics. In 1839 the Royal Norwegian Society of Sciences at Trondheim crowned his prize essay *On the Freedom of the Will* — the first official recognition of his life, at fifty-one. The companion essay, *On the Basis of Morality*, he submitted to the Royal Danish Society in Copenhagen in 1840 — and it was refused the prize although it was the only entry, with a published judgment that managed both to misconstrue his argument and to speak slightingly of Hegel's critics; Schopenhauer published the two essays together in 1841 as *The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics*, with a preface that returns the insult in full and remains a small masterpiece of controlled fury. In 1844 he finally issued the second edition of *The World as Will and Representation*, expanded by a second volume of supplements as long again as the first. It sold no better than the original had. He was fifty-six, and the world had declined his system twice; the reasonable conclusion, which he declined to draw, was that it would never take it at all.

## Parerga and fame (1851–1860)

What changed his reputation was the book he regarded as by-work. *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851) — "by-works and omissions," two volumes of essays and aphorisms written across the 1840s in the accessible, aphoristic style the big book had refused — was placed with a Berlin publisher only through Frauenstädt's persistence, in a small printing, with no fee. It found readers at once, and in April 1853 John Oxenford's long anonymous review, "Iconoclasm in German Philosophy," in the *Westminster Review* carried the discovery to the English-speaking world. Through the 1850s the obscurity inverted: translations, reviews, a stream of visitors and correspondents to Frankfurt, portraits, daguerreotypes, a collected edition planned, disciples organizing. Schopenhauer received fame with a satisfaction he did not pretend to disguise and did not entirely trust; he read his own reviews at the Englischer Hof, annotated his enemies, and enlarged *Parerga* for a posthumous second edition (1862). He also arranged his affairs with characteristic precision: his will left his estate in large part to a Berlin fund for Prussian soldiers disabled in the suppression of the 1848 revolution and for the dependents of those killed — the old anti-revolutionary paying, at the end, the men who had shot down the barricades.

The Frankfurt years are also where the legend gathers — the succession of brown poodles, named Atman (the Sanskrit word for the world-soul, a joke at metaphysics' expense that was also a genuine affection), the misanthropic table talk, the essay on noise written by a man tormented by every slammed door. The anecdotes are mostly late and many are improved in the telling; the poodles, the routine, and the temper are documented well enough without garnish. A third edition of *The World as Will and Representation* appeared in 1859, selling. On September 21, 1860, after a short illness — a chest infection, reported as pneumonia — Arthur Schopenhauer died at home in Frankfurt, seventy-two years old, famous for exactly as long as he had once been ignored in proportion to his deserts, or so he would have said. He is buried in Frankfurt's Hauptfriedhof. Frauenstädt's edition of the complete works (1873–74) and the publication of the manuscript remains made the Nachlass public; the system's second life — in Nietzsche, in Wagner's *Tristan*, in Freud's acknowledged ancestry, in Thomas Mann and Beckett and Borges — belongs to [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).
