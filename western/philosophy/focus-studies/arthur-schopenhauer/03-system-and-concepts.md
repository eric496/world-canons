# Schopenhauer: The System and Its Concepts

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Arthur Schopenhauer](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md) · Arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

## One thought, four books

Schopenhauer insisted, in the preface to the first edition of *The World as Will and Representation* (1818/1819), that his book communicates "a single thought" and that its parts are organically connected, like the limbs of a body rather than the links of a chain: no part is fully understood until the whole is grasped, yet the whole exists only in the parts. That thought can be stated in one sentence: **the world is, on its outer side, representation ordered by the principle of sufficient reason, and on its inner side, one blind, aimless Will — and everything else in the system is the unfolding of what that means.** The four books of the first volume correspond to four ways of considering the one world: as representation (Book 1), as Will (Book 2), as aesthetic contemplation of the Ideas (Book 3), and as the affirmation, suffering, and possible denial of the Will (Book 4). His other works are not additions to the system but supports for it: the dissertation *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason* (1813, substantially revised 1847) lays the epistemological foundation, and the supplementary second volume of the masterwork (1844) fills in the picture without altering it. This is why he demanded that readers read the first volume twice — a demand that is only half arrogance; the system's circular, self-interpreting structure really does reward the second pass.

## The principle of sufficient reason and its fourfold root

The system's outer frame is the **principle of sufficient reason**: nothing is without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise. Schopenhauer's doctoral contribution was to show that this single formula conceals four distinct principles, each governing a distinct class of objects and known through a distinct faculty. Nothing counts as an explanation until you say *which* root is in play; confusing them is, for him, the original sin of philosophy.

1. **The reason of becoming (causality).** Every change in a physical object requires a prior change as its cause. This root governs nature and is known by the *understanding*, the faculty animals share with us, which applies causality directly in perception itself.
2. **The reason of knowing.** A judgment is true only if it has a ground — a perception, another judgment, a concept, or the form of thought itself. This root governs concepts, logic, and science as an edifice of judgments, and belongs to *reason*, the faculty peculiar to humans.
3. **The reason of being.** Positions in space and moments in time determine one another: geometry rests on spatial relations, arithmetic on the succession of numbers in time. This root governs mathematical objects and is known through *pure sensibility*, the a priori intuition of space and time.
4. **The reason of acting (motivation).** Every human (and animal) act follows necessarily from a motive acting on a given character. This root governs actions and is known through *self-consciousness*, our inner awareness of our own willing.

Two consequences matter for everything that follows. First, the principle in all four forms applies **only within the world of representation** — to relations among phenomena, never to the world as a whole or to its inner nature. Asking for the cause of the world, or a reason for existence itself, is a category error the dissertation already rules out. Second, explanation by any root is always *relative*: every reason points to a further reason, in an endless chain. Philosophy that wants the world's inner character must therefore leave the principle behind at exactly the point where Kant stopped — and Schopenhauer's claim is that one narrow door remains open.

## Representation: space, time, causality, matter

Book 1 radicalizes Kant. The world as representation has two inseparable halves: the **object**, structured by the forms of space, time, and causality, and the **subject**, which knows but is never itself known as an object. "The world is my representation" (Vol. I, §1) is not solipsism: it is the claim that objecthood as such is relative to a knowing subject, so that a world of objects without a subject is a contradiction in terms. Schopenhauer accepts Kant's distinction between appearance and thing-in-itself but prunes Kant's machinery: of the twelve categories he keeps only **causality**, arguing that Kant illicitly applied causality beyond experience in deriving the thing-in-itself as the *cause* of our sensations. Space and time he retains as a priori forms contributed by our cognitive constitution — and together they do decisive work, because space and time are what make plurality and individuation possible. He calls them, borrowing a scholastic term, the ***principium individuationis***: only because things are spread out in space and strung out in time can there be *many* things, separate individuals, and hence separate egos whose interests collide.

Perception itself is intellectual: the understanding takes the raw effects on the senses and, applying causality instantaneously and pre-consciously, projects a world of material objects as their causes. **Matter**, accordingly, is not a stuff beneath properties; matter is causality itself made perceptible — "through and through causality," the objective correlate of the understanding's one function. Space, time, and matter belong wholly to the side of representation: they are the spectacles, not the face. Whatever the world is in itself, it is not in space, not in time, and not many.

## The body and the Will as thing-in-itself

The one exception to our confinement among representations is our own **body**, which each of us knows in two radically different ways (Vol. I, §18). From outside, my body is one physical object among others, obeying causal laws. From inside, I know it immediately as *willing*: the act of will and the movement of the body are not cause and effect but one and the same event known in two ways. When I raise my arm, there is not a mental volition that produces a bodily motion; there is a single act, given to outer perception as motion and to self-consciousness as willing.

Schopenhauer generalizes — the system's boldest and most contested move, examined as an argument in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md). If my body's inner nature is will, then the inner nature of every phenomenon, known otherwise only from outside, is will too: one and the same **Will** (always capitalized in translations to mark that it is not anyone's personal volition) objectifies itself in the force that moves the stone, the instinct that builds the nest, and the desire that ruins the life. This Will is the thing-in-itself: it lies outside space, time, and causality, is therefore **one** (plurality requires the *principium individuationis*), **free** (the necessity of the principle of sufficient reason does not reach it), and **groundless** — it has no reason or purpose outside itself; it simply wills, and what it wills is life and more life. "Will" is a name borrowed from its clearest manifestation in us; he is explicit that the word designates a *genus* of which our willing is the best-known species, not an anthropomorphic projection of human psychology onto rocks. Whether the identification is licensed — and whether his own qualification in Volume II, that will is only the *most immediate manifestation* of the thing-in-itself rather than the thing-in-itself exhausted, retreats from it — remains the central interpretive dispute of Schopenhauer scholarship.

## Grades of objectification and the Platonic Ideas

The Will does not appear all at once but in **grades of objectification**, ascending from the universal forces of nature (gravity, magnetism, crystallization) through plant life, animal life in its fixed species-forms, to humanity, where individuation and intellect reach their peak. The grades themselves — the timeless patterns at which the Will's objectivity becomes adequate at each level — Schopenhauer identifies with Plato's **Ideas**. An Idea is not a concept (concepts are abstract, formed by reason after perception; Ideas are perceived, not thought) and not an individual thing (individuals fall under space and time; Ideas do not). The species *horse* as a timeless form is an Idea; this horse, born and dying in time, is its multiplied appearance. In human beings the ascent turns on itself: intellect, developed as the Will's instrument for navigating a complex world, becomes capable of turning around and *knowing* the Will — the precondition of both art and salvation.

## The primacy of will over intellect

The intellect is not the master of the house. It evolved — the anachronism is only apparent; Schopenhauer writes comfortably of nature's economy — as a tool of the Will, a lantern carried by a blind giant: in the famous image, the will is "the strong blind man who carries on his shoulders the lame man who can see" (*WWR* II, ch. 19). Hence the phenomena every introspective moralist notices: we rationalize what we already want; we cannot argue ourselves out of desires; knowledge brightens or dims with interest; character persists through every change of opinion. The intellect knows *choices among motives*, never the willing itself; it is a spectator of decisions it believes it makes. This primacy thesis, worked out empirically in Volume II, is the part of the system that most plainly anticipates later psychologies of the unconscious — Freud acknowledged the anticipation — and it is wholly independent of pessimism: even a sunny metaphysics could keep it.

## Character: intelligible, empirical, acquired

The philosophy of action is a strict determinism wearing a threefold distinction. My **intelligible character** is my will as thing-in-itself: a timeless, unchangeable act of willing outside the principle of sufficient reason, and therefore free in the only sense freedom can bear — it is what it is groundlessly. My **empirical character** is that same character unfolded in time, the fixed pattern of my dispositions, knowable only a posteriori from my deeds, as a plant's nature is known from its growth. Given the empirical character, every act follows with necessity from the motives present: a person can do what he wills, but cannot *will* what he wills — the prize essay *On the Freedom of the Will* (1839) builds the whole case on *operari sequitur esse*, doing follows being. What life adds is an **acquired character**: not a change in what I am, but accurate knowledge of it — learning from experience what I actually want and can do, so that my empirical character expresses itself consistently, without the waste and self-deception of pursuing ends my nature cannot sustain. Moral responsibility survives determinism by relocating: we feel responsible not because our acts were uncaused, but because they reveal *what we are*, and the guilt conscience registers is, at bottom, guilt at existing as this character at all.

## Suffering, boredom, and the pendulum

Because the Will is striving without a final goal, its phenomenal life has a fixed affective structure (Vol. I, §57). All willing springs from need and deficiency, hence from suffering; satisfaction is merely the removal of a pain, negative rather than positive, and it is brief; then either a new want presents itself, or, if none does, the terrible emptiness of **boredom** — which for Schopenhauer is not a trivial mood but direct evidence that existence has no intrinsic value, since a life secured against want must still be filled with distractions to be bearable. "Life swings like a pendulum backward and forward between pain and boredom." Pleasure, on this analysis, is never a positive good added to existence; it is a temporary zero on a scale that runs only negative. Note what the pessimism is and is not: it is a conclusion about the structure of willing, offered with an argument ([04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), Argument 5), not a report of the author's mood — he cheerfully notes that a pessimist may still enjoy his dinner.

## Aesthetics: the pure subject, genius, and music

Book 3 describes the first and most accessible exit. In ordinary perception the intellect serves the will: we see things in their relations to our purposes — as useful, threatening, edible, ownable. In **aesthetic contemplation** this service is suspended. Perception fills consciousness entirely, we forget our individuality and its wants, and we become the **pure subject of knowing**, a clear mirror of the object, perceiving not the individual thing but the *Idea* in it. Liberation is temporary but real: "we celebrate the Sabbath of the penal servitude of willing" (Vol. I, §38). The **genius** is the person in whom this capacity is abnormal and sustained — an excess of intellect beyond what the will needs for survival, turned loose on objective perception; hence the genius's impracticality, and the kinship Schopenhauer (following a long tradition) finds between genius and madness.

The arts arrange themselves by the grade of the Idea they present, from architecture, which exhibits the lowest grades (gravity, rigidity, the conflict of load and support made visible), through landscape gardening and painting, to sculpture and painting of the human form, and finally poetry, whose material is concepts and whose subject is the Idea of humanity itself. Tragedy is the summit of poetic art because it shows the Will at war with itself — the terrible side of life — and its effect is not cathartic pleasure but a quieting of the will in the spectator. **Music stands outside the hierarchy entirely** (Vol. I, §52): it copies no Ideas but is "a copy of the Will itself," expressing not this or that joy or sorrow but joy and sorrow themselves, the inner nature of the world in a universal language. This is why music moves us more immediately than any other art — and why Schopenhauer's aesthetics, detached from the metaphysics by Wagner and others, has had a life of its own.

## Ethics: egoism, justice, loving-kindness, compassion

Book 4's ethics begins from an observation about incentives: every human action springs from one of three — **egoism** (willing one's own well-being), **malice** (willing another's woe), or **compassion**, *Mitleid* (willing another's well-being as one's own). The treatise *On the Basis of Morality* (1840) argues at length that only compassion has moral worth, against Kant, for whom compassion is mere inclination and duty must supply the motive. Schopenhauer's counter is metaphysical, not merely sentimental: the compassionate person *makes less distinction than others between himself and others*, because he sees — obscurely in feeling, clearly in philosophy — that individuation is appearance and the sufferer before him is the same Will as himself. *Tat tvam asi*, "this art thou," is the doctrine's formula borrowed from the Upanishads. Compassion issues in two virtues: **justice**, its negative form, which refrains from harming (injuring another is invading the sphere of a will equal to one's own), and **loving-kindness** or charity, its positive form, which actively helps. Kant's categorical imperative he dismisses as egoism in disguise — a covert appeal to reciprocity and fear of consequences — and theological ethics as obedience to a stronger power. Morality so grounded cannot command; it can only describe the one incentive that dissolves the illusion on which egoism rests.

## Denial of the will, and the nothing

The system's final movement turns on a distinction between *knowing about* the Will and being *quieted* by that knowledge. When insight into the unity of the Will and the universality of suffering becomes complete and constant in an individual, knowledge becomes a "quieter of the will": the motives that once moved him lose their power, the will turns against itself, and what appears is the **denial of the will-to-live** — chastity, poverty, fasting, self-mortification, the whole phenomenon of ascetic holiness that Schopenhauer finds, remarkably, at the core of Christianity rightly understood, of Hinduism, and of Buddhism alike. This is not suicide, which he analyzes as the opposite: the suicide wills life and rejects only its pains, destroying one phenomenon while affirming the Will as heartily as ever (Vol. I, §69). Denial, by contrast, is the Will's own free act of self-abolition in a single phenomenon — possible because the Will as thing-in-itself is free, even though every phenomenal act is necessary. The last section (Vol. I, §71) faces the reader with the consequence: to those still full of the will, what remains after its complete abolition is "nothing" — but conversely, to those in whom the will has turned, "this very real world of ours with all its suns and galaxies, is — nothing." The system ends not with a doctrine but with a gesture toward a state philosophy can point to and cannot describe: the peace of the saints, higher than all reason.

## Self-placement: between Kant and the Upanishads

Schopenhauer placed himself with unusual explicitness. Kant is the foundation — the distinction of appearance and thing-in-itself, the a priori forms — corrected where Kant contradicted himself, and combined with Plato, whose Ideas supply the grades of the Will's appearance. From the Upanishads, which he read nightly in Anquetil Duperron's Latin *Oupnek'hat* and called the consolation of his life and death, and from Buddhism, known to him through the scholarship of his day, come the doctrines he recognized as his own conclusions reached independently: the illusoriness of individuation (*maya*), the identity of self and other (*tat tvam asi*), suffering as the essence of *samsara*, and release through the extinction of craving. Buddhism he judged the finest of religions precisely because it is, on his reading, atheistic and pessimistic like his own philosophy. Against his contemporaries his placement was war: Fichte, Schelling, and above all Hegel he treated not as mistaken colleagues but as charlatans who had corrupted German philosophy for pay — a polemic so violent that it cost him his audience for thirty years and still colors his reception. Modern readers are not required to share the verdict to see the real one it conceals: after Kant, he thought, philosophy could go forward to the Will, or backward into academic system-building; he went, as he saw it, forward and alone.

## Known tensions in the system

The template of this dossier asks for the strains to be named, since they drive the scholarship (see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)). Four recur: (1) **The Kantian limit.** If all knowledge is representation under the principle of sufficient reason, by what right does philosophy speak of the thing-in-itself at all — even to name it "Will"? (2) **The one and the many.** If individuation is mere appearance, whose denial of the will is salvation? The system's ethics needs individuals; its metaphysics dissolves them. (3) **Freedom and necessity.** A timeless, unchangeable intelligible character that is nonetheless *free*, and for which I am responsible, stretches both concepts. (4) **The goal that is not a goal.** Denial is the Will's self-abolition — but a Will that can abolish itself seems neither blind nor aimless in the required sense, and a "nothing" that is also the highest good strains the pessimism that leads to it. Schopenhauer had replies to each; whether they suffice is the live question of the literature.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), but restricted to Schopenhauer's own usage, with later textbook senses flagged where they diverge.

- **Vorstellung (representation):** the world as object for a subject, structured by space, time, and causality. Not a mental picture or idea in the mind — the textbook sense of "representation" as inner copy is exactly wrong here; the perceived physical world itself is representation.
- **Wille (Will):** the thing-in-itself: one, timeless, groundless striving of which all phenomena are objectifications. Capitalized in translation to mark that it is not personal volition, willpower, or a cosmic person.
- **Will-to-live (Wille zum Leben):** the Will as it manifests in living beings: the affirmation of life, self-preservation, and reproduction. A later textbook shorthand sometimes uses it as a synonym for the Will itself; Schopenhauer's Will is broader, appearing also in inorganic forces.
- **Principle of sufficient reason (Satz vom zureichenden Grunde):** the principle that nothing is without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise — in four irreducible forms (becoming, knowing, being, acting). Valid only within representation.
- **Principium individuationis:** space and time, named as the principle by which the one Will appears as a plurality of separate individuals. The Latin phrase is scholastic; Schopenhauer repurposes it.
- **Objectification (Objektivation):** the Will's becoming object, i.e. visible in representation, at definite grades. Not "expression" in a psychological sense: the phenomenon does not symbolize the Will, it *is* the Will as appearance.
- **Idea (Idee):** a grade of the Will's adequate objectification, timeless and outside individuation, perceived (not conceived) in aesthetic contemplation. Schopenhauer's explicit borrowing from Plato — not a concept and not a thought in anyone's head.
- **Pure subject of knowing:** the subject in aesthetic contemplation, freed from willing and individuality, functioning as the clear mirror of the Idea. A temporary state, not a permanent self.
- **Genius:** the sustained capacity for pure perception through an abnormal surplus of intellect over the needs of the will. Not high IQ or talent in general — textbook usage flattens the definition past recognition.
- **Intelligible character:** an individual's will as thing-in-itself: timeless, unchangeable, groundless, the free act of which the whole life is the unfolding. The term is Kant's; the relocation of freedom into it is Schopenhauer's development.
- **Empirical character:** the intelligible character as it appears in time — the person's fixed pattern of dispositions, knowable only from their deeds.
- **Acquired character:** accurate self-knowledge of one's empirical character, won from experience, which lets the fixed character express itself consistently. Not self-improvement: nothing in the character changes.
- **Motivation (Motiv):** causality seen from within — the form the principle of sufficient reason takes for actions, where the cause is a representation acting on a character.
- **Mitleid (compassion):** the immediate participation in another's suffering, in which the barrier between I and not-I is experientially thinned; for Schopenhauer the sole incentive with genuine moral worth and the source of justice and loving-kindness. "Pity" is a misleading translation when it suggests condescension from above.
- **Egoism:** the default incentive of all willing beings: willing one's own well-being, treating one's own existence as absolute and others' as instrumental or null.
- **Denial of the will-to-live (Verneinung des Willens zum Leben):** the Will's turning against itself in a phenomenon, when complete knowledge of its nature quiets all willing; manifests as asceticism and holiness. The opposite of suicide, which affirms the Will while destroying one phenomenon.
- **Affirmation of the will-to-live (Bejahung):** the ordinary condition: the Will willing itself in an individual who maintains and reproduces life, knowing himself in all beings yet, through individuation, failing to recognize it.
- **Eternal justice:** the doctrine that the world's suffering is, metaphysically, the Will tormenting itself — tormentor and tormented being one — so that justice is done not after death but in the structure of existence itself.
- **The nothing (das Nichts):** the closing concept of the system: what the world is to the one in whom the will has turned, and what his state is to those still willing — a relative, not absolute, nothing.
- **Maya:** borrowed from Vedantic usage for the veil of individuation — the world of separate, striving individuals as illusion-like appearance. Schopenhauer uses the Sanskrit term deliberately and often.
