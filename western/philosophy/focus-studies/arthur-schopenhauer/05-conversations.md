# Schopenhauer in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: Arthur Schopenhauer · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/arthur-schopenhauer.md)*

Schopenhauer presented himself as a solitary: one true master (Kant), two kindred spirits (Plato and the sages of India), and a corrupt university establishment to be ignored. The actual record is richer and more interesting. He argued with the dead constantly, lived inside the literary world of Weimar and Frankfurt, acquired disciples who annoyed and sustained him, and — after decades of silence — became one of the most answered philosophers of the modern era. This file traces those conversations in three directions.

## Backward: the inheritance he claimed

### Kant: the master he corrected

Schopenhauer insisted, to anyone who would listen, that he was [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)'s only legitimate heir — and that the inheritance required demolishing half the estate. What he keeps is the critical framework itself: the distinction between the world as it appears to us (representation, structured by the forms of space, time, and causality) and the world as it is in itself. He called this distinction Kant's immortal achievement, and the whole of his own system is built inside it.

What he discards is nearly everything Kant built on that foundation. The twelve categories of the understanding collapse to one — causality — the rest dismissed in a famous jibe as so many "blind windows" in the building (the attack fills the appendix to *The World as Will and Representation*, Vol. I: "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy"). Kant's thing-in-itself, officially unknowable, is identified through the body's double knowledge as Will. Kant's moral theology — God, freedom, and immortality as postulates of practical reason — is thrown out as theology smuggled back into philosophy. And the categorical imperative gets a book-length demolition in *On the Basis of Morality* (1840): duty, Schopenhauer argues, is a theological concept in disguise, and its real psychological root is egoism wearing a uniform. In its place he puts compassion. Modern Kant scholarship does not accept his reading as the last word — his Kant is selective, and deliberately so — but no subsequent Kantian has been able to ignore the charge that the thing-in-itself is the crack in the critical system. That crack was first pointed out by his teacher Schulze (below), and Schopenhauer's whole career can be read as an attempt to walk through it.

### Plato: the Ideas, put to work

From [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) he takes the Ideas — but demotes them. In Plato the Forms stand above the sensible world as its eternal originals. In Schopenhauer they become the *grades of the Will's objectification*: the fixed levels (species, natural forces, archetypes) at which the one Will steps into visibility. Their philosophical work changes accordingly. Because the Ideas can be perceived when the mind stops serving desire, they become the objects of art in Book 3 of the masterwork: the artist is the person who can look at an Idea without wanting anything from it. This is Plato read through Kant and put to a use Plato never intended — a fact Schopenhauer, who ranked Plato with Kant as his two great predecessors, would have cheerfully admitted. Scholars regard his Plato as brilliant and highly selective: the dialectic, the theory of participation, and the political philosophy are all quietly set aside.

### India: the Upanishads and Buddhism

In Weimar in 1813–14 the orientalist Friedrich Majer introduced him to the *Oupnek'hat* — the Upanishads in a Latin translation (1801) of a Persian translation of the Sanskrit. He read it, he said, every night before sleep, and called it in *Parerga* "the consolation of my life," expecting it to be "the consolation of my death." Buddhism he absorbed more gradually, from journals and the scholarship available in the 1820s–40s, and the second volume of 1844 shows a much fuller engagement than the first.

How much did India *form* the philosophy? The standard scholarly verdict — with which Schopenhauer himself agreed — is that it confirmed more than it formed. The core of the system (representation, Will, suffering, denial) was worked out in the Dresden years 1814–18, when his knowledge of Buddhism was still thin and largely secondhand; he read no Sanskrit and worked entirely from translations. What he found in the Upanishads and later in Buddhist sources was an independent civilization-scale corroboration: suffering as the default, individuation as illusion (*maya*), release through the stilling of craving. The affinities are real but partial. The Will has no Buddhist counterpart — Buddhism denies precisely the permanent inner essence his metaphysics asserts — and his closest textual kinship is with Vedantic themes (*tat tvam asi*, "thou art that," which he quotes as the metaphysical ground of compassion) rather than with Buddhist doctrine proper. "Schopenhauer the Western Buddhist" is a reception myth; "Schopenhauer the first major European philosopher to think *with* Indian texts" is the record.

### Goethe: alliance and rupture

Through his mother Johanna's Weimar salon he met Goethe in 1813, and the great man — impressed, and happy to recruit a defender — handed the twenty-five-year-old his optical experiments and the cause of the *Theory of Colors* against Newton. Schopenhauer's *On Vision and Colors* (1816) defended Goethe but rebuilt the theory around the physiology of the retina: color as the eye's own activity, not a phenomenon in the light. Goethe wanted a disciple and got a systematizer with his own agenda; the correspondence cooled, and by the end of the decade the alliance was over. The episode matters beyond optics: it was Schopenhauer's one experience of being taken seriously by a figure he revered, and its failure rehearsed the pattern of his Berlin years — he would rather be right alone than second in anyone's school, including his heroes'.

### The post-Kantian scene: Schulze, Fichte, Schelling, Hegel

His Göttingen teacher G. E. Schulze, author of the skeptical *Aenesidemus* (1792), advised the young Schopenhauer to concentrate on Plato and Kant — advice he followed for life — and posed the problem Schopenhauer claimed to solve: if causality is only a form of our knowledge, Kant has no right to let the thing-in-itself "cause" our representations. The Berlin lectures of [Fichte](../../eras/04-19th-century/johann-gottlieb-fichte.md), heard in 1811–12, began in respect and ended in the contempt recorded in his notebooks. [Schelling](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-schelling.md) he knew only from print. Together with [Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) the two were lumped, in the essay "On University Philosophy" (*Parerga*, Vol. I), as the three sophists of the professorial trade: obscurity as a business model, systems built to flatter state and church, philosophy traded for salary and fame. The polemic should be taken seriously but not at face value. Schopenhauer never engaged the *Phenomenology* or the *Logic* on their own terms; his Hegel is a caricature of the Absolute as a word-machine, assembled from lecture-room mannerisms and the system's public face. The library's [Hegel entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md) gives the actual philosophy. What the feud genuinely documents is an institutional fact: between the salaried system-builders and the private scholar living on capital, early nineteenth-century German philosophy had two incompatible economies of truth, and Schopenhauer chose — and romanticized — his side of it.

## Sideways: the world he actually lived in

**Johanna's salon.** His mother Johanna, a bestselling novelist, ran one of Weimar's famous salons; Goethe was a regular. The salon gave the young Arthur his Goethe connection, his introduction to Majer and the Upanishads, and eventually his bitterest family quarrel — mother and son broke in 1814 and never reconciled. But the salon matters for the dossier because it corrects the hermit legend at its root: Schopenhauer was formed inside literary society, and in old age he recreated a version of it around his Frankfurt dinner table.

**The "apostles."** Fame brought disciples he half welcomed and half mocked. Julius Frauenstädt — his chief apostle, indefatigable promoter, and posthumous editor — systematized, corresponded, and defended; Ernst Otto Lindner of the *Vossische Zeitung* carried the cause into journalism; the younger Julius Bahnsen took the pessimism and radicalized it (below). Schopenhauer received them, corrected them, and complained about them in roughly equal measure.

**Wagner: the conversion.** In the autumn of 1854 Richard Wagner, tipped off by the poet Georg Herwegh, read *The World as Will and Representation* — by his own account four times within a year — and in December 1854 sent its author the printed poem of the *Ring* cycle with a letter of reverence. Schopenhauer annotated the poem, was cool on the verse, and is reported to have judged that Wagner's gift was for music rather than poetry. They never met. But the younger composer absorbed the philosophy whole: *Tristan und Isolde* (premiered 1865), with its metaphysics of longing and its night-world of release from individuation, is the most Schopenhauerian artwork of the century, and Wagner's later essays carried the philosopher's name into every musical capital in Europe. Wagner has no entry of his own in this library; his Schopenhauer chapter is told here and in the [Nietzsche entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md).

## Forward: the answers

### Nietzsche: the deepest answerer

[Nietzsche](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md) found a secondhand *World as Will and Representation* in a Leipzig bookshop in 1865 and underwent the century's most consequential philosophical conversion. *Schopenhauer as Educator* (1874) made the older man the model of the honest philosopher against the university herd. The repudiation, when it came in the 1880s, was total at the level of doctrine: the Will-to-live reinterpreted as will to power, compassion indicted as weakness, pessimism diagnosed in the *Genealogy* (III) as the ascetic ideal's most honest form — honesty in the service of declining life. Yet what survives the break is nearly as much: the genealogical suspicion of morality, the primacy of drives and the body, the seriousness about art, the prose style, and the conviction that philosophy is a confession. Nietzsche is the "Answered by" line's whole argument in one figure: Schopenhauer's most famous reader refuted him by becoming his heir.

### Wittgenstein: the early reader

The [early Wittgenstein](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md) read Schopenhauer young, and the *Tractatus* carries the accent: the world as *my* world, the metaphysical subject as a limit of the world rather than an item in it, death as not an event in life, ethics and aesthetics drawn outside the realm of facts. Commentators have traced these to the Schopenhauerian template for a century; the exact balance of direct borrowing and shared Kantian inheritance remains debated, but the notebooks and testimony leave the familiarity itself in no doubt.

### Freud: the acknowledged precursor

Freud — who has no entry in this library — said on the record that Schopenhauer got there first: the "unconscious Will," he wrote in the *Introductory Lectures* (1916–17), is equivalent to the mental instincts of psychoanalysis, and the *Autobiographical Study* (1925) acknowledged the broad coincidence, down to repression and the dominance of sexuality, while insisting he had read Schopenhauer only late in life. The passage always cited is *The World as Will and Representation*, Vol. II, ch. 19, where the intellect refuses admission to knowledge that would pain the will — a description of repression in everything but name. Scholars remain divided on whether the convergence was wholly independent; the anticipation itself is not disputed, and Schopenhauer's defenders can fairly note that he, unlike Freud, built the drives into a metaphysics and an ethics.

### The literary line

Tolstoy wrote to a friend in 1869, in the middle of *War and Peace*'s aftermath, that Schopenhauer was "the most brilliant of men," and the late Tolstoy's renunciations are unthinkable without him. Thomas Mann put the philosophy on stage: Thomas Buddenbrook, in *Buddenbrooks* (1901), reads the chapter "On Death" from the master's second volume and is consoled by the doctrine that the inner nature is indestructible; Mann's 1938 essay kept the flame in the darkest decade. Proust's aesthetics of music and involuntary memory show affinities that scholars have long noted, though the degree of direct influence is debated. Beckett read him closely in the 1930s and never stopped: the tramps waiting out an existence structured by unfulfilled wanting are Schopenhauer's pendulum staged. None of these writers has a library entry; their readings are part of his reception, taken up again in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

### The pessimist school

Two heirs tried to keep the doctrine and outrun the master. Philipp Mainländer's *Philosophy of Redemption* (1876) inverted the Will-to-live into a cosmic will-to-death — God dead, the universe his slowly decaying corpse, redemption in extinction; Mainländer took his own life the night after the first printed copies of the book reached him. Julius Bahnsen's "Realdialektik" located contradiction in the will itself, denying even the master's exit routes. Neither founded a lasting school, but both show how live an option radical pessimism briefly was.

### The living presence

Two parts of the system thrive today independently of its metaphysics. His aesthetics of music — music as a direct copy of the Will rather than a representation of the world — remains the position that philosophy of music defines itself against or through. And his ethics reached the present by a second door: compassion, for Schopenhauer, extends across the species line, and *On the Basis of Morality* argues that genuine goodness of character shows itself precisely in how a person treats animals. [Peter Singer](../../eras/05-20th-century/peter-singer.md) has cited him as a rare predecessor among major philosophers on animal suffering; contemporary animal ethics, whatever its utilitarian machinery, works in a room Schopenhauer helped build. His writings on compassion likewise feed current debates in moral psychology about whether fellow-feeling or reason is morality's engine.

## Schopenhauer in the nine problems

The library's [themes index](../../themes.md) places him in four of its nine threads; his positions reach into most of the rest.

- **§1 [Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it)** — The world as representation obeys the principle of sufficient reason throughout; the one escape from it is the body's inner knowledge, which reveals the Will.
- **§2 [Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body)** — The body known twice: as representation among objects, and from within as Will; the body *is* the Will made visible, and the intellect its late, tired servant.
- **§4 [Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right)** — Compassion, suffering another's suffering as one's own, is the sole genuine moral incentive; egoism and malice are its failures; beyond morality stands the denial of the Will.
- **§6 [Self & Identity](../../themes.md#6-self--identity--what-am-i-and-what-makes-me-the-same-person-over-time)** — Individuation is a veil (*maya*): in truth one Will appears as many separate selves, and compassion is the seeing-through of the division.

Beyond the index's four placements: on **§3 God & Religion** he is an atheist who respected religion as the "metaphysics of the people" — truth in allegorical dress for those who cannot take it straight. On **§5 Politics** he is nearly silent by design: the state is egoism's hired curb, nothing more. On **§7 Language** he treats concepts as representations of representations — useful copies, always at one remove from perception. On **§8 Science** he confines natural science to the world of representation and reads its forces as the Will's lower grades. On **§9 History** he is Hegel's antipode: history has no plot, no progress, and no world-spirit — only the same Will wearing new costumes, which is why the one honest response to it is not hope but clear sight.
