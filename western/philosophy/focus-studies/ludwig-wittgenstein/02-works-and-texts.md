# Works and Texts — Ludwig Wittgenstein

*Dossier file 02. Every work that matters, in the order this dossier reads them, with editions. The library's [Investigations guide](../../study/wittgenstein-investigations.md) remains the companion for the reading weeks on that book.*

## The corpus, and two citation rules

Wittgenstein published exactly one book of philosophy in his lifetime — the *Tractatus*, some seventy-five pages — and left behind one of the largest unpublished estates in modern philosophy: manuscripts, typescripts, and dictations amounting to some twenty thousand pages, from which editors have been drawing volumes since 1953. His bibliography therefore splits into three strata, and this dossier never lets them blur: the book he published; the book he prepared for publication and saw into near-final form (the *Investigations*, Part I); and the *Nachlass*, everything else, which he did not publish and in most cases did not prepare for publication. The strata matter to every claim made from his texts, and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) treats the editorial question in full.

Two citation conventions govern everything, and the dossier follows the library's:

1. **The *Investigations* is cited by section (§), never by page.** Part I consists of 693 numbered remarks, and the numbering is stable across editions and languages in a way pagination is not. Part II is cited by its own sections (the aspect-seeing discussion is conventionally "Part II, section xi"; in the fourth edition the whole of Part II appears under the title *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*).
2. **The *Tractatus* is cited by its decimal proposition numbers** (1, 1.1, 2.014, 6.54, 7), which mark the remark's logical weight in the book's structure, not paragraph order. The main propositions are seven; nearly everything else is commentary hanging from them.

## The works, in dossier order

### 1. *Notebooks 1914–1916* and the *Prototractatus* — the workshop of the *Tractatus*

**Dates and contents.** The wartime notebooks — three surviving volumes, kept from August 1914 to January 1917, the logical entries in plain script and the personal ones in a simple code — are the quarry from which the *Tractatus* was cut. Published posthumously in 1961 (edited by G. H. von Wright and G. E. M. Anscombe, with Anscombe's translation), they show what the finished book suppresses: the ethics, the religion, the fear of death, growing alongside the logic day by day. The *Prototractatus* is an early complete version of the *Tractatus* itself, assembled in 1917–18 before the final revision; recovered among Russell's papers and published in 1971 (Routledge, edited by B. F. McGuinness, T. Nyberg, and G. H. von Wright, in Pears and McGuinness's translation), it lets scholars watch the numbering system and the remarks on ethics take their final shape.

**How the dossier reads them.** The *Notebooks* in selections, alongside the *Tractatus* — they are the single strongest evidence that the book's famous silence about ethics was a deliberate boundary and not an absence of interest. The *Prototractatus* is a reference text, not a reading assignment: consult it where the *Tractatus*'s compressed wording is in dispute. **Editions:** the *Notebooks* in the Harper/Blackwell volume (Anscombe translation), which prints the German with the English. **Difficulty:** the *Notebooks* are easy to read and hard to weigh — private jottings, not doctrine.

### 2. *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (first published 1921; English edition 1922)

**Publication history, in the house style, because here it matters.** The German text first appeared in 1921 in Wilhelm Ostwald's journal *Annalen der Naturphilosophie* under its German title, *Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung* — so the book is "first published 1921" even where a title page or catalogue gives the journal volume's date otherwise. The book edition followed in 1922 from Kegan Paul in London, bilingual, in C. K. Ogden's English translation (prepared with the help of the young Frank Ramsey), with an introduction by Russell and the Latin title — suggested by G. E. Moore, echoing Spinoza's *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* — under which the world knows it. Wittgenstein disliked Russell's introduction, thinking it misunderstood the book's ethical point (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)), and the dossier repeats the library's warning: read the introduction, if at all, as Russell's view, not as an authorized summary.

**What it is.** Seven main propositions with decimal sub-remarks: the world as the totality of facts; the picture theory of the proposition; logical form as what shows itself and cannot be said; philosophy as the activity of clarification (4.112); the self as a limit of the world; ethics, aesthetics, and the mystical as lying outside the sayable; and the ladder of 6.54 — the book's own propositions to be thrown away once climbed — before the closing silence of 7.

**Editions — the dossier's ruling, consistent with the learning path.** Read it in **D. F. Pears and B. F. McGuinness's translation (Routledge, 1961)**, the path's default for solo study: it is more accurate and more literal than its predecessor, and its wording is the scholarly standard. The **Ogden/Ramsey translation (1922)** is the *historical* text — the version the Vienna Circle and the first English readers actually read — and a few of its renderings (including the famous final sentence) are the ones embedded in the culture; consult it comparatively, do not study from it. The bilingual Routledge volume prints the German en face, which matters for a book whose every word was weighed. **Difficulty:** seventy-five pages and among the densest in philosophy — read a few remarks at a time, with a commentary open (the plan names them).

**The von Ficker letter belongs with this book.** In November 1919 Wittgenstein wrote to the prospective publisher Ludwig von Ficker explaining the book he could not get printed. The letter — quoted short here, as this dossier quotes copyrighted and translated material throughout, paraphrase-first — states that the book's point is an ethical one and that his work consisted of two parts: the part written in the book, and all that he had *not* written; "and it is precisely this second part that is the important one." No interpretation of the *Tractatus* is adequate that cannot accommodate that sentence.

### 3. The transitional writings (1929–1933) — the turn, documented

**"Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929)** is Wittgenstein's only philosophical paper published in his lifetime after the *Tractatus* — prepared for the Aristotelian Society's supplementary volume, and then, characteristically, not read at the meeting: he set it aside and spoke instead about infinity. In it he concedes, in print, that the analysis of color statements breaks the *Tractatus*'s requirement that elementary propositions be logically independent — the color-exclusion problem doing its work. ***Philosophical Remarks*** (*Philosophische Bemerkungen*), written 1929–30 and distilled from the first Cambridge manuscripts, was published posthumously in 1964 (edited by Rush Rhees; English translation 1975). It is the fullest record of the philosophy in mid-turn: verification, meaning, and mathematics being rebuilt while the old vocabulary is still in use. Behind these texts stands the **"Big Typescript"** of 1933 (TS 213), his first attempt to organize the new material into a book — published in 2005 in the original German with an English translation — and the dictations to Friedrich Waismann, through which the Vienna Circle hoped to publish an authorized exposition of his thought and which he eventually abandoned.

**How the dossier reads them.** Selections: the 1929 paper whole (it is short), and the opening parts of *Philosophical Remarks*. Their function in the plan is forensic: these are the documents in which you can watch the picture theory being given up, premise by premise, by the only person entitled to give it up. **Difficulty:** high and technical; excerpt, do not march.

### 4. The Blue and Brown Books (dictated 1933–35; published 1958)

**Status first, because it governs their use.** These are not books Wittgenstein wrote for publication. The *Blue Book* was dictated to his Cambridge class in 1933–34, the *Brown Book* to two students, Alice Ambrose and Francis Skinner, in 1934–35, as preliminary studies toward the book that became the *Investigations*; mimeographed copies circulated among students for two decades (the *Blue Book* was famous in Cambridge before it was in print), and Rush Rhees published both in 1958 as *Preliminary Studies for the "Philosophical Investigations"*. Their authority is that of lecture notes he authorized for circulation — a half-step above the raw *Nachlass*, a half-step below a book.

**Contents and use.** Here the new philosophy first appears in sustained form: the attack on the craving for generality, meaning as use, language-games, family resemblance in embryo. The *Blue Book*'s opening is the gentlest entry into the late work that exists, and the library's late entry recommends it as such. The dossier assigns the *Blue Book*'s opening sections as the bridge between the transitional writings and the *Investigations*. **Edition:** the Harper/Blackwell volume (*The Blue and Brown Books*). **Difficulty:** the lowest in the corpus — deceptively so, as with everything he wrote.

### 5. *Philosophical Investigations* (composed 1936–49; published posthumously 1953)

**Composition and status.** Wittgenstein began sustained work on the book at Skjolden in 1936–37 and revised it through the war years. Part I — 693 numbered remarks, an "album" of sketches in his preface's own description, dated January 1945 — was essentially complete by 1946; he tried to publish it together with the *Tractatus*, withdrew it, and continued revising. Part II is later material (1946–49) on the philosophy of psychology — aspect-seeing, meaning-blindness, the inner and the outer — assembled by the editors from his typescripts. The book appeared in 1953 from Macmillan, edited by G. E. M. Anscombe and Rush Rhees and translated by Anscombe: two years after his death, the second founding text of analytic philosophy arrived as a posthumous edition, and that fact shapes its reception (see [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)).

**Editions — the dossier's ruling, consistent with the learning path and the library's guide.** The current standard is the **fourth edition (Wiley-Blackwell, 2009): Anscombe's translation revised by P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim Schulte**, bilingual, German and English on facing pages. Two changes in it matter. First, the translation is revised throughout — lightly in most remarks, decisively in a few — so quotations should be taken from this edition. Second, Part II is retitled ***Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment***, which signals its lesser, unfinished status honestly; cite it as such. The older **third edition (Anscombe's translation unrevised)** remains entirely usable for a first reading and is the text most older commentary quotes — but check any wording you intend to quote against the fourth edition. **Difficulty:** no single remark is technically hard, and the cumulative effect is very hard; the library's guide, which the dossier uses as its session companion, prescribes the remedy — budget for rereading, not coverage.

### 6. The posthumous shelf — *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*, *Zettel*, *On Certainty*, *Culture and Value*

Four editorial constructions from the *Nachlass*, each named here with its status so that nothing in the dossier cites them as more than they are. ***Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*** (first published 1956; revised edition 1978, edited by von Wright, Rhees, and Anscombe) gathers his writings on logic and mathematics from 1937–44 — his most controversial material, in which proof, rule-following, and the hardness of the logical "must" are re-examined as human practices. ***Zettel*** (1967, edited by Anscombe and von Wright) is a box of clippings — remarks he cut from typescripts of the 1930s and 40s and filed, some of them near-duplicates of *Investigations* material in earlier drafts; miscellaneous, and treasurable exactly as scrapbook. ***On Certainty*** (1969, edited by Anscombe and von Wright) collects the notebooks of his last eighteen months, prompted by G. E. Moore's defenses of common sense: doubt, certainty, and the "hinge" propositions on which questioning itself turns — written up to two days before his death, and the closest thing the late work has to a final statement. ***Culture and Value*** (German edition 1977, edited by von Wright; English 1980, translated by Peter Winch) selects his remarks on art, religion, music, and his own culture across the whole career — the indispensable source for the man, unusable as doctrine.

**How the dossier reads them.** *On Certainty* in selections, as the plan's exit text. The other three are reference and enrichment, sampled where the conversations and reception files direct. **Difficulty:** varies by volume; the *status* difficulty is constant — all four are selections made by editors from material he did not prepare for print, and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) explains what weight that can bear.

## The *Nachlass* problem — a standing caution

Wittgenstein's unpublished estate — manuscripts, typescripts, dictations, some twenty thousand pages, catalogued by G. H. von Wright and now available in full as *Wittgenstein's Nachlass: The Bergen Electronic Edition* (Oxford University Press, 2000) — is primary evidence about his thinking and no kind of book at all. Every posthumous volume above exists because editors chose, ordered, and titled; the choices differ, and scholars dispute them, in particular whether the typescripts he revised repeatedly should outweigh notebooks never prepared for anyone's eyes. What a beginner should do: read the two books and the Blue Book, in the editions ruled above, and let the dossier and the guides cite the *Nachlass* for you where context requires. What a beginner should not do: quote a notebook remark as "Wittgenstein's view" without its date and its unpublished status, or assume that because a selection is beautifully printed it was ever meant by its author to stand as it stands.
