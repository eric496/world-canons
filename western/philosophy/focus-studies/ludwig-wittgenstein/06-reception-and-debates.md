# Reception and Debates — The Two Books and the Industry Between Them

*Focus study: Ludwig Wittgenstein · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entries: [Wittgenstein, Early](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md) · [Wittgenstein, Late](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md)*

Wittgenstein is the rare philosopher whose reception history is split cleanly in two by his own hand: a short book the world received in his lifetime as the charter of a movement he disowned, and a long book the world received after his death as the charter of a movement he never founded. Between the two stands a publishing industry built on twenty thousand unpublished pages. This file traces the succession, then stages the live debates as debates, and closes with the standard misconceptions in full form.

## Reception by era

### The Tractatus's first fame: the Circle's Wittgenstein (1920s)

The *Tractatus* (German 1921; English 1922, in Ogden's translation with Russell's introduction) made its author famous in a way he neither sought nor controlled. In Vienna, Schlick's Circle read it aloud, line by line, across 1924–26 and treated it as the foundation document of logical empiricism; in Cambridge, Ramsey's review and translation work made it required reading for the small world of analytic philosophy. The German original had appeared in Wilhelm Ostwald's *Annalen der Naturphilosophie* (1921) — a journal of scientific philosophy rather than a literary venue, which already skewed the first readership toward the logical and away from the ethical. The author himself contributed to the distortion by absence: having concluded that the book had solved the problems of philosophy, he declined to defend, explain, or revise it in public, leaving the field to readers who valued it for reasons inverse to his own. When he finally began meeting Schlick and Waismann in 1927, the conversations recorded a man repudiating, piece by piece, positions his admirers were treating as his settled doctrine — a lag between the living philosopher and his reception that never fully closed in his lifetime. The fame was of a peculiar kind: the book was admired as much for its aphoristic architecture and closing silence as for its arguments, and its author — by then an Austrian village schoolteacher who had given away his fortune — refused the role of movement leader. He declined invitations, met Circle members only selectively, and made clear that the empiricist construction placed on his book was not his. The first Wittgenstein the world knew was therefore already a reception artifact: the Circle's Wittgenstein, assembled from the propositions they could use and a silence they misread as permission.

### The return and the underground book (1929–1951)

When Wittgenstein resumed philosophy at Cambridge in 1929, he published almost nothing more — a single paper, "Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929), which he was already repudiating as he delivered it. What circulated instead were the traces of his teaching: students' lecture notes; the *Blue Book* (dictated 1933–34) and *Brown Book* (1934–35), typescripts passed hand to hand among students and correspondents; Waismann's records of the Vienna conversations. Through the 1930s and 1940s the "later Wittgenstein" existed as a Cambridge oral tradition with an international postal network — ideas known from dictations and reports years before any of them appeared in print, absorbed by visitors and re-exported, so that when the *Investigations* finally appeared in 1953 its doctrines arrived pre-digested for a profession that had been waiting two decades. That waiting shaped the reception: the book was read less as a new work than as the long-withheld source of a method many already practiced.

### The posthumous Investigations and ordinary-language philosophy (1950s–1960s)

The *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), edited by Anscombe and Rhees from the typescript Wittgenstein had completed by the mid-1940s, became one of the century's central philosophical books. At Oxford, J. L. Austin and [Gilbert Ryle](../../eras/05-20th-century/gilbert-ryle.md) led what was called ordinary-language philosophy — attention to the distinctions embodied in everyday speech as a solvent of philosophical pseudo-problems. The label needs care. Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* appeared in 1949, before the *Investigations* was published, and grew from his own program as much as from Wittgenstein's teaching; Austin's method of patient linguistic fieldwork was his own construction, and he kept a marked distance from discipleship. Their projects are cousins of Wittgenstein's, not copies: where he used invented language-games as objects of comparison in a therapy aimed at release from confusion, Oxford practice tended toward systematic description of usage — closer, some critics complained, to a new scholasticism than to his self-dissolving method. Wittgenstein's own reported coolness toward the professional philosophy industry applied in advance: he had advised his ablest students to leave the academy, and nothing in the record suggests he would have enrolled as the founder of a school.

The backlash was prompt. Ernest Gellner's *Words and Things* (1959), a polemic against the Oxford linguistic philosophy he took to be Wittgenstein's legacy, accused the movement of dressing deference to existing usage as method and of evading every substantive question by reclassifying it as confusion; Russell, who had come to dislike the later work intensely, lent the book his sympathy, and Oxford's refusal to treat the attack seriously became part of the scandal. Whether the charge fairly hits Wittgenstein himself — whose examples are invented rather than lexicographical, and whose target was the craving for theory rather than theory as such — remains contested; as reception history the episode is decisive anyway, because it marks the point at which "Wittgensteinian" became, outside a shrinking circle, a term opponents could use as a diagnosis. The later philosophy's center of gravity shifted in those decades toward Cambridge memories, American departments, and the Continental readers who had never signed up for Oxford practice in the first place.

### The Nachlass industry and the Bergen edition

Because the published corpus is so small relative to what he wrote, Wittgenstein studies became an editorial enterprise. The literary executors — Anscombe, Rhees, von Wright — published volume after volume selected from the *Nachlass*: *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* (1956), the *Blue and Brown Books* (1958), *Notebooks 1914–1916* (1961), *Zettel* (1967), *On Certainty* (1969), *Culture and Value* (1980), and more, each involving judgments about selection, ordering, and translation that specialists still contest. The precondition was von Wright's catalogue of the papers, which imposed a numbering system (manuscripts, typescripts, dictations) that remains the citation standard for unpublished material. The Wittgenstein Archives at the University of Bergen, established in 1990, then changed the evidentiary base for everyone: its *Wittgenstein's Nachlass: The Bergen Electronic Edition* (Oxford University Press, 2000) made the entire Nachlass — some 20,000 pages of manuscripts, typescripts, and dictations, roughly three million words — available in facsimile with diplomatic and normalized transcriptions. A parallel print enterprise, the Vienna Edition, edited the transitional writings of 1929–32 in critical form, giving the least-read period of the career its own textual basis for the first time. Scholarship since 2000 can check any published selection against the whole archive, and genetic questions — how a remark migrated between notebooks on its way into a typescript — became researchable in principle rather than guesswork. The industry cuts both ways, as the debates below show: the archive disciplines interpretation, and it also tempts it, since somewhere in twenty thousand pages almost any Wittgenstein can find a supporting remark.

## Live debates

**(a) Resolute versus traditional readings of the Tractatus.** The sharpest current fight about the early work concerns its ending. The *Tractatus*'s penultimate remark (6.54) instructs the reader who understands its author to recognize the book's propositions as nonsense and throw away the ladder after climbing it. **Traditional readings** — P. M. S. Hacker's is the most fully defended — hold that the book communicates genuine but unsayable truths: about logical form, about the relation of language and world, about the mystical; the nonsense is, so to speak, illuminating nonsense, gesturing at what shows itself. **Resolute readings**, named from Cora Diamond's essay "Throwing Away the Ladder" (in *The Realistic Spirit*, 1991) and developed by Diamond and James Conant, refuse the compliment: on this view, to imagine that the book's sentences convey ineffable truths is to fail to throw the ladder away — to keep, as Conant's wing puts it, one foot on it. The propositions are plain nonsense, like any other; the book's work is transitional and therapeutic, engineering a collapse of the illusion that there was a theory there at all. Intermediate positions exist (some readers distinguish kinds of nonsense or phases of reading), and the dispute drives real consequences: what the saying/showing distinction is, whether the early Wittgenstein held a metaphysics, and how continuous the two periods are. Hacker has pressed a characteristic objection against the resolute camp: a book of plain nonsense could not do the work its admirers claim for it — could not, in particular, have been assembled, argued, and progressively refined through the wartime notebooks with the cumulative rigor the manuscripts display; the resolute reply is that the rigor belongs to the illusion being engineered, since only a maximally seductive version of the picture theory can exhaust its attractions for the reader who must climb it. Nor is the dispute a novelty imposed on the text from outside: the earliest readers already split along a version of the fault line, with Russell's introduction treating the book as conveying a doctrine about the inexpressible while Ramsey doubted that the saying/showing machinery could bear the weight placed on it. The dossier stages this as the open question it is; the library's [early entry](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md) presents both readings in summary.

**(b) The Kripke rule-following industry and its critics.** Kripke's 1982 book ([05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)) precipitated a literature of its own: if no fact about a speaker's past behavior or inner state constitutes meaning *plus* rather than *quus*, then meaning is either skeptical prey or must be rebuilt on communal practice. Critics — Baker and Hacker's commentary is the systematic case, with McDowell and others pressing related lines — argue that the paradox depends on demands Wittgenstein rejected (that grasping a rule must be an interpretation; that justification must be underwritten by a fact of the demanded kind), and that §201's remark that "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation" is the dissolution of the paradox, not its statement. Defenders reply that Kripke made explicit a pressure genuinely present in §§185–242, whatever the master's intentions. One fault line inside the debate deserves its own notice, because it decides what the "sceptical solution" would even be. Kripke's community view makes correctness a matter of agreement with the responses of one's fellows; a long line of objectors — including readers sympathetic to the skeptical paradox — have asked what then becomes of the solitary speaker, the Crusoe who follows rules alone, and whether an entire community could not be systematically wrong on Kripke's accounting, in ways the account has no resources to describe. Defenders answer that the appeal is not to a census but to the weave of practices in which checking itself consists, and that demanding a further fact beneath agreement reinstates the picture under examination. Meanwhile the individualist counter-reading — rule-following as grounded in a speaker's dispositions and training without constitutive reference to a community — keeps its own adherents. The industry now studies both philosophers at once, and the name "Kripkenstein" marks the seam.

**(c) One Wittgenstein or two.** Beneath the other debates sits the framing question the library itself adopts by splitting its entries in two. The rupture reading — two philosophies, early and late, the second repudiating the first — draws its warrant from the author: the preface's "grave mistakes," the abandonment of simples, picturing, and the general form of the proposition. The continuity reading replies that the constant is the conception of philosophy itself: in both periods it is an activity of clarification aimed at the limits of sense, hostile to doctrine, and oriented by what shows itself beyond statement — ethics in 1921, forms of life in 1945. The resolute debate (a) is in part this dispute in disguise, since a therapeutic *Tractatus* narrows the gap the rupture reading needs. Scholars also point to the "resolute" corollary that the middle period — the *Philosophical Remarks* and *Philosophical Grammar* of the early 1930s, transitional works once neglected — now gets the closest genetic study, precisely because the archive (below) lets the turn be watched remark by remark. The dossier's practice follows the preface's: read the new against the background of the old, and let neither unity nor rupture be assumed in advance.

**(d) Wittgenstein and literature.** A quieter reception runs through writing and modernism, and this dossier states it only where it is documented. The *Tractatus* itself is a literary artifact — numbered, aphoristic, closing on silence — and its form drew literary readers early. The documented cases are concrete. Ingeborg Bachmann, who wrote her Vienna dissertation against Heidegger, made Wittgenstein central to her essays and broadcasts of the early 1950s and credited the *Tractatus* with showing how language reaches its limit — a transmission line into postwar Austrian letters. Thomas Bernhard's work engages him directly: the novel *Correction* (1975) builds its central figure on a Wittgenstein-like thinker modeled partly on his friend Paul Wittgenstein, and Bernhard's prose — repetitive, corrective, suspicious of its own sentences — conducts the argument in form as much as in reference. And Marjorie Perloff's *Wittgenstein's Ladder* (1996) made the scholarly case, as argument rather than document, for reading the *Tractatus* alongside the modernist avant-garde — Stein, Duchamp, Khlebnikov — as a work whose meaning lives in its procedures rather than its propositions. Claims beyond these — that this or that novelist "applied" Wittgenstein — circulate widely and are usually analogies in biographical dress; the dossier distinguishes the record from the resemblance.

## The standard misconceptions, in full

**"He refuted his first book point by point and replaced it with a second system."** The preface to the *Investigations* does speak of "grave mistakes" in the first book — and proposes publishing the new thoughts only in contrast with the old, since the new "could be seen in the right light only by contrast with and against the background of my old way of thinking." The later work does not proceed by counter-argument proposition by proposition, and it is not a second system: it offers no replacement theory of meaning, logic, or mind, and its author would have treated the demand for one as the confusion under treatment. There is real repudiation — of the picture theory's generality, of logical simples, of the independence of elementary propositions — and real continuity: philosophy as the struggle against bewitchment by language, clarity as the aim, the most important things as those that resist statement. One lifelong project, one famous self-correction, no second *Tractatus*.

**"'Meaning is use' is his doctrine, full stop."** What §43 actually says is hedged in the sentence itself: "For a large class of cases of the employment of the word 'meaning' — though not for all — this word can be explained in this way: the meaning of a word is its use in the language." The qualifications are the content. Names, samples, and ostensive teaching are cases the slogan fits awkwardly; and treating the sentence as a theory of meaning — to be applied exceptionlessly and defended against counterexamples — reinstates exactly the craving for generality the book diagnoses. It is a reminder about where to look, not a reductive definition.

**"Wittgenstein was a behaviorist."** He addressed the charge directly and denied it, in the *Investigations* itself: "And now it looks as if we had denied mental processes. And naturally we don't want to deny them" (§308). The private language argument targets a picture — meaning fixed by private ostension, the inner object as the real referent — not the existence of sensations, thoughts, or inner life. His "beetle in the box" (§293) makes the contents irrelevant to the word's use; it does not make the box empty by decree, and his extensive writings on the philosophy of psychology are unintelligible as behaviorism.

**"The ladder remark means the Tractatus can be discarded unread."** On every live reading, 6.54 instructs the reader to throw the ladder away *after climbing it* — "he must transcend these propositions, and then he will see the world aright." The resolute reading, the strictest of the three camps, does not license skipping the book; it demands the most laborious reading of all, since the therapy works only if the reader first takes the propositions with full seriousness and watches them collapse from inside. Traditional readers keep a doctrine the resolute readers say was never there; neither camp treats the book as disposable.

**"Wittgenstein was an enemy of philosophy itself."** He was an enemy of a picture of philosophy — as theory-construction on the model of the sciences — and said philosophy "leaves everything as it is" (§124) because its problems are confusions, not data-gaps. But the same man practiced philosophy with unmatched intensity for forty years, held a chair in it, and wrote that the problems are solved "not by giving new information, but by arranging what we have always known" (§109). The therapeutic conception is a position *within* philosophy about what its progress looks like; the verdict that it amounts to abdication is one side of the quietism debate staged in the library entries, not a fact about its author's allegiance.
