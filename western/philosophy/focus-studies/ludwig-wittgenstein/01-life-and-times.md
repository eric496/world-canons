# Life and Times — Ludwig Wittgenstein

*Dossier file 01. The biography, with the world attached; the works' occasions are treated here and their contents in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md).*

## The Palais Wittgenstein (1889–1903)

Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein was born on April 26, 1889, in Vienna, the youngest of the eight surviving children of Karl and Leopoldine Wittgenstein. His father, Karl Wittgenstein (1847–1913), had built a near-monopoly of the Austro-Hungarian steel industry and was by common consent one of the richest men in Europe — an industrialist of formidable will, self-made after a rebellious youth of his own. His mother Leopoldine, née Kalmus, was a gifted pianist. The family palace on the Alleegasse (now Argentinierstraße) was one of the musical centers of Vienna: Brahms was a family friend, Mahler visited, Clara Schumann's circle overlapped with theirs, and the young Bruno Walter and the child Pablo Casals performed in its salon. The household owned multiple Stradivarius and Guarneri instruments; music was not decoration but a second native language, and Ludwig, who played the clarinet seriously, carried absolute pitch and an intense, exacting musicality all his life.

The household was also a pressure chamber. Karl intended his sons for the business and had the elder boys educated privately at home under a regime of tutors aimed at engineering and commerce — a regime that broke against the sons' temperaments with terrible results. Three of Ludwig's four brothers died by their own hands. Hans (1877–1902), the eldest and a musical prodigy who had been composing at four, fled to America rather than enter the firm, and in 1902 disappeared from a boat in Chesapeake Bay; his body was never recovered, and he is presumed to have drowned himself. Rudolf (1881–1904), who wanted the theater against his father's wishes, killed himself in Berlin on May 2, 1904, drinking cyanide in a bar while his favorite music played; he had written to ask about the effects of the poison beforehand. Kurt (1878–1918), the most stable of the three and a career officer, shot himself on the Italian front in the war's last days — October 1918, though some accounts give November — after the troops under his command deserted en masse. The fourth brother, Paul (1887–1961), the pianist, survived two wars and a wound that cost him his right arm in 1914, and rebuilt his career one-handed, commissioning the left-hand concertos (Ravel's and Prokofiev's among them) for which he is still known.

Ludwig grew up inside this: enormous wealth, exacting standards, an adored and dominating father, and bereavement as a recurring family event. He struggled with suicidal thoughts at intervals throughout his life, and the confessional intensity of his later years — his insistence that work on philosophy was work on oneself — is unintelligible without this background. It is also a standing caution: the family's psychiatric history is a fact, and every romantic reading of his difficulty as the price of genius should be resisted on the evidence of what the difficulty actually cost.

## School, engineering, and the turn (1903–1911)

In 1903, at fourteen, Wittgenstein was sent out of the house to school for the first time: the Realschule at Linz, a technical high school, where he was a conspicuous outsider — a rich, formal, dialect-speaking boy among provincial schoolmates, miserable and academically undistinguished. (The school is often noted for having had Adolf Hitler among its pupils in overlapping years; the documented overlap is a curiosity of the registers, nothing more, and no meeting is recorded.) He went on in 1906 to the Technische Hochschule in Berlin-Charlottenburg to study mechanical engineering, and in 1908 to Manchester as a research student in aeronautical engineering — experimental work on kites for upper-atmosphere research at Glossop, and a patented design for a propeller driven by jets at its tips. He was a genuine engineer, competent and inventive.

The mathematics the engineering required drew him down into its foundations, and there he met the two books that changed his life: Frege's *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* and Russell's *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903). In October 1911, unannounced and mid-term, he traveled to Jena to visit Gottlob Frege, then the most important and most neglected logician alive, and talked philosophy with him for hours. Frege's advice settled the matter: go to Cambridge, and study with Bertrand Russell. Wittgenstein arrived at Trinity College, Cambridge, in October 1911, still nominally an engineering research student, and attached himself to Russell with an intensity Russell at first found alarming and then recognized. Russell's letters of the period are the earliest external testimony: within months he was writing that Wittgenstein was "the most perfect example I have ever known of genius as traditionally conceived, passionate, profound, intense, and dominating," and treating him not as a pupil but as the man who would do the "next big step in philosophy." Wittgenstein enrolled as an undergraduate in 1912, was admitted to the Apostles (the Cambridge secret society), formed his closest Cambridge friendship with the young economist-in-training David Pinsent, and in 1913 — the year his father died, leaving him, by the will's design, very rich indeed — withdrew to Norway to think alone.

## Norway, and war (1913–1919)

At Skjolden, at the head of the Sognefjord, Wittgenstein had a small house built on a rock above the fjord (1913–14) and spent the winter of 1913–14 there in near-total isolation, working on the logical problems that became the *Tractatus*. The pattern of the next forty years was set: solitude as a working method, a bare room, and a mind that treated distraction as corruption.

When war came in August 1914 he volunteered for the Austro-Hungarian army, although his inheritance and a hernia diagnosis could have secured him exemption; he wanted, by his own account, to face death and to do something other than think. He served first on a Vistula gunboat and in an artillery workshop in Kraków, then — at his repeated request — as an artillery observer on the Galician front in 1916, where he was decorated for bravery under fire, and finally on the Italian front in 1918, after officer training. The wartime notebooks (kept partly in a simple code) show the philosophy transforming under fire: alongside the logic appear entries on God, death, happiness, and the meaning of life, and it is in the trenches that the *Tractatus*'s ethical core was forged. In the summer of 1918, on leave at Hallein, in despair over the war and over Pinsent's death that May, he completed the manuscript of the *Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung* and left it with his uncle. Pinsent had been killed in a flying accident that May; the book would carry his name in dedication. Wittgenstein returned to the Italian front, and on November 3, 1918, days before the armistice, was taken prisoner near Trento. He spent his captivity, at Como and then in the camp at Monte Cassino, with the manuscript in his rucksack.

He also spent it corresponding — through intermediaries he got the manuscript to Russell — and in December 1919, after his release that August, the two men met at The Hague and went through the book line by line for a week. By then two of the decisions that define the biography had been made. The first was about money: in 1919 he transferred his entire inherited fortune to his siblings (chiefly Paul, Hermine, and Margaret), insisting the transfer be unconditional so that the money could not corrupt him; a smaller act had preceded it — 100,000 crowns distributed in 1914, through the editor Ludwig von Ficker, to Austrian artists and writers, Rilke and Trakl among them. The second was about work: believing the *Tractatus* had solved philosophy's problems, he was done with the subject. He trained as an elementary schoolteacher.

## The schoolteacher years (1920–1926), and the Haidbauer incident

From 1920 to 1926 Wittgenstein taught in village schools in the mountains of Lower Austria — Trattenbach (1920–22), Puchberg am Schneeberg (1922–24), and Otterthal (1924–26) — choosing the poorest and remotest postings deliberately. The record of these years is double, and the dossier keeps both halves. He was, by many pupils' later testimony, an electrifying teacher: he built models and conducted experiments, took bright children far beyond the syllabus, financed further schooling for the most gifted, and in 1926 published a spelling dictionary for elementary schools (the *Wörterbuch für Volksschulen*), the only other book of his to appear in his lifetime. He was also volatile, perfectionist, and given to corporal punishment — boxing ears and pulling hair were within his repertoire, in an era when Austrian village schools were hardly gentle, but by the testimony of former pupils he exceeded local norms in frequency and intensity, especially with slow learners and with girls in mathematics, whom he held to a standard he described with contempt when they failed it.

In April 1926, at Otterthal, the pattern reached its crisis in what Austrians still call *der Vorfall Haidbauer*, the Haidbauer incident. Josef Haidbauer, an eleven-year-old pupil — fatherless, a slow learner — was struck by Wittgenstein two or three times about the head during a lesson and collapsed, reportedly unconscious. Wittgenstein sent the class home, carried the boy to the headmaster's office, and left the school; a parent whose daughter's ears he had previously pulled until they bled confronted him and tried to have him arrested. Wittgenstein resigned on April 28, 1926. Beyond these outlines the record thins, and the dossier marks the thinning precisely: proceedings were begun against him at the district court in Gloggnitz, where he was summoned on May 17, 1926; the judge, suspecting mental illness, ordered a psychiatric report and adjourned the case. The outcome was never officially published. William Warren Bartley's account holds that the hearing exonerated him — and the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography likewise says he was cleared — while Alexander Waugh argues the family used its influence to make the matter disappear; that last claim is Waugh's inference, not a documented fact. What is documented is that Wittgenstein was less than truthful with the court about the extent of his use of corporal punishment, that no psychiatric report on him survives, and that the episode shamed him permanently: ten years later, in 1936, he returned to the villages to confess to former pupils in person and ask their forgiveness, with mixed results. Haidbauer himself died young, around 1929, of causes unconnected in any source with the incident.

## Gardener, architect, and the road back (1926–1929)

After resigning, Wittgenstein worked for three months as a gardener's assistant to the monks at Hütteldorf, considered entering a monastery, and then took up the project that occupied 1926–28: designing, with his friend the architect Paul Engelmann, a house in Vienna for his sister Margaret (Gretl) Stonborough-Wittgenstein — the Palais Stonborough at Kundmanngasse 19. Engelmann had the commission; Wittgenstein progressively became the building's true designer, and the finished house is his in every detail: a severe, proportioned modernist palace of flat surfaces and exact measurements, famously so exact that he had a nearly finished ceiling raised by three centimeters because the room's proportions demanded it — an anecdote repeated by his sister Hermine and consistent with everything else on record. He designed the radiators, the door handles, the window mechanisms. The house, completed at the end of 1928, is the *Tractatus*'s aesthetic executed in stone: nothing decorative, everything in its place, and the whole legible as one thought.

Vienna in these years also brought the encounters that pulled him back to philosophy. From 1927, through Margaret's introduction, he met Moritz Schlick, founder of the Vienna Circle, and began a series of conversations with Schlick and Friedrich Waismann (Carnap and Feigl attended some early meetings). The Circle revered the *Tractatus* as its founding document; Wittgenstein, who despised their scientism, cooperated only on his own terms — dictating material to Waismann for a planned exposition of his views, and at some meetings, by Carnap's account, turning his chair to the wall and reading Tagore's poetry aloud instead of discussing logic. But the conversations did their work, as did Frank Ramsey's visits to Austria and Ramsey's written criticisms — above all the color-exclusion problem, which the *Tractatus* could not house — and by 1928 Wittgenstein's confidence in the old system was cracking. In January 1929 he returned to Cambridge — arriving, the story goes, to be greeted by Keynes with the remark that God had arrived on the 5:15 train; the remark is Keynes's own, in a letter, and can be trusted as such. In June 1929 he took his PhD, submitting the *Tractatus* itself as the thesis, with Russell and G. E. Moore as examiners; Moore's report judged it work of genius. He began lecturing at once, thinking aloud before small classes in his bare rooms at Trinity, and in 1930 he was elected to a fellowship of the College. The second philosophy had begun.

## Professor, porter, exile, and last years (1929–1951)

The 1930s at Cambridge were a decade of dictation, notebooks, and revised typescripts rather than books. Wittgenstein lectured without notes, composing in the act of speaking, to audiences that included Elizabeth Anscombe, Rush Rhees, Norman Malcolm, and for two terms Alan Turing, with whom he argued about the foundations of mathematics in the 1939 lectures — a disagreement preserved in the edited notes and still read as the period's most vivid philosophy of mathematics. He dictated the *Blue Book* (1933–34) and the *Brown Book* (1934–35) to small groups of students; in 1936–37 he returned to the house at Skjolden and there began the manuscript work that became *Philosophical Investigations*. He took British citizenship in 1939 — the application, prompted by the Anschluss, was granted that June — and the same year he succeeded Moore as Professor of Philosophy at Cambridge.

When the Second World War came he refused to sit it out as a professor. From 1941 to 1942 he worked as a dispensary porter at Guy's Hospital in London, carrying medicines and — by the testimony of the staff — advising patients about their dressings with such authority that they were told, when they asked, only that he was a man of mystery; from 1943 to 1944 he was a laboratory technician at the Royal Victoria Infirmary in Newcastle, working on the measurement of wound shock for a Medical Research Council unit. He returned to Cambridge, lectured through 1947, and then resigned the chair to write. The *Investigations*' first part was essentially finished by 1946 — he had tried, and failed, to publish it then with the *Tractatus*, and withdrawn it — but revision continued, and his health was failing.

The last years were spent in motion: Dublin in the winter of 1947–48, working in a hotel room and in a borrowed office; then, in 1948, a remote cottage at Rosro on the Connemara coast of County Galway, where the locals decided the silent foreigner was either a spy or a genius, and where he worked on the manuscripts on the psychology of the *Investigations*' second part; a visit to Norman Malcolm in Ithaca, New York, in 1949. That October, in Vienna, he was diagnosed with prostate cancer. He declined aggressive treatment, returned to England, and in January 1951 moved into the home of his doctor, Edward Bevan, in Cambridge, so as to be spared hospital. There, in an upstairs room at Storey's Way, he wrote almost to the end: the last dated entry in the notebooks that became *On Certainty* is from April 27, 1951, two days before his death. On the night of April 28, aware that he was dying, he gave Joan Bevan, his hostess, a message for his absent friends — reported by her, and so to be cited as reported and not as a stenographic record: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life." He died on April 29, 1951, aged sixty-two, and was buried at St Giles' churchyard, Cambridge, after a Catholic requiem arranged by friends who knew his lifelong, unresolved attraction to the faith he never formally held.
