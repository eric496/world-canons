# Wittgenstein in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: Ludwig Wittgenstein · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entries: [Wittgenstein, Early](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md) · [Wittgenstein, Late](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md)*

Wittgenstein published almost nothing and talked incessantly. His philosophy was made in conversations — with Frege and Russell before the First World War, with Ramsey and Sraffa between the wars, with students in Cambridge rooms — and it has been remade in conversations ever since. This file traces them in three directions: the predecessors who set his problems, the contemporaries who argued back, and the heirs who carried the arguments on, often past anything he would have accepted.

## Backward: the predecessors

### Frege: the first door, and the advice that opened the second

In the autumn of 1911 Wittgenstein, then an engineering researcher in Manchester whose work had driven him to the foundations of mathematics, traveled to Jena to consult [Gottlob Frege](../../eras/05-20th-century/gottlob-frege.md), whose *Begriffsschrift* and writings on sense and reference had shown that logic could be rebuilt as a rigorous formal instrument. The meeting nearly failed — by the standard accounts Wittgenstein argued, Frege was exasperated — but its outcome decided his life: Frege advised the young man to go to Cambridge and study with Russell. The documented sequence matters because it makes Frege, not Russell, the first cause of Wittgenstein's philosophical career: the problems of the *Tractatus* — how a proposition hooks onto the world, what logic shows about the structure of both — are Frege's problems, pursued with Frege's demand that confusion about language is confusion about thought. The correspondence continued, and Frege's recorded reaction to the *Tractatus* itself was largely incomprehension, a first instance of a pattern: Wittgenstein's most important readers were usually also his baffled ones.

### Russell: the shared atomism, and the long divergence

[Bertrand Russell](../../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md) received the importunate Austrian in 1911, judged him a genius within weeks, and became the second partner in what became logical atomism: the conviction that analysis of language down to logically simple constituents would disclose the structure of reality. The *Tractatus* grew out of that shared project and outgrew it — Russell read the finished manuscript in 1919 (sent from the prison camp at Cassino) and supplied the introduction to the 1922 English edition, framing the book as a contribution to logic and the theory of knowledge. Wittgenstein thought the introduction missed the book's point — its ethical center — and said so in correspondence. Russell, for his part, recorded real reservations inside the introduction itself, noting drily that Wittgenstein "manages to say a good deal about what cannot be said." The divergence then became total. Russell never accepted the later philosophy, and in *My Philosophical Development* (1959) he dismissed it: he had, he wrote, not found in Wittgenstein's later work anything that seemed to him interesting, and he did not understand how a philosophy of that sort could have serious adherents. Between the man who wrote the *Tractatus*'s introduction and the man who wrote that sentence lies the whole arc of this dossier: a partnership in analysis that split over what analysis is *for* — construction of an ideal logical language, or therapy for the confusions ordinary language breeds.

The middle of the story is the prison-camp correspondence of 1919, some of the most intellectually intimate letters either man wrote. From Cassino, Wittgenstein explained the book Russell had not yet read: its point, he insisted, was not the logic but what the logic delimited — "the main point is the theory of what can be expressed by propositions... and what cannot be expressed by propositions, but only shown," the distinction on which, he said, "the whole business of philosophy" turned, including the problem of life and the cardinality disputes then dividing them. Russell replied with warmth and with objections in the same breath, and the two met at The Hague that December to go through the manuscript together. The pattern of the whole relationship is already there: no one took the work more seriously than Russell, and no serious reader's understanding diverged from the author's more completely. When Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge in 1929, Russell examined the *Tractatus* for the doctorate — an examination at which, by the standard anecdote, Wittgenstein clapped both examiners on the shoulder and told them not to worry, since he knew they would never understand it. Anecdote aside, the examiners' report was respectful and the degree was granted; but the pupil of 1911 was by then, in everyone's eyes including his own, the author of a book its author no longer believed.

### Moore: common sense as the fixed point

[G. E. Moore](../../eras/05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md) was the quietest of Wittgenstein's interlocutors and, in the end, one of the most productive. The contact ran the whole length of the career: the young Wittgenstein attended Moore's lectures and dictated notes to him in Norway in 1914; Moore, with Russell, examined the *Tractatus* for the 1929 PhD; and the elderly Wittgenstein attended Moore's lectures again and took copious notes on them. Moore's weapon was common sense — the insistence, in "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) and "Proof of an External World" (1939), that he *knew* such things as "here is one hand" with a certainty no skeptical argument could outweigh. Wittgenstein's last work, the notebooks published as *On Certainty*, is a sustained answer to Moore: not a refutation but a reframing. Moore, on his reading, was right that such propositions stand fast and wrong about what their standing fast consists in — they are not items of knowledge at all but the hinges on which questions and doubts turn, the riverbed of thoughts rather than one proposition among others. That the most famous product of Wittgenstein's final eighteen months is a dialogue with a predecessor dead nearly a decade tells you how he worked: the conversation partner did not need to be alive, or even, in the end, answerable.

## Sideways: the contemporaries who argued back

### Ramsey: the critic inside the citadel

Frank Ramsey — mathematician, co-translator with C. K. Ogden of the *Tractatus*, and author of a penetrating 1923 review of it — did what none of the book's admirers did: he pressed its machinery until parts of it gave. In September 1923 Ramsey visited Wittgenstein at Puchberg in Austria, where the ex-philosopher was teaching schoolchildren, and the two went through the *Tractatus* sentence by sentence over about two weeks of daily discussion. The criticisms Ramsey developed — about identity, about the treatment of mathematics, and above all about colour-exclusion, the problem that "this is red all over" and "this is green all over" exclude each other although the book required elementary propositions to be mutually independent — could not be answered from inside the system. Wittgenstein's preface to the *Investigations* names Ramsey's criticism, offered "in innumerable conversations during the last two years of his life" (Ramsey died in 1930, aged twenty-six), as one of the two forces that made him see the "grave mistakes" of the first book. It is the cleanest documented case in his career of a philosophical turn driven by a named critic's objections rather than by solitary doubt. Ramsey himself moved too: his late papers, above all "General Propositions and Causality" (1929), bend toward a pragmatism about laws and conditionals that has struck many readers as converging with the later Wittgenstein from the opposite direction — rules judged by the habits they license rather than by forms they picture. The two never had the decades the convergence deserved; Ramsey's death at twenty-six froze the conversation at its most productive point, which is part of why the preface's gratitude reads as grief as well as acknowledgment.

### The Vienna Circle: the appropriation he disowned

The [Vienna Circle](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md) of Moritz Schlick read the *Tractatus* line by line in the mid-1920s and built logical empiricism partly on it: the verification principle — the meaning of a statement is its method of verification, a formula Schlick framed drawing on Wittgenstein's ideas — and the closing silence read as a license to dismiss metaphysics, ethics, and religion as nonsense. From 1927 Wittgenstein met Schlick and Friedrich Waismann for discussions; Waismann's records of those conversations survive, published posthumously as *Wittgenstein and the Vienna Circle* (1979), and they document a thinker already dismantling the *Tractatus*'s foundations — on verification, on hypotheses, on the status of his own earlier claims — while the Circle was still engraving them on its banners. An abortive collaboration tells the same story from the other side: Waismann was commissioned to write an exposition of Wittgenstein's philosophy, *Logik, Sprache, Philosophie*, which was to present the master's views systematically; the project dragged through the 1930s as its subject kept changing his mind, and the book appeared only decades later, in altered form, long after both men were dead. There could hardly be a cleaner emblem of the relationship: the movement wanted a doctrine it could print, and its unwilling source would not hold still to be printed. But he would not join the Circle, would not let its manifesto claim him, and at meetings reportedly sometimes turned his back and read poetry aloud — Tagore is the name the witnesses give. His dismay is documented in letters and in the testimony collected around the Circle: as he saw it, they had taken the letter against the spirit. The book's silence about ethics was, for its author, reverence for what mattered most; the Circle converted it into a doctrine of dismissal, and built on the *Tractatus* a program — scientistic, anti-metaphysical, confident — that its author spent the 1930s dismantling from within. The library's [Vienna Circle entry](../../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md) tells the movement's side of this story.

### Sraffa: the gesture, and the documented credit

Piero Sraffa, the Italian economist at Cambridge, argued with Wittgenstein regularly for years after 1929, and Wittgenstein's preface makes the strongest claim for any influence of the later period: "Even more than to this — always certain and forcible — criticism I am indebted to that which a teacher of this university, Mr P. Sraffa, for many years unceasingly practised on my thoughts. I am indebted to this stimulus for the most consequential ideas of this book." What the stimulus consisted in is partly conjectural, but the famous story is told by Norman Malcolm on Wittgenstein's own telling: pressed to say what the *logical form* of a Neapolitan gesture of contempt — brushing the chin with an outward sweep of the fingertips — could be, Wittgenstein felt the demand for a hidden logical structure collapse, because the gesture's meaning plainly lives in a conventional human practice, not in a form it shares with a fact. **That story is an anecdote**: it rests on Wittgenstein's later report as recorded by Malcolm, and Sraffa himself, when asked in old age, is reported to have been unable to pick it out from their many conversations. The preface's credit is documentary; the chin-brush is the legend that grew around it — instructive, probably faithful in spirit, and to be cited as anecdote, not as record.

### Turing: the dialogue that survives verbatim

Alan Turing attended Wittgenstein's 1939 Cambridge lectures on the foundations of mathematics, and uniquely among these conversations we have something close to a transcript: the lecture notes taken by students were published in 1976 as *Wittgenstein's Lectures on the Foundations of Mathematics, Cambridge 1939*, edited by Cora Diamond. Turing played the ideal antagonist — the working mathematician who kept asking, in effect, whether Wittgenstein's refusal of foundations meant bridges would fall down, whether a contradiction hidden in a calculus really matters if nobody derives a disaster from it. Wittgenstein's answer — that the trouble with a contradiction is not an engineering catastrophe but confusion about what game is being played — is the later philosophy applied at its most exposed point. Turing has no entry of his own in this library; the exchange matters here because it shows the method against a first-rate opponent who would not be charmed, and because it is documented dialogue rather than report.

### Popper and the poker: a contested anecdote

On 25 October 1946 [Karl Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md) addressed the Moral Science Club at Cambridge; Wittgenstein was in the chair. By Popper's account in his autobiography *Unended Quest* (1974), Wittgenstein, waving a poker he had been using to stoke the fire, demanded an example of a moral rule in response to Popper's claim that genuine philosophical problems exist, and Popper replied: "Not to threaten visiting lecturers with pokers" — whereupon Wittgenstein stormed out. Wittgenstein's supporters and several witnesses gave conflicting accounts: the poker was being used innocently at the fireside; the exit was boredom or a prior arrangement; the witticism, if delivered, was not heard as Popper tells it. **The whole episode is a contested anecdote**, the subject of an entire book of investigation (Edmonds and Eidinow's *Wittgenstein's Poker*, 2001) that ends without a settled verdict; this dossier follows the library entry's ruling and tells it as contested, with the two camps' versions named and neither endorsed. Its documentary value is real but indirect: it records how quickly the two men's genuinely opposed conceptions of philosophy — problems to be solved versus confusions to be dissolved — turned a club meeting into a legend.

## Forward: the heirs

### Anscombe, Rhees, von Wright: the literary executors

Wittgenstein's students became his editors, and the later philosophy exists in print because of them. **G. E. M. Anscombe** — she shares a [library entry](../../eras/05-20th-century/anscombe-and-foot.md) with Philippa Foot — translated the *Investigations*, co-edited it with **Rush Rhees**, and wrote both an introduction to the *Tractatus* (1959) and *Intention* (1957), the monograph that carried his philosophy of action into the mainstream. Rhees, who had studied with him in the 1930s, edited several of the posthumous volumes and defended a demanding, anti-professional reading of the master across a long career. Georg Henrik von Wright, his successor in the Cambridge chair, catalogued the *Nachlass* and made serious editing possible. These are the heirs in the direct line: their selection and arrangement decisions are part of what "Wittgenstein" now means, a fact [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) takes up.

### Winch: the ideas into social science

Peter Winch, Rhees's student, wrote *The Idea of a Social Science* (1958), which applied the rule-following considerations to the study of society: understanding a form of life is not explaining it from outside but grasping its internal rules. The book made Wittgenstein a presence in sociology and anthropology — and made him answerable, in critics' eyes, for a relativism he never stated. It is the clearest case of an heir developing one thread of the later work into a program the master neither launched nor licensed.

### Cavell: the American Wittgenstein

Stanley Cavell — no entry of his own in this library — took Wittgenstein (with Austin) as the starting point for a distinctively American continuation, from the 1958 essay "Must We Mean What We Say?" through *The Claim of Reason* (1979). Cavell's Wittgenstein is preoccupied with skepticism not as an error to be dissolved but as a truth about the human condition to be acknowledged, and his reading carried the *Investigations* into literary studies, where the example-and-voice method fitted disciplines already suspicious of theory. Whether Cavell's emphasis is faithful or appropriative is debated; that he made Wittgenstein a figure in American letters is not.

### Kripke: the loudspeaker

Saul Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) did more than any other single book to set the agenda of later-Wittgenstein discussion in analytic philosophy: it presented the rule-following remarks as a skeptical paradox about meaning — no fact about a speaker constitutes their meaning one thing rather than another — answered by a "sceptical solution" resting on community agreement. Kripke himself flagged the book as presenting "Wittgenstein's argument as it struck Kripke," not a claim of exegesis; scholars who think the paradox is imported, and the community solution alien, have taken to calling its protagonist "Kripkenstein." As interpretation it remains contested; as the conversation's modern loudspeaker it is unmatched — the library entry for the [late Wittgenstein](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md) treats the dispute in its own right, and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) stages the debate below.

## Wittgenstein in the nine problems

The library's [themes index](../../themes.md) places him in three of its nine threads.

- **§1 [Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it)** — Doubt needs grounds and hinges: the *On Certainty* Wittgenstein, whose certainties stand fast not as conclusions but as the riverbed of questioning.
- **§2 [Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body)** — A private language for inner sensations is impossible: meaning needs public criteria, so mind is not a hidden inner chamber.
- **§7 [Language & Meaning](../../themes.md#7-language--meaning--how-do-words-and-thoughts-hook-onto-the-world)** — The index's home thread for him, carrying both entries: the early picture theory and closing silence, and the later recasting of meaning as use within language-games and forms of life.

Beyond the index's three placements, the threads he arguably touches without a line: on **§3 God & Religion**, the *Tractatus*'s mystical and his lifelong respect for religious practice as a form of life; on **§8 Science & Method**, the philosophy of mathematics pursued with Turing — inquiry as practice rather than foundation. The index is a map, not a boundary; the dossier's [03](03-system-and-concepts.md) and [04](04-arguments.md) files work the positions themselves.
