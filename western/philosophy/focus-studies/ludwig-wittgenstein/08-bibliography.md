# Bibliography — Ludwig Wittgenstein

Annotated and tiered: read downward only as far as your questions require. Prices are the usual paperback ranges; the primary texts are *owned*, per the learning path's rule, because library lending windows are shorter than this plan's pace. One warning governs the whole list: Wittgenstein published one book of philosophy in his lifetime, and almost everything else on this page is posthumous and editorially arranged from a *Nachlass* of some twenty thousand pages. Every tier below therefore says, for each item, who made it a book — him, or his editors.

## 1. Primary texts — the editions the plan uses

***Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, trans. D. F. Pears & B. F. McGuinness (Routledge).** The learning path's ruling and this dossier's: the working text for solo study. (German first published 1921; English book edition 1922.) The alternative in the house is the historical translation by **C. K. Ogden, with F. P. Ramsey's help (1922)** — the text the Vienna Circle and the first Anglophone readers actually read, and worth owning in a cheap reprint for exactly that reason: several famous English formulations are Ogden's, and comparing them against Pears & McGuinness is a first lesson in how much of "Wittgenstein" is his translators. Cite by proposition number (1–7 with decimals), never by page.

***Philosophical Investigations*, trans. G. E. M. Anscombe, rev. P. M. S. Hacker & Joachim Schulte, 4th ed. (Wiley-Blackwell, 2009).** Anscombe — his student, literary executor, and translator — produced the classic English text; the 4th edition revises her in places, prints German and English facing, and retitles Part II *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*, which honestly signals its status: Part I is the work he prepared for publication (essentially finished by 1946, withdrawn, published 1953); Part II is material of 1946–49 assembled by his editors Anscombe and Rush Rhees. An older Anscombe paperback is usable for a first reading; check any wording you intend to quote against the 4th edition. Cite Part I by section (§§1–693).

***On Certainty*, ed. G. E. M. Anscombe & G. H. von Wright, trans. Denis Paul & Anscombe (Blackwell; Harper paperback).** The notebooks of 1949–51, written in the last months of his life in response to Moore — never prepared for publication, and read accordingly: this is thinking in progress, in 676 numbered remarks, not a treatise. Week 7's selections come from it. Nearby on the same shelf, unassigned but named so the map is complete: the *Blue and Brown Books* (dictated 1933–35; published 1958) — the most accessible bridge between the two philosophies, and this list's recommended first *extra* primary text; *Zettel* (1967) and *Culture and Value* (1980), editorial clippings from the *Nachlass*, valuable and to be cited as selections, not as books he made.

## 2. First secondary books, in order

1. **Norman Malcolm, *Ludwig Wittgenstein: A Memoir* (1958; 2nd ed. with von Wright's biographical sketch).** Short, humane, and first because it restores the man his students met — the teaching without notes, the impatience with academic philosophy, the intensity — before the interpreters get to work. The library's late entry names it; read it in Week 1 or 2, alongside the life in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md).
2. **Marie McGinn, *Wittgenstein and the Philosophical Investigations* (Routledge Philosophy Guidebooks, 1997).** The clearest single-volume walk through the later philosophy by a scholar who was also on Week 1's *In Our Time* panel: the Augustinian picture, rule-following, and private language expounded patiently and without a sectarian axe. The natural companion for Weeks 3–6 alongside [the library's own study guide](../../study/wittgenstein-investigations.md).
3. **Ray Monk, *Ludwig Wittgenstein: The Duty of Genius* (1990)** — see tier 3; listed here because, uniquely, it belongs in both places: read it third, as the first book-length interpretation, its engine the claim that the life and the philosophy explain each other.

## 3. Lives

**Ray Monk, *Ludwig Wittgenstein: The Duty of Genius* (1990).** The standard biography and one of the great philosophical lives in English: Vienna and the family fortune, the war, the schoolroom, the house in Kundmannsgasse, Cambridge, the return, the last years — written by a philosopher who treats the thought as part of the life-record rather than as decoration. Its governing idea, that Wittgenstein's relentless ethical seriousness *is* the key to both philosophies, is an interpretation and should be read as one; the facts it rests on are the record this dossier's [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) follows. **Brian McGuinness, *Wittgenstein: A Life — Young Ludwig, 1889–1921* (1988)** is the scholarly complement: denser, archival, indispensable on the years through the *Tractatus* — and, as its subtitle says, only those years; the projected continuation never appeared, so for 1929–51 Monk is the life. Read McGuinness when a Week 1–2 fact needs its documentary depth (the wartime notebooks, the von Ficker correspondence's setting), not as cover-to-cover reading inside this plan.

## 4. Commentaries and provocations

**Gordon Baker & P. M. S. Hacker, the analytical commentary on the *Investigations*** — *Wittgenstein: Understanding and Meaning* (1980; 2nd ed. 2005) and *Wittgenstein: Rules, Grammar and Necessity* (1985), with *Scepticism, Rules and Language* (1984) as the pair's direct answer to Kripke. Section-by-section exegesis of unmatched patience, and the house's fullest statement of the **traditional reading**: the paradox of §201 is dissolved, not solved; grasping a rule is not an interpretation; Wittgenstein offers no theory of meaning for a community-based or any other theory to be. That stance is itself a position in the live dispute — the Baker of the later work moved some distance from the partnership's early volumes, and resolute readers charge Baker & Hacker with rebuilding the doctrines the text dismantles — so cite them as the strongest traditional exegesis, not as neutral ground. **P. M. S. Hacker, *Wittgenstein: Insight and Illusion* (1972; rev. ed. 1986)** is the shorter, earlier statement of the same broad reading across both philosophies — the one-volume Hacker to read when the commentary's four volumes are too much, which inside this plan they are.

**Saul Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (Harvard UP, 1982) — a provocation, labeled as such.** The skeptical paradox (no fact constitutes my having meant plus rather than quus), the "skeptical solution" (correctness as community-licensed assertibility), and the private language argument recast as its corollary: the most influential Wittgenstein book of the last half-century, and, on the majority scholarly view represented by tier 4's first entry, not faithful exegesis. Kripke's own preface concedes the presentation is the argument *as it struck him*. Week 5 assigns its core; cite "Kripkenstein," never bare "Wittgenstein," for its conclusions. **Cora Diamond, *The Realistic Spirit: Wittgenstein, Philosophy, and the Mind* (MIT Press, 1991)** — the essays (several with James Conant's parallel work) that founded the **resolute reading** of the *Tractatus*: no ineffable truths, the ladder thrown away with nothing salvaged, and the *Investigations*' method as the early book's own method finally applied consistently. Read after Week 2's outline has made the dispute vivid; its title essay and "Throwing Away the Ladder" are the assigned core if time pinches.

## 5. Reference

- **[SEP, "Wittgenstein"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/)** — by Anat Biletzki & Anat Matar; the current scholarly map of both philosophies and of every dispute this dossier flags (resolute vs. traditional, community vs. individual readings, the *Nachlass* problem). The dossier's first resort, per the learning path's SEP rule.
- **[IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/)** — the plainer first passes, per the path's IEP-before rule for new topics; its topical article ["Wittgenstein: Epistemology"](https://iep.utm.edu/witt-epi/) is the assigned companion for Week 7's *On Certainty* work, mapping the hinge literature (minimal, therapeutic, epistemic, and framework readings) that the plan can only point at.
- ***The Cambridge Companion to Wittgenstein*, ed. Hans Sluga & David G. Stern (1996; 2nd ed. 2018)** — the one-volume state of the question by topic (the *Tractatus*, the later philosophy, mathematics, ethics, the Vienna years), for the debate you meet in the capstone and want in its current form.
- **[PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/)** — the "Ludwig Wittgenstein" category and its subcategories (the *Tractatus*, rule-following, private language) are the working bibliography of the live literature; use them after the SEP article, not before.

## 6. Media (all verified; the assignments are in [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md))

- **BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*: "Wittgenstein"** (first broadcast 4 December 2003; Melvyn Bragg with Ray Monk, Barry Smith, and Marie McGinn; ~42 min). Free on BBC Sounds. The whole two-philosophy arc as a preview — with his biographer and this list's McGinn on the panel.
- **[*Philosophize This!*](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast) (Stephen West)** — two verified episodes: **#097**, the show's Wittgenstein episode, covering both books with the *Tractatus* at its center (assigned as Week 2 review), and **#231, "The Late Work of Wittgenstein — Language Games"** (28 June 2025, ~26 min; Week 4 review). Free; transcripts on the show's site. Popularizer's compression throughout — orientation and review, never evidence.
- **[*The Partially Examined Life*](https://partiallyexaminedlife.com)** — the deepest podcast coverage of this figure, in three verified runs: episodes **7** ("Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*: What Is There and Can We Talk About It?", 2009) and **8** (the *Tractatus* continued, with Carnap) for Weeks 1–2; episodes **55** ("Wittgenstein on Language") and **56** ("Wittgenstein, Continued"), both 2012, on *Investigations* §§1–133 and 191–360, for Weeks 5–6; and episode **309**, "Wittgenstein On Certainty" (Parts One and Two, 2023), on Week 7's text. Seminar-length and argumentative — hosts who disagree with each other and with him. The early episodes now sit in PEL's premium tier (previews free); the plan's assignments are bounded so the previews plus your own text work carry them if you do not subscribe.
- **BBC Radio 3, *Free Thinking*: ["Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* at 100"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/p07x0twx)** (2021; Shahidha Bari with Ray Monk, Juliet Floyd, Dawn Wilson, and Wittgenstein's niece Monica Nagler-Wittgenstein) — the centenary assessment, Week 2's sanctioned alternative.
- **One absence, recorded:** [*History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*](https://historyofphilosophy.net/) has not reached Wittgenstein — its main series stands in the early eighteenth century (episode 502, on the Dutch Republic, October 2026) and moves at a pace that puts the twentieth century years away. As with Nietzsche and Schopenhauer, no Adamson episodes exist to assign; when the series arrives, this list should be revised, not improvised around.
