# Wittgenstein: Two Systems, and the Method That Replaced System

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entries: [Wittgenstein (early)](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md) · [Wittgenstein (late)](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md) · Guide to the Investigations: [study guide](../../study/wittgenstein-investigations.md) · The arguments in full: [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md)*

## One name, two philosophies — and one recurring problem

Ludwig Wittgenstein is the only major philosopher in this library with two entries, and the dossier follows that division because he insisted on it himself: in the preface to the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) he describes his later thoughts as standing in grave contrast to the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (German edition 1921; English 1922), and asks that the new be published with the old so the new can be seen in its right light against the background of what it rejects. The early work is a system: a single, tightly deduced account of world, language, and thought, built to draw the limit of sense once and for all. The later work is the demolition of that system from inside, and the construction — deliberately unsystematic, an "album" of remarks — of a different way of doing philosophy altogether.

What connects them, on any reading, is the problem: **language misleads thought, and philosophy is the struggle against that misleading**. The *Tractatus* already declares that philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity of clarification (4.112). The *Investigations* repeats the declaration with its method inverted: where the early book clarified by analysis down to a hidden logical structure, the later clarifies by description of the surface — by reminding us of the uses we already command. Everything in this file hangs on that inversion. The early system's architecture is presented first, in its own terms; then the turn; then the later method and doctrines; then the coda of *On Certainty*; then the standing question of whether the name covers one thinker or two.

A caution on sources. The *Tractatus* is one short book its author published. The later philosophy reaches us through Part I of the *Investigations*, which Wittgenstein prepared for publication, and then through posthumous constructions — Part II of the *Investigations* (now often printed as *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*), the *Blue and Brown Books*, *On Certainty*, and some twenty thousand pages of *Nachlass*. Citations below are to the stable numbering: decimals for the *Tractatus*, section numbers (§§) for Part I of the *Investigations* and for *On Certainty* (OC), and "Part II, §xi" for the aspect-seeing discussion in Anscombe's arrangement of Part II.

## The early system

### The world is the totality of facts, not of things

The *Tractatus* opens with a correction of the reader's default ontology: "The world is all that is the case" (1); "The world is the totality of facts, not of things" (1.1). What is the case — a **fact** — is the existence of **states of affairs** (2): configurations in which **objects** hang together "like the links of a chain" (2.03), with no connecting link needed beyond the objects themselves. Objects are simple and subsistent — "what is unalterable and subsistent" (2.0271) — and each object carries, written into it, the possibility of all the states of affairs in which it can occur (2.012–2.0141): an object is not first a bare item and later a combinable one; its combinability *is* its form. The determinate way objects are combined in a state of affairs is its **structure**; "form is the possibility of structure" (2.033). States of affairs are independent of one another: from the existence or non-existence of one, nothing follows about another (2.061–2.062). The totality of existing states of affairs is the world (2.04); the totality of facts, existing and non-existing, is reality (2.06, 2.063), and language's job is to hook onto reality at the level of facts — to say how things stand, truly or falsely.

Two features of this ontology do quiet but enormous work. First, objects are demanded, not discovered. Wittgenstein never gave a settled example of a simple object, and the text is frank that the demand is structural: unless there were a substance of the world — objects that subsist independently of what happens to be the case — whether a proposition had sense would depend on whether another proposition was true, and we could sketch no picture of the world at all (2.021–2.0212). Second, the world's contingency is entirely a matter of *which* states of affairs exist; the space of possible states of affairs is fixed by the objects and their forms, in advance of any fact. Possibility is built into the furniture.

### The picture theory

"We picture facts to ourselves" (2.1). A **picture** is a model of reality (2.12): its elements correspond to objects (2.13–2.131), and the fact that the elements are combined in a determinate way *represents* that the objects are so combined (2.15). A picture is itself a fact (2.141) — ink on paper, sounds in air — and what it shares with the reality it depicts, and must share in order to depict it rightly or falsely, is **logical form** (2.18): the possibility of the structure it shares with its object. A logical picture can depict the world (2.19). A picture agrees with reality or fails to agree; it is true or false by comparison with reality, and from the picture alone its truth cannot be told (2.21, 2.223–2.224); there are no pictures true a priori (2.225).

A **thought** is a logical picture of facts (3). A **proposition** is a picture of reality as we think it is (4.01), presented in a perceptible sign. The proposition does not blend with what it depicts: its elements (names, in the fully analyzed limit) stand for objects, and their arrangement shows a possible arrangement of objects. The proposition's **sense** is its agreement and disagreement with possibilities of existence and non-existence of states of affairs (4.2); a proposition is true if things stand as it says, false if not, and meaningful either way — **bipolarity**, the capacity for truth and for falsity, is the mark of a genuine proposition. Ordinary language, Wittgenstein argues, disguises this structure under a misleading surface grammar (4.002, 3.323–3.325): the same word signifies in different ways, different words in the same way, and philosophical confusion is the predictable result. Clarity requires analysis — decomposition of the proposition into elementary propositions whose names reach the simple objects — until the picturing relation stands bare.

### Logical form, and saying versus showing

The system's hinge is a self-limiting theorem. A picture represents its object from outside its own standpoint; it cannot place itself outside its form of representation (2.173–2.174). So a proposition can represent the whole of reality but cannot represent what it must have in common with reality in order to represent it — logical form (4.12). Propositions cannot represent logical form; it is mirrored in them (4.121). What is mirrored cannot instead be stated: "What can be shown, cannot be said" (4.1212).

This **saying/showing** distinction then does work everywhere. The propositions of logic are tautologies: they say nothing (6.1) — but in their saying nothing they show the logical structure of language and world, as contradictions show it by absurdity (4.461). What logic shows cannot be said; the propositions that try to say it — about form, about the world as a whole, about value, and (Wittgenstein is explicit) the propositions of the *Tractatus* itself — are not false but nonsensical, and function, if at all, as elucidations: rungs of a ladder to be thrown away once climbed (6.54). Argument 2 of [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) reconstructs this and presents the still-open war over how to read it.

### Truth-functions and the general form, in outline

All propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions (5): an elementary proposition asserts the existence of a state of affairs, and every other proposition is built by truth-functional operations on these. Wittgenstein's economy here is famous: a single operation — joint negation, the **N-operator** — suffices to generate all truth-functions, by successive application to elementary propositions (5.5), and proposition 6 states the general form of a truth-function, and thereby of a proposition as such, in one formula. The consequences are austere. Logic needs no special "logical objects" or laws of its own subject-matter: its propositions are tautologies, true under every assignment, showing the scaffolding of the world rather than describing any part of it (6.1, 6.12). Mathematics is a logical method, its equations pseudo-propositions (6.2). Causality, induction, and the "laws of nature" belong to the *form* of our descriptions, not to necessities in the world (6.3ff.); the modern worldview's superstition is to mistake the net's mesh for the surface it maps (6.34–6.342).

### The limits of language and the mystical

The boundary drawn by the picture theory is not merely technical. Propositions picture facts; therefore nothing about the world as a whole, nothing about value, can be a proposition's content. "The sense of the world must lie outside the world" (6.41). Ethics cannot be put into words; ethics is transcendental, and ethics and aesthetics are one (6.421). What cannot be stated makes itself manifest: "It is not *how* things are in the world that is mystical, but *that* it exists" (6.44); feeling the world as a limited whole — that is the mystical (6.45). The solution of the problem of life is seen in the vanishing of the problem (6.521), and the book's last proposition draws the moral for speech itself (7). In a 1919 letter to his publisher Ludwig von Ficker, Wittgenstein said the book's point was ethical and that its important part was the one he had *not* written. Read against the system, that is not a paradox: the unsayable — God, value, the meaning of the whole — is not debunked by the limit but cordoned off from factual chatter and shown, in silence, the respect of not being mangled into pseudo-facts. The compressed remarks on the self (5.6–5.64), in which the metaphysical subject figures as a limit of the world rather than an item in it, belong here too and are reconstructed as Argument 12.

### The strains the system already shows

Three tensions are visible in the text itself, and the dossier names them because the later philosophy grows from them. (i) **Independence versus exclusion.** Elementary propositions must be independent (2.061–2.062), yet Wittgenstein himself notes that two colors cannot be at the same place in the visual field, "indeed logically impossible" (6.3751) — and red and green exclude each other in a way that makes each color-proposition's truth settle another's falsity. (ii) **Simples demanded but unexhibited.** The argument for objects is transcendental, not empirical: no candidate simple survived his own scrutiny, and the theory of meaning rests on items no one, including its author, could produce. (iii) **Self-application.** The ladder of 6.54 either saves the book by an honest self-indictment or convicts it of trying to have ineffable doctrine both ways; the resolute and traditional readings of that indictment still divide the scholarship (see Argument 2).

## The turn

### What broke, and when

Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge in 1929 already doubting. Frank Ramsey's criticisms — on identity, on mathematics, and above all on the treatment of elementary propositions — had found their mark in conversations before Ramsey's early death in 1930. The immediate occasion of the return, on Norman Malcolm's memoir account, included a lecture by the intuitionist mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer in Vienna in March 1928, which reawakened his urge to do philosophy. Within months of returning, he published "Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929) — his only philosophical paper of the period — attempting a patch: atomic propositions involving color would carry degrees, so exclusion could be structural. He abandoned the patch almost at once, and its failure taught him the deeper lesson: the whole idea that language's logical structure lay hidden, awaiting a final analysis, had been the mistake. Malcolm also reports the anecdote that became the turn's emblem: the economist Piero Sraffa, after hearing the *Tractatus* doctrine that a proposition and what it describes must share a form, brushed his chin with his fingertips in the Neapolitan gesture of contempt and asked what the logical form of *that* was. The story is anecdote, mediated by a memoir, and should be weighed as such; its point, that meaning lives in gestures, contexts, and uses that the picture theory could not house, is the turn in one image.

### The calculus conception abandoned

In retrospect Wittgenstein diagnosed his early error as a **calculus conception** of language: the idea that language operates like a calculus, according to exact, surveyable rules laid down in advance, with meaning fixed by correlation to objects and correctness by conformity to the hidden rule-book. The diagnosis is the *Investigations'* own: in philosophy "we often compare the use of words with games and calculi which have fixed rules, but cannot say that someone who is using language must be playing such a game" (§81). The crystalline purity of logic, the later book argues, was never a discovery about language; it was a requirement we imposed on it — "a preconceived idea to which reality must correspond" (§107, §108). On the slippery ice of that requirement there is no friction and we cannot walk; the remedy is to return "back to the rough ground" of actual use (§107). Analysis was supposed to reveal the hidden structure beneath practice; the turn relocates everything philosophy needs to the practice itself, in plain view, only unarranged.

### Philosophy turns anthropological

What replaces the calculus is attention to language as a human activity — to what one scholar tradition calls its **natural history**: commanding, questioning, recounting, chatting are "as much a part of our natural history as walking, eating, drinking, playing" (§25). Words are learned in training, used in activities, corrected within practices shared by creatures with common reactions. Philosophy's evidence is accordingly no longer a formal system but the phenomena of our life with words: invented primitive cases, remembered ordinary uses, comparisons. The description of language-games, forms of life, and practices in the next section is therefore not a new metaphysics bolted on where the old one failed. It is a change of evidence and of aim — from construction to description — and it governs everything Wittgenstein wrote after 1929.

## The later method and doctrines

### Language-games

The later work's working unit is the **language-game**: "the whole, consisting of the language and the actions into which it is woven" (§7). The builders of §2 — a builder calling "block," "pillar," "slab," "beam," an assistant bringing the stones he has been trained to bring — are the first and simplest model: a complete primitive language in which the words patently work without naming inner or outer objects in the Augustinian sense. Section 23's famous list gives the real diversity: giving orders and obeying them, describing, reporting an event, speculating, making up a story, play-acting, solving an arithmetical problem, translating, asking, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying. Language-games are introduced as "objects of comparison" (§130) — simplified models that illuminate actual language by similarity and dissimilarity, not as essences of it or baby versions of it. The concept's discipline is negative as much as positive: anyone who thinks they have found the one thing all language-games share has missed the point of the examples, which is the next doctrine.

### Meaning as use

The later work's most quoted sentence is also its most carefully qualified. Section 43: "For a large class of cases of the employment of the word 'meaning' — though not for all — this word can be explained in this way: the meaning of a word is its use in the language." Every word of the hedge is load-bearing. Wittgenstein is not replacing the Augustinian essence ("meaning = object named") with a new essence ("meaning = use"). He is explaining how the *word* "meaning" can be handled for a large class of cases: when puzzled about what a word means, look not for a correlated entity — object, mental image, act of meaning — but at the word's employment, its role in the language-games where it lives. The tools image of §11 makes the same point functionally: the words of language are like the tools in a toolbox, hammer and glue-pot and rule, whose unity is not one function but a family of jobs. Where the Augustinian picture survives inside sophisticated theories — a name must secure its bearer, a description its satisfaction — the correction is applied case by case, as with the name "Moses," which Wittgenstein suggests we treat as resting on a cluster of descriptions rather than one fixed correlation (§79). Argument 4 reconstructs the move; Argument 3 shows it at work against its target.

### Family resemblance

Sections 65–77 are the method's showcase. Challenged to say what is common to all language-games, Wittgenstein turns the challenge on its home ground: consider the proceedings we call *games* (§66). Do not say there must be something common; look and see. Board games, card games, ball games, Olympic games, patience, a child throwing a ball against a wall and catching it: winning and losing appear and vanish; skill matters here, luck there; amusement is present throughout, perhaps, but in ring-a-ring-a-roses the amusement is not a definition's discovery. What we see is "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing," **family resemblances** (§67), as with the build, features, eye color, gait, and temperament that run through a family without any single one appearing in every member. The thread image (§67): a thread's strength lies not in one fiber running its whole length, but in the overlapping of many fibers. Border cases are not defects awaiting repair: a concept with blurred edges (§71) is still a concept — tennis has no rule for how high a ball may be thrown and is a game all the same (§68) — and a boundary drawn for a special purpose is a tool we make, not a line we find (§69). Family resemblance is a counter-example to the Socratic demand, renewed by Frege and embodied in the *Tractatus*, that a concept is understood only when its necessary and sufficient conditions are produced; Argument 5 gives it as an argument.

### Private language

Sections 243–315 attack the picture that the language-game of sensation — "pain," "tickle," the inner life generally — consists of naming objects in a private theater, known to the speaker alone. A language whose words referred to what can be known only to the speaker, so that no one else could in principle understand it (§243), would have to fix its signs somehow: the test case is the diarist of §258, who writes "S" when a certain sensation occurs, fixing "S" by an act of inward attention — a private ostensive definition. But no use is thereby established, because there is no difference in the case between the sensation's recurring and its seeming to the diarist to recur: "whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'." Memory cannot repair the omission (§265: checking a memory by another memory impression is like buying several copies of the same newspaper to confirm its report). The positive account (§244) runs the other way: words for sensations are bound up with the primitive, natural expressions of sensation — the cry, the wince — and are taught as replacements and refinements of them. The full case is Arguments 6 and 8; the target throughout is a *picture of meaning*, not the reality of the inner life, which Wittgenstein repeatedly insists he is not denying.

### Rule-following

The hinge between the language sections and the private language sections is the long consideration of rules (§§185–242). A rule seems to reach ahead of any finite instruction and determine its own applications in unlimited new cases. Wittgenstein's pupil (§185), trained in the +2 series to 1000, continues past 1000 with 1004, 1008 and insists he is going on the same way. Everything finite that he was given — examples, formula, corrections — fits more than one rule, diverging on unencountered cases. Section 201 states the resulting paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule — and with equal right, to conflict with it. The resolution is immediate and must not be amputated from the paradox, as it often is in summary: "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases" (§201). Interpretations — formulae, pictures, mental signs — are themselves signs; a sign does not interpret itself; the regress of interpretations stops not at a further inner item but at **practice**: training, custom, the technique of going on, where justifications run out and, as §217 puts it, the spade is turned at bedrock: "This is simply what I do." Obeying a rule is a practice, and one cannot obey a rule privately, because obeying and seeming to oneself to obey would collapse into one (§202). Argument 7 reconstructs this, with Saul Kripke's celebrated skeptical rewriting flagged as interpretation rather than text.

### Aspect perception

The discussion of **seeing-as** in Part II, §xi (in the fourth edition, *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*) applies the method to perception. The duck-rabbit — a drawing, adapted from a puzzle picture popularized by the psychologist Joseph Jastrow, that can be seen now as a duck, now as a rabbit — stays fixed while what is seen transforms: the beak becomes ears; the creature now faces the other way. Wittgenstein distinguishes *seeing that* something is so, which is reportable and checkable, from *seeing an aspect*, which dawns, is reported by comparison ("now it's a rabbit"), and is subject to the will as ordinary seeing is not — you can try to see it as the rabbit, on instruction. Two temptations are refused together. An aspect is not seen by first seeing neutral lines and then adding an interpretation: when the aspect dawns you see a rabbit, outright — his phrase is an "echo of a thought in sight." Nor is it explained by a second, inner picture that changed while the outer stayed put; positing one repeats, for perception, the private-object picture dismantled in the sensation sections. Continuous seeing of an aspect is distinguished from its dawning, and **aspect-blindness** — a supposed inability to experience the dawning — is explored as a limit case akin to meaning-blindness, the inability to experience a word's meaning. Argument 9 gives the reconstruction. The payoff is methodological: aspect-seeing straddles perception and thought, and its refusal to fit either box is a discovery about our concepts, not a failure to decide.

### Forms of life

Beneath language-games lies the **form of life**: "to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life" (§19). The phrase names the shared background of activity, reaction, and circumstance within which any particular speaking makes sense — what is given, prior to justification, when we speak and understand. Agreement in language, Wittgenstein argues late in Part I, is not agreement in opinions but agreement in judgments and in a form of life (§§241–242): the convergence of responses — in counting the same way, calling the same thing red, finding the same continuation obvious — without which argument, and even disagreement, could not start. If a lion could talk, we could not understand him: his form of life is not ours. What has to be accepted, the given, is forms of life (Part II). Interpreters dispute the term's center of gravity — biological (the shared animal constitution) or cultural (the shared weave of practices) — and the text supports elements of both; the dossier notes the dispute and uses the term in the way the learning path does: as the name for where explanation, in this philosophy, runs out into life as it is actually shared.

### Philosophy as therapy

The method remarks (§§89–133) state the enterprise. Philosophy's problems are not empirical; they are solved "by arranging what we have always known" (§109), by assembling reminders of use for a particular purpose. We must do away with explanation and let description take its place (§109). The task is to bring words back "from their metaphysical to their everyday use" (§116) — words taken on holiday, idling like an engine out of gear, generate the problems (§38, §132). A **perspicuous representation** (§122) — a surveyable arrangement of a word's uses — produces the understanding that dissolves a puzzle by letting us see its source. Philosophy "leaves everything as it is" (§124): it does not legislate for language, reform it, or underpin it with theory. There is not one method but methods, "like different therapies" (§133); the philosopher treats a question like an illness (§255); and the famous aim is modest and complete at once: "to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (§309). Whether this therapeutic quietism is the liberation of philosophy from pseudo-problems or its abdication before real ones is a standing dispute — Russell, who admired the early work, detested this conception — and Argument 10 presents it as one.

## The On Certainty coda

Wittgenstein's last writings (1949–51, posthumously *On Certainty*, 1969) turn the late method on epistemology, in response to G. E. Moore's attempted proofs of the external world ("here is one hand, and here is another"). Wittgenstein's claim is that Moore's certainties — that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before my birth — are misclassified by Moore and skeptic alike as known propositions resting on evidence. They function differently. Doubt itself presupposes certainty: "If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty" (§115). The propositions exempt from doubt are **hinges**: "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (§341); "if I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put" (§343). His other image (§§94–99) is the **river-bed**: propositions of the form of empirical propositions harden into a channel for the fluid ones that inquiry tests; the mythology may change back into flux, "the river-bed of thoughts may shift," but "I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other" (§97; the bank, §99 adds, is partly hard rock, partly sand that washes away and is deposited). At the foundation of well-founded belief lies belief that is not founded (§253); certainty at the base is enacted — shown in how we act and judge — rather than established. The contemporary field of *hinge epistemology* (Duncan Pritchard, Crispin Wright, Annalisa Coliva, in different constructions) develops these remarks into rival theories, and their disagreements — are hinges truth-apt? propositional? rationally evaluable? — are disputes *about* the remarks, not doctrine in them. Argument 11 states the case.

## One thinker, or two?

The library splits the entries; the scholarship splits the man, and this file closes by presenting the split without settling it. For **rupture**: the author himself speaks of grave mistakes in the old way of thinking, abandons the picture theory, logical atomism, the hidden-structure ideal of analysis, and the saying/showing machinery; the difference of method — deduction versus description, system versus album — could hardly be greater; "Wittgenstein I" and "Wittgenstein II" are the trade's standard labels, and some speak of a third in the author of *On Certainty*. For **continuity**: the later criticisms are almost all pre-aimed at positions only the *Tractatus* had made attractive, so the second philosophy is partly unintelligible without the first; the therapeutic conception of philosophy as activity rather than doctrine is already proposition 4.112 of the early book; and on the **resolute** reading of the *Tractatus* (Cora Diamond, James Conant), the early book never offered the metaphysics the rupture story says was abandoned — its propositions were always rungs to discard — so that what changes in 1929 is less the philosophy than its technique. On the **traditional** reading (P. M. S. Hacker, among others), there really was a metaphysics of logical form to surrender, and the resolute reading dissolves real doctrine into therapy anachronistically. A further, quieter continuity case runs through the middle period: the transitional manuscripts of 1929–33 show the doctrines eroding step by step rather than in a single conversion. The Preface's publishing wish — old and new together, the new seen against the old "in the right light" — presupposes contrast and continuity at once, which is perhaps the only verdict the evidence forces: two philosophies, indisputably; one lifelong quarrel with bewitchment by language, just as indisputably.

## Key terms

In the format of the repo [glossary](../../glossary.md), restricted to Wittgenstein's own usage across both periods, with later textbook senses flagged where they mislead.

- **Fact / state of affairs (*Sachverhalt*):** early — what is the case; a configuration of objects in a determinate relation. The world is the totality of facts, not of things (1, 1.1, 2). Not "an event in ordinary talk": a state of affairs may fail to exist, and then it is a possible fact, part of reality (2.06, 2.063).
- **Object (simple):** early — the subsistent, unalterable constituents of states of affairs, whose possible combinations are their form (2.02–2.033). Demanded by the argument for determinacy of sense (2.021–2.0212); no example is ever settled. Later writers' talk of "logical atoms" as a discovered inventory reads a requirement as a finding.
- **Picture (*Bild*):** early — a model of reality whose elements correspond to objects and whose structure represents a possible structure of objects (2.12–2.15); every picture is also a logical picture (2.182). Not a mental image or resemblance-portrait: what matters is shared form, not likeness.
- **Logical form:** early — what a picture must have in common with reality to depict it at all (2.18); mirrored in propositions but not representable by them (4.121). The early system's load-bearing term, and the late work's prime example of a metaphysical mirage.
- **Sense; bipolarity:** early — a proposition's sense is its agreement and disagreement with possibilities of existence and non-existence of states of affairs (4.2); a genuine proposition must be capable of truth and of falsity. A sentence incapable of falsehood is not profound but, on this view, not a factual proposition at all.
- **Saying / showing:** early — propositions *say* that things stand thus; what they *show* — logical form, and with it the mystical — cannot be said (4.121–4.1212). The distinction the resolute–traditional dispute is fought over: conduit of ineffable truths, or the ladder's first rung?
- **Tautology:** early — a proposition true under all truth-possibilities of its components; it says nothing and shows the logic of the world (4.46–4.461, 6.1). Not "a trivial remark": logic's whole content lives here, as structure made visible.
- **The mystical:** early — that the world exists, and the feeling of it as a limited whole (6.44–6.45); what cannot be put into words but makes itself manifest (6.522). A dimension Wittgenstein protects by silence, not a doctrine he teaches.
- **Language-game:** late — the whole of language and the actions into which it is woven (§7); also the simplified models used as objects of comparison (§130). Not "a game played with words" in the recreational sense, and not a technical term with a hidden essence — its own concept is family-resemblance all the way down.
- **Meaning as use:** late — the explanation of "meaning," for a large class of cases though not for all, as a word's use in the language (§43). A redirection of attention from correlates to employment, hedged by its author; quoting it without the hedge reverses its point.
- **Family resemblance:** late — the relation among members of a concept whose similarities overlap and criss-cross without one feature common to all (§§65–67). Not "vagueness" and not license: blurred edges still exclude most things.
- **Ostensive definition:** teaching a word by pointing and naming. Late — shown to presuppose a stage already set: the learner must know what *kind* of item is being named (§§28–33). Cannot be language's foundation, since it works only inside a practice.
- **Rule-following:** late — what is at issue in §§185–242: how a rule determines unlimited applications, answered not by an interpretation but by a practice of going on (§201). "Grasping a rule" names mastery of a technique, exhibited, not an inner act.
- **Private language:** late — a supposed language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, unintelligible in principle to others (§243). Wittgenstein's conclusion is that the supposition dissolves: without a criterion of correctness there is no language (§258). The term is his; the label "the private language argument" is the literature's.
- **Aspect; seeing-as:** late (Part II, §xi) — the dawning or continuous presence of an organization in what is seen, as with the duck-rabbit; neither plain seeing nor seeing plus interpretation — "the echo of a thought in sight." **Aspect-blindness** is its imagined absence.
- **Form of life:** late — the shared pattern of activity and reaction within which a language has its life (§19; §§241–242); the given, where explanation runs out. Disputed between biological and cultural readings; both find text.
- **Perspicuous representation (*übersichtliche Darstellung*):** late — an arrangement of the uses of a word that lets us survey them and see a confusion's source (§122). The later philosophy's product: not a theory, but a way of laying things out.
- **Hinge:** *On Certainty* — a proposition exempt from doubt in practice, on which questions and doubts turn (§§341–343); belonging to the river-bed rather than the waters (§§94–99). Not "a belief we are very sure of": hinges are not known, doubted, or justified in the game's ordinary sense at all.
