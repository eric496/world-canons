# Wittgenstein: The Arguments

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entries: [Wittgenstein (early)](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md) · [Wittgenstein (late)](../../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md) · The concepts these arguments use: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Shorter reconstructions of Arguments 3–7 appear in the [Investigations study guide](../../study/wittgenstein-investigations.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — Wittgenstein's own where he gave one, the literature's where he didn't, labeled as such. Where scholars dispute what an argument even is, the dispute is presented as a dispute. Citation practice: the *Tractatus* by decimal proposition, *Philosophical Investigations* Part I by section (§), its Part II by section in Anscombe's arrangement (§xi), *On Certainty* by section (OC §). Several of these are arguments Wittgenstein later turned against himself; the dossier reports the mature verdict where he delivered one.

---

**Argument 1. The picture theory: a proposition means what it does by picturing a possible fact**

1. The world is the totality of facts, not of things (1.1): what is the case is the existence of states of affairs, determinate combinations of simple objects (2, 2.01, 2.031–2.033).
2. We picture facts to ourselves (2.1). A picture is a model of reality (2.12): its elements are the representatives of objects (2.13), and the elements' standing in a determinate relation to one another represents the objects as so related (2.15). A picture is itself a fact (2.141).
3. Depiction is possible only because picture and pictured share a form: what any picture must have in common with reality, in order to depict it at all — rightly or falsely — is logical form, the form of reality (2.18); a picture has the logical form of representation in common with what it depicts (2.2).
4. A thought is a logical picture of facts (3); a proposition is a picture of reality (4.01), presenting the existence and non-existence of states of affairs (4.1).
5. A picture agrees with reality or fails to agree; it is correct or incorrect, true or false (2.21), and its truth is settled by comparison with reality, never from the picture alone (2.223–2.224).
Therefore, a proposition has sense by depicting a possible state of affairs: its sense is its agreement and disagreement with possibilities of existence and non-existence of states of affairs (4.2), and propositions can be true or false only insofar as they are pictures of reality (4.06). Bipolarity — capacity for truth and for falsity — is the essence of the proposition.

**Textual basis.** *Tractatus* 1.1–2.2, 2.21–2.225, 3–3.1, 4.01–4.06; the demand for simple objects at 2.021–2.0212.

**Objection.** Pressed first by Frank Ramsey and then by Wittgenstein himself: the system cannot house elementary propositions that are *not* independent. "This is red all over" and "This is green all over" exclude each other, yet both are candidates for elementary status; if one settles the other's falsity, premise 1's independence of states of affairs (2.061–2.062) fails — a difficulty Wittgenstein already registers at 6.3751 and confronted openly in 1929. The second pressure: the analysis is never delivered. No elementary proposition, no simple object, is ever exhibited, so the picturing chain's bottom link — names correlated to objects — is a postulate, and critics ask why a theory of meaning should terminate in items demanded by the theory rather than found by it. The third pressure is generality: commands, questions, thanks, and prayers do not obviously picture anything, so either they lack sense (an implausible austerity) or picturing is one function of language among others.

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's own first reply was scope: the theory is a theory of the *proposition*, of factual discourse, and other uses of words were never claimed as pictures. His second, in "Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929), attempted a repair for color-exclusion by letting elementary propositions carry degree; he abandoned it within the year, judging — as the later work's verdict runs — that the fault lay deeper, in the demand for a final analysis and a hidden form. The literature's best defense of the argument as it stands (in the line of David Pears) stresses premise 2's subtlety: a picture is a *model*, related to its object by a rule of projection, not by resemblance, so the theory is untouched by the observation that sentences do not look like facts; and the simples, on this defense, are a structural requirement of determinate sense rather than an empirical inventory. The defense is real, but it defends the theory as the *Tractatus* left it — and the dossier records, as part of the argument's history, that its author is its most consequential critic: the *Investigations* is, in large part, this objection developed into a method.

**Argument 2. Saying and showing: the ladder, and what to do with it**

1. A proposition represents a fact by sharing logical form with it (2.18, 4.01).
2. But a proposition cannot represent what it must have in common with reality in order to represent it: it would have to station itself outside its own form of representation (4.12; compare 2.173–2.174). Propositions cannot represent logical form: it is mirrored in them (4.121).
3. What a proposition cannot represent, it *shows*: propositions show the logical form of reality, they display it; and "what can be shown, cannot be said" (4.121, 4.1212). The propositions of logic accordingly say nothing — they are tautologies — while showing the world's scaffolding (4.461, 6.1). Ethics likewise cannot be put into words (6.421).
4. The *Tractatus*'s own propositions, however, attempt to state truths about picturing, form, world, and self — about precisely what, by step 3, can only be shown. By the book's own criterion they lack sense.
5. Their function is therefore not assertion but elucidation: "My propositions serve as elucidations in the following way: anyone who understands me eventually recognizes them as nonsensical, when he has used them — as steps — to climb up beyond them. (He must, so to speak, throw away the ladder after he has climbed up it.)" (6.54, Pears–McGuinness translation.)
Therefore, the book's doctrine about itself is self-consuming by design: whoever understands its author sees the world rightly and passes over in silence what cannot be spoken (6.54, 7). The Preface frames the whole as the drawing of a limit — to the expression of thoughts, from inside language.

**Textual basis.** *Tractatus*, Preface; 2.172–2.174; 4.12–4.1212; 4.461; 6.1; 6.42–6.421; 6.54; 7.

**Objection.** Ramsey's deflation is the classic counter: "what we can't say we can't whistle either" — if the book's sentences are nonsense, they cannot convey a vision of logical form, and the ladder metaphor banks the content of propositions officially declared void. The objection divides into the two great rival readings, which this dossier flags and does not settle. On the **traditional (ineffability) reading** — defended systematically by P. M. S. Hacker — step 3 marks a real distinction: there are genuine truths about logical form, value, and the world as a whole which language shows but cannot state, and the ladder's nonsense is *illuminating* nonsense. On the **resolute reading** — Cora Diamond and James Conant are its architects — there is no second kind of nonsense and no ineffable doctrine: the book's propositions are plain nonsense, the appearance that they convey truths is the final illusion the therapy removes, and to keep a body of shown "truths" is, in Diamond's phrase, to chicken out. Middle positions also have defenders. The dispute is not decorative: the readings disagree about what the *Tractatus* is, what the later philosophy continues, and what "understanding the author" could mean.

**Reply.** There is no single reply, because the objection *is* the dispute described above; the dossier's duty is to state each side's best move. The traditionalist's strongest card is the von Ficker letter and 6.522 — "there are, indeed, things that cannot be put into words; they make themselves manifest" — language of manifestation that seems pointless if nothing at all is conveyed, together with the book's plain intent to mark off an ethical dimension it treats as the important one. The resolute reader's strongest card is textual discipline: 6.54 says *nonsensical*, without qualification or taxonomy, and 4.1212's prohibition is unrestricted — a reading that salvages sayable-in-showing truths must explain why the ladder passage licenses keeping exactly the philosophy it instructs us to throw away. Wittgenstein never adjudicated; his later writings criticize the *Tractatus*'s doctrines without ever endorsing either party's theory of its nonsense. Choose a side if the capstone asks — but know that you are choosing.

**Argument 3. The Augustinian picture dismantled**

1. The *Investigations* opens with Augustine's account, in the *Confessions* (I.8), of learning language: elders named objects and moved toward them, and the child grasped that the sounds designated the things. Wittgenstein extracts the picture it embodies: every word has a meaning; the meaning is the object for which the word stands; words name objects; sentences are combinations of such names (§1).
2. The picture predicts that naming is language's foundation and essence. But consider the builders (§2): A calls "block," "pillar," "slab," "beam"; B brings the stones he has been trained to bring. Conceive this as a complete primitive language. The words function — orders are given, understood, obeyed — while their role is that of calls within an activity, not names correlated with objects by a meaning-relation; even §1's own shopkeeper, fetching "five red apples," must count, match a sample, and act, none of which "standing for an object" describes.
3. The picture's favored mechanism, ostensive definition — pointing and saying "this is called —" — does not fix meaning by itself. Point at a pencil, and shape, color, material, number, and object are all candidates for what is named; the lesson succeeds only because the learner already commands a stage-setting, a grammar of what kind of thing is being picked out (§§28–31; explaining the chess king works only for someone who already knows a piece in a game is in question, §31).
4. The picture also smuggles. Augustine describes the child's learning as if the child arrived like a traveler in a foreign country — "as if the child already had a language, only not this one," as if it could already think, only not yet speak (§32): the supposedly fundamental process presupposes the conceptual apparatus it was meant to explain.
Therefore, naming is one language-game among others, and its apparent foundational role rests on presuppositions the picture cannot supply. The Augustinian conception describes a region of language — and not even that region's mechanism accurately — taken falsely for the whole.

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §§1–2 (the Augustine quotation and the builders), §§28–33 (ostensive definition and the stage-setting; §32 on Augustine's child), with the toolbox at §11 and the diversity list at §23.

**Objection.** Two fronts. First, fairness: Augustine is telling a conversion memoir, not proposing a theory of meaning, and Wittgenstein seems to attack a fifth-century saint for failing to anticipate the philosophy of language. Second, sophistication: the picture is a straw man for any serious theory after Frege, who distinguished sense from reference precisely so meaning would not collapse into the object named — the refutation, if it works, works against a position nobody competent held.

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's reply to the fairness charge is in the text's own care: §1 does not accuse Augustine of error about his childhood; it says the words "give us a particular picture of the essence of human language," and the target is the picture — a template that presents itself as plain common sense, in Augustine innocently and therefore visibly. To the sophistication charge, the reply is that pictures outlive their refutation in theories: the *Tractatus* itself, written with full command of Frege, built everything on names standing for objects and sentences as their combinations, and the builders case is aimed at that inheritance. A picture is dismantled not by naming a holder but by exhibiting cases it cannot govern: one complete primitive language working without its mechanism is enough to deny the mechanism essence-status. What survives the objection — and should be conceded — is that §1 does not touch a theory of reference designed for formal languages on its own terms; it dissolves a temptation about language as such.

**Argument 4. Meaning as use — with the author's own hedge**

1. The Augustinian mechanism fails as a general account (Argument 3): the words of a language do irreducibly diverse jobs — ordering, describing, reporting, joking, calculating, thanking, praying (§23) — as the tools in a toolbox do (§11), and no single relation to objects covers the diversity.
2. What is common and surveyable across this diversity is each word's employment: the role it plays in the language-games in which it is at home, the techniques of its application, correction, and response (§§7, 23).
3. Where an account of "meaning" is wanted, then, redirect the inquiry: do not ask what entity the word stands for — object, image, inner act — but how the word is used.
Therefore (§43): "For a large class of cases of the employment of the word 'meaning' — though not for all — this word can be explained in this way: the meaning of a word is its use in the language." The qualification is part of the conclusion: the remark explains the word "meaning" for a large class of cases; it does not legislate a new essence of meaning to replace the old one.

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §43; the supporting case-work at §§1–38 and §79 (the name "Moses" held by a cluster of descriptions rather than one fixed correlation).

**Objection.** "Use" is accused of being too slack to be an explanation: everything a word is ever involved in cannot be its meaning, and unless use is restricted to something like truth-conditions or reference, the slogan licenses the undergraduate inference that words mean whatever anyone does with them. A second objection presses the excluded cases: proper names and words for the absent or unobservable seem to work precisely by correlation, as §43's own "not for all" concedes; and a third asks whether the explanation is circular — describing a word's use already requires identifying it as the same word, used correctly, which seems to presuppose the meaning under explanation.

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's defense is built into the stated ambition: he is not offering a theory of meaning of the kind the objection demands, because the demand — produce the single thing meaning *is* — is the Augustinian craving in modern dress. "Use" is not individual behavior but use in a practice, with training, standards, and correction, which is why the license inference fails: a use answerable to nothing is §258's diarist, whose word has collapsed for exactly that reason. On names, §79 shows the treatment in action — the name functions through a cluster of descriptions held with varying firmness, "a whole cloud of philosophy condensed into a drop of grammar" (Part II) being the method's own summary of such work. On circularity, the reply (developed by the literature) is that the explanation is not reductive: §43 does not define meaning in non-semantic terms but redirects a philosophical question to the place where answers actually live — the observable circumstances of employment. Judging this reply requires judging the therapeutic conception itself (Argument 10); here it is enough that the hedge disqualifies the standard undergraduate reading all by itself.

**Argument 5. Family resemblance: a concept without an essence still holds together**

1. The essentialist method demands: if we understand a concept, there must be something common to all its instances, stateable as necessary and sufficient conditions — the demand an interlocutor presses at §65 ("you talk about all sorts of language-games, but have nowhere said what is common to them all").
2. Take the test case Wittgenstein chooses, *games* (§66): board games, card games, ball games, Olympic games, patience, ring-a-ring-a-roses, a child throwing a ball at a wall. "Don't think, but look!" Winning and losing occur in chess and patience but not in the child's ball play; skill is central in chess, displaced by luck in roulette; amusement runs through many and takes different forms in each. Similarities crop up and disappear as we survey.
3. No single feature runs through all and only games — and yet the concept functions: speakers apply it, extend it to new cases (video games, war games), and correct each other's misuse without any definition in hand.
4. Its unity is of another kind: "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing" (§67) — family resemblances, like the build, features, eye color, gait, and temperament that run through a family with no one feature in every member; and, in the thread image, strength without one fiber running the whole length (§67). Where a purpose needs a sharp boundary, we draw one (§69); that we can draw it does not mean it was there, and the concept's blurred edges (§71) are usability, not failure.
Therefore, at least some central concepts — *game*, and by extension *language* and *language-game* themselves — are united by family resemblance, not by a common essence. Definition is a tool for purposes, not the precondition of understanding, and the craving for essence is a picture to be examined rather than a standard to be met.

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §§65–77 (survey at §66, family resemblance and thread at §67, boundaries at §§68–71, drawing a boundary at §77).

**Objection.** The generalization objection: *game* may be an unlucky, deliberately messy case, while *knowledge*, *number*, or *gold* have essences — experiential, mathematical, or microphysical. Philosophers add that absence of a *surveyable* common feature in 1950s examples does not prove none exists; essences can hide (as the essence of water hid in H₂O), and Wittgenstein's "look and see" settles only what meets the eye. The sorites-style worry follows: if boundaries are drawn by us, conceptual objectivity seems lost — who arbitrates a disputed extension?

**Reply.** The argument's claim is methodological, and its modesty is the reply: it does not legislate that no concept has an essence; it removes the *a priori* guarantee that one awaits discovery, and shifts the burden case by case — look and see. Natural-kind cases are conceded by the form of the argument itself: nothing in §§65–67 denies that chemistry finds unity in microstructure; the point is that game-like, practice-embedded concepts — including most of philosophy's own vocabulary — present no hiding place of that kind, and their investigation by definition-hunting has a documented record of sterility (the fate of definitions of *knowledge* after Gettier being the modern exhibit). Disputed extensions are arbitrated the way they visibly are: by appeal to paradigms, purposes, and analogy, which is how the concept lives — the thread holds because the fibers overlap in constrained patterns, not because anything goes. Whether the moral extends to *language* itself is the argument's largest stake, and it is carried by the §23 survey, not by the single case of games.

**Argument 6. The private language argument: no criterion, no language**

1. Suppose a language whose words refer to what can be known only to the speaker — his immediate private sensations — so that another person cannot understand it, not as an uncracked code but in principle (§243).
2. Such a language must fix its signs somehow. The proposed fixing is inner ostensive definition: the diarist concentrates his attention on a sensation, impressing the connection between sign and sensation on himself, and resolves to write "S" whenever that sensation recurs (§258).
3. A sign has a use only if there is a difference between using it correctly and merely seeming to oneself to use it correctly — the general condition on rules (§201–202; Argument 7).
4. In the diarist's set-up there is no such difference. No independent criterion of the sensation's identity was laid down; the only check on a later writing of "S" is his impression that this sensation is the same again, and that impression is itself the thing needing a check. Appealing to memory does not repair this: justifying a memory by another memory-impression is like buying several copies of the morning paper to confirm what the first copy said (§265).
5. Hence Wittgenstein's compressed verdict: in this case whatever seems right to me is right — "and that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'" (§258).
Therefore, the supposed private language has no criterion of correct use and is not a language. Sensation-words instead have their life in public practice: they are connected with the natural expressions of sensation and used in their place — the child cries, and is taught exclamations and then sentences (§244).

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §§243–315, centrally §243 (the definition), §244 (natural expression), §258 (the diary), §265 (the newspaper), with §202 (private obedience to a rule) behind premise 3.

**Objection.** The counterexample objection: a lifelong castaway, or the diarist himself if we drop the stipulation that he never had a public language, seems able to keep a genuine sensation-diary — so privacy as such cannot be the defect. The verificationism objection (pressed by A. J. Ayer in an early review and often since): the argument seems to assume that correctness requires an independent, in-principle public check, which is a theory — verificationism — smuggled in as grammar; why must seeming right collapse into being right, unless meaning has already been defined as public checkability? The scope objection completes the trio: even granting the diary case, what has been refuted is one mechanism — private ostension — not the Cartesian picture of the mind wholesale, and the argument is routinely over-enlisted against solipsism and for conclusions about other minds which it does not draw.

**Reply.** On the counterexample, the Wittgensteinian reply is that it concedes the argument: the castaway's diary works because it is *not* private in §243's sense — it borrows its criteria, its very technique of application, from a public practice, and §243's stipulation precisely strips that borrowing away to test whether privacy alone can sustain a language. It cannot; that is the result. On verificationism, the defense associated with Gordon Baker and P. M. S. Hacker replies that no theory of verification is assumed: premise 3 is earned in the rule-following sections as a point about what a rule *is* — a technique of application — and a "technique" that one uncheckable impression both constitutes and certifies is a picture of a technique, not one. On scope, the honest reply is agreement with limits: the argument refutes a necessarily private language and the inner-ostension model of sensation-words; whether it also dissolves stronger Cartesian claims about the privacy of experience is genuinely contested, and the dossier marks that boundary rather than overclaiming. The argument denies no one's pain. It denies that "pain" gets its meaning by naming an object only its owner can inspect.

**Argument 7. The rule-following paradox: no interpretation reaches the ground**

1. To follow a rule — continue a series, apply a word to a new case — seems to require grasping what the rule demands in indefinitely many cases never yet encountered.
2. Everything available to fix that demand is finite: a formula, a table, a set of examples, training up to some point. Wittgenstein's pupil (§185), taught the +2 series to 1000, is told to go on and writes 1004, 1008, 1012, insisting he is going on in the same way — construing the instruction, as he can, as one that changes step after 1000.
3. Any finite material underdetermines the rule: on *some* interpretation, the pupil's continuation accords with what he was given, and so would indefinitely many deviant continuations. If grasping the rule is having an interpretation, the interpretation is itself a sign — and a sign requires, for its own application, a further interpretation, and so on without end (§198, §201).
4. Hence the paradox of §201: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule; and, Wittgenstein adds in the same breath, to conflict with it — leaving neither accord nor conflict.
5. The paradox rests on the premise built into step 3 — that grasp of a rule must be an interpretation. Withdraw it: "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases" (§201). Justifications terminate, when we press them down, at bedrock: "If I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: 'This is simply what I do'" (§217).
Therefore, meaning and correct action are not grounded in interpretations stacked behind signs, nor in private mental items, but in practice: "obeying a rule is a practice," and thinking one is obeying a rule is not obeying a rule (§202) — the ground of the distinction being training, custom, and the agreement in judgments (§§241–242) within a form of life.

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §§185–242; the paradox and its resolution both at §201; the signpost at §198; practice at §202; bedrock at §217; agreement at §§241–242. (The investigation study guide covers this material at length.)

**On Kripke's reading — flagged, per the standing interpretive dispute.** Saul Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) recasts §§185–242 as a *skeptical paradox* about meaning: his invented function "quus" agrees with "plus" on every sum so far computed but diverges above 56 and 57, and no fact about a speaker — past use, dispositions, inner states — constitutes having meant plus rather than quus. His "sceptical solution" relocates correctness in the community's conditions for licensed assertion. This is an *interpretation*, and a contested one: Kripke presented the argument as Wittgenstein's "as it struck me," not as commentary, and scholars such as Baker and Hacker argue the paradox in §201 is stated to be *dissolved* by rejecting its interpretive premise — not solved by a community theory. The label "Kripkenstein" marks the possibly distinct philosopher of Kripke's book. Nothing in the reconstruction above depends on Kripke; the text's own case is §185's pupil.

**Objection.** Setting Kripke aside, the text's own dissolution attracts the *communitarian* worry: if the seeming-right/being-right distinction needs a practice, and practices are communal, does a solitary rule-follower become impossible — and can a whole community not go wrong together, so that "right" reduces to "what we do around here"? A second objection finds the dissolution quietist: told that grasping is shown in practice, the puzzled reader asks *what in the practice* makes this continuation the right one, and is handed bedrock — which feels like the mystery relabeled, not removed.

**Reply.** On community: the text's requirement is a *practice* — a technique with standards internal to it — not a plebiscite. Sections 241–242 speak of agreement in judgments as the background of truth-talk, not of majority decision about each step; individualist readings in the literature argue that a single agent embedded in a technique can sustain the distinction Wittgenstein needs, and the community-reading remains one contested option among several. On quietism: the Wittgensteinian reply is that the request for a further ground, behind all criteria, asks to step outside every practice while continuing to use its words — the very posture §201's paradox discredits; bedrock is not a label for the mystery but the observation that justification is an activity with a natural terminus, and that the terminus is action (§217: "simply what I do"). Whether this dissolves the problem or declines it is — the dossier records it plainly — the crux on which the late work's admirers and its critics divide.

**Argument 8. The beetle in the box: the private object cancels out**

1. Suppose, as the private-object picture of sensation proposes, that the meaning of "pain" is the private item each speaker knows from his own case alone.
2. Wittgenstein's model case (§293): everyone has a box with something in it that we call a "beetle"; no one can look into anyone else's box, and everyone says he knows what a beetle is only by looking at his own.
3. Now let the contents vary: the thing in a box might change constantly, or be different in every box, or a box might be empty. The language-game with the word proceeds *identically* in every variant — no one ever checks, compares, or corrects by the contents.
4. What can vary without limit, or be absent, while the word's use is untouched, is contributing nothing to the word's meaning: the thing in the box "cancels out" — one can, in Wittgenstein's image, divide through by it.
Therefore, the private object is not the meaning of the sensation-word. If we construe the grammar of the expression of sensation on the model "object and designation," the object drops out of consideration as irrelevant (§293). What fixes "pain" is the weave of expression, circumstance, and response in which the word is taught and used (§244).

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §293 (the beetle), with §244 (natural expression) and the "not a something, but not a nothing either" verdict on the inner at §304.

**Objection.** The overreach objection: the argument seems to make the sensation itself — the felt quality, what-it-is-like — semantically and perhaps philosophically null, licensing the behaviorism Wittgenstein disavowed. If the contents cancel out, why does it matter to *me* whether the box holds agony or nothing? Defenders of qualia add an asymmetry objection: first-person sensation-talk does not behave like talk of a sample checked against instances, but that shows only that its grammar is *different*, not that its object is a fiction; a word could mean a privately known item even if no public check disciplines its use — as, arguably, my use of a private nickname for a recurring dream-image does.

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's reply is written into §304: the conclusion is not that a sensation is nothing, but that it is "not a something either" — not, that is, an *object* doing a name's work. The beetle argument's target is a model of *meaning*: if "pain" meant the boxed item, variation of the item would have to matter to the meaning, and it demonstrably does not matter to any use anyone makes of the word. The felt quality is untouched by the argument — what is removed is its candidacy as the word's referential anchor, the role in which it explained nothing. On the nickname case, the reply is §258's in miniature: a sign whose applications are checked by nothing, not even the practice of the dreamer's waking language from which its words are borrowed, divides through in exactly the beetle's way; real private coinages survive only by parasitism on public grammar. The literature's standard reservation stands and is recorded here: §293 is a compact model case, and moving from it to a general philosophy of mind requires the whole of §§243–315, not the box alone.

**Argument 9. Aspect-seeing: the duck-rabbit, and seeing what does not change**

1. A drawing can be seen as a duck or as a rabbit (the duck-rabbit, adapted from Jastrow). The lines do not change. Yet when the aspect flips, everything about what is seen changes: the beak becomes ears; the creature faces the other way.
2. This is not plain seeing plus an inference. When the aspect dawns, the subject does not examine neutral lines and conclude "rabbit": he *sees* a rabbit, immediately and perceptually — the experience is, in Wittgenstein's phrase, "the echo of a thought in sight." A report in terms of interpretation ("I take it as a rabbit") falsifies the phenomenon.
3. Nor is it plain seeing unassisted by concepts. Seeing an aspect can be taught, commanded, and attempted ("see it as a rabbit — try"), and it fails or succeeds; ordinary seeing does no such thing. One can continuously see the drawing as a duck, never having it dawn otherwise; and Wittgenstein explores **aspect-blindness** — the supposed inability to experience the dawning — as a real conceptual possibility, akin to being unable to experience the meaning of a word.
4. Positing an inner second picture, which changed while the outer drawing stayed fixed, explains nothing: it duplicates the phenomenon inside the head, with the same question recurring for the inner picture.
Therefore, seeing-as is a distinct concept — or family of concepts — straddling perception and thought: an aspect is perceived, but perceived only by someone who commands the relevant concepts and techniques. Its refusal to fit "seeing" or "interpreting" is not a defect of the analysis but the discovery: our perceptual vocabulary has this structure, visible once the case is described rather than theorized.

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations*, Part II, §xi (Anscombe's arrangement; in the fourth edition, *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*, the same discussion). The two kinds of "seeing" and the continuous/dawning distinction are developed across the section; the investigation study guide treats the block in summary.

**Objection.** Cognitive science's reply, in outline: the phenomenon is real but not philosophically refractory. The same retinal stimulus can sustain different perceptual *organizations*, and representational theories model aspect-switching as a change of high-level representation — same input, different content — without any puzzle about grammar. On this view Wittgenstein describes the phenomenology acutely and then refuses the explanatory theory that accounts for it; the "concepts" the description invokes are promissory notes on cognitive mechanisms. A sharper philosophical objection adds that the seeing/seeing-as distinction is itself asserted from cases rather than argued, and aspect-blindness is a stipulated possibility doing work no actual case performs.

**Reply.** The Wittgensteinian reply concedes the science and disputes its jurisdiction: nothing in Part II, §xi denies neural or representational accounts of *how* aspects switch; the argument is about what it is to *see an aspect* — about the grammar of the concept — and there the interpretation model fails first-personally, since no interpretation is experienced, and the inner-picture model fails by regress. Representational content, when it is introduced to do the concept's work, quietly becomes the aspect itself under a technical name. On aspect-blindness, the defense is that the case is a thought experiment in the service of a conceptual contrast — its possibility, not its incidence, is what maps the concept's edges, the standard use of such cases in the later work (compare the builders). The dossier's honest note: this is the argument among the twelve where "dissolution versus explanation" divides readers most cleanly along prior commitments about what philosophy of perception is for — which is itself a Wittgensteinian datum.

**Argument 10. Philosophy as therapy: problems are dissolved, not solved**

1. Philosophical problems do not behave like empirical problems: they are not settled by discoveries, and their persistence across millennia is not for lack of ingenuity. They arise, on Wittgenstein's diagnosis, from the misunderstanding of the logic of our language — from analogies between surface forms of words (every word a name; every sentence a description; every concept an essence) that bewitch the intelligence (Preface; §§109, 111).
2. Words generate confusion when they idle — "when language goes on holiday" (§38), when an expression is lifted from the language-games where it works and set spinning in a metaphysical vacuum (§116's remedy: bring words back from their metaphysical to their everyday use).
3. New theory reproduces the disease: a philosophical theory is built from the same words, under the same pictures, and the craving it answers — for essence, for hidden structure, for a standpoint outside use — is the confusion itself in systematic dress (§§109, 124, 126).
4. What removes a confusion is not information but arrangement: a perspicuous representation (§122) of how the words are actually used, assembled from reminders (§127) and invented comparison cases, until the problem loses its compulsion — the discovery that gives philosophy peace (§133).
Therefore, philosophy is a therapeutic activity, not a doctrine: it "leaves everything as it is" (§124), advances no theses (§128), proceeds by methods "like different therapies" (§133), treats a question as one treats an illness (§255) — and its aim can be stated in one line: "to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (§309).

**Textual basis.** *Philosophical Investigations* §§38, 109, 111, 116, 122, 124, 126–128, 133, 255, 309, 593 (the "one-sided diet" of examples as a main cause of philosophical disease).

**Objection.** The abdication objection, in Bertrand Russell's contemptuous form and many sober ones since: there are real questions — about truth, knowledge, the external world, the validity of induction — and declaring them grammatical confusions does not answer them; a philosophy forbidden to theorize cannot even state Wittgenstein's own results, since "meaning is use" and "obeying a rule is a practice" sound exactly like theses. The conservatism objection (Ernest Gellner's *Words and Things*, 1959, is the locus) adds that sanctifying ordinary use sanctifies the status quo, in language and beyond it. The self-reference objection completes the set: §124's doctrine is itself philosophy interfering with a use of the word "philosophy."

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's reply distinguishes targets: the therapy attacks a *craving* — the demand that every concept have an essence, every grasp an interpretation, every phenomenon a hidden mechanism — not questions as such, and its results are not theses but reminders, checkable by anyone against their own use of the words; §43's hedge is the model of the difference. That some formulations sound theoretical is acknowledged in the literature's standard defense: they are ladders within the later work too, to be replaced by the cases themselves once seen aright. On §124's scope, defenders note the sentence governs philosophy's relation to *language* — it does not legislate grammar — and extending it to a general ban on criticism adds a premise the text does not contain (the investigations study guide flags this inflation as a common misreading). The residue is genuine and should be recorded: whether the conception, faithfully practiced, leaves standing everything that deserves to stand is the central open argument about the late work — quietism as liberation or abdication — and the dossier presents it, here and in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), as unresolved.

**Argument 11. Hinge certainty: doubt presupposes what it cannot doubt**

1. Doubting is a move inside practices of inquiry, and like other moves it has conditions: a doubt needs grounds, and needs far more *not* doubted in order to be the doubt it is. "If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty" (OC §115).
2. G. E. Moore's certainties — "here is one hand," "the earth has existed for a long time past" — do not function in our practices as hypotheses that turned out well. They are not tested, doubted, or (in the ordinary sense) known: they stand fast for us, exempt from doubt, and belong to the scaffolding within which testing occurs. "At the foundation of well-founded belief lies belief that is not founded" (OC §253).
3. Wittgenstein's two images fix their logical role. They are the **river-bed** of thoughts (OC §§94–99): propositions of empirical form hardened into a channel for the fluid propositions inquiry actually tests; the bed can itself shift — "propositions which were not hardened but fluid... and hard ones became fluid" — but the movement of the waters is distinguished from the shift of the bed, though "there is not a sharp division of the one from the other" (OC §97). And they are **hinges**: "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (OC §341); "if I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put" (OC §343).
4. Moore's error and the skeptic's are the same error in opposite directions: both treat a hinge as an ordinary proposition — Moore claiming to *know* it on evidence, the skeptic demanding its justification — whereas its role in the game is precisely to be neither known nor doubted but enacted, shown in how we investigate, act, and judge (OC §§341–343; the testing of anything "takes place already within a system," OC §105).
Therefore, universal doubt is not a heroic rigor but an incoherent demand: it would have to doubt the framework that gives "doubt," "grounds," and "mistake" their sense. Certainty at the foundation is not a proposition supremely well justified; it is the ungrounded way we act, into which justification grows. Skepticism is answered not by a proof of the external world but by a description of the structure that makes proof, doubt, and knowledge possible.

**Textual basis.** *On Certainty* (1969, posthumous; written 1949–51) §§94–99 (river-bed; §97 the shift), §105 (system), §115 (doubt presupposes certainty), §253 (unfounded foundation), §§341–343 (hinges). Moore's "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) and "Proof of an External World" (1939) are the provoking texts.

**Objection.** The relativism objection: if hinges are framework-relative and the river-bed can shift, then nothing in the argument shows *our* hinges true — a community whose bed differs (in cosmology, in tests for madness) is unreachable by reasons that presuppose ours, and the doctrine seems to concede to the skeptic at the meta-level what it denies him at the object level: no hinge can be justified to someone who does not already share it. The truth-aptness dispute within the literature sharpens this: are hinges propositions at all (Crispin Wright's entitlement framework treats them as assumptions we are warranted in trusting without evidence; Duncan Pritchard's hinge commitments are non-propositional and arational; Annalisa Coliva defends a constitutive, moderate reading)? The disagreement about what hinges *are* is wide enough that "hinge epistemology" names a research field, not a result. A related objection presses §97's concession: beds *do* shift — heliocentrism crossed from fluid to bed — so a hinge's exemption is temporary and pragmatic, not logical, and the argument reduces to the banality that inquiry must start somewhere.

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's own texts anticipate the deflationary reading and refuse it: the distinction between waters and bed is drawn *within* the description of our epistemic life, and its point is that "doubt" and "certainty" do not apply uniformly at every level — a doubt about a hinge is not a bigger doubt but a different phenomenon, closer to a conversion or a breakdown of the game (the river-bed passages' contrast between testing and the shift of the channel itself). Heliocentrism's migration is the theory working, not failing: it shows how a proposition can change function over time, passing between bed and water as the surrounding practice changes — the distinction is functional, not a timeless caste. On relativism, the honest reply (and the one the text supports) is concessive in form: Wittgenstein offers no neutral tribunal between world-pictures, and says that where reasons run out, persuasion and conversion are what remain (OC §§609–612: "at the end of reasons comes persuasion"). Whether that concession is a deep truth about rationality's ground or the skeptic winning on points is — as with Argument 10 — a standing dispute, and the contemporary hinge literature is best read as the attempt to do better on both horns than the notes, left unfinished two days before his death, could do themselves.

**Argument 12. The limits of language and the self: solipsism at the boundary of the world**

1. "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world" (5.6): a proposition can picture a fact; the totality of propositions is language; the totality of facts is the world. What lies outside both — the standpoint from which the whole is mine — cannot be one more fact inside them.
2. What the solipsist *means* is quite correct; only it cannot be said, but makes itself manifest: "The world is my world: this is manifest in the fact that the limits of language (of that language which alone I understand) mean the limits of my world" (5.62).
3. The subject that thinks and entertains ideas is not to be found in the world (5.631): no experience presents the metaphysical subject, just as the visual field presents nothing from which it follows that it is seen by an eye (5.633). "The subject does not belong to the world: rather, it is a limit of the world" (5.632).
4. Where in the world is a metaphysical subject to be found? As the eye is to the visual field: present everywhere as the field's boundary, visible nowhere within it (5.633). Philosophy can talk about the self in a non-psychological sense exactly here: "the self of solipsism shrinks to a point without extension, and there remains the reality co-ordinated with it" (5.64; compare 5.641).
Therefore: "solipsism, when its implications are followed out strictly, coincides with pure realism" (5.64). The I of solipsism is not an item among the world's contents but the world's limit; shrink it to that extensionless point and nothing in the world changes — the facts remain exactly as the realist inventories them, and the solipsist's insistence turns out to have been about the limit, not about any content.

**Textual basis.** *Tractatus* 5.6–5.64 (with 5.641 on the philosophical I; the eye and visual field at 5.633, the drawn analogy at 5.6331).

**Objection.** The slide objection: "my language" and "my world" trade on an ambiguity between the language *I* happen to speak (an empirical fact about a person in the world) and language as the condition of depiction as such; Russell complained the whole passage mystifies a confusion between the two, and on the empirical reading solipsism does not coincide with realism but with nonsense of a simpler kind. The second objection is Argument 2's returning in local dress: if what the solipsist means cannot be said, premises 2–4 are ladder-material, and the passage's doctrinal tone — a limit drawn, a subject located at it — is precisely what the resolute reading says must also be thrown away; traditional readers reply that here, as with logical form, something true is *shown* in the world's being a limited, owned totality (6.45 connects the point to the mystical).

**Reply.** Wittgenstein's reply is the passage's own construction: the argument does not infer a soul; it removes one. Each premise *subtracts* — the subject is not a part of the world, not an experience, not an item — and the eye analogy does positive work only by analogy: a limit is not nothing, and is not a thing; it is the boundary that makes the field a field. That the endpoint coincides with realism is presented as the dissolution of solipsism's threat, not its vindication: nothing the solipsist wants to deny the realist turns out to exist as a deniable content. On the scholarly status, the literature's range is the reply's remainder: David Pears defended readings on which the 5.6s state a real, limit-drawing insight about representation; resolute readers treat them as among the most seductive rungs, to be climbed and discarded with the rest. The dossier flags the passage as the point where the early book's logic, its mysticism, and its interpretive war all meet — and settles none of them.
