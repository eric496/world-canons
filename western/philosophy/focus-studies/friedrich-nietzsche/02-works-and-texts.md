# Works and Texts — Friedrich Nietzsche

*Dossier file 02. Every work that matters, in the order this dossier reads them, with editions. The library's [Genealogy guide](../../study/nietzsche-genealogy.md) remains the companion for the reading weeks on that book.*

## The reading order, and the two rules

This dossier adopts the [learning path](../../learning-path.md)'s order for the Nietzsche block exactly, because the order is itself an interpretation: start with the compressed late polemics, build to the one fully systematic book, then widen into the prelude and the joyful science, and only then approach the literary gospel. The path's order is:

*Twilight of the Idols* selections → *On the Genealogy of Morals* whole → *Beyond Good and Evil* selections → *The Gay Science* selections (§§125, 341, 373 at minimum) → *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* last, optional, as a literary pass. The dossier then inserts its additions in their places: *The Birth of Tragedy* at the front (with its 1886 self-criticism), the free-spirit books before the *Genealogy*, and *The Antichrist* and *Ecce Homo* at the end, where they belong chronologically.

The path also lays down two rules, and this dossier repeats them in its own voice because everything depends on them:

1. **Not Thomas Common.** Common's translations (1909–13, in the old Oscar Levy complete edition) were the English Nietzsche for half a century and are still reprinted cheaply. They are dated in diction, Victorian in their smoothing of his violence, and unreliable in detail. A beginner reading Common is reading a muffled Nietzsche. Use the modern translations named below.
2. ***The Will to Power* is not a book by Nietzsche.** It is an arrangement of his unpublished notebooks, made after his collapse by his sister Elisabeth's collaborators and published under his name in 1901 (expanded 1906) as though it were his planned masterwork. He had abandoned the project that bore that title, mining its drafts for the books of 1888 he actually published. Do not cite it, do not read it as a companion volume, and never let a quotation from it settle a question about what Nietzsche thought. The section on the *Nachlass* below states what to do with notebook material instead.

A third rule of the path's, worth restating: **do not start with *Zarathustra*.** Its parodies presuppose the Bible, its doctrines presuppose the *Genealogy* and *The Gay Science*, and its style defeats reconstruction. The dossier ends with it, not begins.

## The works, in dossier order

### 1. *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) — the first book, read with its retraction

**Date and occasion.** Published January 1872 (written 1870–71), full title *The Birth of Tragedy Out of the Spirit of Music*. The young Basel professor's attempt to explain Greek tragedy through Apollo (form, image, individuation, dream) and Dionysus (intoxication, the dissolution of the individual, primal unity), and to prophesy tragedy's rebirth in Wagner's music dramas. Professional philology's verdict was Wilamowitz's pamphlet (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)); posterity's verdict has reversed it, while conceding many of the technical charges.

**How the dossier reads it.** Always with the **"Attempt at a Self-Criticism"** (1886) printed in front of it in modern editions: the preface Nietzsche added fourteen years later, in which he is his own first and best critic — the book was "impossible," badly written, entangled with Wagner and Schopenhauer and with a metaphysics of art he no longer held — while standing by its question: existence justified only as an aesthetic phenomenon, and science itself interrogated as a symptom. Reading the two together teaches the dossier's central skill, watching Nietzsche revise Nietzsche. **Editions:** Kaufmann's translation (in *Basic Writings of Nietzsche*, Modern Library) or Ronald Speirs's (Cambridge Texts, 1999). **Difficulty:** moderate; the Wagnerian finale can be skimmed on a first pass.

### 2. The free-spirit turn: *Human, All Too Human* (1878) and *Daybreak* (1881), in selections

**Dates and occasion.** *Human, All Too Human: A Book for Free Spirits* (1878, with two sequels, 1879–80, later bound as its second volume) is the break with Wagner made public: aphorisms in the French moralist manner, psychological unmasking of metaphysics, religion, and morality, dedicated to Voltaire. *Daybreak: Thoughts on the Prejudices of Morality* (1881) continues the campaign against the morality of custom and contains his earliest sustained psychology of cruelty and conscience — the quarry the *Genealogy* later works systematically.

**What the dossier takes from them.** Selections, not wholes: the chapters on the religious life and on morality in *Human, All Too Human*, and *Daybreak*'s second and third books. Their function in the plan is methodological: this is where the aphorism becomes his instrument, and where the reader learns its demands before the *Genealogy* raises the stakes. **Editions:** *Human, All Too Human* in R. J. Hollingdale's translation (Cambridge Texts) or Marion Faber/Stephen Lehmann (Penguin); *Daybreak* in Hollingdale's (Cambridge Texts). **Difficulty:** low entry, high depth — each aphorism is a door.

**The aphorism, on his own terms.** Why read this way? Because Nietzsche says what his form demands, in the Preface to the *Genealogy* (§8): an aphorism, properly stamped and coined, has *not* been deciphered merely by being read; that is where its *exegesis* must begin — and exegesis, he says, requires the almost forgotten art of *rumination*, the slow chewing-over that philology taught him. The dossier's checkpoint method (book closed, reconstruct, then compare) is simply his instruction operationalized. A reader who collects his sentences as slogans has declined the contract.

### 3. *Twilight of the Idols* (written 1888; published 1889), in selections

**Date and occasion.** Written in a few weeks of August–September 1888 and published January 1889, days after his collapse — subtitled *How to Philosophize with a Hammer*. The learning path begins here because it is Nietzsche at maximum compression: "Maxims and Arrows," the four great errors, the attack on Socrates and on German culture, and the famous formula for his whole philosophy in the closing sections.

**How the dossier reads it.** In selections, as the path prescribes — the dossier's plan names the sections — as a preview of the positions the *Genealogy* will then argue at length. **Editions:** Kaufmann's (in *The Portable Nietzsche*, Viking) or Judith Norman's (Cambridge Texts, in the volume with *The Anti-Christ* and *Ecce Homo*, ed. Aaron Ridley). **Difficulty:** low to read, high to evaluate — its speed is a trap for the slogan-collector.

### 4. *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) — whole

**Date and occasion.** Published November 1887, its full title *On the Genealogy of Morals: A Polemic*. Three essays — on the origin of "good and evil," on guilt and bad conscience, on the meaning of ascetic ideals — written, as he told his publisher, to supplement and clarify *Beyond Good and Evil*. It is his most systematic and most teachable work: continuous prose, stated theses, evidence paraded and challengeable.

**How the dossier reads it.** Whole, in essay order, with the library's [study guide](../../study/nietzsche-genealogy.md) as session companion — its reconstructions, checkpoints, and misreadings are the plan's working apparatus for these weeks, and this dossier does not duplicate them. **Editions:** Kaufmann and Hollingdale (Vintage) — the learning path's default; or Carol Diethe, ed. Keith Ansell-Pearson (Cambridge Texts); or Maudemarie Clark and Alan J. Swensen (Hackett). All three acceptable; pick one and stay in it. **Difficulty:** the highest argumentative density in the corpus, and the most rewarding.

### 5. *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), in selections

**Date and occasion.** Published August 1886 at his own expense, subtitled *Prelude to a Philosophy of the Future*. Nine parts: on the prejudices of philosophers, the free spirit, religious nature, epigrams, natural history of morals, scholars, our virtues, peoples and fatherlands, and the noble — the book in which the *Genealogy*'s arguments appear in compressed, aphoristic form, a year early.

**How the dossier reads it.** In selections after the *Genealogy*, reversing composition order deliberately: the path's logic is that the argued version should discipline the aphoristic one. Key parts: Part One (the prejudices), Part Five (the natural history of morals), Part Nine (what is noble). **Editions:** Kaufmann (Vintage) or Judith Norman (Cambridge Texts, 2002). **Difficulty:** high — the aphorisms here are arguments with the premises suppressed.

### 6. *The Gay Science* (1882; Book V 1887), in selections

**Date and occasion.** Published August 1882 (books I–IV); a second edition in 1887 added the preface and Book V. The joyful science of the free spirit, and the book where the century's most famous passages live: the madman (§125), *amor fati* (§276), eternal recurrence proposed as "the greatest weight" (§341), and the suspicion of science itself (§373, on the "mechanical world" interpretation) — the three sections the learning path names as the minimum are exactly the right minimum.

**How the dossier reads it.** The mandated sections first, then Book IV whole, then Book V selections. Many readers end by judging it his best book: the most various, the least bitter, the place where affirmation is practiced rather than merely preached. **Editions:** Kaufmann (Vintage, 1974) or Josefine Nauckhoff (Cambridge Texts, 2001). **Difficulty:** low in stretches, sudden in its depths — the jokes are load-bearing.

### 7. *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883–85) — last, optional, a literary pass

**Dates and occasion.** Parts I–III published 1883–84; Part IV composed 1885 and printed privately that year in about forty copies (first public edition 1892). Subtitled *A Book for All and None*: a prophet descends from ten years of mountain solitude to teach the Übermensch, the death of God, and eternal recurrence, in a prose that parodies Luther's Bible and the Gospels aria by aria. Nietzsche called it the greatest gift ever made to mankind; it sold negligibly in his lifetime and made his posthumous fame.

**How the dossier reads it.** Last, as the path prescribes, and as literature: the Prologue, "On the Three Metamorphoses," "The Convalescent," and the Fourth Part's ass-festival as a self-parody — with a commentary at hand (the plan names one in [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md)). Its doctrines are the ones the earlier books argue; here they are dramatized, and the dramatization is not an argument. **Editions:** Kaufmann (in *The Portable Nietzsche*) or Hollingdale (Penguin). **Difficulty:** deceptively readable; every page rewards slowness and punishes extraction.

### 8. The 1888 texts: *The Antichrist* and *Ecce Homo*

**Dates and publication history.** Both written in the autumn of 1888. *The Antichrist* (his *The Anti-Christ*, more exactly "The Antichristian") — a systematic curse on Christianity as the historical triumph of slave morality — was published only in 1895, delayed amid sensitivity about its blasphemy; *Ecce Homo*, his autobiography of the works ("Why I Am So Wise," "Why I Write Such Good Books"), was withheld by the estate and first published in 1908. Both are therefore 1888 texts with 1890s–1900s publication lives, and the house style keeps the two facts separate.

**How the dossier reads them.** At the end, in full: *The Antichrist* is the *Genealogy*'s first essay turned prosecutorial, and *Ecce Homo* is the author's own dossier on himself — indispensable, and to be read with the same suspicion he taught, since its self-portrait is triumphant, funny, and not wholly reliable. **Editions:** *The Antichrist* in Hollingdale (Penguin, with *Twilight*) or Norman (Cambridge Texts); *Ecce Homo* in Kaufmann (in *Basic Writings*). **Difficulty:** low prose, high temperature.

### Also on the shelf

The four **Untimely Meditations** (1873–76) — against David Strauss, on history's use and abuse, and the two portraits, *Schopenhauer as Educator* and *Richard Wagner in Bayreuth* — are not assigned whole; the dossier reads the third, *Schopenhauer as Educator*, alongside the Schopenhauer dossier, where it belongs. **"On Truth and Lying in an Extra-Moral Sense"** (1873) requires its label every time it appears in this dossier: it is an essay Nietzsche wrote in 1873 and *never published himself* (first printed in 1896, after his collapse). Its famous definition of truth as "a mobile host of metaphors" is real Nietzsche, but it is a twenty-eight-year-old's notebook-adjacent essay, not a doctrine of the mature books, and it must never be cited without that label.

## The *Nachlass* problem — a standing caution

Nietzsche published or prepared for publication the books above. He also left, at his collapse, thousands of pages of notebooks — drafts, reading notes, abandoned plans, genuine philosophical gold and genuine dross inextricably mixed. Those notebooks (the *Nachlass*) are primary evidence about his thinking and no kind of book at all. Elisabeth's collaborators mined them to construct *The Will to Power* (1901; expanded 1906), imposing headings and an order that flattered the archive's politics; for two generations, "Nietzsche" in most languages meant substantially that construction.

The remedy exists and is named: the critical edition begun by **Giorgio Colli and Mazzino Montinari** — the *Kritische Gesamtausgabe* (KGW, de Gruyter, from 1967) and its study edition, the KSA — prints the works and the notebooks chronologically, from the manuscripts, restoring what Nietzsche actually wrote and when. Montinari's demonstration that the late "Will to Power" project had been abandoned and its materials already consumed in *Twilight* and *The Antichrist* is the single most important fact of modern Nietzsche scholarship.

**What a beginner should do:** read the published books, in a modern translation, and let the dossiers and guides cite the notebooks for you where context requires. **What a beginner should not do:** buy *The Will to Power* as a primary text; cite notebook passages as "Nietzsche's doctrine" without their date and unpublished status; or assume that a sentence he never chose to publish outranks a book he did. The notebooks are the workshop. The books are the work.
