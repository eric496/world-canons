# Nietzsche: The Arguments

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Friedrich Nietzsche](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — Nietzsche's own where he gave one, the literature's where he didn't, labeled as such. Nietzsche rarely argues in treatise form, so the reconstructions make explicit what aphorism and polemic compress; where scholars dispute what an argument even is, the dispute is presented as a dispute. His published works are cited by their standard abbreviations and section numbers, which are stable across translations: **GS** *The Gay Science*, **BGE** *Beyond Good and Evil*, **GM** *On the Genealogy of Morals* (essay and section), **TI** *Twilight of the Idols*, **Z** *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, **HH** *Human, All Too Human*, **EH** *Ecce Homo*. Material that survives only in the notebooks (*Nachlass*) — including the editorial construction *The Will to Power* — is flagged wherever it is load-bearing.

The *Genealogy*'s three essays have full reconstructions in the study guide, [*On the Genealogy of Morals*](../../study/nietzsche-genealogy.md). Arguments 2, 5, and 7 below take the *Genealogy* doctrines up from the system side — what they commit Nietzsche to, and where they are attacked — and cross-refer to the guide rather than re-derive its reconstructions.

**Argument 1. The death of God and the coming nihilism**

1. A culture's highest values — its God, its moral law, its account of why anything matters — hold only so long as they are believed; their authority is the authority of a living faith, not of an argument that survives its believers.
2. The Christian God has become unbelievable to educated Europe — undone from within, since the Christian virtue of truthfulness, turned on Christian dogma itself, forbids the lie of faith (GM III §27: Christian truthfulness draws its "most striking inference, its inference against itself").
3. But the values, meanings, and moral habits built on that belief remain standing after the belief has gone: modern morality, scholarship, and conscience are the God's shadow, still shown on cave walls for millennia after the God is dead (GS §108).
4. A shadow cannot hold forever: when the collapse of the foundation becomes felt, the question "why?" — why live, why suffer, why be good — loses the only answer the culture has, and existence presents itself as valueless.
Therefore, Europe faces nihilism as a historical necessity, not a mood: "what I relate is the history of the next two centuries" — the advent of nihilism follows from the death of God the way a building's fall follows from the removal of its ground (the famous preface to *The Will to Power* states this in the notebooks' material; the published diagnosis is GS §§125, 343, 346 and GM III).

**Textual basis.** GS §125 (the madman in the marketplace: "God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him" — announced to laughing unbelievers who do not grasp that the deed's consequences are still on their way); GS §343 (the "long plentitude of meaning" the event will unfold); GS §346; GM III §§27–28. The madman is a diagnostician, not a celebrant: he arrives too early, and his news is a catastrophe to be understood, not a liberation to be enjoyed.

**Objection.** The argument's second premise is an empirical prophecy, and it can look falsified twice over: billions still believe, and post-Christian Europe generated new sources of meaning — nationalism, communism, human rights, consumer culture — rather than a single collapse into valuelessness. A diagnosis that every successor faith confirms as another "shadow" risks being unfalsifiable.

**Reply.** Nietzsche's own texts absorb the first half of the objection: he predicts precisely that the vacuum will be filled by substitute faiths — the shadows of God (GS §108), the ascetic ideal in secular dress (Argument 7) — and counts them as symptoms, not refutations, since each inherits the old structure of meaning-from-beyond while lacking the old belief's power. The unfalsifiability charge is the serious one, and the literature's best reply concedes its form while denying its force: the diagnosis is a *genealogical* claim about where successor values get their authority (borrowed, unexamined, self-undermining), and it stands or falls on the quality of those case-by-case genealogies — Arguments 2, 3, and 7 are the evidence. It is a research program about modernity, not a date-stamped prediction, and should be graded as one.

**Argument 2. Master and slave morality as a historical typology**

1. Values are created, not discovered: every morality is the sign-language of some type of human being, expressing what that type needs to flourish (BGE §6; GM Preface §6).
2. Creation can begin in one of two directions. It can begin in **affirmation**: a self-glorifying type experiences its own power, wealth, and vitality as good, and only as a pale contrast, an afterthought, calls the low and common "bad." This is the noble mode of valuation, "good/bad."
3. Or creation can begin in **negation**: a type that cannot affirm itself — because it lacks the power the first type enjoys — must define itself against a hostile outside; it says No first, calls the powerful "evil," and only then, as the mirror image of that evil, calls itself "good." This is the reactive mode, "good/evil."
4. The two structures leave different fingerprints: the affirmative type's "bad" is contempt without obsession, while the reactive type's "evil" is fixation — the enemy is needed, imagined, and never forgotten.
Therefore, "morality" as Europe knows it is not one thing but the sediment of two opposing value-creating structures — master morality and slave morality — whose mixture and conflict constitute the history of our conscience (GM I §§2, 7, 10; BGE §260).

**Textual basis.** GM I §§2 (the etymological opening: "good" originally = the noble, the powerful), 7, 10; BGE §260 (the two types stated as a general typology, mixed in all higher cultures). The study guide's reconstruction (a) carries the full historical version, including the slave revolt; this dossier form isolates the *structural* claim — the direction of valuation — which is what the typology commits Nietzsche to even if his ancient history is discounted (Argument 3's objection).

**Objection.** The typology is suspiciously neat: two boxes for three millennia of moral life. Historical moralities — Aristotle's magnanimous man, who is also just and generous; the Christian knight; the Stoic sage — mix assertion and reaction, pride and pity, in ways the schema must either flatten or multiply exceptions to accommodate. And Nietzsche's own etymological evidence (GM I §5: *bonus* from *duonus*, the warrior) is folk philology that specialists have challenged since 1887.

**Reply.** The literature's standard defense (Brian Leiter's is the most careful) is that the typology is a pair of *ideal types* in something like Weber's sense: claims about the psychological structure of valuation, offered as instruments for reading mixed cases, not as a census in which every morality must fall wholly into one box. BGE §260 itself says the two types appear "at times side by side — even in the same man, within one soul." The etymology is genuinely the weakest plank, and defenders typically concede it as illustrative rather than load-bearing: the argument stands if the *psychological* contrast — valuation from fullness vs. valuation from reaction — is recognizable in the phenomena, which is a claim the reader can test without knowing any Latin.

**Argument 3. Ressentiment as creative**

1. The powerless of GM I — those who are denied "the true reaction, that of deeds" — do not simply endure their impotence; the reaction accumulates inwardly as *ressentiment*, a festering, remembered, imaginary revenge (GM I §10).
2. Ressentiment becomes *creative* when it generates values: unable to call himself good from strength, the man of ressentiment constructs a fantasy world in which the strong are "evil" — wicked, damned, to be judged — and the powerless, precisely in their patience, humility, and suffering, are the "good" whom a just God will vindicate.
3. This is a genuine creation, not mere negation: it produces a new table of values with its own virtues (pity, humility, meekness as strengths), its own sanctions (sin, judgment, the afterlife as courtroom), and eventually its own institutions.
4. The creation bears the mark of its origin: because the "good" of slave morality is defined as the negation of the master's qualities, it remains permanently *dependent on its enemy* — it needs the evil one to exist.
Therefore, slave morality is ressentiment become creative and giving birth to values (GM I §10): the slave revolt in morality began "when ressentiment itself became creative."

**Textual basis.** GM I §10, in full; GM I §13 (the lambs and the birds of prey); GM III §15 (the priest as the manager and director of ressentiment, who gives the sufferer's revenge a target: "you alone are to blame").

**Objection.** This is speculative history of the armchair kind: no evidence is or could be offered that actual ancient value-change proceeded from this psychological mechanism, and the mechanism itself — envy transfigured into virtue over centuries — is asserted, not observed. A sterner version: even granting the history, why should a value's origin in resentment count against it? That inference is the genetic fallacy, and Nietzsche of all people — who traces his own philosophy to illness and solitude without embarrassment — cannot wield origins as refutations selectively.

**Reply.** Nietzsche's own defense is built into the *Genealogy*'s preface: he is offering hypotheses for a "real history of morality" that others, with better evidence, are invited to test and correct (GM Preface §§4, 7) — the claims are programmatic, and their author, a philologist who had lost his profession, knew exactly how provisional his archaeology was. To the genetic-fallacy charge, the literature's best reply (developed by Leiter, and in a different key by Bernard Williams in his late work on genealogy) distinguishes two operations: *refutation* by origin, which is indeed fallacious, and *undermining* by origin, which is not — if a value presents itself as pure, eternal, and disinterested, and its actual history is interested, reactive, and recent, the value's *self-understanding* is false even if some independent case for it might be constructed. Genealogy does not conclude "therefore pity is worthless"; it concludes "therefore pity must now argue for itself, in the open, like everything else." Whether that conclusion is modest or devastating depends on how much of the value's authority was the aura — and that is the question the *Genealogy* exists to force.

**Argument 4. The slave revolt and the priestly inversion**

1. On Nietzsche's historical narrative, the priestly caste — weakest in bodily power, strongest in spirit — is the agent that turns ressentiment into a world-historical force: the priest cannot defeat the noble in the noble's arena, but he can redefine the arena (GM I §§6–7).
2. The inversion is radicalized, Nietzsche claims, in ancient Judaism's response to its oppressors: the values of the powerful world — Rome above all — are inverted at the root: "the wretched alone are the good; the poor, impotent, lowly alone are the good… whereas you, you noble and powerful ones, you are in all eternity the evil, the cruel, the lustful, the insatiable, the godless" (GM I §7, paraphrase-tight summary of his ventriloquism of the inversion).
3. Christianity inherits the inversion and universalizes it: the cross — "God on the cross" — becomes the bait by which the slave valuation conquers the Roman world itself; Rome bows before the values of those it conquered (GM I §8, §16: "Rome against Judea, Judea against Rome — there has hitherto been no greater event than this struggle").
4. The priest's instrument is the reinterpretation of suffering: misery is no longer fortune's accident but *desert* — sin — so the sufferer's revenge is satisfied by a cosmic court while his obedience is secured (developed fully in GM III).
Therefore, on Nietzsche's telling, European morality is the long victory of the priestly inversion: the slave revolt succeeded, and its values — humility, pity, equality of souls before God — became the conscience of the victors' descendants.

**Textual basis.** GM I §§6–8, 16; GM III §§15–20 (the priest as physician who must himself be sick). *The Antichrist* (§§24–27, 62) restates the polemic at maximum temperature in 1888.

**Objection.** As history, this is the most vulnerable argument in the *Genealogy*. Nietzsche's portrait of ancient Judaism is drawn largely from hostile secondary sources and from the Christian tradition's own caricatures; specialists in Second Temple Judaism and in Roman history have found the narrative — a unitary priestly people engineering a value-inversion that then "conquers" Rome — unsupported in nearly every particular, and the account slides, critics charge, between "the priests" as a caste, Judaism as a religion, and Christianity as its heir in ways no historian could license. The argument's placement inside a polemic against nineteenth-century anti-Semitism (Nietzsche insists the modern anti-Semites are themselves men of ressentiment, GM's target being the *priestly type* in all nations) is textually real but has not prevented the passages from being read, and misused, as ethnic history.

**Reply.** The honest defense, and the one this dossier adopts, begins by surrendering the narrative *as ancient history*: Nietzsche presents his claims as his argument — a hypothesis about the psychology of value-inversion, armed with the sources available to a lapsed philologist in 1887 — and its historical accuracy is debated by scholars and should not be taught as fact. What defenders claim survives the surrender is the mechanism, now detached from its alleged instance: a subordinated group, led by its interpreters, can win by redefining the terms of worth so that the dominant group's strengths become vices and its own weaknesses virtues — a mechanism students can test against cases (including Christian ones Nietzsche also names, and modern secular ones) where the evidence is richer. The literature's verdict, roughly: Argument 3's psychology may be a permanent acquisition; Argument 4's history is a nineteenth-century construction, and the dossier's study plan reads it under that warning label.

**Argument 5. Bad conscience: the soul as self-cruelty**

1. Human beings were once animals whose instincts — cruelty, aggression, the joy in discharge of strength — operated outward, on prey, enemies, and rivals (GM II §§16–17).
2. The formation of society enclosed this animal "within the walls of society and of peace": the old instincts were suddenly denied their outward object, but an instinct denied discharge does not disappear.
3. Blocked instincts turn inward: the same cruelty that once worked on others now works on the self — the soul takes itself as its object, its enemy, its spectacle. "This secret self-ravishment, this artists' cruelty… as the womb of all ideal and imaginative phenomena" (GM II §18).
4. This internalization is *bad conscience*: not yet morality, not yet guilt, but the "serious illness" — "an illness as pregnancy is an illness" (GM II §19) — that made man deep, interesting, and inward: the soul itself is its product.
Therefore, conscience is not the voice of God in man but the scar tissue of civilization: instincts of cruelty, internalized, manufactured the inner world — and everything later built on it (guilt, sin, duty) is construction on this foundation.

**Textual basis.** GM II §§16–19 (cross-refer to the study guide's reconstruction (b), which walks the essay's whole machinery — punishment, debt, memory — of which this dossier form isolates the internalization step); GM II §18's concession that bad conscience is an illness that *produced* depth is essential to the argument and usually omitted from its slogan version.

**Objection.** The prehistory is a just-so story: a state of nature, a sudden enclosure ("some pack of blond beasts of prey" imposing the state, GM II §17), an instinct hydraulics in which blocked cruelty must flow somewhere. Each element is unevidenced, the hydraulics is metaphor presented as mechanism, and the "conquest theory of the state" contradicts what anthropology knows about gradual social formation. Worse, the argument seems to need the instincts to be *specifically* cruel — a bleak stipulation the evidence of cooperation in our species tells against.

**Reply.** Nietzsche's own hedge is in the text: GM II §16 flags its hypothesis as provisional ("I take it provisionally"), and the essay frames its prehistory as a heuristic — what *must* the genealogy of conscience look like, if conscience is not a divine implant? The literature's defense separates the speculative prehistory from the psychological thesis it dramatizes: that self-directed aggression is real, that the civilized virtues are purchased partly by self-punishment, and that the interior richness we call "the soul" is historically produced rather than metaphysically given. Freud's parallel construction — aggression turned inward as the superego in *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), reached independently and acknowledged by Freud's circle as an anticipation — is regularly cited as the mechanism's independent corroboration in a clinical register. The story is not evidence; the mechanism, its defenders say, is an hypothesis that two research traditions have found work to do with.

**Argument 6. The sovereign individual and the right to make promises**

1. Morality's longest labor was breeding an animal *permitted to promise*: an animal whose will can span time — who can stand security for his own future — against the natural forces of forgetfulness and chance (GM II §§1–2).
2. The instrument was the morality of custom: millennia of mnemonic cruelty — pain as the great memory-aid ("only that which never ceases to hurt stays in the memory," GM II §3) — burned a few imperatives into a forgetful animal until a "memory of the will" existed.
3. The process's ripest fruit, at the end of this long discipline, is the **sovereign individual**: the man who has become master of *himself* — his own conscience, his word reliable as fate — and who may therefore promise, not as obedience to an external law but as the privilege of his strength (GM II §§2–3).
4. This autonomy presupposes no contra-causal free will: the sovereign individual is a *product* of causal history — custom, pain, selection — and his reliability is necessity become inwardly owned, not an exemption from necessity (BGE §21: the "free will" of the theologians, a will that could have done otherwise in identical circumstances, is an artifice invented to make men punishable).
Therefore, genuine agency and responsibility are achievements *within* determinism, not exceptions to it: the right to make promises is earned by the long causation that produced a self strong enough to bind its own future.

**Textual basis.** GM II §§1–3; BGE §21 (the critique of free and unfree will; *causa sui* as "the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far"); HH I §§39, 107 (the middle-period critique of moral responsibility the late argument redeems in a new form).

**Objection.** Critics in the Kantian tradition object that this dissolves responsibility rather than grounding it: if the sovereign individual's reliability is just fortunate causation, praising him is like praising the weather, and the reactive attitudes — blame, gratitude, resentment — lose their point. Conveniently for Nietzsche, they say, he keeps the *prestige* of autonomy (the sovereign individual is clearly a hero of the essay) while denying the metaphysics that makes prestige intelligible.

**Reply.** The reply in the literature (Aaron Ridley's and Christopher Janaway's readings converge here) is that Nietzsche accepts the objection's premise and reverses its valence: yes, agency talk survives only as talk about *kinds of causal organization* — a self whose strongest drive reliably orders the rest (see [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) on the soul as a social structure of drives) *is* what autonomy ever was, and the metaphysical add-on (the uncaused causer) was the fiction. The sovereign individual is praised the way we praise a great river's course: not for escaping nature but for what its nature, fully achieved, can do. That Nietzsche's own rhetoric sometimes forgets this reduction — that he blames priests with a heat his theory should cool — is a fair charge, and defenders generally plead it as the polemicist's license rather than the theory's content.

**Argument 7. The ascetic ideal: willing nothingness rather than not willing**

1. Suffering is bearable; what destroys human beings is *meaningless* suffering — pain with no answer to "why?" (GM III §28).
2. The ascetic ideal — the interpretation of suffering as guilt (sin), and of life-denial as its cure — is the one meaning the suffering masses have historically been given: it made suffering explicable, placed the blame in the sufferer, and directed his ressentiment safely inward (GM III §§13–15, 20).
3. The ideal's apparent opposite, modern science and scholarship, rests on the same foundation: the unconditional will to truth — truth valued above life, happiness, and illusion — is itself a faith, "the ascetic ideal's most recent and noblest form" (GM III §§23–25, 27). Even the atheist scholar still *believes*, in truth.
4. But the ideal contains its own destroyer: the truthfulness it cultivated turns against it — Christian truthfulness forbids the Christian lie about God, and eventually forbids the moral interpretation itself (GM III §27) — leaving the will with its meaning gone.
5. What the ideal nevertheless reveals, beneath all its forms, is something about the will itself: horror of the void is deeper than horror of pain; the will would rather will *nothingness* than not will at all.
Therefore, the ascetic ideal was "the only meaning offered so far," and its self-overcoming opens the present crisis: "man would rather will nothingness than not will" (GM III §28 — the *Genealogy*'s closing line, and the dossier's Arguments 1 and 12 meet here).

**Textual basis.** GM III entire, especially §§1, 11, 13, 23–28; the study guide's reconstruction (c) covers the essay's priest-philosopher-artist typology in detail. GS §344 ("in what sense we, too, are still pious") states premise 3 in miniature.

**Objection.** Premise 3's inclusion of science is the contested step. The scientist's commitment to truth, critics argue, is instrumental and revisable — hypotheses are tools, discarded without existential crisis — and treating it as a *faith* trades on an equivocation between valuing truth as a method and worshipping it as a god. A further objection: the ideal's alleged monopoly ("the only meaning offered so far") ignores the affirmative meaning-systems Nietzsche himself admires — tragic Greece managed suffering without guilt, which is precisely why he admires it; so the ascetic ideal was not the only meaning, only the only *surviving* one, and the argument's drama depends on the stronger claim.

**Reply.** Nietzsche's own answer to the science objection is a distinction: he does not charge working science with piety in its procedures but with piety in its *unexamined presupposition* — that truth is worth any price, that nothing is more necessary than truth (GS §344 asks exactly "why not rather untruth?"). The scholar who would never sacrifice his life-comfort to an illusion yet never asks why truth outranks comfort holds, Nietzsche says, a metaphysical faith. On the Greek exception, defenders reply that the argument is scoped: *after* the slave revolt's victory, within the culture the ideal conquered, no rival meaning survived at scale — the tragedians are the argument's proof that alternatives are *possible*, which is what makes the present crisis an opportunity rather than a fate. The reply is respectable, but the concession should be recorded: premise 3's strength varies with how much of science's self-understanding it fairly describes, and readers divide on it.

**Argument 8. Eternal recurrence as the heaviest weight**

1. Suppose — the thought experiment of GS §341 — a demon tells you: "This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh… must return to you, all in the same succession and sequence."
2. The proposal divides its hearers absolutely: either you would throw yourself down, gnash your teeth, and curse the demon — the proposal is the heaviest possible burden — or you would answer, "You are a god and never have I heard anything more divine."
3. What the division measures is not a belief about the cosmos but a stance toward one's own life: whether it has been lived so that its *exact, unedited* repetition can be willed — suffering, failure, and shame included, nothing edited out.
4. The stance has a name: *amor fati*, the love of one's fate, wanting nothing different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity (EH, "Why I Am So Clever," §10; GS §276).
Therefore, eternal recurrence functions as the selective test and formula of affirmation — "the greatest weight" laid on one's actions (GS §341): live so that you could will this life again, forever.

**Textual basis.** GS §341 (1882 — the doctrine's first published appearance); GS §276 (amor fati as New Year's resolution); Z, "On the Vision and the Riddle" and "The Convalescent" (Zarathustra's own struggle with the thought, including its hardest point: the eternal recurrence of the *small man*). The notebooks of 1881–88 contain sketches of a *cosmological* proof — finite force, infinite time, therefore combinations must repeat — which Nietzsche never published; the cosmological reading (defended by Karl Löwith) rests on that *Nachlass* material and is flagged here as such. The published doctrine is the test.

**Objection.** Two objections dominate. As a test, recurrence seems psychologically rigged: it measures temperament (the already-cheerful pass easily; the melancholic fail through no fault of valuation) — and morally troubling, since willing everything again appears to require affirming the suffering of *others*, not merely one's own, which looks monstrous. As cosmology (if the notebooks are admitted), the sketches commit elementary errors: infinite time plus finite states does not entail recurrence of *this* sequence without further assumptions, as Georg Simmel showed already in 1907.

**Reply.** To the cosmological objection, the existential reading (Nehamas, Clark) simply agrees — and counts the agreement as evidence that Nietzsche's considered doctrine was the test: he had a decade to publish the physics and never did, while he published §341 twice over (GS, then Zarathustra's drama). To the moral objection, the literature's best reply limits the test's scope honestly: the demon addresses *your* life, and the discipline is directed at one's own stance toward one's own past — a weapon against regret and against what Zarathustra calls the spirit of revenge against time and its "it was" — not a license to approve others' suffering, which would in any case be ressentiment's mirror-image rather than affirmation. The temperament objection stands partly unrebutted, and defenders concede it in terms Nietzsche himself would insist on: the test *is* a sieve for types (that is what "selective" means in his vocabulary) — the concession is that §341 sorts people rather than persuading them, and whether a sorting device can also be a philosophy's capstone is a fair question to leave open.

**Argument 9. Will to power as hypothesis**

1. What is "given" to us immediately, Nietzsche argues, is one reality from inside: our world of desires and passions — the drives and affects, with their commanding and obeying (BGE §36).
2. All efficient force we know is of this kind: we have no other model of causation from within; the "mechanical" world of physics is known only from outside, by interpretation (BGE §§14, 22).
3. The methodological economy of §36 is then proposed *conditionally*: suppose nothing else is "given" as real except this world of drives — "may we not be permitted to make the attempt" to interpret all organic and even mechanical process by this one given kind of force?
4. On that supposition, the world viewed from inside, "defined and determined according to its 'intelligible character'" — would be "will to power and nothing else besides" (BGE §36).
Therefore — in the form Nietzsche prints — will to power is offered as an interpretive hypothesis whose warrant is economy and conscience: "supposing that this also is only interpretation — well, so much the better" (BGE §22).

**Textual basis.** BGE §§36, 22, 259 ("life itself is essentially appropriation, injury, overpowering… exploitation" as the organic functions of will to power); Z, "On Self-Overcoming" ("where I found the living, there I found will to power"). The notebooks contain the doctrine's cosmological expansion at length; *The Will to Power* as a book is Elisabeth's construction (see library entry).

**Objection.** The argument's modesty may be tactical: elsewhere Nietzsche asserts without conditionals — life *is* will to power, the world *is* will to power — and the whole *Genealogy* proceeds as if the psychology of drives were established fact. Critics from Heidegger onward have therefore taken §36's conditional grammar as the modesty of a metaphysician who knows his conclusion, not the caution of an experimenter; and even as hypothesis, the doctrine is charged with emptiness — a "force" that explains cruelty and kindness, growth and decay, domination and submission alike risks explaining nothing, since no observation could embarrass it.

**Reply.** The dispute flagged: this is the doctrinal-status fight that [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) maps — Heidegger's metaphysical reading (will to power as the being of beings, the completion of metaphysics) against Maudemarie Clark's argument (*Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy*, 1990) that the mature Nietzsche *abandoned* the cosmological doctrine, retaining the hypothesis in the psychological register where it can do work — and Bernard Reginster's middle construction, will to power as the will to *overcoming resistance*, which gives the hypothesis content enough to be wrong (it predicts, e.g., that unresisted satisfaction will disappoint — a claim about well-being that experience can test). The unfalsifiability charge is answered, on Reginster's line, by that narrowing. The dossier's verdict is a reading rule, not a ruling: take §36's own grammar as the doctrine's charter — a supposition, offered as an interpretation, to be judged by the interpretations it generates (Arguments 2–7 are the generated interpretations) — and treat the cosmic version as the notebooks' Nietzsche, a different evidentiary object.

**Argument 10. No doer behind the deed**

1. Popular morality separates the doer from the deed: behind the strong man's strength there is supposed to stand a neutral subject, free to express the strength or not — as the lambs would have it, the bird of prey could *choose* to be a lamb (GM I §13).
2. The separation is an artifact of grammar: "there is no 'being' behind doing, effecting, becoming; 'the doer' is merely a fiction added to the deed — the deed is everything" (GM I §13). Language's subject-predicate structure seduces us into doubling every event into an event plus its author: "lightning flashes" says one thing twice.
3. The same seduction, turned inward, manufactures the soul, the ego, and the free will: "a thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish" (BGE §17); the will itself, examined, is a complex of sensation, thought, and affect — commanding and obeying within one commonwealth of drives — not a simple faculty standing behind action (BGE §19).
4. The fiction has a function: a doer who *could have done otherwise* can be blamed, punished, and made accountable — the free will was invented, "essentially for the purpose of punishment" (TI, "The Four Great Errors," §7); BGE §21 calls the *causa sui*, the will as its own uncaused cause, a self-contradiction born of the desire to bear guilt.
Therefore, there is no neutral subject behind the deed to praise or blame for being what it is: strength that does not express itself as strength is a grammatical ghost — and the lambs' demand that strength *not* express itself is the slave revolt's founding request (GM I §13).

**Textual basis.** GM I §13 (lightning and flash); BGE §§16–21 (the critique of "I think," of will, of free and unfree will); TI, "The Four Great Errors," §§3, 7, and "'Reason' in Philosophy," §5 (language as metaphysics' smuggler: "we are not rid of God because we still believe in grammar").

**Objection.** The linguistic premise cuts both ways: that grammar requires subjects does not show that subjects are fictions — grammar also requires objects, and tables survive the argument. More pressingly, the argument's own polemic seems to need the doer it abolishes: Nietzsche blames the priests, praises the sovereign individual (Argument 6), and addresses readers as agents who might overcome themselves — performances that presuppose exactly the accountable subject the lightning passage dissolves. Philosophers of language add a narrower complaint: "the doer is a fiction" conflates the *metaphysical* claim (no uncaused subject) with a *semantic* one (subject-terms refer to nothing), and the second does not follow from the first.

**Reply.** The literature's best reply (Janaway's and Leiter's readings agree in outline) distinguishes what the argument targets: not the *reality* of persons or deeds, but the *neutral*, deed-independent subject — the little homunculus who stands behind his strength, exempt from it, and might have chosen otherwise. Persons, on Nietzsche's counter-model, are organizations of drives (BGE §12, §19): real, causal, and praise- or blameworthy in the way Argument 6's sovereign individual is — as *achievements of organization*, not as uncaused causes. As for the polemic's heat: Nietzsche would plead that blame directed at a *type*, as diagnosis, is consistent with denying that the type could have been otherwise at the moment of action — one blames the wolf's nature in a different grammar than the courtroom's, and the *Genealogy*'s whole effort is to teach that second grammar. Whether he always observes the distinction himself is, as with Argument 6, a fair standing charge.

**Argument 11. Perspectivism and objectivity: the more eyes**

1. There is no knowing except from somewhere: every cognition is constituted by the drives, affects, and interests of the knower; a knowledge that dispensed with perspectives would require dispensing with the interpretive activity that knowing *is* (GM III §12: the demand for a knowing "without interpretation" is a demand for an eye "turned in no particular direction" — an absurdity).
2. The opposite of perspectivism is therefore not objectivity but *concealment*: the "pure, will-less, painless, timeless knowing subject" (GM III §12) and the "immaculate perception" Nietzsche mocks (Z, "On Immaculate Perception") are perspectives pretending to be none, and the pretense makes them worse, not better — their interests operate unchecked.
3. Objectivity is reconceived accordingly: not disinterested contemplation but "the ability to control one's Pro and Con and to dispose of them, so that one knows how to employ a variety of perspectives and affective interpretations in the service of knowledge" (GM III §12).
4. The famous consequence follows: "the more affects we allow to speak about a thing, the more eyes, different eyes, we can use to observe the thing, the more complete will our 'concept' of this thing, our 'objectivity,' be" (GM III §12).
Therefore, perspectivism is not the denial of objectivity but its only honest form: objectivity as the mastery and multiplication of perspectives, owned as perspectives.

**Textual basis.** GM III §12 (the canonical statement); BGE §§14, 22 (physics itself as interpretation; the democratic prejudice in reading laws into nature); GS §374 ("our new 'infinite': the world become interpretable in infinite ways"); Z, "On Immaculate Perception."

**Objection.** The self-refutation objection is as old as the doctrine's fame: is perspectivism itself true — absolutely, non-perspectivally true — or is it only Nietzsche's perspective? If the first, it refutes itself by instantiation; if the second, the slave moralist may decline it as one perspective among others, and the *Genealogy*'s claim on our assent evaporates. A related charge: "the more eyes, the more complete" smuggles in a perspective-independent standard of *completeness* — the very view from nowhere, returning as a criterion.

**Reply.** The standard replies are three, and the literature distributes itself among them. (i) *Self-inclusion without self-refutation*: perspectivism applies to itself and survives the application — it claims not that every perspective is equal but that this perspective, on the question of perspectives, explains more (including why its rivals mistake themselves for none); an interpretation that knows itself an interpretation is embarrassed only if it also claims exemption, which GM III §12 expressly renounces. (ii) *The pragmatic criterion*: Nietzsche ranks perspectives not by correspondence to a naked reality but by their fruits — breadth of what they can incorporate, honesty about their own drives, service to ascending life (Leiter's and Reginster's versions differ in emphasis); "completeness" in premise 4 is read as incorporation of more *affects*, not approach to a God's-eye sum. (iii) *The deflationary reading* (Clark): the mature Nietzsche's perspectivism is an epistemology about the mediation of cognition by interests, fully compatible with ordinary truth — and the theatrical relativism belongs to the slogans, not the texts. The dossier records the objection as live because Nietzsche's rhetoric invites it; the replies are the current state of the question, and none is costless.

**Argument 12. The Schopenhauer inversion: the diagnosis accepted, the cure refused**

1. Schopenhauer's diagnosis, the young Nietzsche received whole and the mature Nietzsche never retracted: existence is willing — a striving that springs from lack, is therefore suffering in its structure, and finds no final satisfaction (see the Schopenhauer dossier, [04-arguments.md](../arthur-schopenhauer/04-arguments.md), "Striving, suffering, and pessimism").
2. Schopenhauer's prescription follows, on his terms, rigorously: if willing is suffering, salvation is the denial of the will — the quieting of striving in compassion, aesthetic release, and finally ascetic renunciation.
3. Nietzsche's counter-move is to interrogate the prescription genealogically: what *type* speaks in the denial of the will? His answer: the denial of life is itself a symptom — the ascetic ideal (Argument 7) in philosophical dress, the will of a declining, exhausted form of life turning against the conditions of life as such. A "pessimism of weakness," set against the "pessimism of strength" of the tragic Greeks, who looked at the same terror and affirmed (GS §370).
4. And the diagnosis itself is revised at its root: the will is not Schopenhauer's will-to-live, bent on preservation — life sacrifices preservation to power constantly (BGE §259) — so the inference "willing is suffering, therefore cease willing" loses its first premise: willing, as will to power, requires resistance and even suffering as its material and stimulus, and a life without them would not be bliss but the last man's doze.
Therefore, the single premise where the student divides from the master is the *value* of suffering: for Schopenhauer, suffering's structural place indicts existence and licenses denial; for Nietzsche, the same fact is the condition of everything great, and existence is to be affirmed — recurrence and *amor fati* (Argument 8) being the instruments of the affirmation.

**Textual basis.** GS §370 ("there is a pessimism of strength" — romantic pessimism, Schopenhauer's included, diagnosed as exhaustion); GS §§341, 276; BGE §§36, 259 (will to power against will-to-live); TI, "What I Owe to the Ancients," §§4–5 (the Dionysian affirmation against the Schopenhauerian reading of tragedy); EH, "The Birth of Tragedy," §§1–2 (Nietzsche's own retrospective account of finding the answer — affirmation — while still inside the question); GM III §7 (the philosopher's asceticism, Schopenhauer's included, as a bridge to independence rather than a destination). The Schopenhauer dossier's own treatment of the shared diagnosis and its capstone's Nietzsche prompt are the cross-reference this argument is built to meet: [Schopenhauer, 04-arguments.md](../arthur-schopenhauer/04-arguments.md).

**Objection.** The inversion is vulnerable to the charge of mere revaluation-by-fiat: Nietzsche grants Schopenhauer every premise about the ubiquity of suffering, then simply *prefers* the opposite conclusion, dressing the preference as genealogy. Schopenhauerians reply that the "pessimism of strength" is biographical romance: the philosopher who had to affirm suffering most loudly was a chronically ill invalid whose affirmation reads, unsympathetically, as ressentiment against his own body — Nietzsche's method, applied to Nietzsche, diagnoses the diagnostician. And empirically, the denial Schopenhauer praised produced, in his own life, neither sanctity nor collapse but a comfortable, art-loving longevity — the cure refused by the patient and taken by no one, least of all its author.

**Reply.** Nietzsche anticipated the method-turned-inward and built it into the doctrine: *Ecce Homo* owns the illness as the very condition of his optics ("I took myself in hand, I made myself healthy again" — the convalescent's perspective, claimed as an epistemic privilege, not hidden as a shame), and perspectivism (Argument 11) explicitly licenses judging a philosophy as the confession of its author's type. The Schopenhauerian counter therefore lands only if the types are ranked by something other than the affirmation they can bear — which is the contested ground, stated honestly in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) as the system's deepest open joint. What the inversion argument indisputably establishes is narrower and sufficient for the dossier: that the two systems share a diagnosis and divide on exactly one premise — the value of willing and its suffering — so that a reader who can locate that premise can navigate both dossiers, and both thinkers, from a single map. The verdict between them is the capstone's business, not the reconstruction's.
