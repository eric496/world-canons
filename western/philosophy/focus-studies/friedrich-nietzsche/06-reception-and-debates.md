# Reception and Debates — The Many Nietzsches

*Focus study: Friedrich Nietzsche · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md)*

No modern philosopher has been more thoroughly remade by his readers. Nietzsche died famous but unread in his own sense — famous as a scandal, a prophet, a madman, a brand — and every era since has produced the Nietzsche it needed. This file traces the succession, then stages the live scholarly debates as debates, and closes with the standard misconceptions in their full form.

## Reception by era

### The lifetime: near-silence, then one lecturer

The books barely sold. Through the 1870s and most of the 1880s Nietzsche's print runs were small, his reviews sparse and often hostile or baffled, and the fourth part of *Zarathustra* (1885) he printed privately — a few dozen copies, distributed by hand — because no publisher would take it. He resigned himself, in letters, to being read posthumously: "some are born posthumously" became *Ecce Homo*'s defiant formula. The first public recognition came from outside Germany and outside philosophy: in the spring of 1888 the Danish critic Georg Brandes lectured on Nietzsche at the University of Copenhagen, the first university lectures devoted to him anywhere. Brandes's coinage for the philosophy, in his correspondence with its author, was **"aristocratic radicalism"** — Nietzsche replied that it was the shrewdest thing anyone had yet said about him. Within two years of the Turin collapse the cult had begun; within five, Nietzsche was a European sensation he could not witness.

### The sister's archive: canonization as curation

Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche — widow of the anti-Semitic agitator Bernhard Förster, whose views her brother had despised in print and in family letters — took control of the estate, founded the Nietzsche-Archiv (Naumburg, 1894; moved to Weimar's Villa Silberblick in 1896), and built the Nietzsche the next half-century received. Her editors assembled his notebooks into *The Will to Power* (1901; expanded 1906, over a thousand sections), presented as his unfinished masterwork; her biographies cast him as a lonely, pure, Germanic prophet, smoothing the biography as the editors smoothed the texts. The Nazi-era courtship was mutual and theatrical: Hitler visited the Archive repeatedly, was photographed with the philosopher's bust, and attended Elisabeth's funeral in state in 1935. That the regime could use Nietzsche at all required suppressing what his published books say plainly and often — contempt for anti-Semites, for German nationalism, for the Reich, and the self-description as a "good European" and a descendant of Polish nobles (itself a family myth he cherished). The scholarly verdict is now settled on the mechanism — the appropriation was *engineered by curation* — while its extent remains debated: Kaufmann argued the Nazi Nietzsche was essentially a falsification, while later historians of the reception complicate the "pure distortion" story by noting what the regime's readers could genuinely find in the aristocratic, anti-egalitarian, war-praising pages. Both things are true, and honest reception history holds them together: the texts that refute the appropriation are his, and so are the texts that made the appropriation worth attempting.

### Kaufmann's rehabilitation — and the gentrification charge

Walter Kaufmann's *Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist* (1950) reset the Anglophone Nietzsche: a humane, anti-totalitarian thinker of self-overcoming, whose sister and the Nazis had forged the monster. Kaufmann's translations then gave English readers the books themselves, and for a generation his Nietzsche — existential, psychological, liberal-compatible — *was* Nietzsche. The counter-charge arrived with the scholarly recovery of the texts: that Kaufmann had **gentrified** him, domesticating the anti-democratic politics, the praise of cruelty and rank, the hardness, into a philosophy of private self-cultivation safe for the postwar university. The charge is a live debate, not a verdict: Kaufmann's defenders reply that he worked against a real forgery with the texts then available and never hid the difficult passages, while his critics hold that selection and emphasis did the softening his argument denied. The dossier's rule follows from the fight itself: read the published works whole, in modern critical editions, and let no interpreter — sister, rehabilitator, or revolutionary — pre-chew them.

### The French century and Heidegger's monument

France produced the other modern Nietzsche. Bataille, who had confronted the Nazi use head-on in the 1930s, wrote *Sur Nietzsche* (1945) as wartime meditation; Deleuze's *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962) rebuilt the thought as a philosophy of forces and affirmation (see [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)); Foucault took the genealogy; Derrida took the style and the masks. In this lineage Nietzsche is the thinker of difference, play, and the dissolution of the subject — the postmodern Nietzsche. Standing opposite, and partly behind, is [Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md)'s monumental construction from the lecture courses of 1936–40: Nietzsche as the last metaphysician, will to power as the completion of Western metaphysics. The two readings despise each other's Nietzsche, and both lean — Heidegger most heavily — on the notebooks whose status is contested below.

### The two modern Nietzsches

Current scholarship lives between two consolidations. The **analytic-naturalist** Nietzsche (Brian Leiter's *Nietzsche on Morality*, 2002, is its manifesto) is a methodological naturalist: his psychology of drives and types continuous with science, his targets free will, the soul, and moral facts, his genealogy an empirical debunking. The **postmodern** Nietzsche of the French line is a philosopher of interpretation, masks, and becoming, for whom every stabilization into doctrine is a betrayal. A third, quieter current — the historical-philological scholarship built on the Colli–Montinari critical edition — keeps demonstrating how much of both figures rests on editorial decisions most readers never see. Which Nietzsche is *the* Nietzsche is the field's permanent question; the debates below are where it is fought.

## Live debates

**(a) Will to power: doctrine or hypothesis?** The published texts support both flags. *Beyond Good and Evil* §36 floats the claim explicitly as a supposition — "supposing that" all organic functions could be traced to will to power, the world viewed from inside *would be* will to power and nothing else — and §259 grounds life's essence in appropriation and overcoming; *Zarathustra* asserts that where he found the living, he found will to power. The ringing formula of the world's total character, however, comes from the notebooks he never published. Readers divide among a metaphysical reading (the world's essence), a psychological one (a hypothesis about drives), and a deliberately experimental one (Nietzsche offering an interpretation that knows itself to be one). The dossier's 03 file presents the doctrine's architecture; the honest label is *contested by design*.

**(b) Eternal recurrence: test or cosmology?** The published presentations are existential: *The Gay Science* §341's demon, *Zarathustra*'s "On the Vision and the Riddle," the doctrine as the highest formula of affirmation. But the notebooks sketch physical arguments — finite force, infinite time, therefore finite combinations recurring — and a few scholars (Löwith among them) have taken the cosmology seriously as intended doctrine, against the mainstream test-reading (Nehamas, and the library entry's own framing). Whether Nietzsche abandoned the proofs or merely withheld them is unresolved; what recurrence *does* in the published work is not in doubt.

**(c) The political Nietzsche.** The starting facts are not in dispute: he was an elitist and an anti-democrat, contemptuous of socialism, liberalism, and the franchise alike, and "aristocratic radicalism" (Brandes's phrase) is a fair label for a real position. The dispute is over the distance from there to fascism. On one side: the textual record — anti-antisemitism, anti-nationalism, hatred of the Reich, praise of mixing peoples, the "good European" — plus the archive's documented curation, make "proto-fascist" a chronological and textual absurdity. On the other: scholars warn that acquitting him by his enemies' list alone whitewashes what his celebration of rank, breeding, hardness, and war shares with the century's worst politics, and that liberal readers (Kaufmann first) have managed this discomfort by privatizing him. No comfortable verdict is available in either direction, and this dossier offers none: the anti-egalitarianism is his, the fascism is not, and the space between those truths is where serious readers work.

**(d) The misogyny passages.** The corpus contains passages no context improves: the notorious maxims about women in *Beyond Good and Evil* (§§231–239), the praise of whips and severity scattered through the aphorisms. The most quoted of all — "You are going to women? Do not forget the whip!" (*Zarathustra* I, "On Little Old and Young Women") — is spoken *by an old woman to Zarathustra*, a dramatic setting that matters and that some readers (following Derrida's *Spurs*) extend into a general case: "woman" in Nietzsche is a shifting figure within a rhetoric that undermines its own pronouncements. The counter-reading holds that dramatic framing cannot launder a pattern that recurs in the non-dramatic prose, in the letters, and in the wreckage of 1882; Irigaray's *Marine Lover* presses the charge from inside an avowed love of the thought. The debate is live; the dossier's position is procedural: quote the passages whole, in context, and never let a single line — damning or exculpatory — stand for the corpus.

**(e) Naturalism or anti-naturalism?** Leiter's naturalist reading takes *Daybreak*'s and the *Genealogy*'s causal-psychological explanations at face value: Nietzsche as an early theorist of the driven, unfree self, methodologically allied to the sciences he praises. Anti-naturalist readers reply that the same texts subordinate science itself to value-creation, treat its faith in truth as the ascetic ideal's last form, and place art and legislation of values above any description of nature — a thinker who uses naturalistic explanation as a weapon without enlisting in its metaphysics. Softer positions (Schacht's, and much current work) treat the naturalism as real but non-reductive. The question matters because it decides what genealogy *is*: science by other means, or a rival to science's own self-image.

**(f) How to read the notebooks.** The Colli–Montinari critical edition (from 1967) printed the *Nachlass* chronologically and dissolved *The Will to Power* as a book: Montinari's research showed the "work" to be an editorial artifact — Nietzsche had abandoned the project and consumed much of its material in *Twilight* and *The Antichrist*. Scholars still divide over the notebooks' evidential weight: Heidegger built his reading on them; the philological consensus treats published text as governing. This library's standing rule, applied throughout the dossier: **the published works govern; notebook material is cited as notebook material, flagged, and never silently promoted into doctrine.** Debates (a) and (b) both change shape under that rule, which is precisely why it is stated here.

## The standard misconceptions, in full

**"Nietzsche was a Nazi philosopher before the fact."** He died in 1900, despised anti-Semites and German nationalism in print, broke with Wagner partly over both, and called himself a good European; the Nazi Nietzsche was manufactured after his collapse by his sister's archive and the regime's curators, against the grain of his published books — which nonetheless contain an aristocratic anti-egalitarianism that belongs to him and should not be edited away to complete the acquittal.

**"'God is dead' is a triumphant slogan."** In *The Gay Science* §125 it is announced by a madman to a laughing crowd that does not understand him, and Nietzsche's gloss is dread: the deed is "too great" for us, and the collapse of the Christian value-system opens the age of nihilism his whole later work tries to overcome. The diagnosis is of a catastrophe in progress, not a victory lap.

**"The Übermensch is a biological superman, a racial ideal, a program."** The overman is barely sketched — a figure in *Zarathustra*'s prologue sermons, the counter-image to the "last man" — naming the human possibility of creating values faithful to the earth after God's death. He has no biology, no race, no party, and no timetable; every attempt to give him one has been an appropriation, not a reading.

**"The whip line tells you what Nietzsche thought about women, full stop."** The line is a character's speech inside a parable, given by an old woman — and the corpus also contains unmediated misogynist aphorisms that need no villain to quote them. Both halves are the record; quoting either alone is advocacy.

**"He went mad from syphilis."** The traditional diagnosis — progressive paralysis, the standard nineteenth-century attribution to syphilis — rests on the Basel and Jena clinic records and was long repeated as settled. Recent medical-historical work has proposed alternatives (including a slow-growing brain tumor and a hereditary small-vessel disease, CADASIL), and the documentary base is too thin to close the question. The dossier treats the cause of the collapse as *disputed*, and the romantic legend of the beaten horse in Turin as what it is: beloved, and poorly documented.

**"Zarathustra is his system, and The Will to Power is his book."** *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* is a literary work — parodic gospel, sermons, songs — that resists doctrinal extraction by design; and *The Will to Power* is a selection from notebooks he never assembled, published by editors he never appointed. The systematic book he actually finished is the *Genealogy*, and the dossier reads him accordingly.
