# Life and Times — Friedrich Nietzsche

*Dossier file 01. The biography, with the world attached; the works' occasions are treated here and their contents in [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md).*

## Röcken and Naumburg: the pastor's son (1844–1858)

Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche was born on October 15, 1844, in Röcken, a Saxon village near Lützen, where his father, Carl Ludwig Nietzsche, was the Lutheran pastor. The household was steeped in the pietism of two clerical families: both grandfathers were Lutheran clergymen, and the boy was named for the Prussian king, Friedrich Wilhelm IV, whose birthday he shared. In July 1849 Carl Ludwig died, aged thirty-six, after months of agonizing illness — the sources describe a "softening of the brain" — and the infant brother, Ludwig Joseph, died the following January. Nietzsche was four. Everything he later wrote about Christianity, consolation, and the death of God was written by a man whose first experience of God was a household organized around a young pastor's grave.

The widow Franziska moved the family in 1850 to Naumburg, where Nietzsche grew up in a household of women — mother, grandmother, two aunts, and his younger sister Elisabeth — pious, respectable, and genteel-poor. He was a dutiful, intense child: a model pupil, a composer of hymns and poems, nicknamed "the little pastor" by schoolmates for his earnest biblical recitations. The piety was real, and so was the slow private estrangement from it: by his middle teens his notebooks already hold the critical questions he never stopped asking.

## Pforta and the universities (1858–1869)

In 1858 Nietzsche won a scholarship to Schulpforta (Pforta), one of Germany's famous humanistic boarding schools, a former Cistercian monastery run with monastic discipline: Latin and Greek from dawn, classical philology as the school's religion. He left in 1864 with superb Greek, lasting friendships (Paul Deussen among them), and a faith quietly lost — the confirmation had been gone through, but the allegiance was already gone.

At Bonn in 1864 he enrolled in theology and philology, joined and quickly fled a student fraternity, and followed his teacher Friedrich Wilhelm Ritschl to Leipzig in 1865 when Ritschl quarreled with his Bonn colleague and moved. Leipzig was the making of him. Under Ritschl — the great textual critic who recognized and promoted him with unusual warmth — Nietzsche published precocious articles on Theognis and Diogenes Laertius in the *Rheinisches Museum*, the field's leading journal, while still a student. And in the autumn of 1865, browsing a Leipzig secondhand bookshop, he picked up a book he had never heard of: Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation*. His later account of the discovery — in letters and in his retrospective sketch of the Leipzig years — describes two weeks of sleepless reading; it is his own account, offered years later, and biographers repeat it with that flag attached. What is documentary is the effect. Schopenhauer's pessimism, his contempt for academic philosophy, and his honesty about suffering gave the young philologist a philosophical allegiance he spent the rest of his sane life first serving and then overcoming.

A year of military service with a mounted artillery regiment at Naumburg (1867–68) ended with a chest injury sustained mounting a horse — the wound suppurated, and with it ended any thought of a soldier's life. It was the first of the bodily catastrophes that punctuate the biography.

## Basel: the chair at twenty-four (1869–1879)

In February 1869 the University of Basel, needing a professor of classical philology, asked Ritschl whether his student was ready. Ritschl's testimonial was extravagant — in forty years, he wrote, he had seen no young man mature so early — and Basel appointed Nietzsche, aged twenty-four, extraordinary professor, before he had taken his doctorate. The University of Leipzig remedied that in March 1869, awarding the doctorate without examination and without a dissertation, on the strength of the published articles and Ritschl's recommendation. Nietzsche renounced his Prussian citizenship on taking up the post and was stateless thereafter.

Basel gave him colleagues worth having — above all the historian Jacob Burckhardt, whose lectures on Greek culture he attended and admired, and the theologian Franz Overbeck, who became his most loyal friend — and it gave him Wagner. Nietzsche had been a Wagnerian since adolescence; in November 1868 he met the composer in Leipzig, and from May 1869 he was a regular guest at Tribschen, Wagner's villa on Lake Lucerne, an hour from Basel. He visited more than twenty times. Tribschen — Wagner and Cosima, music, conversation, the sense of standing at the birth of a cultural rebirth — was the emotional center of his early life, and *The Birth of Tragedy* (published January 1872) was its philosophical monument: Greek tragedy as the marriage of Apollonian form and Dionysian ecstasy, modern culture to be redeemed by German music.

The profession answered with a savaging. Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff, a younger Pforta alumnus who would become the century's most powerful classicist, published a pamphlet, *"Zukunftsphilologie!"* ("Philology of the Future!", 1872), dissecting the book's scholarly errors and its Wagnerian presumption. Erwin Rohde, Nietzsche's friend, published a counterblast; Wagner published an open letter of support. The damage was done where it mattered: philology students stopped enrolling at Basel, and Nietzsche's professional standing never recovered. The episode matters for the dossier because it fixed a pattern — the book written for an imagined posterity, the guild's rejection, the defiance that hardened into method.

In 1870, war with France called him up as a volunteer medical orderly — as a Swiss resident and non-citizen he could serve only in that capacity. At the front he contracted dysentery and diphtheria, was invalided home, and the damage to his digestion and general health was permanent. Through the 1870s the Basel routine continued — teaching, the four *Untimely Meditations* (1873–76), deepening intimacy with the painter-philosopher circle around the Wagners — while the friendship's foundations cracked. Nietzsche's private reservations about Wagner's nationalism, his anti-Semitism, and the cult forming at Bayreuth were growing; Wagner's circle read Nietzsche's distance as betrayal.

The turning point was the first Bayreuth Festival in August 1876: the social spectacle of the Reich's notables admiring themselves in Wagner's theater sickened him, and he fled mid-festival to Klingenbrunn in the Bohemian forest, sketching the aphorisms of a new, cooler book. The public rupture came in 1878. That January Wagner sent him the text of *Parsifal*, with its Christian redemption plot; Nietzsche's answering gift was *Human, All Too Human* (published May 1878), dedicated to the memory of Voltaire, a book of Enlightenment aphorisms against metaphysics, romanticism, and German cultural self-satisfaction. The two packages crossed in the post; each man understood the other's meaning. They never met again. Wagner died in Venice in February 1883, and Nietzsche's grief, when the news reached him, was genuine and complicated — the later polemics (*The Case of Wagner*, 1888) are written against a man he never stopped measuring himself against.

## The free spirit and the wanderer (1879–1882)

By 1879 the Basel years had become physically impossible. The migraines — three-day attacks of blinding pain and vomiting — the failing eyes (he was desperately myopic, and reading itself became a rationed activity), and the ruined digestion forced his resignation in June 1879, aged thirty-four, on a modest university pension he drew for the rest of his sane life. What followed was a decade of stateless wandering in search of a climate his body could tolerate: St. Moritz and Sils-Maria in the Engadine each summer, Genoa, Nice, Turin, Rapallo in the winters, cheap rooms, a portable typewriter (a Danish writing ball, bought 1882, among the first used by a major writer), and a production of books that accelerated as his health worsened: *The Wanderer and His Shadow* (1880), *Daybreak* (1881), *The Gay Science* (1882).

At Sils-Maria in early August 1881, walking by Lake Silvaplana near the village of Surlej, he was stopped — by his own later account in *Ecce Homo* — beside a great pyramidal block of stone by "the thought" of eternal recurrence, "6,000 feet beyond man and time." *Ecce Homo* is a self-mythologizing document and the account is attributed here as his; but the notebook evidence corroborates the date and place, and recurrence became the load-bearing thought of everything after.

## Lou Salomé (1882)

In Rome in the spring of 1882, through their common friend Paul Rée and the salon of Malwida von Meysenbug, Nietzsche met Lou Salomé, a brilliant twenty-one-year-old Russian student of philosophy and theology. He proposed marriage almost immediately — through Rée as intermediary, the first time; a second proposal, made directly, was also refused. Salomé proposed instead a chaste intellectual "trinity": she, Rée, and Nietzsche living and studying together. For a few months it existed, in travels and plans and one famous staged photograph at Lucerne (May 1882): Salomé kneeling in a small cart, holding a whip of lilac branches, with Rée and Nietzsche harnessed before it — her joke, his unease preserved in gelatin silver. The summer at Tautenburg, where Salomé stayed near Nietzsche with his sister Elisabeth in attendance as chaperone, was the idyll's high point and its end. By the autumn the arrangement had collapsed, poisoned by Elisabeth's furious opposition — she regarded Salomé as an immoral adventuress and worked against the match by letter and in person — and by the triangle's own tensions. Nietzsche left the episode believing himself betrayed by both Rée and Elisabeth; the friendship with Rée, his closest intellectual companion of the free-spirit years, never recovered.

The documents establish the sequence — the proposals, the refusals, the photograph, the rupture. What they do not establish is motive: whether Salomé ever contemplated marriage, how far Elisabeth's campaign (including a notorious letter to Salomé that Salomé later published) caused the collapse rather than merely hastening it, and how much of Nietzsche's devastation was erotic, how much intellectual loneliness, remain matters of scholarly debate.

## Zarathustra and the great year (1883–1888)

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* came in bursts, in the places his health allowed: Part I at Rapallo in February 1883 (ten days' composition, by his account), Part II at Sils that summer, Part III at Nice in the winter of 1883–84, Part IV in 1885 — this last printed privately at his own expense in an edition of some forty copies, since no publisher would take the risk. The book sold almost nothing. Nobody was listening; the letters of these years alternate between a serenity his friends found eerie and outbursts of desolation at his total neglect. He wrote anyway: *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), a set of defiant new prefaces to his earlier books, the fifth book of *The Gay Science* (1887), and *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887), the most systematic thing he ever published, written as a clarification of the book before it.

Then came 1888, a year of terrifying productivity: *The Case of Wagner* (written in May), *Twilight of the Idols* (August), *The Antichrist* (September), *Ecce Homo* (October–November), and *Nietzsche contra Wagner* (December) — five books, composed at white heat in Turin, whose clarity and euphoria his friends read, in retrospect, as symptoms. That spring Georg Brandes had given the first university lectures on Nietzsche, at Copenhagen, and admiration was beginning to arrive — perhaps ten years too late.

## Turin and the long silence (1889–1900)

On January 3, 1889, in Turin, Nietzsche's mind broke. What can be documented: he collapsed in or near the Piazza Carlo Alberto, was taken back to his lodgings by his landlord, and in the following days wrote a flood of extraordinary letters to friends and public figures — to Burckhardt, Overbeck, Cosima Wagner ("Ariadne, I love you"), the King of Italy — signed alternately "Dionysus" and "The Crucified," announcing world-historical decrees. Burckhardt, alarmed, alerted Overbeck, who traveled to Turin and brought him home.

The famous story attached to that day — that Nietzsche, seeing a horse being beaten in the street, threw his arms around its neck weeping and collapsed — comes from later, anecdotal retellings (it circulates through the Turin landlord's family and journalistic accounts of the following decades), not from any contemporary document. Modern biographers treat it as legend: possible in outline, unverifiable in every detail that makes it affecting. It is printed here as what it is — the best-known anecdote about Nietzsche and among the least well evidenced.

Overbeck delivered him to the psychiatric clinic at Basel and then to the asylum at Jena under Otto Binswanger. The official diagnosis entered in the Jena records was *paralysis progressiva* — general paresis, the tertiary stage of syphilis — and for a century that diagnosis stood in the biographies as fact. It is no longer secure. The evidence for the syphilis infection itself is thin and circumstantial; and since the 2000s clinicians and biographers have proposed alternatives that fit pieces of the record the syphilis story fits badly — among them CADASIL, a hereditary small-vessel stroke disorder (argued from his father's early death of brain disease and Friedrich's lifelong migraines), and a slow-growing tumor behind the right eye (argued from his asymmetrical vision loss). The dossier records the traditional diagnosis as the traditional diagnosis, notes that it is now genuinely disputed, and offers no verdict: the evidence surviving does not support one.

In March 1890 his mother Franziska took him home to Naumburg, nursing him herself; he could still walk, play the piano, and hold fragments of conversation, and then, year by year, less. Franziska died in April 1897, and Elisabeth — widowed, back from the wreck of her husband's colonial venture in Paraguay — moved her brother to the Villa Silberblick in Weimar and took control of the household, the visitors, and the papers. He died there on August 25, 1900, after pneumonia and a series of strokes, and was buried at Röcken beside his father. He was famous by then across Europe. He knew none of it.

## The sister and the archive (an outline)

Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche (1846–1935) had married Bernhard Förster, a leading anti-Semitic agitator of exactly the kind her brother despised in print and in letters, and had emigrated with him to found the "pure German" colony of Nueva Germania in Paraguay (1886). The colony failed; Förster killed himself in 1889; Elisabeth returned to Germany in 1893 and set about becoming her brother's literary executor, a role she pursued with relentless energy for forty years. She founded the Nietzsche-Archiv (1894, at Naumburg; from 1896 at Weimar), controlled access to the manuscripts, edited the published correspondence with strategic omissions, wrote a hagiographic biography, and — most consequentially — arranged selections from the notebooks of the 1880s into *The Will to Power* (1901; expanded 1906), presented to the world as Nietzsche's unfinished magnum opus. It is nothing of the kind: it is an editorial construction, built by his sister's collaborators from material he had abandoned and partly already used, in an order he never made (see [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)).

In the Weimar years she curated her brother as a national prophet, welcomed völkisch and later Nazi admirers to the Villa Silberblick, received Hitler — who was photographed at the archive and accepted the gift of Nietzsche's walking stick — and accepted a state pension from the regime. The Nazi embrace was real but never total: party ideologues were divided over a thinker who had broken with Wagner partly over anti-Semitism, called himself an "anti-anti-Semite," despised German nationalism, and said the things about priests and race that readers can still check on the page. Alfred Baeumler's influential Nazi-era reading got its Nietzsche by amputation, cutting everything that did not fit. The full accounting of the damage — scholarly, political, reputational — belongs to [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md); here it is enough to say that the life ended in 1900 but the custody fight over its meaning ran for another half-century, and that the sister, not the collapse, is the biography's last antagonist.
