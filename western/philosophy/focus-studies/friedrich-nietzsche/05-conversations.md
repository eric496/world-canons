# Nietzsche in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: Friedrich Nietzsche · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md)*

Nietzsche wrote dialogues with the dead as readily as with the living, and several of his most important relationships were with books rather than people. This file traces the conversations in three directions: the predecessors he measured himself against, the contemporaries who actually shared his years, and the readers — often ones he never imagined — who answered him. The deepest of these conversations, with Schopenhauer, has a dossier of its own in this library, and the two are a designed pair.

## Backward: the predecessors

### Schopenhauer: the educator he had to refuse

In the autumn of 1865 the twenty-one-year-old student found a secondhand copy of *The World as Will and Representation* in a Leipzig bookshop and read it, by his later account, as if it had been written for him. The third *Untimely Meditation*, *Schopenhauer as Educator* (1874), made the debt public: the older man was the model of the honest philosopher, the man who thought for himself against the university herd, and Nietzsche's early metaphysics — the Will, art as release from individuation, music as the deepest art — was Schopenhauer's system taken over nearly whole.

The mature break was equally total, and it was a break *within* an agreement. The diagnosis is shared: existence is striving, suffering is structural, morality as preached is a symptom to be explained rather than a truth to be obeyed, and the intellect is the servant of drives it does not control. What Nietzsche refuses is the cure. Where Schopenhauer counsels the denial of the will-to-live, Nietzsche revalues the Will as *will to power* and prescribes affirmation; where Schopenhauer founds morality on compassion, the *Genealogy* indicts pity as the practice of declining life; pessimism itself becomes, in the Third Essay, the ascetic ideal's most honest form — honesty conscripted by sickness. The [Schopenhauer dossier](../arthur-schopenhauer/README.md) tells this conversation from the other side, including the exact premise Nietzsche refuses; read the two dossiers together, because each is the other's sharpest commentary.

### Kant: the shadow behind the early work

Nietzsche read little [Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) at first hand and absorbed him largely through Schopenhauer and Lange, but the shadow is everywhere. *The Birth of Tragedy* still works inside a Kantian frame: appearance on one side, and on the other a thing-in-itself that art — music above all — somehow touches. The mature Nietzsche evicts the tenant. The thing-in-itself becomes, in *Twilight of the Idols*' "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable," an idea that has lost its function: useless, superfluous, refuted. The categorical imperative gets the genealogical treatment: in his reading — and it should be labeled his reading, contested by Kant scholars — unconditional duty is the ascetic ideal in philosophical dress, theology's command surviving as the form of morality after its content has died, with Kant's rigorism about inclination betraying the priestly instinct underneath. What he kept from the Kantian inheritance was the boundary-drawing impulse itself, redirected: not reason limiting itself before the unknowable, but honesty dissolving the unknowable as a bad posit.

### The Greeks: the counter-canon

The professional philologist never stopped being one. Against the serene, rational Greece of the textbooks he set the pre-Platonic philosophers — Heraclitus above all ([Heraclitus of Ephesus](../../eras/01-ancient/heraclitus-of-ephesus.md)): the thinker of becoming, of the world as an eternally living fire and an innocent game, whom Nietzsche praised in *Ecce Homo* as the one ancient in whose proximity he felt altogether warmer and better. In the posthumous *Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks* the early Ionians are his counter-canon: philosophers before philosophy's fall.

The fall has a name. Socrates is Nietzsche's most ambivalent ancient ([Socrates](../../eras/01-ancient/socrates.md)). *The Birth of Tragedy* made him the turning point of world history: the first *theoretical man*, whose optimism — existence is intelligible, therefore justified — killed tragedy. *Twilight*'s "Problem of Socrates" sharpens the charge into physiology: Socrates as *décadent*, his dialectic the weapon of a man who could no longer trust his instincts and needed reasons as a tyrant over them. But the ambivalence is genuine and documented: Nietzsche confessed in a notebook that Socrates stood so close to him that he was almost always fighting a battle with him. The opponent he could not dismiss was, he knew, another physician of culture diagnosing a sickness — and a fellow ironist.

### Wagner: art, then apostasy

The Wagner conversation began as discipleship: the visits to Tribschen from 1869, *The Birth of Tragedy* written under the spell of the music dramas, the metaphysics of music inherited straight from Schopenhauer. It ended as the century's most famous artistic divorce. *Human, All Too Human* (1878) — cool, aphoristic, dedicated to Voltaire — announced the free spirit's break with romanticism, and the two men exchanged their last books in mutual silence: Wagner sent the *Parsifal* poem, Nietzsche sent his. What Nietzsche came to hear in *Parsifal* was surrender: Christianity, nationalism, and anti-Semitism dressed as redemption — in his word, *decadence*, which *The Case of Wagner* (1888) turned into a general theory of modern art as sickness. That he still called Wagner his greatest artistic experience, and meant it, is what makes the settling of accounts a post-mortem on something real rather than a feud.

### Darwin, the English psychologists, and Paul Rée

Three related provocations sharpened the genealogical method. Against Darwin he lodged a pointed objection, repeated from *Beyond Good and Evil* §13 to *The Gay Science* §349: life is not fundamentally self-preservation — a living thing wants above all to *discharge* its strength — and "natural selection" as the English framed it mistakes the exception (struggle, in the crowded island of Malthus) for the rule. The "English psychologists" who open the *Genealogy* get the methodological version of the same charge: their accounts of morality's origin are ahistorical moralizing dressed as history, deriving "good" from utility and forgetting because they never asked what kind of creature needs such derivations.

Paul Rée was the living version. His *Origin of the Moral Sensations* (1877) — Darwinian, aphoristic, cool — stands visibly behind the method of *Human, All Too Human*, and Nietzsche's debt to Rée's stimulus was real before it was repudiated. The *Genealogy*'s Preface performs the repudiation carefully: Rée's hypotheses were among those Nietzsche read in order to say *no* to them, replacing derivation-by-utility with derivation-by-*ressentiment*. The personal rupture — over Lou Salomé, in 1882 — belongs to the biography; the philosophical point is that genealogy was born in an argument with its own first draft.

### Emerson and Stirner: the quiet debt and the alleged one

[Emerson](../../eras/04-19th-century/emerson-and-thoreau.md) is the influence readers most often miss, and the best documented of all. Nietzsche read Emerson's *Essays* from his school years to the end, annotated his copies heavily, and carried them on his wanderings; the self-trust, the cheerfulness, the suspicion of conformity and of morality as custom, the very aphoristic grain of his prose, have an American accent that scholars (notably George Stack's studies of the annotated volumes) have traced in detail. If Schopenhauer was the educator he had to refuse, Emerson was the one he never needed to.

Max Stirner is the opposite case: an influence perpetually alleged and never documented. Stirner's *The Ego and Its Own* (1845; he shares a [sidebar entry](../../eras/04-19th-century/sidebars-feuerbach-and-stirner.md) in this library) anticipates several Nietzschean notes — the ego against every "spook" of fixed idea and sacred cause — and the parallels were already being remarked in Nietzsche's lifetime. But no copy of Stirner's book is documented in his library, no letter or notebook names him, and the routes proposed for indirect transmission (through Lange, through Hartmann, through the general air of post-Hegelian radicalism) remain conjectural. Honest scholarship currently files the matter under striking parallel, unproven debt — which is itself a useful lesson in how influence claims should be evidenced.

## Sideways: the Basel circle and the interlocutor

**Jacob Burckhardt** — who has no entry of his own in this library — was the Basel colleague Nietzsche most respected: the great historian of the Renaissance, whose lectures on the study of history Nietzsche attended and whose cool skepticism about power and culture shadows the early essays. Their correspondence survived the break with professional life, and it was Burckhardt who, receiving one of the delirious January 1889 letters, alerted Overbeck that something was terribly wrong.

**Franz Overbeck** was the truest friend: fellow Basel professor (of church history), housemate, and the man whose loyalty was entirely undogmatic — he never became a disciple, which is why Nietzsche trusted him. In January 1889 Overbeck traveled to Turin and brought his collapsed friend home. His posthumous memoirs are among the most reliable witnesses to the man.

**Lou Andréas-Salomé** belongs here as an intellectual interlocutor, not only as the catastrophe of 1882. The twenty-one-year-old — to whom Nietzsche proposed twice, once by proxy through Rée — was part of the famous Lucerne photograph (she holding a whip over the two men harnessed to a cart, a joke the later caricatures never let go of), and her *Friedrich Nietzsche in seinen Werken* (1894) was the first book-length study of his thought: acute, psychological, and still read. Nietzsche's fury at her belongs to the biography; her standing as his first serious interpreter belongs to the reception.

## Forward: the answers

### Freud and psychoanalysis: the disavowed debt

Freud has no entry in this library, and his Nietzsche is a case study in anxious proximity. On the record, Freud repeatedly insisted he had avoided Nietzsche deliberately — in the Vienna society's 1908 discussions of Nietzsche he remarked that Nietzsche's premonitions and insights often agree in the most astonishing way with the laborious results of psychoanalysis, and that he had long avoided him for precisely that reason: he wanted to come to his findings with an unembarrassed mind. The priority question and the influence question must be kept apart. That Nietzsche anticipated central psychoanalytic motifs — unconscious drives, sublimation, the psychology of *ressentiment* and bad conscience — is plain on the page. That Freud derived them from reading he disowned is a claim his testimony denies and the documentary record cannot settle; scholars are divided, and the dossier leaves it there.

### Heidegger: the completion of metaphysics

[Heidegger](../../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md) devoted lecture courses of 1936–40 to Nietzsche and made him the pivot of the history of Being: the *last metaphysician*, in whom Western metaphysics completes itself — will to power as the being of beings, eternal recurrence as the permanentizing of becoming, and Nietzsche's revaluation as still a value-thinking inside Platonism's inverted frame. It is a monumental reading, and a violent one: critics have charged that Heidegger's Nietzsche, forced onto the question of Being and reconstructed heavily from the notebooks, is as much Heidegger's construction as Kaufmann's was his. Both charges, note, turn on the same editorial problem treated in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

### Deleuze, Foucault, and the French Nietzsche

Gilles Deleuze — no library entry — published *Nietzsche and Philosophy* in 1962 and restarted the French century: genealogy recast as a typology of forces, active and reactive, with *ressentiment* and bad conscience as reactive force's triumphs, and evaluation replacing interpretation as philosophy's task. Michel [Foucault](../../eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md) took the method rather than the doctrines: his 1971 essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" is the library's clearest statement of what genealogy becomes as historiography — a refusal of *origins* (Ursprung) in favor of descent (Herkunft) and emergence (Entstehung), history as the record of accidents, errors, and reversals written on bodies. Jacques [Derrida](../../eras/05-20th-century/jacques-derrida.md)'s *Spurs* (1978) added the Nietzsche of style and masks. Between them, these readers made Nietzsche the philosopher of post-structuralism — a fate examined, not simply celebrated, in the next file.

### The existentialists and the analytic recovery

The existentialists claimed a Nietzsche of self-creation: Jaspers's great 1936 study mediated him to a generation, and [Sartre](../../eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md)'s philosophy of radical self-making breathes Nietzschean air even where the citations are sparse; Camus wrestled him in *The Rebel* as the thinker who took nihilism seriously enough to try to pass through it. The Anglophone analytic recovery came later and on different terms: Alexander Nehamas's *Nietzsche: Life as Literature* (1985) made the aphorisms philosophically respectable by reading the self as a literary creation, and Brian Leiter's *Nietzsche on Morality* (2002) consolidated a naturalist Nietzsche — methodologically continuous with the sciences, hostile to free will and the soul on empirical-psychological grounds. Neither Nehamas nor Leiter has a library entry; they mark the two poles — literary and naturalist — between which current scholarship still swings.

### The feminist readings

No honest forward trace can skip the readers who took the misogyny head-on. Luce Irigaray — no library entry — wrote *Marine Lover of Friedrich Nietzsche* (1980), a critique conducted as an address to him: sensuous, unsparing, and structured as love refusing blindness, pressing him on the feminine as the matter his philosophy of becoming could celebrate everywhere except in women. Other feminist readers have split between treating the notorious passages as masks within a self-undermining rhetoric (the line associated with Derrida's *Spurs*) and treating them as plain, recurrent prejudice that no context dissolves. The passages themselves, and the whip line in its dramatic setting, are dealt with directly in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## Nietzsche in the nine problems

The library's [themes index](../../themes.md) places him in four of its nine threads.

- **§3 [God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason)** — God is dead — killed by our own truthfulness; Christian morality stands exposed as the slave revolt in values.
- **§4 [Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right)** — Genealogy unmasks "good and evil" as slave morality's invention; beyond it stand self-overcoming and the creation of one's own values.
- **§7 [Language & Meaning](../../themes.md#7-language--meaning--how-do-words-and-thoughts-hook-onto-the-world)** — Truths are metaphors worn smooth: language herds experience into common concepts and hides that every concept falsifies becoming.
- **§9 [History & Progress](../../themes.md#9-history--progress--does-history-have-a-direction-and-can-we-know-it)** — Genealogy is history turned into a critical weapon; his early essay weighs monumental, antiquarian, and critical history by their service to life.

Beyond the index's four placements: on **§1 Knowledge** his perspectivism — no immaculate perception, objectivity as the disciplined multiplication of perspectives — is arguably the thread's most consequential modern entry, and its absence there is the index's choice, not his irrelevance. On **§2 Mind & Body** the self is a commonwealth of drives and the body "a great reason": psychology as morphology of the will to power. On **§5 Politics** the index does not place him, though his aristocratic radicalism is a real and troubling position — the debate over it is staged in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md). On **§6 Self & Identity** stands self-overcoming: "become who you are" as a task of giving style to one's character. On **§8 Science & Method** genealogy itself is the methodological legacy, joined to his charge that even science rests on a faith — the ascetic ideal's latest form — a charge the *Genealogy*'s Third Essay aims at scholars as much as priests.
