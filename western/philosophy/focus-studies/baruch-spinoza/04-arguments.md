# Spinoza: The Arguments

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Baruch Spinoza](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) · The system these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — Spinoza's own where he gave one, the literature's where he didn't, labeled as such. Where scholars dispute what the argument even is, the dispute is presented as a dispute. Citation follows the stable scholarly convention: **E1P14** = *Ethics*, Part I, Proposition 14 (D = definition, A = axiom, S = scholium, Pref = preface, App = appendix); **TTP** and **TP** by chapter; letters by number.

---

**Argument 1. Substance monism: there can be only one substance**

1. A substance is what is in itself and is conceived through itself; a mode is what is in another and is conceived through another (E1D3, E1D5) — and everything is one or the other (E1A1–A2).
2. Two or more distinct things are distinguished either by a difference of attributes or by a difference of modes (E1P4).
3. But a substance cannot be distinguished from another substance of the same attribute by its modes, because substance is by nature prior to its modes (E1P1): strip the modes away in thought, and the substances themselves remain undifferentiated.
4. Therefore, in the same nature or attribute there cannot be two or more substances (E1P5); and two substances with different attributes have nothing in common with each other (E1P2), so neither can cause the other (E1P3, by E1A4–A5), hence no substance can be produced by another (E1P6).
5. Existence therefore belongs to the nature of substance itself: a substance is self-caused, and cannot be conceived not to exist (E1P7); nor can it be finite, since finitude is limitation by another of the same kind, and there is no other of its kind (E1P8).
6. God is by definition a substance consisting of infinite attributes, each expressing an eternal and infinite essence (E1D6), and, since existence belongs to substance's nature, God necessarily exists (E1P11).
Therefore, except God no substance can be or be conceived (E1P14), and whatever is, is in God, and nothing can be or be conceived without God (E1P15) — one substance, everything else its modes.

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* I, Definitions 1–6, Axioms 1–7, Propositions 1–15; the scholium to E1P15 answers the divisibility and corporeality worries; fuller walkthrough in the study guide, [*Ethics* Part I](../../study/spinoza-ethics-part1.md).

**Objection.** The classic objection is that the proof buys its conclusion in premise 1 and 3. Descartes accepted that minds and bodies are conceived through themselves *in the relevant sense* while depending on God for existence; Spinoza's tightened definition simply legislates created substances out of existence. And premise 3 has drawn fire since the beginning: why can't two substances share an attribute and differ primitively, or by their modes understood as belonging to them essentially? On Leibniz's alternative, reality contains infinitely many substances, each expressing the whole universe from its own point of view, and nothing in the bare concept of substance forbids it. If distinguishing-by-modes is rejected as too quick, the chain from E1P5 to E1P14 breaks at its first link.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own defense is that his definitions are not stipulations: E1D3 makes explicit what "existing in itself" must mean once the alternative — a thing both independent enough to be a substance and dependent enough to be produced — is seen to be incoherent; a produced substance would have to be conceived through its producer (E1A4), contradicting its definition. The literature's best development (in the tradition of Edwin Curley's and Don Garrett's reconstructions) adds that premise 3 trades on a genuinely strong principle: within one attribute there is no *explanatory room* for a second substance — any distinguishing feature would have to be either an attribute (shared, by hypothesis) or a mode (posterior, by E1P1). The reply concedes what it should: reject the definitional framework, and monism does not follow. Spinoza's wager is that no rival framework explains conception, causation, and existence with equal economy — a wager readers are still taking sides on.

**Argument 2. God exists necessarily — and everything follows necessarily**

1. God's essence involves existence: to conceive God is to conceive a substance whose nature cannot be conceived except as existing (E1D1, D6; E1A7: if a thing can be conceived as not existing, its essence does not involve existence).
2. No cause outside God could prevent God's existence, since outside substance nothing exists (E1P15); and God's essence itself contains no contradiction.
3. Therefore God necessarily exists (E1P11 — demonstrated three ways, with the scholium adding that the argument is a priori what the proof of a triangle's properties is: existence follows from the nature conceived).
4. From the necessity of the divine nature there must follow infinitely many things in infinitely many modes (E1P16): the greater the reality a definition expresses, the more follows from it, and God's definition expresses absolute infinity.
5. God acts from the laws of his nature alone, compelled by none (E1P17), and God is the immanent cause of all things (E1P18).
6. In nature there is nothing contingent: all things are determined by the necessity of the divine nature to exist and act in a certain way (E1P29).
Therefore, things could have been produced by God in no other manner and in no other order than they have been produced (E1P33) — there are no unactualized possibilities, for God or for anything else.

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* I, Propositions 11, 16–18, 29, 33 (with E1P33S1 on "contingent" as a defect of our knowledge, and E1P17S on divine intellect and will).

**Objection.** Two standard attacks. First, E1P11 is an ontological argument, and inherits the family weakness Kant made canonical: existence is not a predicate or perfection contained in a concept; from "God's concept involves existence" it follows only that *if* God exists, he exists necessarily — not that anything answers to the concept. Second, E1P33's modal collapse proves too much for many readers: it makes God's own nature the only possible one and converts every truth into a necessary truth, including the premises of this very argument — a result some scholars (Curley prominently) have doubted Spinoza can consistently want, since it leaves the difference between demonstrating and merely unfolding a definition invisible.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own resources against the Kantian move are real, if not decisive: his God is not one being among others whose concept might be empty — the argument of E1P8 and the second demonstration in E1P11 work from the existence of *something* (us, at minimum) to the necessary existence of a self-caused substance, since an existing mode requires a substance whose existence is not borrowed. Against the modal worry, his reply is to accept the consequence with composure: necessity all the way down is not a reductio but the doctrine (E1P29, P33), and the appearance of contingency is diagnosed, not accommodated, as ignorance of causes. The literature remains split on whether the modal system of Part I is as total as P33's words suggest; the dossier presents the dominant reading — necessitarianism — as Spinoza's, with the minority reading flagged in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).

**Argument 3. Against teleology: final causes are human fictions**

1. Men are born ignorant of the causes of things, but conscious of their appetites — they know that they seek what is useful to them, and that they act for ends.
2. Judging nature by their own case, they suppose that all natural things also act for ends, and that a God or gods direct everything for human use: eyes were made for seeing, the sun for giving light, storms as punishment.
3. When events refuse this reading — a falling roof-tile kills a passer-by — the explanation retreats to God's inscrutable will: what Spinoza calls "the sanctuary of ignorance," a name for our ignorance masquerading as its explanation.
4. Purposive explanation inverts the true order: it takes what is in fact an effect of a thing's nature and our relation to it (the usefulness of eyes to us) and treats it as the prior cause of the thing's existence.
5. A perfect being, lacking nothing, cannot act in order to obtain something it lacks; a God who acts for ends would be a God in want (and the ends alleged are always, tellingly, human ends).
Therefore, all final causes are nothing but human fictions; nature has no end set before it, and every doctrine built on providence, desert, and cosmic reward or punishment is imagination, not knowledge.

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* I, Appendix (with E1P17's corollaries and E1P29–P33 on the necessity of Nature's order).

**Objection.** The standing objection is that the argument proves too much — against Spinoza himself. Organisms look designed: hearts are *for* pumping, and biology still finds purposive language indispensable. Worse, Spinoza's own conatus (Argument 6) describes every thing as *striving* toward its preservation — an apparently teleological notion at the center of his physics and ethics. If striving is real, final causation is real in at least one case; if it is a metaphor, Part III's foundations are metaphorical. A second objection presses premise 5: classical theists from Aquinas onward deny that a perfect being's acting for ends implies deficiency — God acts for the diffusion of goodness, not from need — so the argument defeats only a crude, needy God.

**Reply.** Spinoza's reply to the teleology-in-his-own-system charge is a distinction, partly explicit in the Appendix and E4 Preface, partly developed by the literature: conatus is not conscious purpose and not design. A thing's striving is the necessary expression of its essence — the heart's "purpose" is shorthand for what follows from its structure, exactly as a triangle's properties follow from its essence without the triangle aiming at them. Purposive *language* is harmless as description; what the Appendix bans is purpose as *explanation* — a designer standing behind nature, arranging it for ends, above all for ours. To the Aquinas-style reply: Spinoza would respond that an end pursued without lack is an end in name only, and that the Appendix's target is the inference from nature's arrangements to a purposive governor of them; on the narrower theological point, the schools genuinely disagree, and the exchange is presented here as unfinished.

**Argument 4. Parallelism: the order of ideas is the order of things**

1. The knowledge of an effect depends on, and involves, the knowledge of its cause (E1A4): the order of conception mirrors the order of causation.
2. Whatever follows formally from God's nature under any attribute follows objectively in God, in the same order and connection, as the idea of it (the demonstration of E2P7, from E1A4 and the attribute doctrine of Part I).
3. Substance thinking and substance extended are one and the same substance, comprehended now under this attribute, now under that (E2P7S).
Therefore, "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" (E2P7) — not by interaction, but by identity: "a circle existing in nature and the idea of the existing circle, which is also in God, are one and the same thing, which is explained through different attributes" (E2P7S).

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* II, Proposition 7 and scholium; reinforced by E2P21S (mind and body as one and the same individual under two attributes) and E3P2 (neither body nor mind determines the other).

**Objection.** Two pressures. First, the demonstration is accused of smuggling: E1A4 says knowledge of an effect involves knowledge of its cause, from which a parallel *order of knowledge* follows — but the identity of the orders of *being* (that every bodily event has its exactly corresponding idea, and vice versa) needs the further premise that God's thought contains an idea of everything in extension, which critics say is asserted rather than earned. Second, the identity it concludes is found unstable by both sides: realists about the mental complain that if the idea of the circle simply *is* the circle under another attribute, the explanatory difference between physics and psychology becomes a difference in vocabulary only; dualists complain in reverse that an "extended idea" is a contradiction. The correspondence, on this objection, is either magic or bookkeeping.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own answer to the second worry is built into the attribute doctrine: the attributes are conceptually independent (E1P10), so explanation within Thought never borrows from Extension, while the *thing* explained is one — the double description is not verbal but tracks two genuine, irreducible ways the one essence is expressed. To the first, the literature's defense (Garrett and others) points to E2P3 — in God there is necessarily an idea of his essence and of everything following from it — and E2P5, which together ground the totality of ideas in the attribute of Thought itself, not in our noticing them. Whether E1A4 can bear this weight remains the live technical question in the literature on Part II; the doctrine itself — identity of order, no cross-attribute causation — is not in dispute as an attribution.

**Argument 5. No free will: conscious of our desires, ignorant of their causes**

1. Will is not a faculty standing above individual volitions; there are only particular volitions, each determined by a cause, which is determined by another, and so on to infinity (E1P32, E2P48–P49).
2. A free cause, in the only clear sense (E1D7), acts from the necessity of its own nature alone; but every volition in a finite mind has causes outside that volition — in the body's constitution, its history, and the infinite chain of finite causes (E1P28, E2P48).
3. What men call their freedom is an inference from their experience: they are conscious of their actions and appetites, and ignorant of the causes by which those appetites are determined (E2P35S, E3P2S).
4. Hence the letter to Schuller: if a stone thrown through the air were conscious, it would believe — knowing its striving to continue moving, and nothing of the throw — that it was flying of its own free will. "This is that famous human freedom, which all men boast of having, and which consists solely in this, that men are conscious of their desire, and ignorant of the causes by which they are determined."
Therefore, there is in the mind no absolute, or free, will; the mind is determined to will this or that by a cause, which is also determined by another, and this again by another, and so to infinity (E2P48).

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* I, Proposition 32; II, Propositions 35S, 48–49; III, Proposition 2, scholium; Letter 58 to G. H. Schuller (1674), the flying-stone passage; the diagnosis already in E1 Appendix.

**Objection.** The responsibility objection, pressed since Spinoza's own correspondence (his exchange with Willem van Blyenbergh in 1665): if no one could have done otherwise, praise, blame, remorse, and exhortation lose their sense — including Spinoza's own exhortations in Parts IV–V, and including the very act of arguing, which seems to presuppose that a reader can be *moved by reasons* rather than merely pushed by causes. A modern variant asks whether the determinism is even an advantage: the stone passage assumes that consciousness of desire without knowledge of causes is the whole phenomenology of choice, which deliberators deny — deliberation, they say, presents alternatives *as open*, and that presentation is not explained away by ignorance.

**Reply.** This is Spinoza's own, and it is twofold. To the practice objection: praise, blame, and exhortation are themselves causes — affects and institutions are how a necessitated world modifies necessitated agents, and the *Ethics* is written as one such cause (see Argument 8). The stone is not an argument that deliberation is unreal but that its feeling of openness is compatible with determination: reasons *are* causes in the attribute of Thought, and a mind moved by an adequate idea is moved necessarily — but by its own nature rather than by chance impacts, which is all the freedom Spinoza offers and, he argues, all the word can coherently mean (E1D7). To Blyenbergh he adds that good and evil, and with them responsibility, are relative to a model of human nature (Argument 7), not registrations of uncaused choice. Whether redefining freedom rescues it or changes the subject is the standing dispute his position invites; the position itself — no *liberum arbitrium*, complete determination, freedom as understood necessity — is among the least ambiguous in the system.

**Argument 6. Conatus: nothing endeavors its own destruction**

1. No thing can be destroyed except through an external cause: nothing has in itself anything by which it could destroy itself, since a thing's definition affirms its essence rather than negating it (E3P4).
2. Things are contrary in nature insofar as one can destroy another (E3P5) — destruction is always an encounter between a thing's power and a greater external power.
3. From the given essence of any thing, some things necessarily follow (E1P36); and the power by which a thing acts is God's power as expressed in that thing's essence (E1P34, E3P6 demonstration).
Therefore, each thing, insofar as it is in itself, endeavors to persist in its own being (E3P6); and this endeavor — the *conatus* — "is nothing but the actual essence of the thing" (E3P7), involving indefinite time (E3P8).

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* III, Propositions 4–8, with the human specification at E3P9 and its scholium (appetite and desire); restated in the library entry's Signature arguments.

**Objection.** The counterexamples are vivid: suicide, self-sacrifice, the moth and the flame. If conatus can never will destruction, these must all be reclassified as external compulsion — and critics charge that the reclassification makes the doctrine unfalsifiable: whatever a thing does, including destroying itself, is redescribed as perseverance or as overpowering, so the principle rules nothing out and explains nothing. A second, subtler objection targets premise 3: E3P6's demonstration moves from "God's power is expressed in the thing" to "the thing necessarily endeavors to persist," but a thing's being an expression of infinite power does not obviously entail a direction — persistence rather than dissolution — in its activity.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own reply is structural, and it is important that he made it a theorem rather than an excuse: E3P4 does not say things never perish or never turn against themselves in appearance; it says a thing's essence, being an affirmation, contains no principle of its own negation, so when a living thing acts self-destructively, the explanation must cite the affects and external causes that have captured its striving — which is why E4P20S insists that no one kills himself from the necessity of his own nature, but only as compelled by external causes contrary to that nature, whether a tyrant's order (his example is Seneca) or the hidden workings of disordered affects. The literature's defense of premise 3 (Garrett's is the most cited) reconstructs the move through E3P4–P5: since whatever follows from a thing's essence follows from God's power *as that essence*, and destruction would negate the essence, nothing destructive of the thing can follow from the essence alone. Whether that closes the unfalsifiability worry — or only relocates it into the definition of "external" — remains a fair and open question, and the entry's study question on this argument presses exactly there.

**Argument 7. Good and evil are modes of thinking — and the model of human nature**

1. Good, evil, perfection, imperfection "indicate nothing positive in things, considered in themselves": we call a house perfect against the idea of a house we formed, music good or bad relative to a listener's constitution, a man good against what we want men to be (E4 Preface).
2. The same thing can be at once good, and bad, and indifferent, relative to different natures and different respects — so the terms cannot name intrinsic properties of things.
3. Nevertheless human beings necessarily form a general idea of a human being — a **model (exemplar) of human nature** — and judge particular people, actions, and institutions by their approach to or falling away from it.
4. The model is not arbitrary: it is fixed by what human nature, adequately understood, actually is — a conatus whose power is increased by adequate ideas and by other rational human beings (E4P18S, P35; the model's construction is examined in the objection below).
Therefore, in the *Ethics*' technical usage, "by good I shall understand what we certainly know to be a means for us of approaching nearer to the model of human nature we set before ourselves; by evil, what we certainly know prevents us from becoming like that model" (E4 Preface) — normative language rebuilt on a descriptive foundation.

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* IV, Preface; supported by E1 Appendix, E2D6 ("by reality and perfection I understand the same thing"), E4D1–D2, D8 (virtue as power), and the correspondence with Blyenbergh.

**Objection.** The relativism objection: if good and evil are modes of thinking, indexed to a model we happen to form, then the model itself needs a justification it cannot get from the system — why *this* exemplar? A different temperament forms a different model, and Spinoza has sawn off the branch (objective value) on which any answer must sit. The system seems to fall between two stools: official subjectivism about value, and an ethics that in practice speaks of reason's dictates with complete confidence, telling us what we *must* do if we would be free.

**Reply.** Spinoza's reply, assembled from E4 Preface, E2D6, and E4P24–P28, is that the model is constrained by metaphysics, not taste. Perfection is reality: a thing is more perfect as it has more power of acting, and the model of human nature is simply human nature understood at its highest power — the exemplar is not chosen but discovered, as one discovers what a healthy body is by understanding bodies, not by consulting preferences. Good is then definable without remainder: what genuinely aids the conatus of a being of our kind, with adequacy of knowledge as the test ("what we *certainly know*"). The literature's development (Garrett's "benchmark" reading) adds that the model functions like a physician's model of health: relative to the organism, certainly, but not therefore a matter of opinion. Whether this fully answers the objection — or converts relativism about value into a naturalism that still owes an account of why power is *good* — is a genuine and well-populated dispute; note that Spinoza has a final, characteristic move available: the question assumes an 'ought' standing outside nature, which is the teleological picture Argument 3 already refused.

**Argument 8. Bondage, and freedom through understanding the affects**

1. The force by which a man perseveres in existing is limited, and infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes (E4P3).
2. It is impossible that a man should not be a part of Nature and undergo changes of which he is not the adequate cause (E4P4); hence insofar as his ideas are inadequate, he necessarily acts as a patient — he is in **bondage**, at the mercy of fortune and of affects he does not understand (E4 Preface, P2).
3. But an affect which is a passion ceases to be a passion as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea of it (E5P3); and insofar as the mind understands all things as necessary, it has greater power over the affects (E5P6).
4. The mind can be brought to relate its affects to the idea of God — the fixed point no external cause can remove — and to order its thoughts by that connection (E5P14–P20, with the E5P20S summary of the remedies).
Therefore, freedom is real but graduated: not exemption from necessity, but the increasing proportion of our life of which we are the adequate cause — the mind's power over the affects, exercised through understanding, is the only liberation the system allows or needs.

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* IV, Propositions 2–4 and Preface; V, Propositions 1–20 (the "remedies of the affects"), especially P3, P4, P6, and the scholium to P20; the concession that governs the whole therapy at E4P7 and E4P14.

**Objection.** The intellectualism objection, as old as the book: understanding why I am angry does not stop the anger. Hume sharpened the general form — reason alone moves nothing; a passion is opposed only by a contrary passion — and ordinary experience seems on his side: insight and compulsion coexist in every clinic. If E5P3 means the passion vanishes when adequately conceived, it is refuted daily; if it means something weaker, the liberation advertised by Part V's title shrinks accordingly.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own reply begins by conceding the objection's best point before the objector makes it: "an affect cannot be restrained or taken away except by an affect contrary to, and stronger than, the affect to be restrained" (E4P7) — reason, he agrees, has no direct lever on feeling. The therapy of Part V works by a different mechanism, and E5P3 is precise about it: a passion is an affect *of which we are not the adequate cause*, experienced in confusion; forming a clear and distinct idea of it does not annihilate the bodily modification but changes the mind's causal role in it — the affect is now also the object of an action (an act of understanding), which is itself accompanied by joy, an active affect that competes with the passion on the passion's own terms (E5P3 corollary, P4 scholium). The literature notes that this is a causal, not a magical, theory of insight: understanding helps precisely by generating stronger, better-connected affects, and by removing the imagination's reinforcements (the imagined freedom of the hated object, E5P5–P6). It promises power over the affects, never invulnerability — E4P4 already guaranteed that some passivity is permanent in a finite mode.

**Argument 9. The intellectual love of God — and the eternity of the mind**

1. The third kind of knowledge proceeds from an adequate idea of God's attributes to adequate knowledge of the essences of things (E5P25, P31); from it arises the greatest possible contentment of mind (E5P27).
2. The joy that arises from this knowledge, accompanied by the idea of God as its cause, is the **intellectual love of God** (E5P32 corollary); this love is eternal (E5P33) and is itself part of the infinite love with which God loves himself — not as infinite, but as expressed through the essence of the human mind (E5P36).
3. The mind's essence, like every essence, is eternally conceived in God: there is in God an idea expressing the essence of this or that human body under a species of eternity (E5P22).
4. The human mind can be destroyed absolutely only with the body's duration — memory and imagination perish with it (E5P21) — but insofar as the mind involves that eternal idea of its body's essence, "the human mind cannot be absolutely destroyed with the body, but something of it remains which is eternal" (E5P23); "we feel and know by experience that we are eternal" (E5P23S).
Therefore, blessedness is the mind's activity in the third kind of knowledge: not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself (E5P42) — an eternity the mind has insofar as it knows, not a survival the person enjoys after death.

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* V, Propositions 21–23 (with scholia), 25–36, 42; the definitions of eternity at E1D8 and of the second and third kinds of knowledge at E2P40S2.

**Objection.** Here the objection must begin with the interpretive dispute itself, because what this argument even concludes is the most contested question in Spinoza studies. **Reading (i), personal immortality** — a surviving part of me awaits a posthumous life — is affirmed by the rhetoric of "remains" and denied by E5P21 and P34 (the mind is subject to passions, memory, and imagination only while the body endures) and by Spinoza's explicit refusals elsewhere; most scholars reject it. **Reading (ii), the timeless-aspect reading** — the mind is eternal only *qua* its adequate ideas, which are not biographical events but truths eternally contained in God's thought, so the "eternal part" is the mind considered *sub specie aeternitatis*, larger in proportion as the mind knows more (E5P38–P40) — commands wide support. **Reading (iii)** comprises stricter reconstructions (Donagan, Garrett) that attempt to give (ii) a precise metaphysics via the idea of the body's essence in God. Against all of these stands Bennett's celebrated complaint that the demonstrations of E5P21–P23 do not succeed in connecting their premises to their conclusions — that the doctrine is, as argued, not intelligible enough to be either true or false.

**Reply.** The literature's best reply to Bennett concedes the compression but contests the verdict: read with E1D8 (eternity is not duration) and E5P29 (the mind conceives things under a species of eternity exactly insofar as it conceives them through God's essence), Proposition 23 says something statable — a mind's adequate ideas are not annihilated with the body's duration because they were never merely temporal items; they are modes of God's thought, eternal as he is, and the mind *participates* in them now. On this reading the doctrine is the completion of Part V's therapy, not an eschatological bonus: the more of the mind that consists in adequate knowledge, the less of it death can take, and E5P38 draws exactly that practical corollary ("the more the mind understands things by the second and third kinds of knowledge, the less it is acted on by affects... and the less it fears death"). Whether you find this a profound consolation or a change of subject performed with great dignity is, the dossier judges, the question the whole system finally leaves open; Spinoza's last word on it is not a proof but a confession that the road is difficult and rarely found — "all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare" (E5P42S).

**Argument 10. Against Descartes: no pineal commerce, because there are not two things to connect**

1. Descartes held that the mind, a thinking substance, is united to the body, an extended substance, and located their commerce in the pineal gland, which the soul could move, thereby redirecting the animal spirits and moving the body.
2. But no proportion or connection has been specified — by Descartes or anyone — between a motion of an unextended thing and the motion of an extended one: how much motion, in which direction, determined by what law? Spinoza's preface asks what Descartes could even have meant by the union of two natures "so different," and charges that the hypothesis explains the union by something "more occult" than the occult qualities of the schoolmen (E5 Preface).
3. In fact the body cannot determine the mind to thinking, and the mind cannot determine the body to motion or rest (E3P2): causes act only within their own attribute (E2P6).
4. What experience actually presents is one individual, whose volition and bodily movement are not cause and effect but the same event — the mind being the idea of the body (E2P13), and the body's capabilities and the mind's being strictly correlative (E3P2S: no one has yet determined what the body can do).
Therefore, the interaction problem is not solved but dissolved: decisions and movements correspond without commerce, because the mind and the body are one and the same thing, conceived now under the attribute of Thought, now under Extension (E2P21S).

**Textual basis.** *Ethics* V, Preface (the pineal critique); II, Propositions 6–7, 13, 21S; III, Proposition 2 and its long scholium.

**Objection.** The dissolution charge: identity explains correspondence by definitional flat — of course the two series agree, we have defined them as one — while the felt datum Descartes was honoring, that my decision *makes* my arm rise, is redescribed rather than explained, and reappears inside each attribute as the brute correlation of E2P7 (Argument 4). There is also a historical-fairness objection: Descartes's considered view (in the correspondence with Elisabeth, who posed the interaction problem to him in 1643) treats the union as a primitive notion known by living it, not a mechanism at all — so the pineal passage Spinoza mocks may be a weaker target than Descartes's real position.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own reply to the "felt datum" is Argument 5's: the feeling that a decision moves a body is the consciousness of an appetite with ignorance of its causes, now doubled by ignorance of the body's causes — E3P2S's point is that nobody knows what the body can do from its own structure, so nobody is in a position to be surprised that "decisions" and movements are one event viewed twice. On Descartes's primitive union, the preface's rejoinder is implicit and sharp: a union declared primitive and inexplicable is the sanctuary-of-ignorance maneuver imported into psychology — the same pattern Argument 3 refuses in theology. The literature adds that Spinoza's position need only beat the alternatives: any theory that posts commerce between heterogeneous substances owes the how, and three centuries of successors (occasionalism, pre-established harmony, epiphenomenalism) can be read as paying Descartes's bill in installments. Whether identity is an explanation or an exceptionally well-motivated refusal of one remains discussable; the critique of the gland has not been answered on its own terms.

**Argument 11. The TTP separation: theology teaches obedience, philosophy seeks truth — therefore freedom to philosophize**

1. The foundation of the state and of social peace is obedience: justice and charity practiced toward one's neighbor (TTP chs. 13–14).
2. Scripture's own teaching, when its moral core is separated from its narratives and its accommodated language, requires for salvation nothing beyond this obedience: the universal faith's dogmas (ch. 14) are only those without which obedience is impossible — that God exists, is one, is supreme, is to be worshiped through justice and charity, and so on — and they are dogmas of *practice*, whose truth or falsity, philosophically considered, does not affect their function.
3. Theology's domain is therefore obedience, and philosophy's is truth; neither is the handmaid of the other (ch. 15) — theology must not dictate metaphysics, and philosophy must not be suppressed in the name of a piety it does not touch.
4. The state can command actions; it cannot command minds without absurdity and danger, since belief follows perceived evidence, not orders, and citizens forced to profess what they do not believe become hypocrites and resentful subjects (ch. 20).
Therefore, freedom to philosophize — to judge and to say what one judges, within the laws that govern actions — is not merely compatible with piety and public peace; it is required by them: "the end of the state is really freedom" (TTP ch. 20).

**Textual basis.** *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670), chapters 13–15 (the separation; the seven dogmas of universal faith at ch. 14) and chapter 20 (freedom to philosophize); supported by ch. 7's method for interpreting Scripture by Scripture.

**Objection.** Two fronts. From the side of religion, the separation was heard in 1670 — and is still heard — as a demotion dressed as a division of labor: if Scripture's dogmas need not be *true*, only useful for obedience, then theology has been quietly handed over to utility, and the TTP is atheism with a diplomatic visa — which is exactly what its first readers charged. From the side of politics, the Hobbesian objection: a sovereign who cannot govern opinion cannot govern; publishing seditious judgments *is* an action, and the line between judging and inciting is precisely what a state at peace cannot leave to private judgment.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own reply to the religious charge is that the demotion runs the other way: it is the theologians who degrade faith by staking it on propositions (about cosmology, miracles, metaphysics) that inquiry can refute, whereas faith staked on obedience alone is invulnerable to criticism and identical in every confession — the separation *protects* religion from philosophy by giving each its own object. To the Hobbesian: ch. 20 argues on the objector's own ground of stability. A state that criminalizes judgment manufactures the sedition it fears — men who cannot speak think worse, and conspire; Amsterdam's prosperity under tolerated judgment, against the record of persecuting states, is his standing exhibit. Spinoza does keep limits: speech that in itself breaches the contract — direct incitement to abolish the laws — falls under the sovereign's right over actions. Where exactly the judging/inciting line sits is a question the chapter leaves to practice, and critics from his century to ours have located it in different places; that the separation itself is the founding document of the modern argument is, by contrast, close to consensus.

**Argument 12. Political naturalism: right is power — and the multitude's state**

1. The right of nature as a whole extends as far as its power: God's right is his power itself, and nature's laws are not commands imposed on power but descriptions of what power does (TTP ch. 16; TP ch. 2).
2. Each individual's natural right is therefore determined, not by sound reason or moral desert, but by its actual power — as the big fish eat the smaller "by sovereign natural right," and no fish is unjust for doing what its nature determines (TTP ch. 16).
3. Men cannot live or preserve themselves alone; when a multitude agrees — driven by hope, fear, and common interest — to direct its combined power by one mind, as it were, the resulting sovereign holds a right equal to the multitude's pooled power, and lasts exactly as long as that power, and the affects sustaining it, last (TTP chs. 16–17; TP chs. 2–3).
4. A regime is stable in proportion as power is arranged so that rulers and ruled alike find their interest in the common safety; a state whose affairs depend on the good faith of one man places the whole commonwealth's power in the keeping of one affect-driven mode (TP chs. 5–7).
5. Democracy — sovereignty vested in the multitude as a whole, exercising it through its own assemblies — departs least from the natural state (no one transfers right to another so completely that they cease to be consulted), and multiplies beyond capture the hands in which power lies (TTP ch. 16; TP ch. 11, unfinished).
Therefore, democracy is the most natural and most self-stabilizing regime: not because the multitude is wise, but because right being power, the constitution that lodges power where power actually resides has the least distance to fall.

**Textual basis.** *Theological-Political Treatise*, chapters 16–17, 20; *Political Treatise*, chapters 2–3 (right and power), 5–7 (the mechanics of stability), and chapter 11 (democracy — where the unfinished treatise breaks off).

**Objection.** The normativity objection first: if right is power, nothing condemns the successful tyrant — his right, while he holds the state, is exactly as good as the democracy's, and "ought" has been defined out of politics, leaving a naturalism that can describe regimes but never judge them. The second objection is the **multitude problem**, and Spinoza hands it to his critics himself: the multitude of the TTP Preface is governed by affects rather than reason, "inconstant and changeable," terrifying when it does not fear — so premise 3's pooled power is pooled *passion*, and premise 5's conclusion seems to entrust the state to the least rational agent available. Nor can the theory be completed from the text: the *Political Treatise* breaks off at the very beginning of its treatment of democracy, so the regime the argument crowns is the one whose mechanics we do not have.

**Reply.** Spinoza's own reply to the normativity charge is that it mistakes his project: right-as-power is not a license but a *physics of legitimacy* — the tyrant's right is coextensive with his power, yes, and that is precisely the doctrine's critical edge, since it predicts and explains his fragility better than any theory of his supposed legitimacy: a rule maintained against the multitude's affects is power already draining away, whatever the parchment says. Normative judgment re-enters, as everywhere in this system, through reason's knowledge of what actually preserves and increases power — constitutional design in the TP is engineering, not exhortation. On the multitude, his answer (developed by the literature from the TP's institutional chapters) is that the alternative is not reason but *one man's* affects plus a court's: democracy does not require the multitude to be wise, only structures in which its combined power — and the fear and hope of its members — are channeled by laws no faction can capture, with large councils making private passion statistically ungovernable into policy; a multitude deciding together is, he argues in the TP, less likely to agree on an absurdity than a single ruler is to commit one. The honest residue stands: the unfinished chapter 11 means the crown of the theory is an argument sketch, and every completion of it in the literature is partly conjecture.
