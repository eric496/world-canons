# Bibliography — Spinoza: What to Read, in What Order, and Why

Tiered like the study plan: the texts first, then the companions in the order to read them, then the wider shelf for when a particular fight catches you, then reference and media. Every media item below was verified to exist as described; one famous absence is recorded where it occurs.

## 1. Primary texts

**Edwin Curley (trans.), *The Collected Works of Spinoza*, Vols. I–II (Princeton University Press, 1985; 2016).** The scholarly standard in English, and the dossier's citation base. Vol. I carries the early works (the *Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect*, the *Principles of Descartes' Philosophy* with the *Metaphysical Thoughts*, the correspondence through the Blyenbergh exchange) and the *Ethics*; Vol. II carries the rest, including the *Theological-Political Treatise*, the *Political Treatise*, and the later letters (Letter 58 among them). Curley's translations are accurate, philosophically literate, and — decisively — consistent in terminology across the corpus, which matters for a thinker whose argument runs on defined terms.

**Edwin Curley (trans.), *Ethics* (Penguin Classics, 1996).** The plan's reading text for the *Ethics*: the same Curley translation as *Collected Works* I, in a cheap paperback with a brief introduction. Proposition numbering (E1P14, E2P40S2) is identical in every serious edition, so nothing in this dossier depends on owning the Princeton volumes for the *Ethics* weeks.

**Samuel Shirley (trans.), *The Ethics and Selected Letters* (Hackett, 1982); and *Spinoza: Ethics, Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect, and Selected Letters* (Hackett, ed. Seymour Feldman, 1992).** The sanctioned alternative translation — plainer, sometimes freer, with a long and honorable classroom history. The combined Hackett volume is the budget buy for this plan: it covers Weeks 1, 4, and 5's letter assignments in the same covers as the *Ethics*. Shirley's renderings of key terms occasionally differ from Curley's; the dossier follows Curley, and any friction you meet is translation choice, not error.

**The *Theological-Political Treatise*:** the **Cambridge edition (ed. Jonathan Israel; trans. Michael Silverthorne and Jonathan Israel, Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy, 2007)** — the plan's TTP text, with Israel's substantial introduction on the work's radical-Enlightenment setting — **or Curley's translation in *Collected Works* II**. Either serves; do not read the TTP in a nineteenth-century translation, of which several circulate free online: the political vocabulary has not survived them.

**Carl Gebhardt (ed.), *Spinoza Opera* (Carl Winter, 1925).** Named for completeness: the critical edition of the Latin (and Dutch) texts, four volumes, on which all modern translations rest. You will not read Spinoza in Latin this year; you should know the name, because every serious secondary work cites it.

## 2. First secondary — read in this order

**Steven Nadler, *Spinoza's Ethics: An Introduction* (Cambridge University Press, 2006).** The first companion, and the plan's default guide to the whole *Ethics*: a lucid, part-by-part walk through the argument by the leading Anglophone Spinoza scholar, written for readers meeting the geometric order cold. If the study plan's Part I guide is the session companion for Part I, Nadler is the companion for Parts II–V.

**Steven Nadler, *Spinoza: A Life* (Cambridge University Press, 1999; 2nd ed., 2018).** The standard biography — the Portuguese-Jewish community, the *cherem*'s documentary record, the Hague circle, the death and the posthumous publication — and the source against which popular dramatizations (below) should be checked. Read alongside Week 1, or in full between the *Ethics* weeks and Week 7; the TTP chapters land differently with the 1670s in your head.

**Steven Nadler, *A Book Forged in Hell: Spinoza's Scandalous Treatise and the Birth of the Secular Age* (Princeton University Press, 2011).** The Week 7 companion: the TTP's composition, banning, and clandestine career, and the case for its founding role in modern biblical criticism and the secular state. Narrative and scholarly at once; the liveliest of the three Nadlers.

**Beth Lord, *Spinoza's Ethics: An Edinburgh Philosophical Guide* (Edinburgh University Press, 2010).** The alternative companion — proposition-sensitive, brisk, and particularly good on Parts IV–V and the political afterlife. Choose Lord *or* Nadler's *Introduction* as your single companion, not both; the plan needs one voice in Session B, not a seminar.

## 3. The wider shelf — for when a fight catches you

**Jonathan Bennett, *A Study of Spinoza's Ethics* (Hackett; Cambridge University Press, 1984).** The great analytic antagonist: a close, uncharitable-in-the-best-way reading that finds the metaphysics partly indefensible and says exactly why, proposition by proposition. Read **after** the plan, never during — his objections (to the definitions, to the attribute doctrine, to Part V) are the standard test every reconstruction in this dossier must survive, and they are most useful once your own Spinoza is standing. Capstone prompt 5 is Bennett's challenge staged.

**Edwin Curley, *Behind the Geometrical Method* (Princeton University Press, 1988).** Short lectures on what the geometric form is doing — whether the definitions are stipulations, real essences, or hypotheses about the world's causal structure, and how much of the system survives each answer. The natural next step after Bennett: the translator's defense of the method Bennett prosecutes.

**Gilles Deleuze, *Spinoza: Practical Philosophy* (trans. Robert Hurley; City Lights, 1988; French orig. 1970, expanded 1981).** The other Spinoza: affects, bodies, and what a body can do, read as an ethics of joyful encounters and increased power, with a famous glossary. Deleuze's Spinoza is a genuine interpretation, not a distortion — but it is a *French* Spinoza, and it will re-sort your Part III permanently. Read after the plan, alongside or against Bennett, and notice that the two antagonists agree about almost nothing except the text's power.

**Don Garrett (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Spinoza* (Cambridge University Press, 1996).** The survey volume: essays on the metaphysics, the mind, the affects, the politics, and the historical setting, by the field's senior figures. The place to go when a dossier topic needs a second scholarly voice before you commit to a capstone verdict.

**Rebecca Goldstein, *Betraying Spinoza: The Renegade Jew Who Gave Us Modernity* (Yale University Press, Jewish Lives, 2006).** The readable life-and-ideas: a novelist-philosopher's search for the man inside the *cherem* story, weaving the biography with her own intellectual memoir. Genuinely enjoyable and philosophically serious; verify its conjectures against Nadler's *Life* before citing them.

**Matthew Stewart, *The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz, Spinoza, and the Fate of God in the Modern World* (W. W. Norton, 2006).** Popular narrative — labeled as such. A dual biography built around the young Leibniz's 1676 visit to The Hague, dramatic in structure and thesis-driven (Spinoza as modernity's secret author). Fine as an epilogue read; it is not evidence, and its Leibniz is simplified for the plot. The dossier's rule stands: popularizations orient, scholarship argues.

## 4. Reference

**[SEP, "Spinoza"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spinoza/) — Steven Nadler.** The standard scholarly survey, covering the metaphysics, epistemology, psychology, ethics, and politics with a full bibliography; the learning path's per-stage ritual (skim in Week 1, *read* after the capstone) applies to this article in particular, since its compressions are invisible until you have the text.

**[IEP, "Benedict de Spinoza"](https://iep.utm.edu/)** — the article the plan assigns *before* the primary reading (the learning path's IEP-first rule for this figure: IEP's Spinoza coverage is unusually accessible for a system this geometrical), with two verified spin-off articles for the week's they serve: "Benedict de Spinoza: Philosophy of Religion" (for Week 7; https://iep.utm.edu/spinoza-religion/) and "Spinoza on Free Will and Freedom" (for Weeks 3–5; https://iep.utm.edu/spinoza-free-will-determinism/).

**[PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/), category "Baruch Spinoza."** The research index: use it to chase the Part V eternity debate and the parallelism objection past the *Cambridge Companion*, filtering by the proposition under dispute (E5P23, E2P7) rather than by "Spinoza" whole.

**Clare Carlisle, Spinoza walk-through, *The Guardian* (2011).** Eight short pieces walking the *Ethics*' core, written for general readers by a Kierkegaard scholar who takes Spinoza seriously — the plan's sanctioned mid-week filler, linked via the Partially Examined Life's index page: https://partiallyexaminedlife.com/2011/03/29/clare-carlisles-spinoza-walk-through-via-the-guardian/
