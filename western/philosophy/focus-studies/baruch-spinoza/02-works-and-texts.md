# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

Spinoza published one book under his own name, one anonymously, and left the rest in a desk. The corpus is therefore small, finished in the wrong places, and partly recovered from manuscripts nobody saw for two centuries — which makes the order in which you read it a real decision rather than a formality. This file presents every work that matters in **the order this dossier reads them**: the two early treatises that show the system forming, the Descartes exposition that taught him his method, the *Ethics* itself, the two political works, and the correspondence — the record of the system thinking aloud. Citation, editions, and the dossier's reading-text ruling close the file.

## A note on citation before the works

Cite Spinoza the way scholarship does: never by page. The *Ethics* is cited as **E + part + unit** — E1P14 is Part I, Proposition 14; add d (definition), a (axiom), c (corollary), s (scholium), app (Appendix): E1d3, E1p14s, E1app. The library's [guide to *Ethics* Part I](../../study/spinoza-ethics-part1.md) teaches the notation in full; this dossier uses it throughout. The *Theological-Political Treatise* and the *Political Treatise* are cited by chapter (TTP 20; TP 2). Letters are cited by number in the standard numbering where the number is secure, and otherwise by correspondent and topic — the safer practice, since letter numbering has shifted between editions.

## 1. Short Treatise on God, Man, and His Well-Being (c. 1660)

The earliest surviving statement of the system, written in Dutch for the friends who gathered around him — by the internal evidence, around 1660 — and never prepared for print. Its fate is part of its meaning for a student: the work vanished with the manuscripts, **two copies surfaced only in the nineteenth century** (the first in the 1850s), and it was first printed in 1862. Read it therefore as what it is — a draft, not a lost scripture. Much of the mature philosophy is already here in prose: one substance, God as the whole of nature, the denial of final causes, the passions analyzed, human blessedness as knowledge. But the arguments are looser, some doctrines differ in emphasis or detail from their *Ethics* forms (the treatment of the attributes and of God's providence is visibly transitional), and the geometrical machinery is absent. **Use:** read it after the *Treatise on the Emendation* and before the *Ethics*, as the system's first photograph — invaluable for development, inadmissible as evidence against the mature text where the two disagree. It is also the work Spinoza's circle actually studied together, some of it cast as dialogue; that pedagogical warmth makes it the gentlest full statement he ever wrote.

## 2. Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect (begun late 1650s–early 1660s; unfinished)

His methodological introduction, left incomplete: how the intellect, starting from the confused ideas of ordinary life, can purify itself, distinguish kinds of perception (report, random experience, inference, and direct intuitive knowledge), and learn to form adequate ideas of things through their causes. The opening pages — the famous autobiographical preface, in which the narrator renounces riches, honor, and sensual pleasure as unstable goods and resolves to seek a good "whose discovery and acquisition would confer a continuous and supreme joy" — are the closest Spinoza ever came to a spiritual autobiography, and even they should be read as a constructed prologue rather than a diary. The treatise breaks off mid-argument (the dating of its composition is itself debated; the library entry and the scholarship place its beginnings around the turn of the 1660s). **Use:** read it early, for the method and the four kinds of knowledge that Part II of the *Ethics* will rebuild; do not be detained by its unfinished state — the *Ethics* supersedes it, and the dossier treats it as the doorway, not a room.

## 3. Principles of Descartes' Philosophy, with Metaphysical Thoughts (1663)

Descartes's *Principles of Philosophy* (Parts I–II, with material from III) recast **in geometrical order**, dictated, by the preface's account, to a young student Spinoza was tutoring, then revised for the press by Lodewijk Meyer, who published it in 1663 — **the only book to carry Spinoza's name in his lifetime**. Two cautions govern its use. First, it is an exposition: Meyer warns the reader in his preface that Spinoza does not endorse everything he demonstrates here (on the freedom of the will and the nature of substance they already diverge). Second, the appended *Metaphysical Thoughts* (*Cogitata Metaphysica*) is Spinoza's own voice working scholastic problems — being, essence, God, duration — and rewards closer attention than the exposition it rides behind. **Use:** read it as the dossier does, for three things — the geometrical method learned on someone else's system before he turned it on his own; the baseline of official Cartesianism against which every *Ethics* departure becomes measurable; and, in the *Metaphysical Thoughts*, the young philosopher's fingerprints. Selections suffice; nobody's understanding of Spinoza is hostage to all of it.

## 4. Ethics, Demonstrated in Geometrical Order (composed c. 1661–1675; published 1677)

The masterwork, and the center of this dossier. Five parts: **I**, Of God — substance, attributes, modes, necessity, and the Appendix's demolition of teleology; **II**, Of the Nature and Origin of the Mind — parallelism, the theory of ideas, the kinds of knowledge; **III**, Of the Origin and Nature of the Affects — *conatus* and the derivation of the emotions; **IV**, Of Human Bondage — weakness before the passions, and the rational life sketched against it; **V**, Of the Power of the Intellect, or On Human Freedom — the therapy of the affects and the intellectual love of God. Some 260 propositions in all, with definitions, axioms, postulates, demonstrations, corollaries, and scholia.

**The geometric order, honestly explained.** Spinoza presents the whole *more geometrico*: each part opens with definitions and axioms, and every proposition is demonstrated from earlier results, as in Euclid. The form is not ornament and not (only) intimidation. It *is* the metaphysics performed: if all things follow from God's nature with necessity, then a philosophy of those things should exhibit the same necessity in its own order of reasons, each truth hanging from the ones that ground it. It is also, frankly, beginner-hostile: the definitions are stipulative and load-bearing (Definition 3 of Part I quietly contains the whole system), the demonstrations are compressed to the edge of intelligibility, and much of the most important philosophy is displaced into the scholia, where Spinoza finally argues in prose. Three rules make it readable, and this dossier's plan enforces all three: (i) read the scholia as part of the argument, not commentary on it; (ii) reconstruct each block of propositions in your own numbered premises, book closed, before any secondary discussion — the library's Part I guide models the discipline, proposition by proposition; (iii) never read the *Ethics* unguided — the learning path's ruling, which the dossier keeps: pair every block with the guide, the SEP article, or a commentary from [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md). A draft of the first three parts existed by 1665; the whole was complete by 1675; Spinoza suspended its publication that year (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)), and it appeared in the *Opera Posthuma* of 1677.

## 5. Theological-Political Treatise (1670)

The book he published anonymously, with a false Hamburg imprint, in the middle of the *Ethics*'s composition — and the founding text of modern biblical criticism. Twenty chapters in two great movements. Chapters 1–15 dismantle the theological use of Scripture: prophecy is analyzed as vivid imagination rather than knowledge; miracles are argued to be impossible as violations of nature's fixed order (ch. 6); Scripture must be interpreted from Scripture's own history — its languages, authors, and occasions — and not by philosophy (ch. 7); the Pentateuch's Mosaic authorship is dismantled on internal evidence (ch. 8–9, building on Ibn Ezra's hints); and faith is separated from philosophy outright, faith requiring only obedience and piety, philosophy only truth (chs. 13–15). Chapters 16–20 then build the politics on the psychology: the natural right of each person is coextensive with their power; the state arises from the transfer and pooling of that right; democracy is the most natural form of state; and the treatise ends with its famous conclusion — that the true end of the state is **freedom**, and that a commonwealth endangers itself, not its safety, when it tries to rule minds (ch. 20). **Use:** this dossier reads the TTP *properly* — in full, with its scriptural chapters given the same weight as its political ones — rather than in the summary pass the learning path assigns it, because the two halves are one argument: the historical reading of Scripture is what clears the ground on which the free state is built.

## 6. Political Treatise (begun c. 1675–1676; unfinished, 1677)

His last work, breaking off in its eleventh chapter, mid-discussion of democracy, when death stopped it. Where the TTP argues from Scripture's politics outward, the *Political Treatise* is austere political science: men are led by affect more than by reason, so institutions must be designed for the creatures we are; right is coextensive with power throughout nature, and a state's right is the power of its multitude; monarchy (chs. 6–7) and aristocracy (chs. 8–10) are then engineered, constitutionally, against the failure modes his psychology predicts. It is unfinished and he knew its predecessor's fate; it is also, in the judgment of much recent scholarship, the more original political book. **Use:** read its opening chapters (1–5) in full and the constitutional chapters in selection; its function in the dossier is to complete the politics the TTP began and to show the affects of Part III doing institutional work.

## 7. The correspondence (1661–1676)

Some eighty letters survive, roughly half of them Spinoza's, and in this corpus the letters are not garnish — they are essential primary reading, the one place the geometrical voice relaxes into explanation. The dossier assigns them at the points where they illuminate the works: the exchange with **Blyenbergh** (Letters 18–24 and 27), the Dordrecht grain broker whose persistent questions about evil and Adam's sin drew out Spinoza's denial that evil is anything positive; **Letter 32, to Oldenburg**, with the worm living in the blood — perceiving the particles around it as wholes and unable to conceive how all are governed by the universal nature of the whole — his standing image for the human mode inside nature; **Letter 58, to Schuller** (answering a question put through Tschirnhaus), with the conscious stone: a stone thrown through the air, if it could think, would believe it moved of its own free will, being conscious of its striving and ignorant of its causes; **Letter 50, to Jelles** — "determination is negation," four words that later philosophy, Hegel above all, would make load-bearing; and the 1675 exchange with **Oldenburg** over the *Theological-Political Treatise* and the suspended *Ethics*, where the public management of dangerous ideas is itself the topic. Read letters by correspondent and topic as well as by number; the dossier cites them both ways, since numbering varies between editions.

## 8. Compendium of Hebrew Grammar (unfinished, 1677) — named, not assigned

Written in his last years for friends who wished to read the Hebrew Bible in its own language, and breaking off incomplete. Its interest is real but specialist: a rationalist, almost structural grammar that treats Hebrew as a natural object rather than a sacred exception, from the author of the TTP's seventh chapter. The dossier names it so the corpus is complete on the page; it assigns none of it. (Two short treatises on the rainbow and on the calculation of chances, printed after his death under his name, belong to the same category — attributed, minor, and read, if at all, by specialists.)

## Editions and translations

**The critical text** is Carl Gebhardt's *Spinoza Opera* (4 vols., Heidelberg, 1925), which established the Latin (and Dutch) texts all modern scholarship cites; it is a reference instrument, not a reading copy. The standard English corpus is **Edwin Curley's *The Collected Works of Spinoza*** (Princeton): **Volume I (1985)** — *Short Treatise*, *Emendation of the Intellect*, *Principles of Descartes' Philosophy* with the *Metaphysical Thoughts*, the *Ethics*, and the early letters; **Volume II (2016)** — the *Theological-Political Treatise*, the *Political Treatise*, the remaining letters, and the *Hebrew Grammar*. Curley is the scholarly standard: accurate, consistent in terminology, annotated, and the translation the SEP and most current Anglophone scholarship quote.

For the working reader this dossier follows the learning path's ruling, which offers two *Ethics* texts. **Default: Curley's Penguin *Ethics* (1996)** — the same Curley translation as the Collected Works, in an affordable single volume; it is the text this dossier's study plan assumes, and consistency with it is worth more than any marginal difference between good translations. **Alternative: Samuel Shirley's translation (Hackett)** — a slightly more Latinate, literal version, fully acceptable, and the better match if you are also reading Shirley's translations of the other works. The short rule from the Part I guide holds across the dossier: pick one translator for the *Ethics* and do not alternate mid-reading, because renderings of the key terms differ enough to confuse.

For the **TTP**, read it in Curley (Collected Works, Vol. II) to stay terminologically inside one translator's Spinoza; the Cambridge Texts edition — edited by Jonathan Israel, translated by Michael Silverthorne and Israel (2007) — is an equally respectable standalone, with the fullest scholarly introduction to the treatise's politics, and is the better choice if you are reading the TTP on its own rather than within this dossier. For the letters and the early works, Curley's Collected Works is the natural home; Shirley's *Complete Works* (Hackett, ed. Michael L. Morgan) is the one-volume alternative. **Avoid** as working texts the old public-domain Elwes translations (Bohn/Dover, 1883–84): historically important — the library entry quotes Elwes for its public-domain excerpt — but Victorian in diction and superseded in accuracy. And avoid any *Ethics* that presents the propositions without the scholia: that amputates the argument while keeping its skeleton.
