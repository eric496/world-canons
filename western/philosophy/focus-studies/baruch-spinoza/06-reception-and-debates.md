# Spinoza Received — Reputation, Recovery, and the Live Debates

*Focus study: Baruch Spinoza · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md)*

No modern philosopher has had two such different lives. The first Spinoza — the one the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries knew — was a byword: the atheist of Amsterdam, author of a banned book, refuted by men who had often not read him. The second is a founder: of biblical criticism, of liberal political theory, of the philosophy of mind's dual-aspect tradition, and (depending on the teller) of the radical Enlightenment itself. This file tells the story of the replacement, and then takes up the debates that remain genuinely open — because the recovery did not settle Spinoza; it made him arguable.

## The atheist of the Republic of Letters

The immediate reception was suppression and refutation. The *Theological-Political Treatise* was denounced from pulpits within months of its anonymous appearance in 1670, condemned by synods, and formally banned by the Court of Holland in 1674; the posthumous *Ethics* joined it on the Catholic Index in 1679. A small industry of refutations followed — theologians and Cartesians demonstrating, to their own satisfaction, that the system collapsed into atheism and fatalism. The portrait that fixed him for a century, though, came from a greater writer than any of the refuters: Pierre Bayle's article "Spinoza" in the *Dictionnaire historique et critique* (1697) — one of the longest articles in the work, and the era's dominant source on its subject. Bayle's Spinoza is a paradox engineered for maximum disturbance: the first thinker, Bayle says, to make atheism into a system, and a man of exemplary private virtue. The combination was the point — if an atheist could be virtuous and a metaphysics this rigorous could deny God, the confessional order had a problem it could not answer by burning books. Most of the eighteenth century, Hume included, read Spinoza through Bayle's lens: the doctrine absorbed at second hand, the man half-admired, the name a charge to hurl. "Spinozist" functioned in the Republic of Letters the way excommunication had in the congregation — as a boundary marker, not a description.

## The underground and the explosion of 1785

Out of public view, the texts circulated: in Latin and then in French translation, in clandestine manuscripts, in the radical fringes of the Dutch and French Enlightenments, where Spinoza's name could stand as code for a whole program — one substance, no providence, no priestcraft, toleration as principle. The underground became impossible to ignore in 1785, when Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi published his *Letters on the Doctrine of Spinoza*, reporting private conversations in which [Lessing](../../eras/03-early-modern/gotthold-ephraim-lessing.md) — Germany's most beloved man of letters, four years dead — had declared himself a Spinozist: *Hen kai pan*, one and all; "the orthodox concepts of the divinity are no longer for me." The *Pantheismusstreit* that followed was German philosophy's public coming-of-age. Moses Mendelssohn, Lessing's friend, rushed to defend a "purified" Spinozism compatible with rational religion; Jacobi countered that all rational metaphysics, consistently pursued, ends in Spinozism — and Spinozism in fatalism and atheism — so that reason itself must be leapfrogged by faith. Both sides lost control of the outcome: a generation of young Germans, Herder (whose *God: Some Conversations* (1787) recast Spinoza as a theist of nature), Goethe, the Romantics, and the Idealists, concluded not that Spinoza was dangerous but that he was *theirs*. The library's [Lessing entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/gotthold-ephraim-lessing.md) tells the controversy's other half; its consequence here is simple — after 1785, Spinoza could no longer be merely refuted. He had to be answered, absorbed, or out-built.

## Canonization in the nineteenth century

The nineteenth century made him a classic, on several competing templates. German Idealism absorbed him: Fichte and Schelling built systems that begin where Spinoza ends and try to recover the subject his substance lacks, and Hegel's lectures installed him as philosophy's necessary beginning — while teaching students to read him through Hegel's own acosmism charge (see [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md)). The Romantics canonized the man: Novalis's "God-intoxicated" Spinoza, Schleiermacher's "holy, rejected" one. Heine, reporting German thought to French readers in *Religion and Philosophy in Germany* (1834), gave the recovery its most quoted image — that all our contemporary philosophers, perhaps without knowing it, see through glasses that Baruch Spinoza ground; the line is Heine's and is quoted here as it is commonly quoted, from the essay that did more than any other to make Spinoza a European rather than a sectarian possession. In France, Renan made him a secular saint of free inquiry; at The Hague, the bicentenary of his death in 1877, with Renan's address as its centerpiece, and the raising in 1880 of a statue near his last house on the Paviljoensgracht, both drew organized religious protest — the surest sign that the name still had power to scandalize two centuries on.

## The twentieth century: three reconstructions

Twentieth-century scholarship rebuilt him three times, along national lines. Harry Wolfson's *The Philosophy of Spinoza* (1934) presented the scholastic Spinoza: two volumes tracing nearly every proposition to its medieval Jewish, Arabic, and Latin sources — a Spinoza who is the last of the medievals rather than the first of the moderns, and a permanent corrective to readings that float him free of his formation. In France, the postwar decades produced the structural and political Spinoza: Martial Gueroult's austere proposition-by-proposition reconstruction (from 1968), Althusser's claim of Spinoza for a materialism without teleology or a subject — the first thinker, on his reading, to pose the problem of ideology — and then Deleuze and Negri, whose creative appropriations are traced in [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md). In the Anglophone world, the reconstruction was editorial and argumentative: Bennett's *Study* (1984) subjected the *Ethics* to analytic cross-examination — finding real arguments, some surviving, some (above all in Part V) failing outright — while Curley's translation and scholarship restored the texts to a state in which such examination was possible. By the century's end Spinoza had journals, societies, and a scholarly infrastructure; the *Opera Posthuma*'s anonymous scandal had become a syllabus.

## The current landscape

The framing that now organizes the popular and much of the scholarly imagination is Jonathan Israel's: in *Radical Enlightenment* (2001) and its sequels, Spinoza stands as the intellectual source of a coherent, subterranean tradition — materialist, democratic, tolerant, anti-clerical — that Israel argues drove the Enlightenment's transformative wing. The thesis has been enormously influential, and it is contested in the way big theses are: critics charge it with flattening a plural Enlightenment into a single Spinozist storyline, with treating influence as a pipeline rather than a field of appropriations, and with underplaying theological and moderate currents. The dossier records the frame as what it is — the most powerful current narrative about Spinoza's historical role, held with varying confidence inside the scholarship and not to be confused with a consensus. Alongside it flourishes the quieter, permanent work: new editions and translations, a mature secondary literature on every part of the system, and continued recruitment of Spinoza by philosophers of mind, environmental ethicists, and political theorists of democracy and affect.

## Live debates

**(a) The labels fight: atheist, pantheist, panentheist, acosmist.** Every era's label encodes its own anxiety. Bayle's "systematic atheist" stresses that Spinoza's God has no intellect or will as persons have them, chooses nothing, and hears no prayers. Novalis's "God-intoxicated man" stresses the reverence — the intellectual love of God is a religious attitude by any functional test. The panentheist proposal (God is in everything, but God is also more than the totality of things) recruits the distinction between substance and its modes: God is not the sum of finite things but their infinite ground. Hegel's "acosmism" denies the world rather than God the reality. The fight persists because it is really two questions wearing one label: what must God be like for theism to be true (personality? ultimacy?), and how much of the *Ethics*' devotional language is doctrine and how much register. The entry's rule stands: distrust any one-word summary.

**(b) The attributes: subjective or objective?** *Ethics* I, definition 4, defines an attribute as "what the intellect perceives of a substance, as constituting its essence" — and the scholarship divides on the verb. On the *subjective* reading (associated with the nineteenth-century German tradition and, in another form, Wolfson), attributes are the intellect's ways of conceiving a substance whose essence is not itself differentiated into thought and extension. On the *objective* reading (Gueroult, Curley, and much recent work), attributes are real, distinct expressions of the one essence — thought and extension genuinely are what substance is, and the definition describes how intellect registers what is there. The stakes are the whole metaphysics: subjectivity threatens to make the two known attributes projections; objectivity must explain how one essence can be two (and infinitely many) without division.

**(c) The eternity of the mind.** "The human mind cannot be absolutely destroyed with the body, but something of it remains which is eternal" (*Ethics* V, proposition 23). It is the most disputed page in the system, precisely because Spinoza also denies personal immortality, memory, and duration after death. The readings: a *deflationary* one (Donagan, and in spirit Curley), on which what is eternal is the adequate ideas themselves — the eternal truths the mind contains, not a surviving subject; a *literal* one (defended in modern form by Nadler), on which the mind qua idea of the body has a genuinely eternal aspect that is still somehow *this* mind's; and Bennett's verdict that the doctrine, once its proofs are examined, is among the least intelligible things Spinoza wrote. No reading commands the field, and a dossier's honest service is to make the reader feel why each is tempting.

**(d) One politics or two?** The *Treatise* of 1670 reads, in its last chapters, like a founding liberal text: freedom to philosophize as the state's own interest, democracy praised, theology disarmed. The unfinished *Political Treatise* of 1677 is colder: right is power without remainder, constitutions are machines for managing the multitude's affects, and even democracy is analyzed as an *imperium* whose stability is a technical problem. Readings divide between *continuity* — the later work supplies the realistic foundations the earlier one presupposed, and its democracy remains the most absolute (least alienated) regime — and *revision*, on which the older Spinoza is a theorist of managed multitudes whose liberalism was a moment. Negri's Spinoza belongs to the first camp radicalized; more institutional readings, attentive to the *Treatise*'s constitutional machinery, resist both the radical and the liberal appropriation. The texts support a real argument, not a slogan.

**(e) The neuroscience appropriation.** Antonio Damasio's *Looking for Spinoza* (2003) brought the *conatus* and the affects to a mass audience, mapping Spinoza's joy and sadness onto a neurobiology of feelings and homeostasis. Its scholarly standing should be stated plainly: it is a popular appropriation by an eminent neuroscientist who reads Spinoza as a forerunner, not a work of Spinoza scholarship; historians of philosophy note that the mapping is loose — Damasio's emotion/feeling distinction is his own, homeostasis is his gloss on the *conatus* rather than Spinoza's concept, and the metaphysics that gives the affects their meaning in the system does not survive the transfer. The broader "affect theory" in the humanities draws on Spinoza often at a similar distance. None of this makes the appropriations worthless — Spinoza has always been read by recruitment — but the dossier distinguishes the recruitment from the record.

**(f) The geometric method: proof, therapy, or rhetoric?** The *Ethics* presents itself as demonstration, and its first readers already objected that the conclusions are smuggled into the definitions. Three standing readings: the method as *proof* — the system's self-understanding, defended by those who think the Part I definitions capture real essences and the deductions transmit their necessity; the method as *therapy* — on a family of readings influenced by the model of philosophy as spiritual exercise, the geometrical ascent is a discipline for transforming the reader's affects, and its value lies in the reorientation it performs rather than in formal compulsion; and the method as *rhetoric* — an architecture of persuasion whose Euclidean dress lends stipulated premises an air of inevitability (the charge implicit in Leibniz's private criticisms and explicit in several modern ones). Most working scholars hold some alloy of the three; the proportions are the debate.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **"He was excommunicated for atheism."** The *cherem* of July 27, 1656 cites "abominable heresies" and "evil opinions" but records no specific charges, and its grounds remain undocumented — scholarly candidates range from his denial of the soul's popular immortality and of Scripture's divine authorship to communal discipline and his refusal of outward conformity. He was not banned for the *Ethics*, which did not yet exist; "atheist" was the era's general term for the heterodox, not his self-description; and he neither recanted nor converted, living thereafter as neither Jew nor Christian in any confessional sense — a position the modern word "atheist" describes no better than "pantheist" does.
- **"God or Nature means nature-worship."** The slogan identifies God with nature's infinite, self-caused order — with *Natura naturans*, substance as active essence. A tree, a stone, a tide are modes: God as they are in God, not God as a collection of scenery. Nothing in the *Ethics* licenses worshipping objects; the intellectual love of God is directed at the intelligible order of the whole, and it expressly cannot ask for love in return.
- **"His determinism teaches resignation."** This inverts the doctrine's practical point. The free man of Part IV "thinks of death least of all things, and his wisdom is a meditation on life"; passivity — being driven by external causes through inadequate ideas — is *bondage*, and understanding one's affects is precisely the passage from passivity to activity and power. Fate accepted blindly is superstition in Spinoza's analysis; necessity understood is freedom. The sage of the *Ethics* is the most active figure in the system, not the most resigned.
- **"He was a recluse, grinding lenses in a garret."** The record shows a man embedded in networks: a wide correspondence across Europe (Oldenburg, Leibniz by visit and intermediary, Tschirnhaus, Blyenbergh), the Rijnsburg and Hague circles, visitors received, a landlord's family he lived among for years, and an invitation to a Heidelberg chair declined on grounds of intellectual freedom, not misanthropy. The solitude was chosen working conditions for a man with dangerous opinions and a modest income — a social strategy, not a temperament myth.
- **"Pantheism settles what he thought."** The word postdates him — Toland coined "pantheist" in 1705 — and every use since has smuggled in the user's own verdict, devotional or accusatory. What settles what he thought are the arguments: one substance, infinite attributes, modes, necessity, and a love of God built on knowledge. The dossier's advice is the entry's: retire the label to the reception history, where it belongs, and argue about the system.
