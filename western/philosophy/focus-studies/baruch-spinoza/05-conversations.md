# Spinoza in Conversation — Backward, Sideways, Forward

*Focus study: Baruch Spinoza · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md)*

Spinoza published almost nothing under his own name, held no university post, and belonged to no church — yet few philosophers have been more thoroughly *in* conversation. He built his system out of Descartes's parts, argued with Maimonides across five centuries, conducted much of his philosophy by letter, and then spent two hundred years being answered by people who often knew him only through his enemies. This file traces those conversations in three directions; the reception that made them possible is in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

## Backward: the inheritance, and the breaks

### Descartes: the Trojan horse

Everything in Spinoza's formation runs through [Descartes](../../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md). The inheritance is real: the rationalist confidence that reality is intelligible through clear ideas, the geometrical order of demonstration, the vocabulary of substance, attribute, and mode, even the proof-strategies for God's existence. The only book Spinoza published under his own name in his lifetime, the *Principles of Descartes' Philosophy* (1663), presented Descartes's physics and metaphysics in geometrical form — a credential in Cartesian circles and, in retrospect, a Trojan horse: its appendix, the *Metaphysical Thoughts*, already bends the master's terms toward conclusions Descartes would have refused, and the preface (by his friend Lodewijk Meyer) warns readers not to attribute everything in it to Spinoza himself.

The break is total where it matters. Descartes's plural substances — God, minds, bodies — become exactly one substance, of which minds and bodies are modes. The mind–body interaction problem, which Descartes solved with a gland and Spinoza regarded as a confusion rather than a puzzle, is refused outright: mind and body are one thing under two attributes, so nothing needs to cross a gap. The *cogito*'s self-certain subject is demoted — the mind is the idea of the body, not a little sovereign — and Descartes's free will, defended in the *Passions*, is denied as an illusion of consciousness ignorant of its causes. Spinoza presents all this not as anti-Cartesianism but as Cartesianism completed: take the definitions seriously, he implies, and monism is where they lead. Whether that is fair to Descartes is a standing scholarly question; that it is how Spinoza wanted to be read is not.

### Maimonides and the medieval Jewish tradition

Spinoza's first philosophy was [Maimonides](../../eras/02-medieval/maimonides.md), learned in Amsterdam's community schools, and the *Guide of the Perplexed* stands behind him twice over — once as a resource, once as a target. The resource: Maimonides' negative theology, which denies that terms like "wise" or "powerful" mean the same thing said of God and of us, is an ancestor of Spinoza's refusal of an anthropomorphic God; so is the very project of reading Scripture philosophically. The target: Maimonides' hermeneutic rule — where Scripture's literal sense conflicts with demonstrated truth, reinterpret it allegorically — is rejected by name in the *Theological-Political Treatise* (chapters 7 and 15). Scripture, Spinoza argues, must be explained from Scripture, by a historical method attentive to language, authorship, and occasion; its teaching is obedience and justice, not metaphysics, and harmonizing it with philosophy corrupts both. The break over God is deeper still: Maimonides' God, however purified of attributes, remains a transcendent intellect and will; Spinoza's God neither chooses nor purposes, and "intellect" and "will" as we know them belong to *Natura naturata*, not to God's essence. Other medieval Jewish voices — Crescas's critique of Maimonidean Aristotelianism, with its emphasis on God's love — have been proposed as sources, and scholars still argue over how much of Spinoza's formation was traditional and how much already *marrano* skepticism; what is not disputed is that the argument with Maimonides is one he never stopped having.

### Scholastic vocabulary, turned against its owners

Between Descartes and Maimonides stands the scholastic lexicon Spinoza inherited from both: *substance*, *attribute*, *mode*, *causa sui*, essence and existence, objective and formal reality. He keeps the words and changes their grammar. A substance defined as "in itself and conceived through itself" is a scholastic commonplace until Part I of the *Ethics* draws out what follows: there can be only one. The repurposing is deliberate method, not confusion — he argues from premises his opponents' own textbooks grant — and it is why early readers could find the *Ethics* simultaneously familiar and blasphemous.

### Hobbes: the parallel naturalist

The *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670) appeared nineteen years after [Hobbes](../../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md)'s *Leviathan*, and the two books are the century's twin founding texts of political naturalism. The agreements are striking: a state of nature described without moral cosmetics, natural right analyzed in terms of power, religion as the state's business, Scripture read historically (Hobbes's *Leviathan*, Part III, is a genuine precursor). The divergences define Spinoza's originality: where Hobbes's subjects alienate their right to a sovereign, Spinoza holds that right is coextensive with power and can never be wholly transferred — a multitude that obeys from hope and fear retains its power, so the state rests on the multitude's affects, not on a covenant's fiction. Where Hobbes prefers monarchy, Spinoza calls democracy "the most natural" regime, closest to the freedom nature grants. Scholars debate the debt itself: Spinoza owned *De Cive*, but how directly the *Treatise* answers *Leviathan* — and how much flows independently from his metaphysics — remains contested, and the dossier presents it that way.

### Machiavelli: the Florentine's realism

The unfinished *Political Treatise* opens by promising to treat politics as [Machiavelli](../../eras/03-early-modern/niccolo-machiavelli.md) had: from how men actually are, governed by affects, not as moralists wish them to be. Spinoza calls Machiavelli "most acute" and takes from him the founding insight that a state is an affective machine whose stability depends on channeling hope, fear, and ambition rather than preaching against them. The debt is methodological before it is doctrinal, and it separates both men from the utopian tradition Spinoza mocks in the *Treatise*'s first chapter.

### The Amsterdam circle: his real university

Having declined the actual university (Heidelberg's offer, 1673), Spinoza's intellectual institution was his circle: the Latin lessons of the ex-Jesuit Franciscus van den Enden; the friends — Meyer, Jarig Jelles, Pieter Balling, Jan Bouwmeester, Simon de Vries — for whom the *Short Treatise* was drafted and who would edit the *Opera Posthuma*; the Collegiant milieu of dissenting, anti-confessional Christians among whom he lived at Rijnsburg. The circle was audience, sounding board, and eventually literary executor: the *Ethics* exists in print because they published it.

## Sideways: philosophy by letter, and one famous visit

Spinoza's surviving correspondence is not marginalia; it is where several doctrines get their sharpest statements. With **Henry Oldenburg**, secretary of the Royal Society, he exchanged letters from 1661 on God, matter, and (through Oldenburg) Boyle's experiments — a correspondence that cooled as Oldenburg recoiled from the *Treatise* and warmed again before Spinoza's death. With the grain merchant **Willem van Blyenbergh** (1664–65) he conducted the great exchange on evil: evil and sin, Spinoza insists, are not positive things but privations relative to our ideas — an answer that satisfied him so completely and Blyenbergh so little that he eventually broke off the exchange with unusual impatience. With **Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus** (1674–76), the most technically formidable of his correspondents, he faced the hardest question his system invites: if the intellect perceives only two attributes, what entitles us to say there are infinitely many — and could a mind constituted differently know a third? The letters, routed through Georg Hermann Schuller, show Spinoza conceding the shape of the problem while holding his ground. It was also Schuller who served as intermediary for the most consequential visit of Spinoza's last year: **Leibniz** came to The Hague in November 1676, and the two talked for days — optics, physics, and, by Leibniz's own later account, philosophy. What followed is the classic pattern of an anxious debt: Leibniz's mature system, with its plurality of substances and its pre-established harmony between soul and body, is shaped at point after point as an answer to Spinoza's monism and parallelism, while his published references to Spinoza are overwhelmingly refutations and, in the *Theodicy*, distancing. His private notes show the deeper engagement his public posture conceals. (A remark often put in Leibniz's mouth — that Spinoza might have corrected himself had he lived longer — circulates widely; its documentation is thin, and the dossier rests on the documented record instead: the visit, the correspondence, and the notebooks.)

## Forward: the answerers

**[Locke](../../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md).** No meeting was possible — Spinoza died in 1677, and Locke's Dutch exile began in 1683 — but Locke moved in Holland among men who had known Spinoza's circle, and the parallels between the *Treatise*'s twentieth chapter and Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) are real: inward belief cannot be coerced because it cannot be commanded; the magistrate's business is civil peace, not souls. Whether this is influence, mediated through the shared Dutch milieu, or parallel development from shared problems is genuinely unsettled, and honest accounts hedge it exactly so.

**[Hume](../../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md).** Hume knew Spinoza chiefly through Bayle's hostile portrait, and in the *Treatise of Human Nature* calls the monism a "hideous hypothesis" — yet the two are the century's paired "atheists" in the public imagination, and their psychologies face each other instructively. Hume makes reason the slave of the passions; Spinoza would reject the inversion, not the naturalism. For him an affect can be checked only by a stronger affect, but reason is not inert information: adequate ideas are themselves active affects, and understanding is how a passion changes its nature. Where Hume despairs of reason's power and celebrates its weakness, Spinoza builds a therapy on its strength.

**The German recovery** begins with **[Lessing](../../eras/03-early-modern/gotthold-ephraim-lessing.md)** and the Pantheism controversy (treated fully in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md)): Jacobi's 1785 report that Lessing had confessed Spinozism forced German philosophy to decide what it thought. **[Kant](../../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)** knew Spinoza largely at second hand and classed him among the dogmatic metaphysicians the *Critique* supersedes; but Jacobi's counter-charge — that Kant's own thing-in-itself, pressed consistently, collapses into Spinozism — made Spinoza the shadow interlocutor of the whole critical enterprise. **[Hegel](../../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)** completed the rehabilitation and the domestication at once. His lectures declare that being a Spinozist is the necessary beginning of philosophy — the library entry gives his line as "either Spinozism or no philosophy" — and generations of students met Spinoza through Hegel's reading of him. That reading includes the famous charge of **acosmism**: Spinoza's world, Hegel argues, is not deified but dissolved — only substance is real, and finite things vanish into it. Later scholarship has largely treated the acosmism charge as Hegel's construction rather than Spinoza's doctrine, pointing to the reality the *Ethics* grants modes; it is presented here as Hegel's influential interpretation, contested, not as the verdict.

**The Romantics** took the other path: where Hegel absorbed Spinoza into a system, they loved him as a saint of immanence. Schleiermacher's *Speeches* (1799) invite the cultured despisers of religion to offer "a lock of hair to the manes of the holy, rejected Spinoza"; the phrase that stuck — the "God-intoxicated man" — is attributed to Novalis and transmitted through the Romantic circle's common stock, its exact coinage less certain than its currency. **[Marx](../../eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)** came to Spinoza as a young Hegelian: his 1841 notebooks excerpt the *Theological-Political Treatise* and the correspondence at length, and scholars still dispute how deep the early encounter runs in the mature work — the materialism of the *Ethics*, its refusal of teleology, and its theory of the multitude are the usual candidates. **Einstein** has no library entry (he is here as a reader, not a philosopher of the canon), but his answer to Rabbi Herbert Goldstein's cable in 1929 is the most famous twentieth-century act of allegiance: "I believe in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the orderly harmony of what exists" — with the corollary, stated in the same telegram, of disbelief in a God concerned with human fates and actions.

The century's creative appropriations — none of these figures has a library entry, and the dossier says so rather than inventing links — include **Deleuze**, whose two Spinoza books (1968, and the *Practical Philosophy* of 1970/81) made *expression* the key to the system and Spinoza, in his framing, the "prince of philosophers"; **Negri**, whose *The Savage Anomaly* (1981) read the *Political Treatise*'s multitude as revolutionary *potentia*, a line continued in the *Empire* literature with Hardt; and the analytic recovery that made Spinoza arguable-with again in Anglophone departments: **Jonathan Bennett's** *A Study of Spinoza's Ethics* (1984), respectful in method and hostile in several verdicts (Part V's eternity doctrine he found close to unintelligible), and **Edwin Curley's** monument of scholarship — his translation of the *Collected Works* (1985–2016) and the interpretive work that made the modern classroom Spinoza possible.

## The themes trace

The library's [problem index](../../themes.md) places Spinoza in six of its nine threads — a measure of the system's reach:

- [§1 Knowledge & Skepticism](../../themes.md#1-knowledge--skepticism--what-can-we-know-and-how-do-we-know-it) — the three kinds of knowledge, rising from confused imagination to *scientia intuitiva*, which sees things under a species of eternity.
- [§2 Mind & Body](../../themes.md#2-mind--body--what-is-mind-and-how-does-it-relate-to-the-body) — mind and body as one thing under two attributes; the interaction problem dissolved rather than solved.
- [§3 God & Religion](../../themes.md#3-god--religion--does-god-exist-and-what-follows-for-faith-and-reason) — *Deus sive Natura*; Scripture teaches obedience, not philosophy; miracles are ignorance of causes wearing a title.
- [§4 Ethics & the Good Life](../../themes.md#4-ethics--the-good-life--how-should-i-live-and-what-is-right) — good and bad as names for what aids or hinders our power of acting; freedom as understood necessity, ending in blessedness.
- [§5 Politics & Justice](../../themes.md#5-politics--justice--how-should-we-live-together-and-who-may-rule) — right extends exactly as far as power; democracy as the most natural regime.
- [§8 Science & Method](../../themes.md#8-science--method--how-should-inquiry-proceed-and-what-does-it-prove) — explanation by efficient causes alone; superstition begins where purposes are projected onto necessity.

The index does not place him in §6 (Self & Identity), §7 (Language & Meaning), or §9 (History & Progress) — accurately, in the main: the mind's eternity borders §6 without becoming a theory of personal identity, and there is no philosophy of history in a system where the whole has no direction. §7 is the arguable omission: his account of words as signs of things as imagined (Part II, and the *Treatise*'s hermeneutics) is a real philosophy of language in embryo, even if the index leaves it unlisted.
