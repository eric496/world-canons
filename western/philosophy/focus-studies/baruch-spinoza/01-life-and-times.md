# Life and Times: Amsterdam, the Cherem, and the Lens Grinder

Spinoza's life is short, outwardly quiet, and documented in unusual ways: the richest sources are the archives of a community that expelled him, the inventory of his few possessions taken after his death, and two early biographies — by Jean-Maximilien Lucas, a French physician who admired him, and Johannes Colerus, a Lutheran minister who did not but interviewed his landlord's family. Where this account rests on those biographies rather than on documents, it says so. The record is thin in exactly the places legend rushes to fill, and the dossier's rule is the archive's rule: hedged where the record is thin.

## A converso childhood in Amsterdam

Baruch Spinoza was born on November 24, 1632, in Amsterdam's Jewish quarter near the Vlooienburg, and named for the three languages of his world: Baruch in Hebrew, Bento in Portuguese, Benedictus in the Latin he would later write in — all meaning "blessed." His parents belonged to the Portuguese "Nation": descendants of *conversos*, Jews of Portugal and Spain who had lived outwardly as Christians under the Inquisition and who, reaching the tolerant Dutch Republic, returned openly to Judaism. Amsterdam's Portuguese Jews built, within two generations, one of the most prosperous and learned Jewish communities in Europe — and one of the most anxious about its respectability, since its members' Judaism was recently reclaimed, its wealth conspicuous, and its security dependent on Dutch goodwill.

His father, Michael (Miguel) de Spinoza, was an importer of dried fruit and olive oil from Iberia, repeatedly elected a *parnas* — a lay elder — of the congregation, and a founder of its charitable and educational institutions. His mother, Hannah Debora, Michael's second wife, died in 1638, when Baruch was five or six; a stepmother, Esther, raised the household and died in 1653. Michael died in March 1654. The family business, which Baruch would briefly co-run with his brother Gabriel as "Bento y Gabriel de Spinoza," was respectable but burdened with debt — a fact that matters for what followed.

## The schoolboy and the merchant's son

The congregation's school, Talmud Torah, with its seminary Ets Haim ("Tree of Life"), gave Spinoza the standard elite curriculum: Hebrew, Torah with its commentators, Talmud, and the medieval Jewish philosophers. He studied under the community's leading rabbis — the conservative Saul Levi Morteira and the more worldly Menasseh ben Israel, later famous across Europe — and the early biographies agree he was a brilliant pupil, marked out, in the tradition's phrase, as a future light of the congregation. Alongside the Jewish curriculum he absorbed medieval Jewish rationalism, above all Maimonides, whose *Guide of the Perplexed* taught that Scripture and philosophy must be reconciled or Scripture reinterpreted. Spinoza's mature work can be read as that lesson turned against the reconciliation itself.

Around 1650 he left formal study to work in the family firm. The drift outward seems to have begun here, and the decisive instrument was Latin. By the early biographies' account — later scholarship accepts the core of it — Spinoza studied Latin with **Franciscus van den Enden**: a former Jesuit from Antwerp who ran a private Latin school in Amsterdam, taught the classics through theatrical performance, and held religious and political views radical enough that, two decades later, he was hanged in Paris (1674) for conspiring against Louis XIV. Whether van den Enden merely gave Spinoza Latin or also gave him his first taste of Cartesianism and classical freethinking is genuinely uncertain; the dossier flags the strong version of the van den Enden influence as tradition, not record. What is certain is that through Latin, Spinoza gained the new philosophy then convulsing Dutch universities — Descartes above all — and a circle of liberal Christian and freethinking friends outside the congregation: the world of the Collegiants and the Amsterdam booksellers.

The last years inside the community show a man already half outside it. After Michael's death in 1654, Spinoza and Gabriel ran the firm together, but Baruch used Dutch civil courts in ways the community discouraged — litigating a debt, and contesting with his sister Rebekah over their father's estate; the early biographies add that, having won the estate suit, he renounced all but a bed, a story whose core (the lawsuit and a formal renunciation of the debt-laden inheritance through the Amsterdam Orphan Chamber in March 1656) is documented, whatever embroidery the bed has acquired. He was ceasing to observe, ceasing to attend, and — in conversations reported later — ceasing to believe.

## The cherem of 1656

On July 27, 1656 — the 6th of Av, 5416 — the lay elders (*parnassim*) of the Talmud Torah congregation pronounced against the twenty-three-year-old a *cherem*, a writ of excommunication, in language of extraordinary ferocity even by the standards of such instruments. Its curses are modeled on the curses of Deuteronomy, on Joshua's ban against Jericho, and on Elisha's curse of the boys; it forbids every member of the community to speak with him, read anything he has written, or come within four cubits of him. The text survives in the congregation's register. What it does not contain is any specific charge: it speaks of "abominable heresies which he practiced and taught" and "monstrous deeds," without naming one.

That silence is permanent, and this dossier will not pretend otherwise. The **precise grounds of the *cherem* are not documented**, and everything said about them is inference. The leading hypotheses, which are compatible and probably compounded, are these. (1) *Doctrine*: testimony gathered later — above all a 1659 report to the Spanish Inquisition by a friar who reported conversations with Spinoza and with the physician Juan de Prado (himself banned in 1658) — attributes to him the views that God exists only "philosophically," that the soul dies with the body, and that the Law was not divinely given; these match the philosophy he later published, and the community had already disciplined the critic Uriel da Costa a generation earlier. (2) *Conduct and communal order*: he had stopped living as an observant Jew, used gentile courts against fellow congregants, and renounced his inheritance outside communal arbitration — each a challenge to a self-governing minority's fragile jurisdiction, in a decade when the congregation was acutely protective of its standing. (3) *Self-exclusion*: some scholars note that the writ's violence reads like the formalization of a rupture Spinoza had already made himself; he never appealed it, never sought readmission, never converted to Christianity either, and reportedly answered the ban with something like relief at being forced onto the path he had chosen — that last detail is Lucas's, and is flagged as such.

What is not hypothesis is the sequel: the *cherem* was never revoked in his lifetime (a symbolic lifting was debated, and declined, as recently as the 2010s), and it fixed his reputation in advance. Philosophy in the Dutch Republic would be pursued, by this man, with no institution to answer to and no reputation to protect.

## The lens grinder among friends

Spinoza's answer to expulsion was a trade. He had learned **lens grinding** — polishing lenses for microscopes and telescopes to an optical standard that working scientists, Christiaan Huygens among them, respected — and it supported him, modestly and really, for the rest of his life. It was also a declaration: manual work, no patronage, no salary, nothing owed. He lived first in Amsterdam, then from 1661 in **Rijnsburg**, a village near Leiden and a center of the Collegiants — dissenting Protestants who rejected confessional churches for free inquiry — renting a room in a surgeon's house (the cottage survives as the Spinozahuis museum). In 1663 he moved to **Voorburg**, near The Hague, and in 1670 to **The Hague** itself, first to the Stille Verkade and from 1671 to the house on the Paviljoensgracht of the painter Hendrik van der Spyck, where he died.

Around him formed the circle that functioned as his university and his publisher. **Henry Oldenburg**, secretary of the new Royal Society in London, visited Rijnsburg in 1661 and corresponded with him for years. **Lodewijk Meyer**, physician and philosopher, edited and prefaced his one named book; **Johannes Bouwmeester**, another physician, translated and corresponded; **Simon Joosten de Vries**, a young Amsterdam merchant-scholar, formed a study group around Spinoza's drafts and, dying in 1667, left him an annuity — which Spinoza, by Colerus's account, persuaded the heir to reduce from 500 to 300 guilders. **Jarig Jelles**, a merchant turned Collegiant, and **Pieter Balling** were older friends and theological fellow travelers; the bookseller **Jan Rieuwertsz**, Amsterdam's great publisher of forbidden books, printed what others would not. The inventoried facts of the life fit in one room: a bed, a desk, lens-grinding tools, some 160 books sold after his death — Descartes, Hobbes, Machiavelli, Grotius, the Hebrew Bible and its critics — and a signet ring engraved with a rose and his motto: *Caute*, cautiously.

## The one named book, and the anonymous bomb

In 1663, at Meyer's urging and for his Voorburg pupils, Spinoza published the *Principles of Descartes' Philosophy*, Descartes's system recast in geometrical order, with an appendix of his own, the *Metaphysical Thoughts*. It is the **only book published under his name in his lifetime** — an exposition of another man's philosophy that its preface (by Meyer) carefully warns does not represent its author's views where the two diverge. Meanwhile the system of his own had been maturing: an early statement (the *Short Treatise*) circulated in Dutch among his friends from about 1660, and a draft of the *Ethics* in some form by 1665. Around 1665 he set it aside to write the book the moment seemed to demand.

The *Theological-Political Treatise* appeared in early 1670, in Latin, anonymously, with a false imprint — "Hamburg, at the press of Henricus Künraht" — designed to shield its actual Amsterdam publisher. It argued that Scripture must be read historically, that prophecy and miracle dissolve under that reading, that faith and philosophy have separate provinces, and that the security of the state requires *libertas philosophandi*, freedom to philosophize. The disguise held for weeks. Spinoza was identified almost at once, the book was denounced from pulpits as a work "forged in hell," and in July 1674 the Court of Holland formally banned it, in company with Hobbes's *Leviathan*. It was also read, everywhere, and it made "Spinoza" a European byword for atheism a full seven years before the *Ethics* appeared.

## War, the chair declined, and Leibniz's visit

The 1670s brought the Republic's crisis and Spinoza's most considered public choices. In the disaster year 1672, French invasion collapsed the government of Johan de Witt and a mob lynched De Witt and his brother in The Hague. Colerus reports that Spinoza, enraged, prepared a placard reading *Ultimi barbarorum* — "you worst of barbarians" — to post at the site, and that his landlord locked him in to prevent his being torn apart too. The anecdote is early and widely credited, but it is Colerus's anecdote; the dossier records it as such. In 1673 he traveled to French-occupied Utrecht, apparently hoping to meet the Prince de Condé (who had departed), and came home under whispered suspicion of spying — an episode that says more about his reputation than about his errand.

In February 1673 came the offer that tested his independence: through Johann Ludwig Fabricius, the Elector Karl Ludwig of the Palatinate invited him to a **professorship of philosophy at Heidelberg**, promising, in the Elector's name, "the most ample freedom to philosophize," on the single condition that he not disturb the publicly established religion. Spinoza declined, in a letter of March 30, 1673 whose stated reasons are exact and characteristic: he did **not know within what limits that freedom would have to be confined** in order not to seem to disturb the established religion; he foresaw contention his temperament wished to avoid; and he valued the quiet of his present life. He stayed a tenant and a lens grinder, and finished the *Ethics*.

Its publication he then suspended himself. In the summer of 1675 he traveled to Amsterdam to see the *Ethics* through the press and stopped the printing when rumor spread — reported to him by letter — that a book of his was forthcoming "in which I tried to show that there is no God," and that theologians were preparing complaints to the authorities. He told Oldenburg he would postpone and clarify; the clarification was never enough, and the book stayed in his desk.

The last distinguished visitor came in November 1676: **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**, aged thirty, already famous, passing through The Hague and eager — through intermediaries in their circle — to meet the lens grinder. What is documented is solid: they met several times over some days; Spinoza showed him portions of the unpublished *Ethics*; they discussed Descartes, physics, and the new philosophy, and Leibniz's notes and his later annotated reading of the *Opera Posthuma* survive. What is embroidery is the embroidery: the single marathon conversation, the suggestion that Leibniz came mainly to steal or to spy, and Leibniz's own later public distancing ("I have met Spinoza" minimizations) belong to the politics of reputation, his own included. The two most original systems of the century sat in one room on the Paviljoensgracht; the record supports conversations, plural, and nothing more operatic.

## Death and the posthumous book

Spinoza's health had never been strong, and his last winter was a decline his friends watched across the autumn of 1676. He died on **February 21, 1677**, a Sunday afternoon, in his rooms on the Paviljoensgracht, aged forty-four, with his landlord's family in the house and his physician friend — by Colerus's account, Lodewijk Meyer had visited that day — recently departed. He was buried in a rented grave in the Nieuwe Kerk; the grave was later cleared, as rented graves were, and no remains survive.

The cause of death is not recorded. The usual account, found in the early biographies' report of a long-standing lung complaint, is **phthisis** — consumption, a wasting disease of the lungs. The further hypothesis that fine glass dust from two decades of lens grinding produced or aggravated a silicosis-like condition is a later, plausible, and **unverifiable** speculation: no autopsy, no clinical record, and a family history that includes his mother dying young make the occupational theory one possibility among others. The dossier states it as a hypothesis, exactly as the library entry does, and no more firmly.

His friends moved within months. The manuscripts from his desk — the completed *Ethics*, the unfinished *Political Treatise*, the early *Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect*, a *Compendium of Hebrew Grammar*, and his letters — were carried to Amsterdam, and before the end of 1677 Jan Rieuwertsz published them as the ***Opera Posthuma***, with no publisher's name or place, edited by the circle (Jelles wrote the preface; Meyer likely supervised the editing). A Dutch translation, ***De Nagelate Schriften***, in Jan Hendriksz Glazemaker's rendering, followed almost simultaneously, putting the *Ethics* into the vernacular within the year. In June 1678 the authorities banned the *Opera Posthuma* throughout Holland. The banned book outlived the ban: the century that begins by forbidding Spinoza ends with Lessing, Goethe, and Hegel confessing that philosophy itself runs through him — a reception this dossier takes up in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).
