# Plato: The Arguments

*Focus study · [Dossier home](README.md) · Library entry: [Plato](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md) · The architecture these arguments build: [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) · Study guide: [*Republic*](../../study/plato-republic.md)*

Each argument below is reconstructed in numbered premises in the house style: what the argument proves, the reconstruction, its textual basis, the strongest standard objection, and the best reply — the speaker's own where the dialogue gives one, the literature's where it does not, labeled as such. Citations are by **Stephanus number**, the standard pagination of Plato's Greek text, printed in the margins of every serious edition and translation.

One framing caution governs the whole file, and it is not boilerplate. These are arguments **voiced by characters in dramas** — by Socrates, by Socrates reporting the priestess Diotima, by an unnamed Eleatic Stranger — and Plato never speaks in his own person. Attributing an argument to "Plato" therefore always involves a step beyond the text, and how big a step is exactly what the two great reading-strategies dispute. **Developmentalists** (Vlastos's version is the benchmark) take the early dialogues' Socrates to represent the historical Socrates — elenctic, professing ignorance, holding no doctrine of separate Forms — and the constructive arguments of the middle dialogues (*Phaedo*, *Republic*, *Symposium*) to be Plato's own, voiced through a Socrates who has become a mouthpiece; on this reading the late dialogues revise the middle ones. **Unitarians** (Shorey; in a subtler form, Kahn) take the corpus as one developing *presentation* of a stable vision, the early aporetic dialogues deliberately withholding doctrines their author already held. Each argument below is stated in the form its dialogue gives it, with the attribution dispute named where it changes what the argument is. The reconstructions also do not smooth over the dialogues' own inconclusiveness: several of these arguments are given, by Plato, to speakers who concede their limits, and two (Arguments 9 and the *Parmenides* material in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)) are objections Plato aimed at himself.

**Argument 1. The Euthyphro dilemma: piety is not "what the gods love"**

*What it proves:* that a value-property cannot be defined by someone's attitude toward it — that the pious is not pious *because* the gods love it — and, by extension, the template for every later "divine command" problem. The dialogue is early; on the developmentalist reading, this is the historical Socrates' own argument-form at work.

1. Euthyphro proposes, as his fourth attempt at a definition, that the pious is what all the gods love, and the impious what all the gods hate (9e).
2. Socrates poses the disjunction (10a): is the pious loved by the gods *because it is pious*, or is it pious *because it is loved* by the gods?
3. Euthyphro agrees the pious is loved because it is pious — the loving follows and responds to the piety, as being carried follows a thing's being carried, and being seen follows its visibility (10a–c): the attitude tracks the property; it does not constitute it.
4. But if being god-loved were what piety *is*, the order would have to run the other way: the pious would be pious because loved. The two claims cannot both state the essence.
5. Therefore "loved by the gods" names, at most, an *affection* (*pathos*) of the pious — something that happens to it — and not its *essence* (*ousia*), which is what a definition must give (11a–b). The proposed definition fails.

**Textual basis.** *Euthyphro* 9e–11b; the question at 10a; the essence/affection distinction at 11a–b. The dialogue ends in *aporia* (15e–16a): Euthyphro hurries off, and no definition survives.

**Objection.** The dilemma is not exhaustive. A sophisticated divine-command theorist (the literature's strongest version is Robert Adams's modified divine command theory) can grasp the second horn while denying the arbitrariness: the pious is constituted by the commands of a God whose nature is necessarily loving and just, so the commands could not have commanded cruelty — the property is attitude-dependent without being arbitrary, because the attitude-holder's character is fixed by necessity. A second objection targets premise 3: Euthyphro's agreement is dialectical, extracted from a confused interlocutor; a defender of the definition could instead insist that "because" in premise 2's two horns runs in different senses (explanatory vs. constitutive), dissolving the contradiction.

**Reply.** The dialogue gives Socrates no further reply — the objection and its counter are the literature's. The standard defense of the dilemma's force is that it relocates rather than settles the question: if God's character fixes the commands, then the goodness of that character is doing the definitional work, and the same question recurs one level up — is God's nature good because it is his, or his because it is good? — which is premise 2 again. The argument's historical yield is the distinction itself, between a property and an attitude toward it, and it survives the polytheistic setting that occasions it: monotheism removes the quarreling gods of Euthyphro's earlier attempts (6a–8b) but not the disjunction at 10a.

**Argument 2. Meno's paradox and recollection: inquiry is possible because the soul already knows**

*What it proves:* that inquiry into what one does not know is neither impossible (as Meno's paradox claims) nor the reception of new information from outside, but the recovery — by questioning — of knowledge the soul already possesses; in the *Meno* this serves the search for virtue's definition, and in the *Phaedo* it becomes a premise about the soul's pre-existence.

1. Meno's paradox (80d): a man cannot search for what he knows (he has no need), nor for what he does not know — for he does not know what to look for, and would not recognize it if he found it.
2. If the paradox held universally, inquiry and learning would be impossible; but learning demonstrably occurs, so something is wrong with the paradox's assumption that the inquirer's state is either full knowledge or blank ignorance.
3. Socrates' alternative (81a–d): the soul is immortal and has seen all things, here and in the underworld; learning is therefore *recollection* — the soul recovering, under questioning, what it already knows latently.
4. The demonstration (82b–85d): an enslaved boy with no geometry instruction is asked — only asked — about doubling a square. He answers wrongly (double the side: 82e), recognizes his error and is perplexed (84a–b), then, still only questioned, works through to the correct construction on the diagonal (85a–b).
5. Socrates' gloss (85c–d): the opinions were "in him"; questioning stirred them "like a dream," and repeated questioning would make them exact knowledge — without anyone teaching him.
Therefore, inquiry is possible because its object is already, latently, in the soul: the paradox's blank-ignorance horn is false. (In the *Meno* Socrates at once hedges: of the argument's details he would not insist — but that we are "better and braver and less helpless" believing inquiry possible than believing it is not, he will defend in word and deed, 86b–c.)

**Textual basis.** *Meno* 80d (the paradox — Socrates calls it an "eristic argument"), 81a–86c (recollection and the demonstration); recollection redeployed at *Phaedo* 72e–77a.

**Objection.** The demonstration is rigged, say the critics — the standing objection since antiquity's commentators and sharpened by modern ones (Vlastos, "Anamnesis in the *Meno*," 1965, presses it sympathetically; others less so): Socrates' "questions" draw the figure, supply the vocabulary, and cue every step, so the scene shows guided invention or skillful teaching, not recollection of a previous existence. More fundamentally, the boy's success shows at most *innate cognitive capacity* — a mind structured to construct geometry — which is a weaker, naturalistic thesis that needs no pre-existent soul: the argument equivocates between "the knowledge was in him" as latent content and as latent ability.

**Reply.** The dialogue's own reply is the modesty of premise 5's gloss: Socrates presents recollection as the best available dissolution of the paradox, not as a demonstrated metaphysics, and the *Meno* ends with virtue's teachability still undecided. The literature's defense (Dominic Scott's is the most developed) distinguishes two grades of recollection in the corpus: the *Meno*'s, in which questioning recovers latent *beliefs* a teacher's questions help form, and the *Phaedo*'s, in which sensible equals trigger recovery of a *standard* (Equality) that was never sensible content at all — the second is the load-bearing version, and the guided-invention objection, which works against a boy being walked through a proof, does not touch the judgment that two sticks fall short of a standard neither stick exhibits. Whether the weaker, capacity-only reading suffices for the *Phaedo*'s immortality use is contested; see Argument 3.

**Argument 3. The *Phaedo*'s affinity argument: the soul is like the divine and incomposite, the body like the mortal**

*What it proves:* not, strictly, immortality — the argument's own conclusion is comparative — but that the soul belongs to the order of the invisible, changeless, and incomposite, and is therefore "altogether indissoluble, or nearly so" (80b): the first of the dialogue's escalating attempts, built to be tested by the objections that follow it in the text.

1. Things divide into two kinds (78b–79a): the composite, which can be dissolved into its parts and is liable to change, and the incomposite, which, having no parts, cannot be dissolved and remains constant. Dissolution — death, for a living thing — is the fate of composites.
2. The Forms are incomposite, invisible, and always the same (78c–d); sensible things are composite, visible, and never constant.
3. The body is of the visible, composite kind; the soul is invisible (79b) and, when it investigates by itself, "goes to what is pure and eternal and immortal and unchanging, and being akin to it, remains with it" (79d) — it reasons, rules the body rather than being dragged by it, and grasps what the senses cannot.
4. What is akin to the incomposite and divine is fitted to rule and to resist dissolution; what is akin to the composite is fitted to be ruled and to perish (79e–80a).
Therefore, the soul is "most like" the divine, deathless, and indissoluble, the body "most like" the human, mortal, and dissoluble — and at death the soul departs, pure if it has practiced philosophy, to what is like itself (80b–81a).

**Textual basis.** *Phaedo* 78b–80b (the argument), within the dialogue's death-day frame; the recollection argument it presupposes at 72e–77a; the caveat in premise 1's conclusion is Socrates' own ("or nearly so," 80b).

**Objection.** The dialogue itself raises the two strongest objections, and both are better than the argument they answer. **Simmias' harmony objection** (85e–88d): the soul may be invisible and finer than the body yet stand to it as the attunement (*harmonia*) stands to a lyre — invisible, incorporeal, and beautiful, but a product of the body's blended elements, perishing the moment the instrument breaks; likeness to the divine in invisibility does not confer the divine's indestructibility. **Cebes' weaver objection** (87a–88b): grant that the soul outlasts any one body, as a weaver outlasts many cloaks he has woven and worn out; it does not follow that the soul outlasts its *last* body — it may be worn down by successive embodiments and perish with one of them, so affinity plus longevity still falls short of immortality. A modern form of Simmias' point: invisibility and cognitive kinship are properties the affinity argument borrows from the Forms, but the soul's *causal dependence* on the body — evident in every injury, intoxication, and disease, which Socrates himself notes (79c) — is exactly what a harmony would show.

**Reply.** Socrates' reply to Simmias is drawn from recollection (92a–95a): if the soul were an attunement, it could not pre-exist the body — but the argument of 72e–77a showed it does, so it is no attunement; he adds that harmonies admit of degree while souls, as souls, do not, and that a harmony cannot rule or oppose its components as the soul opposes the body's appetites. His reply to Cebes is an admission that the affinity argument is insufficient — hence the dialogue's final argument (Argument 4), which attempts the stronger conclusion from the theory of Forms itself. Scholars dispute how much Plato meant the affinity argument to carry: read developmentally, it is a provisional stage its author knew to be weak; read as rhetoric for a deathbed, its "or nearly so" is the honest signal. Either way, the dialogue grades it below the proof that replaces it.

**Argument 4. The *Phaedo*'s final argument: the soul, bearer of life, cannot admit death**

*What it proves:* the dialogue's strongest case: that the soul is not merely long-lived but *deathless* and, by a final step, indestructible — because it essentially brings life, and what essentially brings a form cannot admit that form's opposite. This is the argument Cebes' cloak objection forced into existence.

1. The "safe" theory of causation (100b–101c): a thing is beautiful by Beauty, large by Largeness — particulars have their characters by participating in the corresponding Form.
2. Opposites exclude opposites (102a–103c): what is tall in one comparison is short in another, but Tallness itself never admits Shortness; more sharply, things that essentially bear a form repel its opposite — snow, essentially bearing cold, cannot admit heat (it perishes or withdraws rather than becoming hot snow); fire cannot admit cold; the number three, bearing oddness, cannot admit the even.
3. The soul is defined by what it brings to whatever it occupies: a body, when soul enters it, is alive (105c–d). Soul essentially bears **life**, as fire bears heat and three bears oddness.
4. Death is the opposite of life (105d). What essentially bears a form cannot admit that form's opposite (from 2); therefore the soul cannot admit death — the soul is *deathless* (*athanatos*) (105e).
5. The final step (106a–107a): what is deathless, when death approaches it, cannot be destroyed by it — and if even the deathless could be destroyed, nothing could be preserved from destruction; Socrates argues that the deathless must also be *indestructible* (*anōlethros*), as God and the Form of Life itself are agreed to be.
Therefore, when death comes to a man, the mortal part of him dies, but the immortal part "goes away intact and indestructible" (107a).

**Textual basis.** *Phaedo* 95e–107b (the "second sailing" and the final argument); the safe causation at 100b–101c; the essential-bearer cases at 103c–105c; the conclusion at 105e–107a. Socrates closes by returning the whole case to hypothesis: the first hypotheses, even if trustworthy, must be examined more clearly (107b).

**Objection.** The argument's joints are visible, and the literature has pressed all three. (i) Premise 5's slide: "deathless" (cannot *be* dead) does not entail "indestructible" (cannot *cease to exist*) — a soul might be incapable of being a dead soul yet simply cease, as fire, incapable of being cold, ceases when quenched; Socrates' support for the step is an appeal to general agreement about God, the weakest move in the dialogue. (ii) The analogy in premises 2–3 trades on equivocation: snow and fire are *substances* with essential properties; the soul's bearing of life may be definitional in a thinner sense — "soul" just names whatever animates — so that its "refusal" of death shows only that a dead animator is not an animator, not that an animator cannot stop. (iii) Even granted, the conclusion secures the survival of *soul*, not of *the person*: nothing in the argument preserves memory, character, or identity — the very things Simmias and Cebes, and the reader, care about at a deathbed.

**Reply.** The dialogue's own reply is Socrates' closing deflation (107b): the hypotheses need further examination, and the myth that follows (107c–115a) is offered as a "noble risk" (114d), not as knowledge — Plato builds the epistemic health warning into the text. The literature's defense distinguishes the argument's formal core from its analogical dress: formalized with Forms as essential causes, the inference from "essentially F" to "cannot admit the opposite of F" is valid *given* the theory of causation in premise 1, so the real contest is over that theory and over premise 5 — which most modern reconstructions concede is asserted rather than proven. The dossier's judgment, following the dialogue's own grading, is that the final argument is the strongest immortality argument Plato wrote and still falls short of demonstration at premise 5; that it does so at the deathbed of Socrates, before friends who need it to work, is part of what the dialogue is *about*.

**Argument 5. The Gyges challenge and the *Republic*'s answer strategy: justice must be shown good in itself**

*What it proves:* strictly, this is the dialogue's problem-statement and method, and its "conclusion" is a research program the remaining nine books execute — but it is stated with an argument's rigor, and no reconstruction of the *Republic* is honest without it. Glaucon and Adeimantus, voicing the strongest case *against* justice they can (they are Plato's brothers, and they want to be refuted), renew Thrasymachus' challenge in a form Book I's refutations cannot touch.

1. Goods divide into three kinds (357b–358a): those welcomed for themselves alone (harmless pleasures); those welcomed both for themselves and for their consequences (knowledge, health); and those burdensome in themselves, welcomed only for their consequences (exercise, medical treatment). Glaucon's demand: show that justice belongs in the **second** class — good in itself, apart from reputation and reward.
2. The received view, stated as a genealogy (358e–359b): doing injustice is good and suffering it is evil; men who cannot do the first without the second compact to abstain — justice as a compromise of the weak, practiced unwillingly.
3. The Ring of Gyges (359c–360d): give a just man and an unjust man each a ring of invisibility, and both will end at the same place — seduction, theft, murder — because no one is just willingly; remove detection, and justice evaporates. (The story is Herodotus' Gyges recast: Plato's ancestor-of-Lydia's-king finds the ring on a corpse in a chasm.)
4. Adeimantus' sharpening (362d–367e): all praise of justice in the tradition praises its *rewards and reputation*; the perfectly unjust man with a perfect reputation for justice will prosper, and the perfectly just man with a reputation for injustice will be scourged, racked, and crucified (361e–362a). The defense must therefore strip away reputation entirely and show what justice *itself*, in the soul that has it, does to its possessor.
5. Socrates' method (368c–369a): justice in one person is hard to discern, as small letters are; look first at justice "in larger letters" — in a city — then read the finding back into the individual.
6. The transfer (434d–441c): having found justice in the city as each class doing its own work (433a–434d), the inquiry returns to the soul and finds the same structure — reason, spirit, appetite — and the same definition: each part doing its own work, reason ruling (441c–442d).
Therefore, the answer strategy: justice will be shown to be the soul's internal order and health — a good of the second class — and injustice its civil war and disease, so that the perfectly unjust man of premise 4 is, whatever his reputation, the most miserable of men (the conclusion Book IX draws, Argument 8 below).

**Textual basis.** *Republic* II, 357a–367e (the challenge); the city founded at 368c–372e; IV, 434d–441c (the transfer and the tripartite soul). The guide's [Book II section](../../study/plato-republic.md) gives the passage map.

**Objection.** The strategy's weakest joint is the analogy itself — the standing objection since Bernard Williams's "The Analogy of City and Soul in Plato's *Republic*" (1973): the inference requires that "just" be predicated of city and soul in the same sense, but a city is just, Plato says, *because* its citizens are ordered in a certain way, and a soul is just because its *parts* are — the relata differ, the "larger letters" may spell a different word, and in places the argument seems to define the soul's justice by the city's and then read it back, a circle. A second objection strikes premise 3: Gyges shows at most that *undetected* injustice is tempting, not that justice is only fear of detection — Glaucon's "no one would" is an empirical conjecture about motivation dressed as a thought experiment's result.

**Reply.** The reply available to the text — Plato's own, by structure rather than by statement — is that the analogy is heuristic scaffolding, and the soul's division is *not* inferred from the city's classes: it is argued independently in Book IV from the principle of opposites and the conflict cases (thirst refused, Leontius' anger; Argument summary in [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md)), so the transfer at step 6 is a comparison of two independently established structures, not the derivation of one from the other. Williams's deeper point — that the predication still needs the structures to be the same *in the relevant respect* — remains the literature's live question, and the defense now standard (Lear, Ferrari) reads the analogy as running in both directions deliberately: city and soul interpret each other. On Gyges, defenders note the challenge needs only the weaker claim Glaucon actually needs: that reputation, not justice itself, is what ordinary morality visibly tracks — which is what Adeimantus' premise 4 establishes from the tradition's own praises.

**Argument 6. The philosopher-king: those who know the Good must rule**

*What it proves:* the *Republic*'s most famous and most contested inference — that the ideal city's evils end only when political power and philosophy coincide — stated in Book V as the third and greatest "wave" of paradox, and defended by the definition of the philosopher that the rest of Book V supplies.

1. A city ordered as Books II–IV describe — classes fixed by nature and education, guardians ruling — is possible only if its rulers rule by *knowledge* of what is genuinely good, not by opinion, custom, or appetite (the argument of Books II–IV; the rulers' wisdom is the city's, 428a–429a).
2. Knowledge, as distinct from opinion, is of the Forms, and its highest object is the Good (Book V's closing argument, 474b–480a; Book VI, 504a–509c): the philosopher is defined as the one who loves and can grasp "what is always the same in all respects" (479e), where the lover of sights and sounds grasps only the many beautiful things.
3. Such knowledge is rare, requires the long mathematical and dialectical education of Books VI–VII, and its possessors characteristically *do not want* to rule — they would rather contemplate — so they must be compelled (519c–520e; 540a–b).
4. If rulers lack this knowledge, the city is ruled by opinion aiming at honor or wealth — the regimes whose degeneration Book VIII traces to tyranny.
Therefore (473c–e): "until philosophers rule as kings in the cities, or those who are now called kings and rulers genuinely and sufficiently philosophize — until political power and philosophy coincide... cities will have no rest from evils, nor, I think, will the human race."

**Textual basis.** *Republic* V, 471c–480a (the third wave proposed and the philosopher defined); 473c–e (the thesis); VI–VII for the knowledge and the education; the compulsion to rule at 519c–521b and 540a–b. The "waves" are Socrates' own framing: the first (equal education and duties for women, 451c–457b) and the second (community of wives and children among guardians, 457c–466d) precede it, and he expects the third to drown him in laughter and disbelief (473c).

**Objection.** The objections come in two families. The *political* objection (Popper's is the famous form): the argument licenses unchecked rule by a self-certifying epistemic class — those who claim knowledge of the Good cannot be corrected by those who lack it, and premise 2's knowledge/opinion line gives the ruled no standing to object; every safeguard in the city (censorship, the noble lie, rigged lotteries) serves the rulers' certainty. The *epistemological* objection cuts underneath: premise 2's philosopher is defined by a knowledge — of the Good — that Socrates expressly declines to state (506d–e), so the qualification for absolute power is a credential no one can inspect, including, apparently, its holder's examiners.

**Reply.** The text's own replies are three. (i) The city is offered as a *paradeigma* — a model in speech for seeing justice — whose realizability Socrates separates from its truth: it makes no difference to the argument about the soul whether it can be founded anywhere (472b–e); at the end it exists "in heaven" for the individual to found in himself (592b). (ii) The rulers' reluctance is a structural safeguard, not an accident: power is assigned to the only class that does not want it, is paid no wage in property or family, and rules by turns under compulsion — Plato's answer to premise-3 corruption is institutional, a full two millennia before the modern versions. (iii) On the credential: the fifty-year curriculum with its examinations (535a–540c) is Plato's attempt to make the qualification inspectable by *process* where the object itself cannot be exhibited. The literature remains divided on whether these replies answer Popper or concede his charge by relocating the city to heaven; the dossier records the division, following the entry's rule that the authoritarian features are in the text and are not softened here.

**Argument 7. Sun, Line, and Cave: one epistemological argument in three images**

*What it proves:* the *Republic*'s epistemological centerpiece, delivered as three coordinated images rather than a deduction: that cognition comes in grades matched to grades of reality; that the highest grade — dialectical knowledge of the Forms, ordered by the Good — is the human mind's proper state; and that education is the painful turning of the whole person from the lowest grade toward the highest. Socrates insists the images *are* the account available: asked for the Good itself, he offers its "offspring" instead (506d–e).

1. The Sun (506d–509c): as the sun is the cause, in the visible realm, of sight and of the visibility and growth of visible things, so the Good is the cause, in the intelligible realm, of knowledge and truth and of the very being of the things known — while standing itself "beyond being" (509b). Knowledge and its objects depend on the Good as sight and its objects depend on the sun.
2. The Divided Line (509d–511e): divide a line into the visible and intelligible; divide each again in the same ratio. Four segments, four states of mind: *eikasia* (imagination: shadows, reflections), *pistis* (belief: the physical things whose shadows those are), *dianoia* (thought: mathematics, reasoning from hypotheses it cannot itself ground, using diagrams as images), *noēsis* (understanding: dialectic, ascending from hypotheses to the unhypothetical first principle). Clarity of cognition is proportional to truth of object.
3. The Cave (514a–520a): prisoners chained from birth take shadows on a wall for the whole of reality; one, freed and dragged upward by force, is blinded by fire, then by sunlight, and only gradually sees things as they are, the sun last. Returning below, his eyes full of darkness, he is ridiculed by the prisoners — and, if he tried to free them, they would kill him (517a).
4. The gloss (518b–521b): the images together state the theory of education the city's curriculum enacts — the capacity for knowledge is already in the soul, as sight is in the eye; education is the art of *turning* the whole soul, not of inserting sight into blind eyes (518c–d); and the one turned owes the city a return, to rule in the cave he has escaped (519c–520e).
Therefore, opinion about appearances — the prisoners' epistemic condition, and the ordinary citizen's — is not a lesser *amount* of knowledge but a different *state of the whole soul*, and only the completed ascent qualifies a person to judge either the intelligible world or the cave.

**Textual basis.** *Republic* VI, 506d–511e; VII, 514a–521b. The guide's [reconstruction (c)](../../study/plato-republic.md) states the three images in compressed form; this is the argumentative reading of them.

**Objection.** The objection is as old as the images' fame, and the guide states it: analogy is not argument. The Sun tells us the Good *illuminates* intelligibles as the sun illuminates visibles, but what the Good is, how it causes being, and how a mind grasps it remain unstated — and Plato concedes this in the framing (premise: Socrates "owes" us the account and pays in images). The Line's mathematics leans on an unexplained proportion and a contested claim — that mathematical thought is intrinsically inferior to dialectic because it leaves its hypotheses unexamined — which the history of mathematics has not obviously ratified. And the Cave can be read against its author's politics: every liberator's followers think they have left a cave; the image gives no mark by which the true ascent is told from a change of prisons.

**Reply.** The reply the literature standardly offers on Plato's behalf is about the images' *office*: they are not premises in disguise but a structural model — the claim is that the *relations* (dependence of visibility on a source not itself one of the visibles; proportionality of clarity to reality; conversion as reorientation rather than information) hold in the epistemic case, and that claim can be assessed where the model came from: the independently argued distinctions of Book V (knowledge vs. opinion) and the deficient-equals reasoning of the *Phaedo*. The images gather those arguments into one picture of a cognitive *life*; they do not replace them. On the Cave's self-application problem, defenders point to the text's own controls: the ascent is marked by increasing *explanatory* power (the freed man can account for the shadows, the fire, the mechanism — 516c, 520c) and by reluctance to return, marks a mere change of ideology does not characteristically show. Whether those marks suffice is, again, a live dispute — and the dossier notes that the kill-the-liberator line (517a) is the dialogue's own acknowledgment of how the cave receives its would-be freeers, an unmistakable backward glance at the trial of Socrates.

**Argument 8. That justice pays: the function argument and the tyrant of Book IX**

*What it proves:* the *Republic*'s direct answer to Glaucon (Argument 5) in its two installments — Book I's compressed version against Thrasymachus, and Book IX's completed comparison — that the just life is happier than the unjust life *in itself*, measured from inside the soul that lives it.

1. The function argument (352d–354a): everything has a function (*ergon*) — what it alone does, or does best — and a corresponding excellence (*aretē*) by which it performs that function well: eyes see, ears hear, a pruning knife cuts.
2. The soul's function is "living" itself, and with it managing, deliberating, ruling (353d–e). A soul performs this function well by its excellence and badly by its vice.
3. Justice has been provisionally identified as the soul's excellence and injustice as its vice (the identification is Book I's inheritance from the craft discussion — and its provisional character is exactly what Books II–IV repair).
4. Therefore the just soul lives well, and living well is being happy; the unjust soul lives badly. Thrasymachus' claim — that injustice, perfected, is stronger, freer, and happier — fails at its first step.
5. The Book IX completion (576b–588a): the five regimes and souls of Book VIII are ranked by their own experience. Only the philosopher has tasted all three families of pleasure — of learning, of honor, of appetite — while the profit-lover and honor-lover have never tasted the pleasure of knowing what is; taken as judge by experience, argument, and reason, the kingly (just) soul's life is most pleasant and the tyrant's most painful (580a–583a).
6. The tyrant himself, examined (571a–576b, 587b–588a): his soul is ruled by a master *erōs* — a drone-appetite that expels shame and moderation — making him in waking life what others are only in dreams; enslaved, fearful, friendless, and least able to do what he wishes, he is the most miserable of men, and the actual tyrant, outwardly freest, is worse off than the private tyrannical soul (579d–e).
Therefore, justice pays — not in reputation or reward (Glaucon's excluded evidence) but in the currency the challenge demanded: the internal condition and felt life of the soul itself.

**Textual basis.** *Republic* I, 352d–354a; VIII–IX, 571a–592b; the three-pleasures comparison at 580d–583a; the true/false pleasure argument (pleasure as filling, and the comparative reality of the fillings) at 583b–588a; the composite image of the soul at 588b–589b.

**Objection.** Two objections dominate. Against the function argument: its machinery equivocates — "function" slides between an organ's characteristic activity and a thing's *point*, and step 3's identification of justice as the soul's excellence is precisely what Thrasymachus denies; Socrates himself ends Book I confessing he has feasted out of order and still does not know what justice is (354a–c), which is Plato's own admission that the argument as stated begs the question. Against Book IX: the comparison of pleasures rigs its tribunal — the philosopher is made judge of pleasures he alone has tasted, but the tyrant might as plausibly claim experiences (of power) the philosopher has never tasted; and premise 6's portrait is of the *failed* tyrant, mad and friendless, whereas Glaucon's challenge specified the *successful* unjust man, cool-headed and complete. Julia Annas's widely shared judgment on the whole reply: it answers the question "why be moral?" by changing morality's meaning to psychic health, which the skeptic about morality may simply decline.

**Reply.** The replies are the dialogue's own architecture. To the first: Book I's argument is *programmatically* provisional — Plato has Glaucon reopen the entire question because the function argument's step 3 was unearned, and Books II–IV earn it by the independent route of the tripartite soul: justice is the excellence of the soul not by stipulation but because it is the soul's performing its function in internal order (Argument 5, step 6). To the second: the literature's defense of the tribunal notes that asymmetry is the argument, not its flaw — the philosopher has deliberately tasted the lower pleasures (they are available to every embodied soul) and rejected them; the tyrant *cannot* have tasted the philosopher's, since tasting them requires the ordered soul he lacks; and the "successful tyrant" is addressed by the claim that tyrannical *erōs* is a structure of appetite that does not admit a cool-headed instance — moderation in the service of a master appetite is, on the psychology of Book IX, already the beginning of its overthrow. Annas's objection stands as the deepest: whether an account of morality as psychic health answers the amoralist, or declines his question, is the *Republic* literature's standing fault line, and the dossier leaves it open.

**Argument 9. The Third Man: Plato's self-objection to the Forms**

*What it proves:* nothing, directly — it is a regress argument against the Theory of Forms, stated by Plato himself in the *Parmenides*, and its importance is diagnostic: it isolates the two assumptions that jointly generate the regress, and thereby defines the space in which every subsequent defense of Forms (and Aristotle's alternative) must operate.

1. **One over many:** whenever a number of things are all large (or beautiful, or equal), there is one Form, Largeness itself, over them all, by virtue of which they are large.
2. **Self-predication:** the Form itself is large — Largeness is not merely the standard of largeness but itself a large thing.
3. From 1 and 2: consider the original large things *together with* Largeness itself. They constitute a new many, all of them large; by 1, there must be a second Form over this expanded group, by virtue of which *it* is large.
4. The second Form is also large (by 2), so the construction repeats: a third Form over the enlarged group, and so on without end.
5. Therefore the theory that was to explain the many by one Form generates infinitely many Forms for each character — and each Form's unity, the theory's central selling point, is destroyed.
The regress needs, besides one-over-many, the **non-identity** assumption: the Form over a group is distinct from (not a member of) every group it ranges over — otherwise step 3's expanded group might be closed by the original Form itself.

**Textual basis.** *Parmenides* 132a–b (stated against the young Socrates, who has just proposed the theory in its *Phaedo*/*Republic* form). Aristotle took the argument over and gave it its name — "the third man" — referring to it at *Metaphysics* A.9, 990b17 (= 1079a13) and *Sophistical Refutations* 178b36. The modern analysis begins with Gregory Vlastos, "The Third Man Argument in the *Parmenides*" (1954; postscript 1963), which first isolated self-predication and non-identity as the regress's hidden premises.

**Objection.** The objection here *is* the argument, so the critical question is aimed at its premises, and both sides of the scholarly dispute deserve naming. On Vlastos's original reading, premises 2 and 3 attribute to Plato assumptions he genuinely held and had not noticed jointly entailed a regress — the *Parmenides* records Plato discovering a real flaw in the middle-dialogue theory. Other readers (the literature following, e.g., Meinwald and Rickless) dispute the attribution: whether Plato's texts actually commit him to self-predication in the regress-generating sense — Largeness being large *in the same way* a large stick is large — or whether the young Socrates' theory, not the mature one, is the target. The argument's validity *given* its premises is not in serious dispute; its premises' ownership is.

**Reply.** "Plato's reply," insofar as one exists, is the dialogue's own framing: Parmenides does not conclude that Forms are nothing — he concludes that the young Socrates, who cannot yet defend them, needs dialectical training (135a–c), and the *Sophist* (Argument 10) revises the theory in directions the regress motivates: Forms are not isolated self-predicating monads but a weaving of kinds, and "participation" for Forms among themselves becomes communion rather than containment. The literature's standard repairs work the premises severally: deny self-predication for the relevant cases (a Form is what largeness *is*, not a large thing — predication here is identity, not instancehood); or restrict one-over-many to non-Form groups; or deny non-identity. The entry's formulation is the dossier's: participation is not ordinary resemblance between two things of the same kind, and the regress targets a naive version of the theory — with the honest addition, which the *Parmenides* itself forces, that Plato nowhere in the surviving corpus states the repair, so its attribution to him is reconstruction, not quotation.

**Argument 10. The *Theaetetus* against "knowledge is perception": the self-refutation and the flux**

*What it proves:* the negative half of Plato's most searching epistemological work: that knowledge cannot be perception, because the Protagorean doctrine that would make it so refutes itself, and the Heraclitean flux that would underwrite it leaves nothing stable enough to be perceived, named, or known. The dialogue ends in *aporia* — all three definitions of knowledge fail — which is itself the datum: on the developmentalist reading, the late Plato is dismantling the easy equations of epistemology before the *Sophist* rebuilds; on either reading, the refutations stand on their own.

1. Theaetetus' first definition (151e): knowledge is perception. Socrates identifies it with Protagoras' doctrine — man is the measure of all things; as each thing appears to me, so it is for me (152a) — and with its metaphysical underpinning, the Heraclitean thesis that all things are in flux (152d–e).
2. **The self-refutation** (*peritropē*, 169d–171d): Protagoras concedes that whatever anyone believes is true for the believer. But most people believe Protagoras' doctrine is *false*. Therefore, by his own doctrine, his doctrine is false for them — and since he grants their belief its truth, he grants his own doctrine false; indeed, if no one believes it, it is true for no one. A doctrine that must count its own rejection as true refutes itself out of its own mouth.
3. **The flux pressed to its limit** (181c–183c): if *everything* is changing in *every* respect at *every* time, then nothing has any stable character even for an instant — including the perception and the words that report it. One cannot correctly say a thing is "thus" rather than "not thus" — the language of the theory dissolves with its objects, and perception becomes as unstatable as knowledge.
4. **The positive residue** (184b–186e): perception through the senses delivers colors, sounds, hardness — but *being*, sameness and difference, likeness and unlikeness, are not delivered by any sense; the soul grasps these "common" terms (*koina*) "by itself," comparing and reasoning over what the senses supply. Knowledge therefore cannot *be* perception, since the judgments in which knowledge lives employ tools perception does not provide.
Therefore, the first definition fails — and with it, any epistemology that makes the senses self-sufficient. (The dialogue continues: knowledge as true belief fails on the jury case — a jury can be persuaded to a true belief about an event only eyewitnesses *know* (200d–201c); knowledge as true belief with a *logos* fails through all three senses of *logos* the discussion can construct (201c–210a). Socrates departs for his indictment, and Theaetetus is left, he says, with better offspring for the next attempt.)

**Textual basis.** *Theaetetus* 151e–186e (the first definition and its refutation); the self-refutation at 169d–171d; the flux at 181c–183c; the *koina* at 184b–186e; the second and third definitions at 187b–210d.

**Objection.** The self-refutation's power is disputed (Myles Burnyeat's "Protagoras and Self-Refutation in Plato's *Theaetetus*," 1976, is the standard careful statement, on Plato's side). A relativist of a sophisticated kind can refuse premise 2's framing: truth-for-a-believer is not a property Protagoras needs to *assert* simpliciter, and his doctrine can be restated as a thesis about the incorrigibility of appearances — what seems to me cannot be false *for me* — which survives the observation that most people reject it: their rejection is true for them, and the doctrine, addressed to each subject severally, never claims a court above the subjects in which it could lose. Critics also note that premise 3 refutes only the *radical* flux — every respect, every instant — which Heraclitus' subtler defenders (and arguably Heraclitus) never held.

**Reply.** The dialogue's reply is premise 4, which does not depend on either refutation: even if relativism and a moderated flux survive as doctrines about *appearance*, knowledge involves the soul's own work over the commons — being, sameness, truth — and no sense organ performs it. On the flux: Socrates' target is the doctrine as Theaetetus' definition needs it — perception always veridical because always of a world with no stability to get wrong — and a moderated flux, with stable kinds within it, is no longer available to underwrite infallible perception; it is, in fact, a step toward the *Sophist*. That the dialogue supplies no replacement definition is the point most often missed by readers who treat it as a failure: the *Theaetetus* clears the ground, and whether Plato thought the clearing was itself the teaching (an elenctic, Socratic reading) or the preface to a constructive account given elsewhere is — once more — the developmentalist/unitarian dispute in miniature.

**Argument 11. The *Sophist*'s communion of kinds: how falsehood and not-being are possible**

*What it proves:* the late dialogues' constructive counterweight to Arguments 9 and 10 — the Eleatic Stranger's solution to Parmenides' ban on speaking of "what is not": false statement is possible because **not-being is not the opposite of being but the *different*** — and the solution costs the middle-dialogue theory of Forms its isolation: the greatest kinds blend, and dialectic is the knowledge of their weaving.

1. Parmenides' prohibition (237a; 258d–e): "what is not" cannot be thought or spoken, for to speak of it is to treat it as something; hence falsehood — saying what is not — seems impossible, and the sophist, who produces false appearances, shelters behind the impossibility.
2. The battle of gods and giants over being (246a–249d): materialists define being as body; the "friends of Forms" define it as changeless intelligible reality. Against both, the Stranger proposes a mark: being is **power** (*dynamis*) — anything that has any capacity to act or be acted upon, in any degree, *is* (247d–e).
3. Against the friends of Forms (248a–249d): if being is wholly changeless, it cannot be *known* — for knowing is an acting-upon or a being-acted-upon — unless knowing and life and soul are excluded from what wholly is, a price the Stranger calls unacceptable: reality must include motion and life and soul and intelligence (248e–249a), and Forms known by us must, *as known*, stand in relation.
4. The communion of kinds (*koinōnia tōn genōn*, 251a–259e): some kinds blend and some do not, as some letters combine and vowels run through all. The five "greatest kinds" — Being, Motion, Rest, Same, Different — interweave: Motion *is* (partakes of Being), is the *same* as itself, is *different* from Rest; and crucially, **Different** runs through every kind, so each kind *is not* (is different from) all the others, while *being* itself.
5. Therefore (259e): the interweaving of forms with one another (*symplokē eidōn*) is the condition of discourse itself; and a statement is false not when it says "what is not" in Parmenides' sense, but when it says of a subject things *different* from the things that are the case about it (263b–d) — Theaetetus sits; "Theaetetus flies" combines a real subject with a kind, Flying, that is different from every kind that holds of him.
Therefore, falsehood is possible, the sophist can be defined (as a maker of false images, 260c–268d), and Parmenides is answered on his own ground — at the stated price: Forms are not sealed monads, and "separation" cannot mean isolation.

**Textual basis.** *Sophist* 236d–264b (the problem of not-being and its solution); the *dynamis* proposal at 247d–e; the critique of the friends of Forms at 248a–249d; the communion at 251a–259e; false statement at 260c–263d; the weaving thesis at 259e.

**Objection.** Two standard objections. (i) The price in step 5 may be the whole theory: if Forms blend and partake of one another, the *Phaedo*'s assurance that each Form is "itself by itself," pure and unmixed (78c–d, 100b), is abandoned, and with it the argument-patterns (Arguments 2–4) that relied on Forms as unqualified standards — the *Sophist* does not reconcile the revision with the earlier dialogues; it simply proceeds. Unitarians and developmentalists divide precisely here: revision of a doctrine versus clarification of what "separation" always meant. (ii) The analysis of falsehood solves the problem for *predicative* statement ("Theaetetus flies") but struggles with false existential and identity statements, and some readers (the debate runs from Frege's appreciative reading onward) judge that "different from what holds" relocates the mystery: which kinds *hold* of a subject is the fact of the matter, and the machinery of kinds names that fact rather than explaining it.

**Reply.** The literature's reply, on Plato's behalf, distinguishes the two problems the dialogue runs together and credits it separately: as an answer to Parmenides — how "what is not" can be *said* without contradiction — the Difference solution is exact and was historically decisive; it is the ancestor of every later logic of negation as exclusion. As a revision of the Forms, the reply concedes the tension and reframes its significance: the communion restricts blending to *kinds* and their participation in the greatest kinds — Motion does not become Rest by both partaking in Being — so Forms retain distinct natures while losing isolation; whether the middle dialogues ever needed isolation, or only distinctness, is the interpretive question the objection and the reply now share. The dossier's closing observation is the Stranger's own method: the argument is delivered not by Socrates but by a visitor from Parmenides' city, treating Plato's own earlier theory as one of the positions to be tested — the corpus arguing with itself, in the open, one last time.
