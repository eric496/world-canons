# Focus Study: Plato (428/427 – 348/347 BCE)

## The figure in brief

Plato was born in Athens in 428/427 BCE, into one of the city's great aristocratic families, and he lived through its catastrophe: the loss of the Peloponnesian War, the bloody oligarchy of the Thirty — in which his own relatives were prominent — the restored democracy's execution of his teacher Socrates in 399 BCE, and the long decline that followed. His response to that wreckage was to invent systematic philosophy. Writing only dialogues, in which he never speaks in his own voice, he took Socrates' street-corner questions — what is justice, what is knowledge, what is courage — and built them into metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, political theory, psychology, and aesthetics, each constraining the others: the Theory of Forms answers Heraclitus' flux with Parmenides' changeless being; the *Republic* makes justice a condition of the soul before it is a rule for conduct, and argues that cities will have no rest from evils until philosophers rule or rulers philosophize (473c–d); the *Symposium* turns desire itself into a ladder from one beautiful body to Beauty itself. Around 387 BCE he founded the Academy, the institution through which philosophy became a discipline with a home, and taught there for forty years; Aristotle was his pupil for twenty of them. Uniquely among ancient thinkers, his work survives whole — the dialogues as arranged by Thrasyllus, plus a disputed body of letters — so we can watch him build a theory in the middle dialogues and attack it himself, harder than any critic has, in the *Parmenides* and the *Sophist*.

## Why he rewards weeks of study

First, *method*: Plato does not merely hold doctrines, he teaches the discipline's characteristic activity — define your term, state the argument, survive the objection, revise — and the early dialogues are still the best training in it ever written, because they show the activity happening between people rather than reporting its results. Second, *position*: every later figure in this library is in conversation with him, whether they know it or not — Aristotle answers him, Augustine Christianizes him, Descartes rebuilds on ground he cleared, and Alfred North Whitehead's famous sentence in *Process and Reality* (1929), that the European philosophical tradition "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato," is an exaggeration with a core of plain fact. Third, *the corpus teaches judgment*: because everything survives, Plato is the one ancient author whose development, self-criticism, and changes of mind can be studied from the texts themselves — and the live scholarly disputes about him (the historical Socrates, the chronology, the unwritten doctrines, the *Republic*'s politics) are arguments conducted over evidence the reader can inspect.

## The study plan at a glance

The library's [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Plato in **Stage 1** (Ancient Foundations), week 3, as the stage's first *core* figure — reached after the Presocratics at map level and Socrates read through Plato, and read in the path's fixed order: *Euthyphro*, *Apology*, *Crito*, *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium* selections, then the *Republic* on the selective route. This dossier keeps that order and deepens it rather than replacing it: the dialogues whole where the path selects, the *Gorgias* and *Theaetetus* added as the plan's extensions, and the *Republic* worked with the library's book-by-book guide. [07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md) lays out a **6-week plan** at the learning path's weekly rhythm — on the order of six weeks of Stage 1 desk study, with the plan file itself carrying the audited hour total, week by week, including the daily card queue and assigned media. It runs inside Stage 1 at the Plato block; Aristotle, whose Stage 1 block follows immediately, reads throughout as Plato's first and greatest respondent, and the dossier is built to hand you over to him.
