# Study Plan — Plato in Six Weeks

## What this plan is, and where it plugs in

The [learning path](../../learning-path.md) meets Plato in **Stage 1 (Ancient Foundations)**, as a core figure (●) at row 3, with Socrates at row 2 read *via* Plato — Socrates exists for us through the dialogues. The path's Plato block, spread across its weeks 2–3, runs *Euthyphro* → *Apology* → *Crito* → *Meno* → *Phaedo* (57a–95b, first pass) → *Symposium* (201d–212c only) → *Republic* I–IV, VI–VII, X on the selective MIT pattern, and its known stall point is the *Republic*'s length: the path warns against pushing through all ten books and arriving at Aristotle exhausted. This dossier keeps the path's order and its editions, and expands the block to six weeks: the *Phaedo* read whole instead of to 95b, the *Symposium* read whole instead of in excerpt, the complete *Republic* across two weeks with [the library's study guide](../../study/plato-republic.md) as the assigned companion, and two extension weeks the path's budget cannot fund — the *Gorgias* and the *Theaetetus*, which Stage 1 otherwise meets only through Adamson's audio.

Run it as one block in place of Stage 1's Plato weeks, before Aristotle (row 4) — the path's sequence is load-bearing here, since Aristotle's criticism of the Forms is Stage 1's second half and reads as a reply only if Plato came first. The dossier's other Stage 1 neighbors stay in their own weeks around it.

**Total cost, stated honestly: ≈34 hours of scheduled study across six weeks (~5.5–6 hrs/week), plus the daily [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) queue (~10 min/day ≈ 7 hrs across the plan) — ≈41 hours all-in.** And the accounting against the path, stated plainly: **these hours sit on top of the learning path's Stage 1 budget, they are not hidden inside it.** The path already spends roughly two of Stage 1's weeks on Plato inside its 60 desk hours; this dossier replaces that selective pass with six full weeks, so count a net addition of roughly 25 hours to Stage 1 (≈85 desk hours for the stage, ≈118 all-in) if you run the dossier as written. The trimmed Stage 1 in the path's §10 is incompatible with this dossier — choose one.

**Reading texts.** Follow the path's rulings exactly. *Five Dialogues* (Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Meno, Phaedo), trans. G. M. A. Grube, ed. John M. Cooper (Hackett, 2nd ed.) — Weeks 1–2. *Republic*, trans. Grube, rev. C. D. C. Reeve (Hackett) — Weeks 3–4. *Gorgias*, *Symposium*, and *Theaetetus* in *Plato: Complete Works*, ed. Cooper (Hackett, 1997) — Gorgias trans. Donald J. Zeyl, Symposium trans. Alexander Nehamas & Paul Woodruff, Theaetetus trans. M. J. Levett, rev. Myles Burnyeat — Weeks 5–6. **Not Jowett** — the path's ruling, and the reason is practical, not snobbish: Victorian renderings in cheap reprints often lack the Stephanus numbers every secondary source cites. Cite everything by Stephanus number (e.g. 514a), never by page; the page targets below are approximate lengths in the named editions, given so you can budget sessions.

**The guide is your companion, by design.** The library's [study guide to the *Republic*](../../study/plato-republic.md) — book-by-book walkthroughs, four reconstructions, eighteen checkpoints with answers, the misreadings — is not duplicated here. Weeks 3–4 assign its book sections, reconstructions, and checkpoint sets by number; the checkpoints printed below are the dossier's own additions, aimed at what the guide leaves to [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md).

## Week 1 — Socrates on trial: *Euthyphro*, *Apology*, *Crito* (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** The three trial dialogues whole, in the path's order, all in *Five Dialogues*. *Euthyphro* (2a–16a, ~20 pp.): the search for a definition of piety that fails five times and poses the dilemma that outlives the failure. *Apology* (17a–42a, ~30 pp.): the defense speech — the oracle, the gadfly, professed ignorance, the refusal to beg or to stop. *Crito* (43a–54e, ~13 pp.): the refusal of escape, argued through the personified Laws. Short texts; read them slowly anyway. This is the elenchus at work before it has a theory attached, and Week 2's doctrines are answers to questions posed here.

**Alongside.** [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md) and [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md) — the life, the trial's Athens, and how to tell the Socrates of these dialogues from Plato's later mouthpiece (the Socrates problem is flagged there and in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md); it is a live scholarly framing, not a settled chronology).

**Media.** *Preview:* *Philosophize This!* #003, "Socrates and the Sophists" (Stephen West, June 2013, ~39 min) — the golden-age Athens setting and the Sophists Socrates argues against, before you open the *Euthyphro*. *Review:* Peter Adamson, [*History of Philosophy Without Any Gaps*](https://historyofphilosophy.net/podcast/classical-greek/socrates-and-plato), ep. 18, "In Dialogue: the Life and Works of Plato," and ep. 16, "Method Man: Plato's Socrates" — the second is on the *Apology* specifically; take it after your own reading. *Optional, watch-after (the path's line, narrowed to this week's texts):* Yale PLSC 114 (Steven B. Smith), lectures 2 ("Socratic Citizenship: Plato, *Apology*") and 3 ("Socratic Citizenship: Plato, *Crito*").

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *State the Euthyphro dilemma, and why it survives the dialogue's failure to define piety.* Is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved (10a)? Either answer costs something: the first makes piety independent of divine will (the gods recognize a standard they do not set), the second makes it arbitrary (anything the gods happened to love would be pious). The target in later dress is divine-command theory. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "The Euthyphro dilemma."
2. *What does Socrates claim to know in the Apology?* That he does not know what he does not know (21d, 23a–b) — human wisdom as knowing one's ignorance. The elenchus is that claim operationalized: examine anyone who claims knowledge and exhibit the gap.
3. *Why does the Crito's Socrates not escape, when escape is arranged and the verdict was unjust?* The Laws' argument (50a–54d): he has agreed, by living seventy years under them, to obey or persuade; disobeying when a verdict goes against him destroys the laws' authority as such. Note the tension with the *Apology*'s promise to disobey an order to stop philosophizing (29c–d) — and hold it; the dossier does not dissolve it for you. [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md).
4. *Whose doctrines are these — Socrates' or Plato's?* The standing scholarly answer for the early dialogues: closest to the historical Socrates we can get, with Plato's own system arriving in the middle dialogues. That developmental picture is widely held and genuinely contested; the deck's Socrates-problem card states it as a framing under dispute, and so should you. [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md).

**Reconstructions.** The Euthyphro dilemma, first blank-page reconstruction (200–400 words); it enters the +2d/+1wk/+3wk rhythm below.

**Cards.** First 6 of [cards.tsv](cards.tsv) (the Euthyphro dilemma; the Laws' argument in the *Crito*; elenchus; Socratic ignorance and virtue as knowledge; the Socrates problem; Form — early, deliberately: you will need the word all six weeks) — after the week's reading, never before.

## Week 2 — Recollection and the death day: *Meno*, the *Phaedo* whole (~6 hrs)

**Primary.** *Meno* (70a–100b, ~45 pp. in *Five Dialogues*): Meno's paradox (80d — how can you search for what you do not know?), the enslaved boy questioned to a geometry result he was never taught (82b–85b), recollection introduced as the answer, and the closing distinction between knowledge and true opinion, tethered like the statues of Daedalus (97a–98a). Then the *Phaedo* **whole** (57a–118a, ~65 pp.) — the path's first pass stops at 95b; the dossier reads through: the cyclical argument (70c–72e), recollection from equals (72e–77a), the affinity argument (78b–84b), Simmias' harmony objection and Cebes' cloak objection (84c–88c, 87a–88c), the reply and the final argument from causation (95b–107b), and the death scene itself (107c–118a). MIT's 24.200 handout on the *Phaedo* — the five arguments, separated — is the path's assigned Session B companion here, and it earns the assignment.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) — Forms, participation, anamnesis, and the doxa/epistēmē distinction in the dossier's own terms before Week 3 builds the city on them.

**Media.** *Watch, after the dialogue* (the path's assignment, mirrored exactly): Shelly Kagan's Yale *Death* course ([PHIL 176](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176), Open Yale Courses, free), **lectures 4, 6, and 7** — Kagan argues with Socrates' immortality proofs line by line, and is the standing model of how to evaluate an argument you admire. ≈2.3 hrs, counted **inside** this week's desk hours, displacing secondary reading, per the path. *Review:* Adamson ep. 21, "We Don't Need No Education: Plato's *Meno*," and ep. 24, "Famous Last Words: Plato's *Phaedo*."

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *What exactly does the slave-boy scene show, and what does it not show?* That a boy with no geometry instruction can be brought, by questions alone, to double the square — presented as answers drawn out, not put in. Not that the questioning was neutral: Socrates draws the figures and orders the steps, which is the standing objection (guided invention vs. recollection) the entry itself raises. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "Recollection."
2. *Run the Phaedo's recollection argument from equals.* We judge sensible equals deficient against Equality itself; that standard was not supplied by the senses; we must therefore have known it before birth; so the soul pre-exists. Then name which premise a modern reader presses first. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "The *Phaedo* immortality arguments."
3. *What is the final argument (105b–107a), and why is it the strongest of the set?* The soul is essentially the bearer of life; what brings life cannot admit death, its opposite, any more than fire admits cold; so the soul is deathless. It is the strongest because it turns on what soul *is* rather than on cycles or resemblances — and the most exposed for the same reason: everything rides on the essentialist premise.
4. *Simmias and Cebes: which objection survives Socrates' reply?* Simmias' soul-as-harmony (85e–88c) makes the soul a byproduct of the body — answered by recollection's priority claim. Cebes' cloak (87a–88c) grants pre-existence but asks why the soul does not wear out over many lives — the objection the final argument exists to meet. Judge whether it meets it; Kagan's lectures are your sparring partner.

**Reconstructions.** Recollection (from the *Meno*); the Phaedo final argument. The Euthyphro dilemma gets its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +7 (recollection — Meno; recollection from equals; the final argument; Simmias and Cebes; anamnesis; participation; doxa vs. epistēmē). Deck at 13.

## Week 3 — The city in speech: *Republic* I–V, with the guide (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** *Republic* Books I–V (327a–480a, ~165 pp. in Grube/Reeve), with [the guide](../../study/plato-republic.md) in the guide's own three-pass rhythm: its book section skimmed before each book, your reconstruction written after each book before you open the guide's version, critique last. Book I's failed definitions and Thrasymachus; Book II's challenge restated at full strength (Gyges, 359c–360d) and the city founded; Books III–IV's education and the four virtues, ending in the tripartite soul (436b–441c) and justice as each part doing its own work (443c–444e); Book V's three waves, the third being philosopher-kings (473c–d).

**The guide.** Its sections on Books I–V; reconstructions **(a)** (the refutation of Thrasymachus) and **(b)** (the tripartite soul and the city–soul analogy) written book-closed before checking; its **checkpoints 1–9 and 14** as this week's extended set, Session C, book-closed.

**Alongside.** [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) (the tripartite soul; the Good, in advance of Book VI) and [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) ("Gyges and the challenge"; "The philosopher-king").

**Media.** *Preview:* BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*, ["Plato's Republic"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b08vwn6h) (first broadcast 29 June 2017; Melvyn Bragg with Angie Hobbs, M. M. McCabe, and James Warren; ~49 min as podcast) — the whole dialogue's question, *is it always better to be just than unjust?*, mapped by three specialists before you start; McCabe is also Week 6's Adamson guest, which is a feature. *Review:* Adamson ep. 25, "Soul and the City: Plato's Political Philosophy." *Optional, watch-after:* PLSC 114 lectures 4 ("Philosophers and Kings: Plato, *Republic*, I–II") and 5 ("…*Republic*, III–IV").

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *What does Gyges' ring actually test?* Not whether injustice pays when detected badly, but whether justice is chosen for itself when every consequence is switched off (359c–360d): invisibility removes reputation, punishment, and reward at one stroke. If your answer appeals to any of those, Glaucon's challenge is still standing. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "Gyges and the challenge."
2. *State the principle of opposites and what it licenses (436b, 439a–d).* The same thing cannot do or suffer opposites at the same time, in the same respect, toward the same object. Thirst urges drink while something forbids it: opposed pulls toward one object cannot issue from one undifferentiated source — so appetite and reason are distinct. The argument proves functional sources of motivation; whether they are literal *parts* is the debate, and the guide's reconstruction (b) objection keeps it open.
3. *Justice is "each doing its own work" — in the city and in the soul, or in the soul by analogy with the city?* Keep the direction of the argument straight: the city is the heuristic, "larger letters" (368c–369a); the conclusion the dialogue needs is about the soul, and the analogy is its instrument, not its evidence. (Guide, checkpoint 6; misreading 1.)
4. *Why must the philosopher be compelled to rule (519c–521b — read ahead in Book VII if needed)?* Because having seen the Good, contemplation beats governing on the philosopher's own scale of goods; reluctance is the qualification, and ruling is a debt to the city that educated them. A ruler who wants the job has, on this theory, already failed an entrance requirement. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "The philosopher-king."

**Reconstructions.** Gyges and the city–soul argument (guide (b) is the check, not the source); recollection gets its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +7 (Gyges; the tripartite division; justice as internal order; philosopher-king; the three waves; the noble lie; the function argument). Deck at 20.

## Week 4 — The Good, the Cave, the fall: *Republic* VI–X, with the guide (~6 hrs)

**Primary.** *Republic* Books VI–X (484a–621d, ~150 pp.), same guide rhythm. Book VI: the philosopher's nature, the Ship of State, the Sun (506d–509c), the Divided Line (509d–511e). **Then stop: read Simon Blackburn's *Plato's Republic: A Biography* now** — after Book VII per the path's ruling, never before; it is short (~180 pp., an evening or two inside Session B across this week and next) and lands hardest with the Cave fresh. Book VII: the Cave (514a–521b) and the curriculum. Books VIII–IX: the decline of regimes and the tyrant's misery — the dialogue's answer to Glaucon completed. Book X: imitation twice removed (595a–602b), the ban and its qualification, the Myth of Er.

**The guide.** Its sections on Books VI–X; reconstructions **(c)** (the Good: Sun, Line, Cave) and **(d)** (poetry and imitation) written before checking; its **checkpoints 10–13 and 15–18** as the extended set.

**Alongside.** [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md) — "The Cave"; "The Third Man" (read it this week: the *Parmenides* is not assigned as primary text, but the regress is the dossier's capstone material and belongs with the Forms at their fullest statement); and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) on Popper, before Blackburn's own political verdicts reach you unframed.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 26, "Ain't No Sunshine: the Cave Allegory of Plato's *Republic*," after Book VII; ep. 33, "Last Judgments: Plato, Poetry and Myth," after Book X. *Review, whole-figure:* *Philosophize This!* #004, "Plato" (1 July 2013, 45 min) — West's single-episode Plato (Symposium, Forms, Cave, politics); test its compressions against four weeks of text. *Optional, watch-after:* PLSC 114 lecture 6 ("Philosophers and Kings: Plato, *Republic*, V") — Smith's *Republic* run ends at Book V, so his politics close pairs with this week's decline books as commentary, not coverage.

**Checkpoints (the dossier's own).**
1. *The Sun analogy explains the Good by refusing to?* Socrates declines to state what the Good is (506d–e) and gives its "offspring" instead: as the sun makes visible things visible and gives them growth, the Good makes intelligible things intelligible and gives them being. What the analogy supplies is the Good's *structural role* — condition of knowledge and of being — and the guide's reconstruction (c) objection (metaphor doing metaphysics' work) is the right pressure to apply next. (Guide, checkpoint 11.)
2. *Name the Line's four segments and their objects, from memory (509d–511e).* Images (imagination, *eikasia*); physical things (belief, *pistis*); mathematical objects reasoned from hypotheses (thought, *dianoia*); Forms grasped by dialectic (understanding, *noēsis*). The Line is the Cave drawn as a diagram — same ascent, two notations. [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md).
3. *Education as turning, not pouring (518b–d) — what follows for teaching?* If knowledge cannot be inserted into a soul like sight into blind eyes, teaching is reorientation of a capacity already present: the whole soul turned from becoming toward being. State one practice this rules out and one it rules in, in your own study habits first.
4. *Why is the tyrant the dialogue's final exhibit (571a–588a)?* Book IX closes Glaucon's case on his own terms: the perfectly unjust man, outwardly all-powerful, is inwardly ruled by lawless appetite — the most miserable of men (587b–588a). If the answer to "why be just?" is psychic health, the tyrant is its negative proof. (Guide, checkpoint 16.)

**Reconstructions.** The Cave as epistemology (guide (c) is the check); the philosopher-king. The Euthyphro dilemma gets its +3-week touch — notice it is now a tool you reach for, not a text you recall.

**Cards.** +6 (the Cave; the Good; the Divided Line; mimesis, twice removed; the Third Man; the Myth of Er). Deck at 26.

## Week 5 — Rhetoric and desire: *Gorgias* selections, the *Symposium* whole (~5.5 hrs)

**Primary.** *Gorgias* in selection (Complete Works, Zeyl): 447a–481b whole — rhetoric defined and dismantled with Gorgias (449a–461b: rhetoric as a knack of flattery, the pastry-baking of the soul, 462b–466a), then Polus (466a–481b): power, and doing injustice worse than suffering it (469b–479e). Callicles (481b–522e) starts this week — read 481b–499b, his praise of natural justice and the refutation of hedonism; the conclusion (499b–522e) and the closing myth (523a–527e) finish in Week 6's primary session, deliberately: the myth of judgment reads differently after the *Theaetetus*. Then the *Symposium* **whole** (172a–223d, ~55 pp.) — the path's excerpt was Diotima alone (201d–212c); the dossier restores the speeches she answers: Phaedrus, Pausanias, Eryximachus, Aristophanes' myth of the halved humans (189c–193d), Agathon, and Alcibiades' entrance (212c–223d), without which Diotima's ladder (210a–212a) has no dramatic weight.

**Alongside.** [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) — Plato against the Sophists, the sideways conversation this week's first text stages, with Gorgias himself pressed there on whether rhetoric is a craft of truth or a knack of flattery. The flattery argument gets no numbered reconstruction in [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md); your own Session C reconstruction (below) is the treatment, built from the text before you check it against [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md) on erōs for the week's second half.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 20, "Virtue Meets its Match: Plato's *Gorgias*," and ep. 31, "Wings of Desire: Plato's Erotic Dialogues" (Symposium, Phaedrus, Lysis). *Optional:* ep. 32, "Frisbee Sheffield on Platonic Love" (~25 min) — the interview to take if Nussbaum's objection (below) bites, since Sheffield's *Plato's Symposium: The Ethics of Desire* is the standard modern answer to it.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Rhetoric as flattery: what is the argument's machinery (462b–466a)?* Every genuine craft aims at its object's good and can give an account; flattery guesses at pleasure without knowledge of the good. Rhetoric : justice :: pastry-baking : medicine — it produces the appearance of health in the soul as cookery produces the taste of food in the body. If the analogy fails, where — in the craft/flattery distinction, or in rhetoric's placement?
2. *Doing injustice is worse than suffering it (469b–479e): what carries the proof?* The Polus exchange turns on shame and on punishment as medicine for the soul: the unjust man who escapes punishment is like a patient refusing the cure. Name the premise a modern reader refuses first — most refuse that punishment benefits the punished — and see what remains standing.
3. *Climb Diotima's ladder in order (210a–212a).* One beautiful body; beauty in all bodies; beauty of souls; of laws and practices; of knowledge; then Beauty itself. At each rung, what is the lover asked to give up, and what — on Nussbaum's objection, recorded in the entry — is lost when the individual beloved becomes a rung? [03-system-and-concepts.md](03-system-and-concepts.md), on erōs.
4. *Why does Alcibiades speak last (212c–223d)?* The dialogue's own counterexample-in-person: eros aimed at Socrates, who embodies the ascent and refuses the exchange Alcibiades offers. Doctrine, then drama's verdict on doctrine.

**Reconstructions.** The *Gorgias* flattery argument; recollection gets its +3-week touch; the city–soul argument gets its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +4 (rhetoric as flattery; doing vs. suffering injustice; erōs and the ladder; dialectic). Deck at 30.

## Week 6 — What is knowledge? The *Theaetetus* selections, and the capstone (~5.5 hrs + essay)

**Primary.** Finish the *Gorgias* (499b–527e: Callicles answered, the true rhetoric sketched, the myth of judgment, 523a–527e). Then the *Theaetetus* in selection (Complete Works, Levett/Burnyeat): the prologue and Socrates as midwife (142a–151d); knowledge as perception, with Protagoras' measure-doctrine and the flux theory it rides on (151e–186e — read 184b–186e, the refutation's core, slowest); knowledge as true belief, with the wax tablet (191c–196c) and the aviary (197a–200c) as models of error; and the final definition — true belief with an account (*logos*) — and its failure (201c–210b). The dialogue ends in aporia (210b–d): knowledge undefined, the midwife's patient delivered of wind-eggs. That ending is the content: Stage 1's knowledge thread closes with the question open, exactly where Stage 3's Descartes and Hume will find it.

**Alongside.** [05-conversations.md](05-conversations.md) and [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) in full — the reception (Aristotle's answer first of all), the developmentalism dispute, and the misconceptions, as capstone raw material. [08-bibliography.md](08-bibliography.md) for what to read next, and when.

**Media.** *Review:* Adamson ep. 22, "I Know, Because the Caged Bird Sings: Plato's *Theaetetus*" (the wax tablet and aviary, in Adamson's telling), and ep. 23, "M. M. McCabe on Knowledge in Plato." *Capstone support:* ep. 27, "Second Thoughts: Plato's *Parmenides* and the Forms" — the Third Man in twenty minutes, before you write about it.

**Checkpoints (book closed).**
1. *Why does perception fail as knowledge (184b–186e)?* The senses deliver colors, sounds, hardness — but being, sameness, likeness, and number are grasped by the soul itself, through no organ; knowledge lies in reasoning about what is given, not in the giving. Perception is not false; it is not yet judgment.
2. *What do the wax tablet and the aviary each model, and what error can neither model?* The tablet (191c–196c): false judgment as mismatching a present perception to the wrong memory-imprint. The aviary (197a–200c): possessing knowledge vs. having it in hand — the bird caught wrongly. Neither models error in pure thought, where no perception is being matched at all — the gap the *logos* definition then fails to close.
3. *State the Third Man regress in three sentences.* A particular and its Form are both large; by the reasoning that introduced the Form (likeness needs a Form over both), a further Form is required over particular-plus-Form; and so on without end. Then give the standard reply — participation is not ordinary resemblance between two things of a kind, and the regress needs both self-predication and non-identity, of which Plato can qualify one — and say which premise *you* would surrender. [04-arguments.md](04-arguments.md), "The Third Man."
4. *The dialogue on knowledge ends knowing nothing. What has it established?* Three definitions refuted, two models of error built and broken, and the measure-doctrine's self-refutation (171a–c: Protagoras' doctrine, believed false by everyone else, is by its own lights false for them — and he must concede it). Aporia as survey: you now know the shape any answer must fit. Compare the *Euthyphro*'s ending — the dossier's first week — and say what six weeks have changed about the failure.

**Reconstructions.** The *Theaetetus* argument (three definitions, three refutations); the city–soul argument gets its +3-week touch; the Cave gets its +1-week touch.

**Cards.** +4 (the *Theaetetus*: three definitions; separation — and Aristotle's objection in seed; developmentalism vs. unitarianism, *flagged contested*; the Forms in the late dialogues, *flagged contested*). Deck complete at 34, and permanent in the FSRS queue.

## Capstone essay (800–1,200 words)

Write in Week 6, after the comparative checkpoints, book closed for the first draft. **The gate relationship, stated exactly:** Stage 1's gate essay is *"Is virtue knowledge, habit, or neither?"* — Plato/Socrates against Aristotle, with the Stoics or Epicurus as third voice. **Prompt 1 below is built to serve as the Plato half of that gate essay**: write it here at dossier length, and when the stage gate comes due (after the Aristotle and Hellenistic weeks), expand it with Aristotle's doctrine of habituation as the rival answer rather than starting cold. The other prompts stand alone as dossier capstones.

1. **Virtue is knowledge — Plato's case, at full strength (the gate-essay prompt).** The *Apology*'s examined life, the *Meno*'s tethered true opinion, the *Gorgias*' refusal to separate knowing the good from doing it: reconstruct the Socratic-Platonic claim that virtue is knowledge and no one does wrong willingly. Then press it with the phenomenon it seems unable to digest — the person who knows better and does worse — and say whether Plato's psychology (tripartite soul, Week 3) answers the phenomenon or concedes it by dividing the knower.
2. **The city–soul analogy: proof, or picture?** The *Republic*'s central inference runs from a city's structure to a soul's. Reconstruct the inference at its strongest (the principle of opposites does independent work — use it), then judge: does the analogy *evidence* the tripartite soul, or illustrate a conclusion reached elsewhere? Close with what survives of the definition of justice (443c–444e) on each verdict.
3. **Plato against Plato: the Third Man.** The *Parmenides* presses the regress against the theory the *Phaedo* and *Republic* built. Reconstruct the regress (Adamson ep. 27 as your check), give the standard reply's two pressure points (self-predication, non-identity), and take a position on what Plato is doing: refuting his own theory, refining it, or exercising the reader. The developmentalist and unitarian framings of that question are contested in the scholarship — say which you are assuming, and flag it as an assumption.
4. **Education as turning.** The Cave claims education reorients a whole soul rather than stocking an empty one (518b–d). Develop the claim from the Line and the Cave together; apply it to one modern information environment of your choosing — name its shadows, its chains, and its painful turning; then give the objection the library entry's study questions pose: that the "freed" prisoner may only have exchanged one cave for another. Answer it, or concede it and restate what the allegory still shows.
5. **Does the ladder keep the beloved?** Diotima's ascent (210a–212a) moves from one body to Beauty itself. Nussbaum's charge: the individual beloved is used as a rung and discarded. Sheffield's reply (Adamson ep. 32): erōs aims at flourishing, and the ascent enlarges rather than abandons its objects. Judge the dispute from the text — including Alcibiades, who arrives immediately after the ladder is described, as if the dialogue itself were filing the objection.

## Mastery checklist — you are done when you can…

- □ State the Euthyphro dilemma and why it still matters for divine-command theory. *(The path's Stage 1 exit checkpoint, verbatim.)*
- □ Reconstruct Meno's paradox and the recollection answer, and say what the slave-boy scene does not prove.
- □ Lay out the *Phaedo*'s immortality arguments in order, give Simmias' and Cebes' objections, and say which argument the final one is and why it is the strongest — and the most exposed.
- □ Reconstruct the Allegory of the Cave *as an argument* (not a story summary), plus one objection to it. *(The path's Stage 1 exit checkpoint, verbatim.)*
- □ Run Gyges' ring as a controlled experiment: which variables it removes, and what would count as passing the test.
- □ Derive the tripartite soul from the principle of opposites and the thirst case, and state the exact point where the city–soul analogy stops being evidence.
- □ State the three waves, name the greatest, and explain why the philosopher must be *compelled* to rule.
- □ Give the four segments of the Divided Line with their objects, and the Sun's structural claim about the Good, without notes.
- □ State the Third Man regress, the two assumptions it needs, and the reply that surrenders one of them — flagging the reading you assume as contested where it is.
- □ Reconstruct the *Gorgias*' flattery argument and the doing-vs.-suffering-injustice proof, naming the premise each stands on.
- □ Climb Diotima's ladder rung by rung and state Nussbaum's objection and Sheffield's reply.
- □ Show why the *Theaetetus*' three definitions fail, and what its aporetic ending establishes — then contrast Plato with Aristotle on forms and universals in five sentences, which is Stage 1's exit checkpoint and next week's work. *(Bridge to row 4.)*
