# Life and Times: Athens, Socrates, and the Founding of the Academy

Plato's biography is the opposite problem from his philosophy: the works survive entire, and the life survives in scraps. No contemporary wrote his life. Our fullest ancient source, Diogenes Laertius' *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers* (Book III), was composed in the third century CE — some six centuries after Plato's death — and mixes documentary material with anecdote, wonder-stories, and the gossip of intervening schools. The *Seventh Letter*, if genuine, is Plato's own account of his political hopes and Sicilian failures; its authenticity is disputed, and this file cites it as what it is — the principal source, under a standing question mark. Between those poles lie Plato's own dialogues (which name his relatives freely and himself almost never), Aristotle's testimony, and the histories of Thucydides and Xenophon for the world around him. The dossier's rule is the archive's rule: the narrative below distinguishes, at each step, between what is recorded, what is reported, and what is legend. The legends are included — they shaped how antiquity saw him — and labeled.

## An aristocrat's childhood in a city at war

Plato was born in Athens in 428/427 BCE (the conventional date; the ancient chronographers' figures allow a year or two of play, and some modern scholars argue for 424/423). He was born into the heart of the Athenian aristocracy, during the Peloponnesian War that Athens was slowly losing to Sparta. His father, Ariston, of the deme Collytus, was said — by a tradition Diogenes Laertius reports and modern scholars treat cautiously — to trace descent from Codrus, the legendary last king of Athens. His mother, Perictione, daughter of Glaucon, came from a family connected to Solon, the great lawgiver of the sixth century: Plato himself reports the kinship in the *Timaeus* (20e), where Dropides, an ancestor of Perictione, appears as a relative and friend of Solon. Tradition says the boy's birth name was Aristocles — his grandfather's name — and that "Plato" was a nickname from *platys*, "broad": broad shoulders, says one version, from his wrestling years; a broad forehead, says another. The story is late tradition, repeated by Diogenes Laertius and others, and it is uncertain; the dossier keeps the library entry's caution and prints it as such.

The family was large and distinguished. Plato had two older brothers, Glaucon and Adeimantus — the two who press Socrates hardest in the *Republic*, and who had already distinguished themselves, Plato tells us, at the battle of Megara when he was still small — and a sister, Potone, whose son Speusippus would succeed Plato as head of the Academy. Ariston died while Plato was still a child, and Perictione remarried: her second husband was her own uncle Pyrilampes, an associate of Pericles and several times Athens' ambassador to the Persian king, so Plato grew up in a household joined to the Periclean democracy as well as to the old aristocracy, with a half-brother, Antiphon, who appears in the *Parmenides* as the man who gave up philosophy for horses. His education would have been the standard elite curriculum of letters, music, and gymnastics; a later tradition that he studied painting and wrote poetry and tragedies before Socrates is just that — tradition, unverifiable, though the dialogues' literary power makes it easy to believe he had the gifts.

## The family's shadow: the Thirty

The defining political fact of Plato's youth came in 404 BCE, when Sparta's victory ended the war and Athens was handed to the Thirty, an oligarchic junta charged with rewriting the constitution, which ruled by proscription and execution for some eight months. Perictione's younger brother Charmides was one of the Thirty's governing circle (he held office among the Ten who governed the Piraeus), and her first cousin Critias — poet, sophist, and the most intellectually formidable of the group — was the junta's leading spirit. Both were Plato's close kin, and both died in 403 BCE fighting the democratic resistance at Munychia, when the Thirty fell.

The *Seventh Letter* (324b–325a) says that the young Plato, connected to the regime by family and expecting a share in public life, watched the Thirty make the preceding democracy look golden by comparison — they tried to implicate Socrates in an arrest, and Socrates refused — and turned away in disgust. The letter's authenticity is disputed (see [02-works-and-texts.md](02-works-and-texts.md)), but the facts it frames are not: Plato's family stood on the losing, tyrannical side of Athens' civil trauma, and the democracy that replaced the Thirty would, within four years, execute his teacher. Any account of Plato's lifelong hostility to existing regimes, democratic and oligarchic alike, starts from that double disillusion. He puts Critias and Charmides in his dialogues often, and handsomely — the *Charmides* is named for his uncle — without ever mentioning the Thirty.

## Socrates

Plato met Socrates and became his devoted follower; the meeting's date is not recorded — the later tradition (Diogenes Laertius) has Plato come to him at about twenty, after earlier instruction from Cratylus, a Heraclitean, and the story that Socrates dreamed of a swan on his breast the night before Plato was brought to him is an omen-story of exactly the kind this file labels legend. What is not legend is the fact of the discipleship: Plato belonged to Socrates' circle through the last years of the century, and the dialogues are unintelligible except as its fruit.

In 399 BCE Athens tried Socrates on charges of impiety and corrupting the young, convicted him, and executed him with hemlock. Plato was twenty-eight or twenty-nine. Plato himself tells us where he stood: the *Apology* (33e–34a) names Plato, son of Ariston, as present in the courtroom, one of those who offered surety for a fine. The *Phaedo*, the dialogue of Socrates' last day, gives the opposite datum with famous economy: listing the friends present in the cell, Phaedo adds that "Plato, I believe, was ill" (59b) — absent from the deathbed he would immortalize. The illness is the record Plato himself chose to leave; readers have speculated about it for two millennia, and the honest dossier leaves it where he left it.

The execution completed what the Thirty had begun. In the *Seventh Letter*'s retrospective framing (325b–c), the restored democracy, which Plato had expected to govern justly, put to death "the justest man of his time" — and Plato concluded that all existing cities are misgoverned and incurable short of a transformation in who holds power. Whether or not the letter is his, that is the argument the *Republic* would spend ten books making.

## The wandering years and the first Sicilian voyage

After Socrates' death, the tradition (Diogenes Laertius again) has Plato withdraw with other Socratics to Euclides at Megara, a day's journey west — the friends dispersing, plausibly, until feeling in Athens cooled. What followed, in the ancient accounts, was travel: to Cyrene to hear the mathematician Theodorus; to Egypt, to learn from priests; to Italy, to the Pythagoreans. The dossier reports these as the tradition gives them — Egypt especially became a set-piece of ancient biography for every sage — and notes that the dialogues give no confirmation beyond showing real knowledge of Pythagorean mathematics and Italian affairs. Somewhere in these years, most likely, Plato began writing: the short, Socratic dialogues that modern stylometry groups as early are usually placed in the decade after 399.

The consequential journey is documented better, by the *Seventh Letter* (326b–328d) and by Plutarch's *Life of Dion*. Around 388/387 BCE Plato traveled to Sicily, then the richest Greek world in the west, and to the court of Dionysius I, tyrant of Syracuse. There he formed the friendship that governed his Sicilian hopes: Dion, the tyrant's brother-in-law, a young man of wealth and standing who took Plato's teaching — that a ruler's life should be ordered by reason rather than appetite — as a personal program. The teaching did not take with Dionysius. A famous anecdote in the later sources has the tyrant ask Plato what he thought of tyrants and receive a bleak reply, and has Plato then sold into slavery on Aegina — Athens' enemy — and ransomed by Anniceris of Cyrene. The story appears in Diogenes Laertius and other late sources and is not corroborated by the *Seventh Letter*; scholars have doubted it for centuries, not least because it fits too neatly the pattern of the philosopher persecuted. It is printed here as tradition, not record. What is firmer is the outcome: Plato returned to Athens, and Dion remained his devoted correspondent and his doorway back to Syracuse.

## The Academy

Around 387 BCE — the date is conventional, inferred from the Sicilian return and the years that follow — Plato founded the school that defines his middle life. Its site was a grove sacred to the hero Academus, just northwest of Athens' walls, with a gymnasium; Plato owned property nearby. The Academy was not a university in the modern sense — no degrees, no curriculum fixed by statute — but a community of inquiry that endured, in changing forms, for centuries: members lived a shared intellectual life, and mathematics stood at its head. The inscription tradition loves to quote — "let no one ignorant of geometry enter" — appears only in late commentators, centuries on, and belongs to the legend file. What is documented is the work: the school attracted students in philosophy, mathematics, and astronomy from across the Greek world, and produced, among others, the mathematician Eudoxus of Cnidus, whose astronomy the school's questions drove. Speusippus, Plato's nephew, and Xenocrates followed Plato as its heads; the institution outlasted them all, surviving until the Roman sack of Athens and, in revived forms, far beyond.

In the Academy's first two decades Plato wrote the dialogues on which his fame rests: after the early Socratic pieces, the great constructive works — *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, and the *Republic* itself, conventionally dated to around 375 BCE. The school gave him what Socrates never had: a place to think in decades rather than in afternoons, and students who were themselves philosophers.

## Syracuse again: the second and third voyages

Dionysius I died in 367 BCE, and Dion — now uncle and closest counselor to the young Dionysius II — persuaded Plato to return and educate the new tyrant in philosophy: the *Republic*'s program, attempted on a real throne. Plato went, though the *Seventh Letter* (328c–330b) presents him as going reluctantly, out of obligation to Dion and shame at being a man of words only. The experiment failed in the way courts make such experiments fail. Dionysius took up philosophy as a court fashion and would not submit to its discipline; courtiers who feared Dion's influence worked on the tyrant's suspicion; within months Dion was exiled on a charge of conspiracy (366 BCE), and Plato, kept at Syracuse in a kind of honorary detention in the citadel, was eventually allowed to sail home in 365, with Dionysius' promise — extracted and later repeated — that both he and Dion would be recalled.

They were not, for six years. In 361 BCE Dionysius renewed the invitation, pledging that Dion's case would be heard if Plato came, and Plato went a third time — the letter (339a–341b) says, against his judgment, because refusing again would betray Dion. The pledge was broken on arrival: Dion's property was confiscated rather than restored, his friends were watched, and Plato found himself effectively a prisoner in the palace precinct while soldiers' mutinies and court factions closed around him. He got out in 360 BCE through the intervention of Archytas of Tarentum, the Pythagorean statesman-philosopher, who sent a ship and an embassy for him. Plato and Dion met at Olympia that summer; Dion thereafter resolved on force, and in 357 BCE he sailed with a small company and took Syracuse — a vindication that curdled within three years into Dion's assassination (354 BCE) by Callippus, a man who had come to Sicily in Dion's own expedition from Plato's own circle. The *Seventh Letter*, if it is Plato's, is the long self-defense of a man who had twice been made a fool by a tyrant and had lost his best political hope to murder. Even readers who doubt its authorship treat its author as well informed; the dossier uses it flagged, and no Sicilian date in this file depends on it alone where the tradition offers firmer ground.

## Aristotle, and the last years

In 367 BCE, the year of the second voyage, a seventeen-year-old from Stagira in Macedon joined the Academy: Aristotle. He stayed twenty years — as student, then as teacher and researcher in his own right — until Plato's death dissolved the bond. The relationship matters to this dossier beyond biography: Aristotle is Plato's first reader, his most exact reporter (our chief source for the "unwritten doctrines" Plato apparently taught orally), and his greatest critic, the founder of a rival school in critical dialogue with everything the Academy taught. The Stage 1 block that follows Plato in the learning path is Aristotle, and the conversations are continuous.

Plato's late work changed character. The dialogues of the final group — *Theaetetus*, *Parmenides*, *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Critias* — are more technical, more self-critical, and less Socratic: Socrates recedes or falls silent, and strangers and elders lead. Last of all came the *Laws*: twelve books of detailed legislation for a second-best city, Magnesia, ruled not by philosopher-kings but by law itself — the *Republic*'s concession, or correction. Plato died with the *Laws* unfinished. Tradition (Diogenes Laertius 3.37) reports that it was left written on wax tablets and that Philippus of Opus transcribed and edited it, dividing it into books; the *Epinomis*, transmitted as its appendix, is often assigned to Philippus as well. Plato died in Athens in 348/347 BCE, in his eighty-first year by the conventional dates; the story that he died at a wedding feast comes from Hermippus, an early but anecdotal source, and is flagged accordingly. He was buried at the Academy. His will — an unusually concrete survival, preserved by Diogenes Laertius — leaves property, frees a slave, and names executors; the nephew Speusippus succeeded him as head of the school, which did not die with him.
