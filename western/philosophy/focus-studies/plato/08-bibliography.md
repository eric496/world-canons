# Bibliography — Plato

Annotated and tiered: read downward only as far as your questions require. Prices are the usual paperback ranges; the primary texts are *owned*, per the learning path's rule, because library lending windows are shorter than this plan's pace. One fact governs the whole list, and it is the opposite of the Wittgenstein problem: Plato's works survive **complete** — the dialogues as arranged in antiquity by Thrasyllus, plus letters of disputed authenticity (the *Seventh Letter*, widely but not universally accepted, is the consequential one). The scholarly problems here are chronology and interpretation, not survival.

## 1. Primary texts — the editions the plan uses

***Five Dialogues: Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Meno, Phaedo*, trans. G. M. A. Grube, ed. John M. Cooper (Hackett, 2nd ed., 2002).** The learning path's ruling and this dossier's for Weeks 1–2: clear modern English, Stephanus numbers in the margins, introductions pitched at exactly this plan's reader. Grube's Plato is the standard American classroom text for a reason — it disappears under the argument.

***Republic*, trans. G. M. A. Grube, rev. C. D. C. Reeve (Hackett, 1992).** The path's ruling for Weeks 3–4, and the translation [the library's study guide](../../study/plato-republic.md) quotes. Allan Bloom's translation (Basic Books) is the respected literal alternative the guide names, useful for comparing disputed passages; it is a comparison copy, not the working text. **Avoid Jowett** (1871) as a first read — Victorian, flowery, and in cheap reprints often without Stephanus numbers; the entry quotes him once, flagged as the public-domain exception that proves the rule.

***Plato: Complete Works*, ed. John M. Cooper (Hackett, 1997).** The one-volume Plato: every dialogue in modern translations by named specialists, with Stephanus numbers throughout. The plan draws Weeks 5–6 from it — *Gorgias* (trans. Donald J. Zeyl), *Symposium* (trans. Alexander Nehamas & Paul Woodruff), *Theaetetus* (trans. M. J. Levett, rev. Myles Burnyeat) — and it puts the unassigned dialogues (*Phaedrus*, *Parmenides*, *Timaeus*) on the same shelf. If you buy one Plato book beyond the two above, it is this one.

**For Greek readers: the Oxford Classical Texts.** *Platonis Opera*, vol. I (Clarendon Press, 1995), ed. E. A. Duke, W. F. Hicken, W. S. M. Nicoll, D. B. Robinson & J. C. G. Strachan — Tetralogies I–II (*Euthyphro*, *Apology*, *Crito*, *Phaedo*, *Cratylus*, *Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Statesman*) — is the modern critical text, replacing Burnet's 1900 volume for those dialogues; the remaining tetralogies still stand in Burnet's (1901–1907). Nothing in this plan requires it.

## 2. First secondary books, in order

1. **Simon Blackburn, *Plato's Republic: A Biography* (Atlantic Books, 2006; Books That Changed the World series).** First because the path assigns it first, with a timing rule: **after *Republic* Book VII, never before** ([07-study-plan.md](07-study-plan.md), Week 4). Short (~180 pp.), argumentative, and written by a distinguished philosopher who is not a Platonist — its chapters on the Cave's afterlife and the *Republic*'s political legacy give [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md) a named opponent to argue with. His politics are on the page and dated in places; read him as a strong reader, not a referee.
2. **Julia Annas, *An Introduction to Plato's Republic* (Clarendon Press, 1981).** The standard scholarly introduction to the dialogue, chapter by chapter: patient on the city–soul analogy, exact on the metaphysics of Books V–VII, and honest about where the arguments strain. The book to open when Week 3–4's reconstructions and the guide's versions disagree with yours and you need a third, weightier voice. (The BBC's *In Our Time* reading list cites the 1998 Oxford reprint; it is the same text.)
3. **C. D. C. Reeve, *Philosopher-Kings: The Argument of Plato's Republic* (Princeton University Press, 1988).** The deepest single-book reconstruction of the *Republic*'s central argument — that knowledge of the Good qualifies and obliges rule — by the scholar who revised your translation. Read third, after Blackburn's provocations and Annas' map, when the question has narrowed to whether the philosopher-king argument can be made valid, and at what price in premises.

## 3. Advanced and specialist

**Gregory Vlastos, *Platonic Studies* (Princeton University Press, 1973; 2nd ed. 1981) — flagged as classic and contested.** The essays that set the analytic agenda for Plato scholarship — on the Third Man, on Socrates, on the degrees of reality — written with unmatched logical precision. The flag: Vlastos's Socrates is a construction of the developmentalist reading, his "degrees of being" reading of the Forms has been pressed hard by the next entry on this list, and several essays' conclusions are now minority positions. Read him as the strongest statement of a research program, not as its settled result — his Third Man analysis included, before Week 6's capstone.

**Gail Fine, *On Ideas: Aristotle's Criticism of Plato's Theory of Forms* (Clarendon Press, 1993).** The rigorous modern treatment of the Forms under attack: what Aristotle's criticisms actually were, what the theory they aimed at actually claimed (self-predication, separation, the regresses), and how much of Plato survives his best student. The natural post-dossier book — it is Stage 1's Plato–Aristotle contrast written at full scholarly depth, and Week 6's Third Man checkpoint is its doorway.

***The Cambridge Companion to Plato*, ed. Richard Kraut (Cambridge University Press, 1992).** The one-volume state of the question by topic — the Socratic dialogues, the middle period's metaphysics and ethics, the late dialogues, Plato on art and on women — by the editor who also wrote the SEP article below. For the debate you meet in the capstone and want in its current form. (A companion to the single work also exists: *The Cambridge Companion to Plato's Republic*, ed. G. R. F. Ferrari, 2007 — the next shelf over, when Weeks 3–4 generate a question Annas does not settle.)

## 4. Reference

- **[SEP, "Plato"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato/)** — by Richard Kraut (Northwestern); first published 2004 and the current scholarly map of the life, the dialogues' chronology, and the middle period's metaphysics and ethics. The dossier's first resort, per the learning path's SEP rule (§1 and introduction before the primary text, the rest after). SEP's satellite entries — notably "Plato's *Theaetetus*" and the articles on Plato's ethics and political philosophy — are the deepening moves for Weeks 5–6.
- **[IEP, "Plato"](https://iep.utm.edu/plato/)** — the plainer first pass, per the path's IEP-before rule: biography, influences (Heraclitus, Parmenides, the Pythagoreans, Socrates), and a period-by-period walk through the dialogues with the authenticity and dating controversies stated rather than smoothed. The better first read before Week 1 if the SEP's density daunts.
- **[PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/)** — the "Plato" category and its subcategories are the working bibliography of the live literature (the Third Man industry alone runs to decades); use after the SEP article, not before.
- **Kenny, *A New History of Western Philosophy*, Vol. 1** — the learning path's history spine for Stage 1, alongside throughout; its thematic chapters (knowledge, ethics, God) are the spiral in print.

McCabe on Knowledge in Plato"; ep. 24, "Famous Last Words: Plato's *Phaedo*"; ep. 25, "Soul and the City: Plato's Political Philosophy"; ep. 26, "Ain't No Sunshine: the Cave Allegory of Plato's *Republic*"; ep. 27, "Second Thoughts: Plato's *Parmenides* and the Forms"; ep. 31, "Wings of Desire: Plato's Erotic Dialogues"; ep. 32, "Frisbee Sheffield on Platonic Love" (~25 min); ep. 33, "Last Judgments: Plato, Poetry and Myth." This is Stage 1's audio spine arriving on schedule — unlike the twentieth-century dossiers, no absence needs recording here.
- **[*Philosophize This!*](https://www.philosophizethis.org/podcast) (Stephen West)** — two verified early episodes: **#003, "Socrates and the Sophists"** (23 June 2013, ~39 min; Week 1 preview) and **#004, "Plato"** (1 July 2013, 45 min; Week 4 review) — the whole figure in one sitting: Symposium, Forms, Cave, politics. Free; transcripts on the show's site. Popularizer's compression throughout — orientation and review, never evidence.
- **BBC Radio 4, *In Our Time*: ["Plato's Republic"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b08vwn6h)** (first broadcast 29 June 2017; Melvyn Bragg with Angie Hobbs, M. M. McCabe, and James Warren; ~49 min as podcast). Free on BBC Sounds. Three specialists on the dialogue's one question — is it always better to be just than unjust? — assigned as the preview to the *Republic* weeks; the programme's own reading list points at Annas (tier 2), correctly.
- **Shelly Kagan, *Death* (Yale PHIL 176, [Open Yale Courses](https://oyc.yale.edu/philosophy/phil-176))** — lectures **4, 6, and 7** assigned in Week 2, after the *Phaedo*: the immortality arguments examined line by line by a philosopher who wants them to work better than they do. Free, with video, audio, and transcripts. The full 26-lecture course returns as the learning path's Stage 5 capstone extension — do not start it here.
- **Steven B. Smith, *Introduction to Political Philosophy* (Yale PLSC 114, Open Yale Courses; session list at [openmedia.yale.edu](https://openmedia.yale.edu/projects/iphone/departments/plsc/plsc114.html))** — the Plato lectures are 2 ("Socratic Citizenship: Plato, *Apology*"), 3 ("…*Crito*"), 4 ("Philosophers and Kings: Plato, *Republic*, I–II"), 5 ("…*Republic*, III–IV"), and 6 ("…*Republic*, V"); lectures 7–9 are Aristotle. All optional, watch-after, per the path's ruling; Smith reads the *Republic* as a political scientist among philosophers, which is precisely its value as a second voice.
