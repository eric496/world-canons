# Plato Received — Twenty-Four Centuries of Footnotes, and the Fights Still Open

*Focus study: Plato · [Dossier home](README.md) · [Library entry](../../eras/01-ancient/plato.md)*

No philosopher has been received more continuously, or more variously, than Plato. He has been a school curriculum, a heresy, a state secret in manuscript, a church father *avant la lettre*, a totalitarian, a liberal's nightmare, and a mathematician's patron saint — often two of these in the same century. The dialogues survived entire when nearly all his rivals' works perished, and their open, dramatic form invites each age to cast its own Plato in the leading roles. This file tells the reception by era, states the four live scholarly controversies at their current state of play, and then gives the standard misconceptions in full form — the long version of the library entry's "Misreadings to avoid."

## Antiquity: the school, the skeptics, the theologians

Plato's first reception was institutional. The Academy he founded (c. 387 BCE) passed from Speusippus to Xenocrates to a succession of heads who systematized the dialogues into doctrine, and then, under Arcesilaus (head from about 268 BCE) and Carneades, turned skeptical for two centuries, claiming Plato's inconclusive dialogues as warrant for suspending judgment altogether. The skeptical Academy is the reception's great fork: everything later can be sorted by whether it reads the dialogues as doctrine in dramatic dress or as instruments of inquiry that deliberately refuse to conclude. From the first century BCE the "Middle Platonists" re-dogmatized him into handbooks, while commentators began the line-by-line exegesis that fills late antique libraries. [Plotinus](../../eras/01-ancient/plotinus.md) (204/5–270 CE) gave antiquity its definitive Plato: the *Enneads* read the dialogues as a theology of the One, Intellect, and Soul, and Neoplatonists like Proclus (410–485 CE) commented on the *Republic*, *Timaeus*, and *Parmenides* as sacred texts, harmonizing Plato with Aristotle and with Greek religion. The Athenian school ended in 529 CE, when the emperor Justinian's measures against pagan teaching closed it — though how direct the line from that school to Plato's own foundation remained by then is debated by historians. Plato survived antiquity, as almost no rival did, complete.

## The medieval Plato: one dialogue in the West, a tradition in the East

For the Latin Middle Ages, "Plato" meant something startlingly narrow, and the dossier states it plainly: **the Latin West's Plato was mostly the *Timaeus*, in Calcidius' partial Latin translation** (fourth century CE — it covers roughly the first half of the dialogue, to 53c) **with his long commentary, supplemented by indirect reports** — Augustine's Platonism, Boethius' references, Macrobius, the *Phaedo* and *Meno* translations made by Henricus Aristippus in twelfth-century Sicily and barely circulated. From that fragment the medieval West built a Plato who was above all a cosmologist: the maker of the world, the doctrine of the world-soul, the mathematical order of the heavens. His ethics and politics were known mainly at second hand. The result is a reception with a permanent skew — when the full Plato finally arrived, the West met a stranger wearing a familiar name.

The Islamic world received more of him, and earlier. The Baghdad translation movement (ninth–tenth centuries CE) rendered Platonic material into Arabic — some dialogues in full or summary form, the *Laws* among them, and Galen's compendia of the *Timaeus* among others — and al-Fārābī (c. 870–950) made Plato half of his project of harmonizing the two sages, Plato and Aristotle, writing a *Philosophy of Plato* that reads the dialogues as a single curriculum of the soul's perfection. [Averroes](../../eras/02-medieval/averroes.md) (1126–1198), Aristotle's great commentator, also wrote a paraphrase of the *Republic* — it survives in Hebrew translation — treating Plato's city as political science in Aristotle's sense. Through these channels Plato reached the Islamic and Jewish traditions as legislator and metaphysician at once, long before Latin Europe had him whole.

## The Renaissance recovery and the making of the modern Plato

The whole Plato returned to the Latin West in a single publishing event: **Marsilio Ficino's complete Latin translation of the dialogues, published in Florence in 1484** under Medici patronage. Greek manuscripts had been accumulating in Italy for decades — Chrysoloras had taught Greek in Florence from 1397, and Plethon's lectures at the Council of Florence (1438–39) kindled Cosimo de' Medici's Platonic enthusiasm — and Ficino framed the corpus theologically, as an ancient wisdom parallel to revelation. The Renaissance Plato was accordingly a sage of love and cosmic harmony, the *Symposium* and *Timaeus* in the foreground.

The modern scholarly Plato is a nineteenth-century invention. Friedrich Schleiermacher's German translations (1804–28) established the reading still with us: the dialogues' literary form is inseparable from their philosophy, and they form a planned, unfolding whole. Karl Friedrich Hermann answered with the developmental reading — Plato's thought *grew*, and the dialogues track the growth — while George Grote defended the dialogues' dramatic integrity against both system-builders and chronologists. Around 1900 stylometry (pioneered by Lewis Campbell, named by Wincenty Lutosławski) began sorting the dialogues by measurable features of their Greek, and John Burnet and A. E. Taylor made the era's boldest counter-claim: that Plato's Socrates is essentially the historical one, Forms and all. Later decades added the great commentary tradition, the Popper shock treated below, the [Straussian](../../eras/05-20th-century/leo-strauss.md) esoteric reading, feminist recoveries and critiques, and renewed attention to the dialogues as literature. None of these Platos has displaced the others; they coexist, uneasily, in the footnotes.

## Live debates

**(1) Developmentalism versus unitarianism.** The most technical of Plato's controversies starts from the one result most specialists accept: stylometry robustly separates a late group of dialogues — *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Critias*, *Laws* — whose Greek measurably differs from the rest. Everything beyond that is disputed. **Developmentalists** read the stylistic groups as stages of a mind: an early, Socratic Plato of aporetic definition-hunting; a middle Plato of Forms, recollection, and the *Republic*'s system; a late Plato who criticizes the Forms in the *Parmenides* and ends with the *Laws*' second-best city. G. E. L. Owen's famous 1953 paper re-dating the *Timaeus* — and the afterlife of its partial withdrawal — showed how much doctrine hangs on a date. Gregory Vlastos gave the view its canonical form: *Socrates* (1991) distinguishes the philosopher of the early dialogues ("SocratesE") from the middle-dialogue spokesman ("SocratesM"), separated by ten sharp doctrinal differences. **Unitarians**, from Paul Shorey's *The Unity of Plato's Thought* (1903) to Charles Kahn's *Plato and the Socratic Dialogue* (1996), reply that the dialogues are proleptic installments of one design: the early works withhold doctrines they already presuppose, the apparent ruptures are pedagogical staging, and a dramatic author cannot be periodized like a filing cabinet. Kahn's Plato wrote every dialogue looking forward to the whole; doctrinal development, where it appears, is in the *presentation*, not the position. No verdict is available, and the working compromise — stylometry gives relative order, not intellectual biography; local development in particular doctrines is demonstrable, global narratives are wagers — satisfies neither camp, which is usually the mark of an accurate one.

**(2) The Socratic problem.** [Socrates](../../eras/01-ancient/socrates.md) wrote nothing. The witnesses disagree: Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BCE) caricatures a head-in-the-clouds natural philosopher and sophist; Xenophon's Socrates is a practical moralist of conventional piety; Aristotle, writing a generation later with no disciple's axe to grind, testifies that Socrates pursued universal definitions in ethics and did *not* separate them, while Plato did — the single most load-bearing sentence in the whole debate; Plato's own portraits evolve from the definition-hunter of the short dialogues to the metaphysician of the *Republic*. **Vlastos's hypothesis** holds that Plato's early dialogues give us substantially the historical Socrates' philosophy — identifiable by consistent doctrinal signatures, and separated from Plato's inventions by exactly the line Aristotle draws — while the middle dialogues' Socrates is Plato's mouthpiece. The critics answer from several directions: that Plato the dramatist cannot be sorted into transcript and invention by doctrinal checklist without circularity (the "early" group is defined by the traits then credited to Socrates); that Xenophon's and Aristophanes' portraits fit Vlastos's Socrates no better than Plato's late one; and, in Kahn's version, that the early dialogues were composed as literary prologues to a philosophy their author already held. The honest current state: Aristotle's distinction between Socratic definition and Platonic separation is widely accepted; everything finer-grained — which dialogues, how much, whose voice — remains contested, and may be underdetermined by the evidence we have.

**(3) The unwritten doctrines.** Aristotle reports, in the *Physics* and *Metaphysics*, that Plato's ultimate principles were the One and the Indefinite Dyad — "the great and the small" — and that Forms and numbers were derived from them. No such doctrine appears in the dialogues. The *Seventh Letter*, if genuine (its authenticity is itself disputed), has Plato insist that his real views cannot be put in writing like other studies. The **Tübingen school** — Hans Joachim Krämer's *Arete bei Platon und Aristoteles* (1959) and Konrad Gaiser's *Platons ungeschriebene Lehre* (1963) — built from these testimonies a systematic esoteric Plato: the dialogues are deliberate protreptic withholdings, and the oral teaching in the Academy, a metaphysics of two principles, is the philosophy's crown, recoverable from the indirect tradition. The opposing tradition descends from Harold Cherniss's *The Riddle of the Early Academy* (1945), which argued that Aristotle systematically misunderstood or misrepresented his teacher, that the ancient testimony is too tangled to reconstruct a doctrine from, and that the dialogues must be self-sufficient because Plato made them so. The Anglophone mainstream remains closer to Cherniss; German and Italian scholarship keeps the Tübingen reconstruction in play, and a mediating position — Plato did lecture on principles, the testimony proves something was there, but its content cannot be recovered with the confidence the Tübingen reconstructions claim — has many adherents. The dossier states the three positions and takes none.

**(4) Popper and the Republic.** Karl [Popper](../../eras/05-20th-century/karl-popper.md)'s *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), written in wartime exile, charged that the *Republic* is the founding document of totalitarianism: a historicist theory of inevitable political decay, a caste state frozen by eugenics and censorship, rule by a guardian elite armed with the noble lie — Plato as the first great enemy of the open society, and the ancestor through Hegel of its modern ones. The scholarly pushback was immediate and specific. M. I. Levinson's *In Defense of Plato* (1953) audited Popper's quotations and translations line by line and found systematic distortion — passages trimmed, contexts reversed — and most classicists since have judged the portrait a misreading: the city of the *Republic* is built "in speech" as a magnified model for investigating justice in the soul, its proposals advanced by a character in a dialogue, in an Athens whose democracy had executed the author's teacher. But the honest pushback has a limit, and this dossier will not soften past it: the censorship of poetry, the breeding program, the abolition of guardian families, and the noble lie are in the text, not in Popper's invention, and they are illiberal by any standard the modern reader can apply. Defending Plato therefore proceeds, where it proceeds, not by denying the features but by contesting their function — thought experiment, reductio, reluctant prescription — and by weighing the *Laws*' retreat to a law-governed second best. The current mainstream: Popper's case as *scholarship* is largely rejected; his provocation permanently changed what a responsible reader of the *Republic* must face. Both halves of that sentence are the finding.

## Whitehead's verdict

The most quoted modern judgment is a sentence of [Alfred North Whitehead](../../eras/05-20th-century/alfred-north-whitehead.md)'s, from *Process and Reality* (1929): "The safest general characterization of the European philosophical tradition is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato." Whitehead immediately qualified it — he did not mean the systematic scheme of thought later scholars have "doctrinally extracted" from the dialogues, but the wealth of general ideas scattered through them — and the qualification is the verdict's accurate half. As a census of influence it exaggerates: the Presocratics supplied the problems, Aristotle the logic, and the Hellenistic schools much of the ethics. As a claim about the *Republic*, the *Theaetetus*, and the *Timaeus* having set the problem-set — universals, knowledge and opinion, soul and body, the city and the soul, the ordered cosmos — it remains difficult to falsify, and the twenty-four centuries surveyed above are its evidence.

## Misconceptions, in full form

- **"Plato speaks directly through Socrates in every dialogue."** Plato never speaks in his own voice in the dialogues at all — he is the one major philosopher of antiquity present in his works only as their absent author (the *Apology* mentions him, in the third person, among those present at the trial). Socrates leads most early and middle dialogues, but not all and not throughout: the *Laws*' speaker is an Athenian Stranger, the *Sophist* and *Statesman* are led by an Eleatic Stranger while Socrates listens, the *Timaeus* hands the stage to its title character, and even in the *Phaedo* the narration is at two removes — Phaedo retelling the death scene to Echecrates, with Plato noted as absent, ill. Whether the middle-dialogue Socrates is Plato's mouthpiece is Vlastos's hypothesis, a scholarly position with arguments, not a fact the dialogues declare. Reading every Socratic speech as Plato's signed opinion erases the dramatic form Schleiermacher taught us to see — and with it, the possibility that Plato is testing positions rather than delivering them.
- **"Atlantis was Plato's literal claim about history."** Atlantis exists in exactly two places: the *Timaeus* (24e–25d) and the unfinished *Critias*, both as a story inside the fiction. The text's own framing is a chain of tellings — Egyptian priests to Solon, Solon to his descendants, down to Critias, who retells it as a "true story" suited to illustrate, in action, the ideal city Socrates had described the previous day. That is the frame of a thought experiment warming up, not of historiography: no ancient author outside the dialogues' own tradition treats Atlantis as reportage, Plato never mentions it elsewhere, and the *Critias* breaks off mid-sentence with the tale unfinished — an exhibit, not a chronicle. Atlantis-hunting as literal geography is a modern industry, launched by Ignatius Donnelly's *Atlantis: The Antediluvian World* (1882). What scholars do debate is the tale's *function* — many read the drowned naval empire, swollen with hubris and defeated by an idealized ancient Athens, as Plato's mirror for his own imperial Athens; the story works as political allegory precisely because it is not a claim about the past.
- **"Plato wanted to ban all art."** The *Republic*'s target is precise, and it is not art. Books II–III censor the *content* of stories told to young guardians — gods behaving badly, heroes fearing death — as a measure in the education of one class in an ideal city. Book X's famous attack is on *imitative* poetry's credentials: the imitator produces copies of copies, at third remove from truth, and feeds the soul's irrational part at reason's expense — Homer is criticized as an educator who knew nothing of the crafts, wars, and virtues he sang about. Even there, the ban has stated exceptions: hymns to the gods and praises of famous men remain (607a), and Socrates calls the quarrel between philosophy and poetry an *ancient* one (607b), as if inheriting a lawsuit rather than filing it. Against the flat reading stand the dialogues themselves — myths, dramas, images like the Cave and the charioteer, composed by antiquity's most literary philosopher — and the *Ion*, the *Phaedrus*, and the *Symposium*, where inspired making is treated with a seriousness no mere censor would bother with. Plato's question is what *authority* art may claim, not whether it may exist.
- **"Plato was the enemy of democracy, full stop."** The *Republic*'s critique (Book VIII) is of a failure mode, not of self-government as such: democracy's excess of freedom dissolves all authority, factions arm, and the people's champion becomes the tyrant — a pathology Athens' own history, from the war's demagogues to the Thirty and back, gave Plato reason to study. His complaint is that Athens' democracy made flattery its epistemology and killed Socrates; it is a citizen's indictment of a particular democracy's particular disease, delivered by a man who never proposed restoring the oligarchy his own relatives had disgraced. And the *Laws*, his last word in politics, builds something recognizably different: a law-governed mixed constitution that blends the "two mother constitutions," monarchy and democracy (693d), with elections, audit of officials, and the rule of law standing above every ruler — the philosopher-kings are gone. None of this makes Plato a democrat; the *Republic*'s arrangements are more authoritarian than the democracy he criticizes, and honesty keeps that in view. But "enemy of democracy full stop" cannot survive contact with the *Laws* or with the specificity of the *Republic*'s diagnosis.
- **"The Cave says the physical world is fake."** It says something harder. The Allegory of the Cave is introduced as an image of "our nature in its education and want of education" (*Republic* VII, 514a): the prisoners' failure is epistemic, not ontological — they take images for the whole of reality because images are all their position lets them see. The freed prisoner's painful ascent ends with seeing the *same* world — the fire, the artifacts, the landscape, the sun — in its true order and proportions; nothing dissolves, and the visible sun is genuinely seen. Plato's Divided Line gives belief about physical objects a real, if subordinate, cognitive standing: *doxa* is of what *is*, imperfectly apprehended — a deficiency of intelligibility, not an illusion or a simulation. The Cave's sting is reserved for the return: the freed man, eyes adjusted to the light, is nearly useless back in the dark and risks being killed by prisoners who prefer their shadows — Socrates' fate, and the philosopher's political problem. The allegory is about what education costs and why it is resisted, by a thinker who thought the senses deliver a world that is real but not self-explanatory.
