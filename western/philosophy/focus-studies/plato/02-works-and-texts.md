# Works and Texts: The Corpus in Reading Order

Plato's bibliography has a shape no other ancient philosopher's has: it is complete. Where the Presocratics survive in quotations and Aristotle in lecture drafts, Plato's dialogues come down entire — every work the ancient canon assigned to him, preserved by unbroken copying from antiquity through Byzantium to the Renaissance. That completeness is the dossier's great working advantage: the development of a philosophy can be watched in the texts themselves, including the moments when Plato turns on his own theories. It is also the dossier's standing caution, stated once here and carried everywhere: **Plato never speaks in his own voice in the dialogues.** He is not a character in any of them. Other people — Socrates above all, but also Parmenides, Timaeus, Diotima reported by Socrates, the Eleatic and Athenian Strangers — voice the doctrines, in dramatic situations, against named opponents. "What Plato believed" is therefore always a conclusion drawn from texts he assigned to speakers, never a quotation from the author; the full problem — the Socrates problem — is treated in [06-reception-and-debates.md](06-reception-and-debates.md), and this file marks the fact at the point where the corpus is described. Citation first, because with Plato citation is navigation.

## A note on citation before the works

Plato is cited by **Stephanus numbers**: the page numbers of the three-volume edition published at Geneva in 1578 by Henri Estienne — Latinized, Henricus Stephanus — with each page divided into five sections lettered **a** through **e**. The number is printed in the margin of every serious modern edition and translation, and it fixes a passage the way a chapter-and-verse fixes a biblical one: the *Republic*'s philosopher-king sentence is at 473c–d, the Allegory of the Cave opens at 514a, the *Apology*'s defense stands or falls at 24b–28a. References in this dossier take the form *work + Stephanus page* (e.g. *Euthyphro* 10a, the dilemma). The standard Greek text behind the modern translations is Burnet's Oxford Classical Text (*Platonis Opera*, 1900–1907); the dossier reads in translation, below, and cites by Stephanus so that any passage can be checked across editions.

## The corpus: Thrasyllus, stylometry, and the doubtful

The canon comes from **Thrasyllus** (d. 36 CE), scholar and astrologer at the court of the emperor Tiberius, who arranged the works he accepted as genuine into nine **tetralogies** — groups of four, after the tragic festivals — comprising 35 dialogues plus the *Letters*, 36 titles in all (Diogenes Laertius 3.56–61 preserves the arrangement). The tetralogies are an ancient filing system, not a chronology and not a reading order: the *Republic* sits in the eighth tetralogy between the *Clitophon* and the *Timaeus*. The dossier does not read in Thrasyllus' order; it reads in the learning path's.

Modern order rests on **stylometry** — the statistical study of Plato's Greek: particles, sentence rhythms, vocabulary, and habits of phrasing that shift measurably across his writing life. From Lewis Campbell's work in the 1860s onward, stylometry has supported a rough three-group division that scholarship broadly accepts at its center and disputes at its edges. **Early** dialogues: short, Socratic, definition-seeking, usually ending without an answer (*Euthyphro*, *Apology*, *Crito*, and their kin). **Middle**: constructive and doctrinal — Forms, recollection, immortality, the tripartite soul (*Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus*). **Late**: technical, critical, Socrates displaced (*Parmenides*, *Theaetetus* at the threshold, *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Laws*) — the late group's core membership (*Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Critias*, *Laws*) is stylometry's firmest result. The edges are genuinely contested: individual placements shift between scholars (the *Timaeus* was long read as middle-period; the *Gorgias* sits on the early/transitional border in most schemes), and **unitarian** readers resist the developmental picture itself, holding that the dialogues are dramatically unified works written to be read whole rather than fossils of an evolution. The dossier uses the three groups as the standard scholarly tool they are, flags contested placements, and draws no doctrinal conclusion from chronology alone.

Authenticity needs the same care, because the Thrasyllan canon is generous. Several works it includes are now widely doubted or rejected: **Alcibiades I** is the important case — for centuries it stood canonical, the Neoplatonists made it the first dialogue a student read, and only since Schleiermacher (early nineteenth century) has suspicion become the majority view; it is now widely doubted, though it still has defenders. **Alcibiades II**, **Hipparchus**, the **Rival Lovers**, the **Theages**, and the **Minos** are generally judged spurious by current scholarship. The **Epinomis**, transmitted as the *Laws*' appendix, is often assigned to Philippus of Opus, the *Laws*' editor (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). Beyond the canon entirely stands the *Appendix Platonica* — short pieces such as the *Definitions* and *Axiochus* — which no current scholarship defends. Nothing in this dossier's reading list is on the doubtful shelf: the dossier reads works whose authenticity is not in serious question.

The **Letters** are their own case: thirteen letters transmitted under Plato's name, most regarded as spurious by nearly all scholars. The exception that matters is the **Seventh Letter**, the long apologia for Plato's Sicilian life, which has serious defenders and serious doubters and no verdict; the dossier's rule, applied in [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md), is to use it flagged — its author is at minimum well informed — and to rest no claim on it alone where better evidence exists.

## The works in the dossier's reading order

The order is the learning path's Stage 1 order — *Euthyphro* through *Republic* — with the path's two extensions, *Gorgias* and *Theaetetus*, placed after the *Republic*, where the plan sets them: each is a full dialogue on a single problem, read as a return to Socratic method at a higher level of difficulty.

### 1. Euthyphro (early)

A short dialogue on the courthouse steps, its dramatic date days before Socrates' own trial: Socrates, indicted for impiety, meets Euthyphro, who is prosecuting his own father for homicide, and asks him what piety is. Euthyphro's definitions fail in sequence, and the dialogue ends, as the early dialogues do, without one. Its permanent deposit is the **Euthyphro dilemma** (10a): is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because the gods love it? — the fork on which every later divine-command theory of ethics still hangs. **Difficulty:** low; the cleanest first specimen of the Socratic method in action — definition demanded, candidates tested, confidence dissolved. **Place in the development:** the early method at its purest, and the dossier's chosen first text, per the learning path.

### 2. Apology (early)

Not a dialogue but a speech — Plato's reconstruction of Socrates' defense at his trial in 399 BCE, in three movements: the defense proper, the counter-proposal after conviction, and the address to the jury after the death sentence. Socrates describes his mission (the Delphic oracle's verdict that no one was wiser, 21a–23c), professes his ignorance, refuses to beg or to stop philosophizing (29d: he will obey the god rather than the Athenians), and faces death without fear on the ground that fearing it pretends to know what no one knows (29a–b). **Difficulty:** low as argument, unmatched as rhetoric; Xenophon's *Apology* survives as a rival account, a first lesson in how even near-contemporary sources diverge. **Place in the development:** the dossier's portrait of the historical Socrates at his most historically anchored — and the standing exhibit in every later argument about philosophy and the city.

### 3. Crito (early)

The morning of an offered escape: Crito comes before dawn to the cell, money ready and the guards suborned, and Socrates refuses to go. The dialogue gives the early corpus its one sustained piece of positive argument: the personified **Laws of Athens** speak (50a–54d), contending that Socrates, who lived under them seventy years, begat and educated his children under them, and could have left at any time, has an agreement he may not break merely because the city has acted unjustly toward him — that verdicts, once given, must stand or no city can. **Difficulty:** low; short, lucid, and still the founding text of political obligation. **Place in the development:** the ethics of the *Apology* completed — the same man who would not obey an order to stop philosophizing will not break a law to save his life, and the dossier reads the two together as one position, not two.

### 4. Meno (transitional)

The question is whether virtue can be taught — and before it can be asked, Meno's paradox (80d) asks whether inquiry is possible at all: you cannot search for what you know (no need) or for what you do not know (you would not recognize it). Socrates' answer introduces the doctrine of **recollection**: questioning an uneducated enslaved boy through a geometry problem (82b–85b), he argues that the answers are drawn out of the boy, not put into him — learning is the recovery of what the soul already possesses. The dialogue's second half tests whether virtue is knowledge (and so teachable) by the hypothetical method, and ends inconclusively: virtue, as things stand in actual cities, appears to come by divine dispensation rather than teaching. **Difficulty:** moderate; the geometry scene is elementary, the framing arguments are not. **Place in the development:** the hinge of the corpus — the aporetic early manner carrying the first unmistakably Platonic doctrine, and the dossier's bridge from Socrates to Plato.

### 5. Phaedo (middle)

Socrates' last day, narrated by Phaedo at Phlius to an audience of Pythagoreans: the conversation runs from the exclusion of suicide through a sequence of arguments for the soul's immortality — from opposites, from recollection, from the soul's affinity with the Forms — to Socrates' intellectual autobiography (96a–102a), his disappointment with Anaxagoras, and his "second sailing" to explanation by Forms; then the final argument and the closing myth, and the death scene itself, the most famous in ancient prose (117a–118a). **Difficulty:** moderate to high; the arguments interlock and must be tracked separately. **Place in the development:** the middle system in one dialogue — Forms, recollection, and immortality fused — and Plato's absence from the scene he writes ("Plato, I believe, was ill," 59b) is the corpus' most famous authorial notation (see [01-life-and-times.md](01-life-and-times.md)). The dossier follows the learning path: 57a–95b on the first pass, the remainder on the return.

### 6. Symposium (c. 385–370 BCE)

A drinking party at the tragedian Agathon's house, recollected at two removes: seven speeches in praise of *erōs* — Phaedrus, Pausanias, the doctor Eryximachus, Aristophanes (with his myth of the halved human beings), Agathon, Socrates, and the drunken irruption of Alcibiades at the end. Socrates' speech reports the priestess **Diotima**, whose ladder (201d–212c) climbs from love of one beautiful body, to beauty in all bodies, to souls, practices and laws, to knowledge, and finally to Beauty itself — eternal, unchanging, not beautiful in one respect and ugly in another. **Difficulty:** moderate; the literary surface is the easiest in the middle corpus and the philosophical payload is carried by ordering, contrast, and irony, so it repays the slow second read the dossier schedules. **Place in the development:** the doctrine of Forms reached by desire rather than by argument — the middle system's erotic engine. The learning path's Stage 1 selection is Diotima's speech; this dossier reads the dialogue whole, since the ladder corrects speeches the reader needs to have heard.

### 7. Republic (c. 375 BCE)

The masterpiece, and the dossier's center of gravity — ten books on the question Glaucon renews in Book II: is justice good in itself, apart from its reputation and rewards? The city-soul construction, the tripartite soul, the censorship of poetry, philosopher-kings (473c–d), the Form of the Good, the Sun, the Divided Line, the Cave, the decline of regimes, the tyrant's misery, the banishment of imitative poetry, and the Myth of Er: the library's [guide to the *Republic*](../../study/plato-republic.md) is this dossier's companion for the work — book by book, with reconstructions, checkpoint questions and answers, and the selective route (Books I–IV, VI–VII, X) for a first pass — and this file endorses its rulings rather than restating them. **Difficulty:** the highest in the reading list before the *Theaetetus*; long, architectonically layered, and endlessly argued over, beginning with whether its city is a program or a model "in speech" (592a–b) — a dispute the guide presents without settling. **Place in the development:** the middle period's summit, in which every earlier thread of the dossier — definition, recollection, Forms, the soul, the philosopher's fate in the city — is woven into a single work.

### 8. Gorgias (extension; transitional, on the early/middle border)

The longest of the Socratic dialogues, and the fiercest. Against the rhetorician Gorgias, his pupil Polus, and finally the young Callicles — who argues, without euphemism, that conventional justice is a trick of the weak and the natural right is the stronger's appetite indulged — Socrates defends the whole Socratic ethic: that doing injustice is worse than suffering it, that the tyrant's power is impotence, that pleasure and the good are not the same (495e–499a against Callicles), and that rhetoric as practiced is flattery, a counterfeit of the political art as pastry-baking is of medicine (464a–466a). It closes with a judgment myth told, Socrates insists, as truth. **Difficulty:** moderate to high — the prose is dramatic and direct, the concessions Socrates extracts are technical, and Callicles must be allowed his full force or the dialogue collapses into a sermon. **Place in the development:** the early manner at its most combative, with the middle system's ethical psychology already forming; read in this dossier after the *Republic*, whose Books I–II replay its confrontation with Thrasymachus, its argument can be measured against Plato's mature answer.

### 9. Theaetetus (extension; late, at the threshold; c. 369 BCE)

The question is the *Meno*'s, inverted and pursued with late-period rigor: what is knowledge? Theaetetus, a young mathematician dying of wounds and dysentery after a battle near Corinth — the frame dates the dialogue's present to 369 BCE — proposes three definitions in turn: knowledge is perception (151e, tested as Protagoras' doctrine that man is the measure, and Heraclitus' flux beneath it); knowledge is true judgment (187b); knowledge is true judgment with an account, a *logos* (201c). Each is examined and each fails, and the dialogue ends in aporia — Socrates the midwife leaving his patient delivered of wind-eggs but better prepared to conceive again (210b–d). **Difficulty:** high; the most technical work on this list, and the ancestor of every later epistemology. **Place in the development:** the late Plato's self-examination — no Forms appear on stage (their absence is itself a debated datum), and the *Sophist* takes up, the next dramatic day, exactly where this dialogue stops.

## Editions and translations

**The corpus text** for this dossier is **Plato: Complete Works, ed. John M. Cooper (Hackett, 1997)** — the standard one-volume Plato in English: every dialogue in the Thrasyllan canon (doubtful ones included and flagged), translated by the leading scholars of each work and revised under a single editor, with Stephanus numbers throughout. It is the edition to own. One discipline applies, the same the learning path imposes: pick one translation of a work and stay with it through that work — Plato's key terms are load-bearing, and equivalents differ between translators enough to confuse a reader who switches mid-dialogue.

For the opening sequence the named text is **Five Dialogues: Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Meno, Phaedo, trans. G. M. A. Grube, ed. John M. Cooper (Hackett)** — the learning path's ruling for Stage 1: Grube's English is plain, exact, and classroom-tested, and the volume contains, in order, the dossier's first five readings in the translations the path prescribes. For the ***Republic***, use **Grube's translation as revised by C. D. C. Reeve (Hackett)** — the path's and the [guide's](../../study/plato-republic.md) named edition; Allan Bloom's translation (Basic Books) is the guide's named literal alternative for comparison on disputed passages. For the ***Symposium***, the Nehamas and Woodruff translation (in *Complete Works*, and as a Hackett standalone) is the modern standard for this dossier. The ***Gorgias*** is read in *Complete Works* in Donald J. Zeyl's translation; the ***Theaetetus*** in *Complete Works* in M. J. Levett's translation as revised by Myles Burnyeat.

**Avoid, as a first read, Benjamin Jowett.** The Victorian Plato (1871) is a monument of English prose and was the English world's Plato for a century, but the learning path rules it out for this library, and the dossier keeps the ruling: the language is flowery and archaising where Plato is plain or funny, the translations paraphrase at exactly the points where an argument turns on a word, and cheap reprints frequently lack the Stephanus numbers on which all citation — including every page of this dossier — depends. A reader who has finished the dossier's sequence may enjoy Jowett comparatively, as the guide allows Bloom; no beginner should start there.
