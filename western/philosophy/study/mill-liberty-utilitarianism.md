# Study Guide: Mill's *On Liberty* and *Utilitarianism*

**Work:** *On Liberty* (1859) and *Utilitarianism* (serialised 1861; book form 1863) · **Author:** John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) · **Composed:** *On Liberty* 1859; *Utilitarianism* 1861/1863
**Covers:** *On Liberty* chapters 1–3 in full, with chapters 4–5 in summary; *Utilitarianism* chapters 2 and 4 in full, with chapters 1, 3, and 5 in summary — with a reading plan, chapter-by-chapter guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [John Stuart Mill](../eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

For *Utilitarianism*, read George Sher's edition (Hackett) — the plan's named edition, with a helpful introduction and notes. For *On Liberty*, use Stefan Collini's edition (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought) or John Gray's combined volume (Oxford World's Classics), which conveniently pairs the two works in this guide. The learning path makes Mill the one author where its usual edition rule relaxes: his prose is unusually clear, and a public-domain text is genuinely fine here. What matters most is a text with chapter divisions intact, since this guide navigates by chapter rather than by page.

A note on quotations: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows Mill's own wording, stable across modern editions. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

On dates: *On Liberty* appeared in 1859, the year after Harriet Taylor Mill's death, and is dedicated to her memory. *Utilitarianism* was serialised in *Fraser's Magazine* in 1861 and published in book form in 1863. The two works are companions: *Utilitarianism* states the ethical theory, and *On Liberty* shows what it says, applied to political life, about the limits of power over the individual.

## How to use this guide

Mill is the clearest prose stylist in this library, and that clarity is a trap: it is easy to finish a chapter feeling you understand it without being able to state its argument. His arguments are long, cumulative, and full of qualifications that do real work — the harm principle turns entirely on what counts as "harm," and the higher-pleasures argument on who counts as a competent judge.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a chapter, read its section below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each chapter, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

The learning path places Mill in Stage 4, after Kant and alongside Harriet Taylor Mill, and recommends: *Utilitarianism* chapters 2 and 4 first, then the whole of *Utilitarianism*, then *On Liberty* chapters 1–3. This guide follows that order in its reading plan but presents *On Liberty* first chapter-by-chapter, because the harm principle is the idea most readers arrive with. Read in either order; what matters is reading both works, not one alone. A reader who knows only *On Liberty* will mistake Mill for a theorist of rights; one who knows only *Utilitarianism* will wonder where liberty comes from. Together, they answer each other.

One further caution: Mill's target is not only the state. It is the tyranny of the majority exercised through opinion — custom, respectability, and public disapproval. Most of the coercion he describes does not arrive with a policeman, which is why the essay still bites.

## Reading plan

The two works together run to about 200 pages in paperbacks. At about two hours of primary reading per week, the program below takes about six weeks; the first pass can be compressed into four by reading summary chapters as summaries.

| Week | Reading | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Utilitarianism* ch. 2 | The theory stated: happiness, higher pleasures, the swine objection |
| 2 | *Utilitarianism* ch. 4, then chs. 1, 3, 5 as summaries | The "proof" of utility, sanctions, and justice |
| 3 | *On Liberty* ch. 1 | The harm principle and the tyranny of the majority |
| 4 | *On Liberty* ch. 2 | Freedom of thought and discussion |
| 5 | *On Liberty* ch. 3 | Individuality and experiments in living |
| 6 | *On Liberty* chs. 4–5 as summaries; re-read *Utilitarianism* ch. 2 | Limits, applications, and the two works together |

Do not rush *Utilitarianism* chapter 2: it contains the whole theory in miniature, and every later chapter defends something asserted there. Do not skip *On Liberty* chapter 2 either — its case for free discussion is the one Mill thought most in need of defense in an age that already believed itself tolerant.

## Chapter-by-chapter guide

### *On Liberty*, Chapter 1 — Introductory: the tyranny of the majority and the one very simple principle

Mill opens with liberty as a struggle against rulers: first against the prince, checked by constitutional limits; then, in democratic ages, against a subtler danger. When the people rule themselves, the old question — who rules? — seems answered, and a new one is missed: what limits should there be on society's power over the individual? A majority can tyrannise through law, but also through opinion — the "tyranny of the prevailing opinion and feeling," imposing its ideas as rules of conduct and frowning down dissent. Mill claims to fear this social tyranny more than political despotism, because it leaves fewer means of escape and penetrates more deeply into life.

The chapter then states the principle, in words worth learning exactly: "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." The individual is sovereign over himself — over his own body and mind. Society may remonstrate, reason, persuade, and entreat, but may not compel a person for their own good.

Two qualifications matter immediately. First, the principle is stated for "a civilised community" and for adults in the maturity of their faculties; children, and those Mill calls "barbarians," are excluded as not yet capable of being improved by free discussion. This passage, entangled with Mill's East India Company career, is a genuine blemish scholars debate — as a developmental view of the principle's institutional form, or as plain imperial prejudice. The guide takes no settled position; note the tension. Second, the principle governs compulsion, not persuasion: advice, criticism, and disapproval of harmless conduct are not forbidden, only coercion, by law or by the crushing weight of opinion.


**Main argument:** Power over the individual needs a justification, and only preventing harm to others supplies one.

### *On Liberty*, Chapter 2 — Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion: the case for free discussion

Chapter 2 is Mill's great epistemic argument, aimed at a reader who already believes in free speech in principle. Such a reader, Mill complains, usually defends discussion only for opinions they think true or harmless, and is ready to suppress opinions they are sure are false. That is no defense of liberty at all, because it assumes the very infallibility the argument denies.

The argument proceeds by cases. If the silenced opinion is true, society loses the chance to exchange error for truth — and the loss is worse than it looks, because even a true opinion held without understanding its grounds is a dead dogma, not a living belief. If it is false, its collision with truth still does good work: it forces defenders of truth to understand what they believe and why, instead of repeating unexamined formulas. And the most common case is the third: received and dissenting opinions are each partly true, each containing part of the truth the other lacks. Doctrines that look like complete systems — moral and religious orthodoxies among them — are usually partial truths, exaggerated into falsehood by being held alone. Free discussion is therefore the mechanism by which partial truths correct each other, not a luxury for an age of settled opinion.

Mill also insists on liberty of expression, not merely of thought: unexpressed thought cannot do this work. And discussion is not unlimited: opinions lose immunity when expression becomes positive instigation to a mischievous act — his example is telling an angry mob that corn dealers are starvers of the poor. Context decides: the same words to a newspaper reader are protected discussion; to an excited mob outside a corn dealer's house, incitement. Whether that line can bear the weight Mill puts on it is a live question in free-speech theory.


**Main argument:** Silencing discussion harms society in every case — whether the silenced opinion is true, false, or partly true — because truth is kept alive only by being contested.

### *On Liberty*, Chapter 3 — Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being: experiments in living

If chapter 2 defends free discussion, chapter 3 defends free action: liberty of tastes, pursuits, and plans of life, subject only to the harm principle. Mill's target is custom. People inherit their plans of life rather than choosing them; they ask what is customary or respectable, rather than what suits their character, and end up living lives that are not theirs.

Against custom, Mill sets individuality as a personal and social good. Exercising choice — observation, reasoning, judgment, self-control in forming a life — develops faculties conformity leaves unused. And society benefits, because progress depends on "experiments in living": new ways of life, tried by individuals, that may prove better than received ways. Genius is rare and fragile; it needs freedom to breathe, and a society crushing eccentricity gets fewer geniuses and fewer experiments. Eccentricity is not a nuisance to tolerate but a price a healthy culture pays for progress — and a sign of its health.

Note the grounding: chapter 3 defends individuality in the name of utility, appealing to the permanent interests of humanity as progressive beings. Whether that grounding can support the strong, almost rights-like protection chapter 1 seems to promise is a central interpretive dispute about the essay; hold it open as you read chapters 4 and 5.


**Main argument:** Individuality is an element of well-being and an engine of social progress, and custom is its standing enemy.

### *On Liberty*, Chapter 4 — Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual (summary)

Chapter 4 draws the line between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct, then complicates it. Conduct affecting only the agent, or others only with their free and undeceived consent, is the individual's own business: society may advise and disapprove, but may not coerce. Conduct harming others, or violating a definite obligation, may be punished by law or opinion.

The complications are where the chapter earns its keep. First, compulsion is allowed where a duty is owed: a fair share of common burdens, testimony, common defense, children's education — obligations to others, not paternalism. Second, self-regarding conduct can become other-regarding through circumstances: a soldier or policeman drunk on duty commits a fault affecting others. Third, conduct harmful only by example is a gray zone; here Mill draws a firm line: example alone does not justify compulsion, though it may justify disapproval. The chapter tests the principle against hard cases and concedes the boundary is not always sharp — it needs judgment in application, not merely assertion.

**Main argument:** The principle's boundary is duty and definite harm, not vague social dislike — but applying it requires judging real cases, not sorting them by formula.

### *On Liberty*, Chapter 5 — Applications (summary)

Chapter 5 applies the principle to concrete policy questions, and the applications reveal its bite. On trade: trade is a social act affecting others, so it falls within society's jurisdiction — yet Mill defends free trade on utilitarian grounds, warning against interference without a clear harm. On drink: he opposes prohibition; the state may regulate sale and punish drunkenness when it causes harm, but may not forbid drinking for a person's own good. On education: the state may require and even pay for it, but must not monopolise it, because a state education molding citizens to its own pattern is despotism over the mind. On gambling and public houses: regulate where they create public harm, but do not criminalise private conduct on moral grounds alone.

Read chapter 5 as a test of the whole essay. Where its applications seem inconsistent, ask whether the inconsistency is in Mill or in the assumption that the harm principle is a simple rule. It is a standard to be applied with attention to consequences, and chapter 5 shows what that looks like when cases get messy.

**Main argument:** The harm principle, applied to real policy, permits regulation where others are genuinely affected and forbids it where only the agent's own good is at stake — and the difference between those two is often a matter of fact, not slogan.

### *Utilitarianism*, Chapter 1 — General Remarks (summary)

Chapter 1 is scene-setting. Moral philosophy, Mill complains, lacks the settled first principles the sciences enjoy, and rival theories have been argued for centuries without agreement. He does not pretend to prove his first principle from neutral ground — that, as chapter 4 argues, is not the kind of proof a first principle admits. Instead he states what is at stake: actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse. He warns against judging utilitarianism by caricature — happiness as only sensual pleasure, or the theory as demanding constant calculation; both are answered in chapter 2. His framing claim: no moral theory escapes appeal to consequences in the end, and the question is whether utility is acknowledged as the standard or smuggled in unacknowledged.

**Main argument:** The dispute is over the first principle of morals, and utilitarianism deserves a fair hearing before it is judged.

### *Utilitarianism*, Chapter 2 — General Principles: happiness, quality, and the swine objection

Chapter 2 states the theory and defends it against its most famous objections. The creed holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse. By happiness Mill means pleasure and the absence of pain; by unhappiness, pain and the privation of pleasure. The standard is not the agent's happiness alone but that of all concerned, counted impartially — the greatest happiness of the greatest number, each counting equally.

The "doctrine worthy only of swine" objection — that a pleasure-based theory reduces human beings to animals — is answered with higher pleasures. Pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity: pleasures of intellect, imagination, feeling, and moral sentiment are higher than mere sensation, and a competent judge — someone who has experienced both kinds — would prefer the higher even at the cost of discontent. Hence: better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. The test of quality is the preference of those acquainted with both, and Mill claims no one would trade a life of higher pleasures for lower pleasures alone — though people often choose lower pleasures from infirmity, temptation, or lack of opportunity, which shows weakness of will or circumstance, not a judgment that the lower is better.

The chapter also answers two further objections. To the charge that utilitarianism demands too much — we cannot calculate consequences before every act — Mill replies that we need not: mankind has learned from experience general rules (do not lie, do not steal, keep promises) whose observance tends to promote happiness. This is the seed of the act-versus-rule dispute dominating later commentary. To the charge that happiness is too vague or unattainable, Mill replies that happiness is not a life of rapture but a life with moments of exalted enjoyment, few pains, and many pleasures — and that unhappiness's main causes are remediable: poverty, disease, bad laws, and selfishness, not human nature itself.


**Main argument:** The greatest-happiness principle, rightly understood — with quality of pleasures, impartiality, and general rules — is a credible standard for right action, not a philosophy for swine.

### *Utilitarianism*, Chapter 3 — Of the Ultimate Sanction of the Principle of Utility (summary)

Chapter 3 asks not whether utility is the right standard, but what motivates people to follow it. External sanctions are familiar: hope of reward or fear of punishment from others and from God. But the ultimate sanction, on which the theory's grip really depends, is internal: conscience, the pain of violating an internalized standard and the pleasure of living up to it. Mill's conscience is naturalistic — built by sympathy, education, association, and experience, not a supernatural voice — so it can be educated toward utility rather than merely obeyed. Critics ask: if conscience is merely a product of association, why should its deliverances carry authority? Mill's reply, in effect, is that this confuses origin with justification. Do not skip this short chapter: chapter 5's account of justice depends on moral feelings, educated by sympathy, as the raw material of a sense of justice.

**Main argument:** Utility's ultimate sanction is internal — conscience, educated by sympathy and experience — not merely external reward and punishment.

### *Utilitarianism*, Chapter 4 — Of What Sort of Proof the Principle of Utility is Susceptible (summary in outline; reconstruction below)

Chapter 4 is the most disputed short chapter in 19th-century ethics, and repays the closest reading here. Mill concedes that first principles cannot be proved in the ordinary sense: you cannot prove happiness good by reasoning from more certain premises, because any such premises would presuppose a standard of good. What can be offered is weaker but, Mill thinks, sufficient: evidence that people do desire happiness, and nothing else except as a means to it or as part of it.

The argument runs in three steps, each attacked since. First, the only proof that something is visible is that people see it, and audible that people hear it; so the only proof that something is desirable is that people desire it. Second, each person desires their own happiness, and nothing else except as a means to happiness or as part of it — virtue, first desired as a means, can come to be desired for itself, as part of happiness. Third, since each person's happiness is a good to that person, the general happiness is a good to the aggregate of all persons, and therefore the standard of right action. The reconstruction below sets this out formally, with the circularity objection and a reply.

**Main argument:** The principle of utility admits no direct proof, but the fact that happiness is universally desired is evidence that it is desirable, and the aggregate of individual happinesses is the general happiness.

### *Utilitarianism*, Chapter 5 — On the Connexion between Justice and Utility (summary)

Chapter 5 is the longest chapter, and the one that most complicates any simple reading of Mill as a crude consequentialist. Justice is the moral idea people feel most strongly about and are least willing to trade away, and Mill thinks that feeling needs explaining. His explanation has two parts. First, justice names moral rules concerning the essentials of well-being more nearly than any other rules: above all, security — the assurance that person, property, and expectations will not be arbitrarily violated. Security is the most vital interest, because without it no other good can be securely enjoyed, which is why rights feel inviolable: they protect utilities too important to sacrifice to ordinary expediency. Second, the sentiment of justice grows out of two natural feelings — self-defense and sympathy — enlarged until resentment at harm to oneself becomes resentment at harm to anyone.

The chapter then faces hard cases where justice and utility seem to conflict, such as punishing an innocent person to prevent a riot. Mill's answer is that the appearance of conflict confuses utility in a particular case with utility in the long run, as a system of rules: a rule permitting punishment of the innocent would destroy the very security on which utility depends. Whether that reconciliation succeeds is debated — critics say Mill smuggles rights in as independent constraints or defines utility so broadly it can justify anything; defenders call it his achievement. Chapter 5 is where you should test your own verdict.


**Main argument:** Justice is not an opponent of utility but its most stringent part: rights protect the security without which happiness itself is impossible.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The harm principle (*On Liberty*, ch. 1)

**Premises:**

1. The individual is sovereign over their own body and mind; society's power over a person needs a justification.
2. A person's own good, physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant for compelling them against their will — compulsion for their own sake treats an adult as a child.
3. Preventing harm to others is a sufficient warrant: each person's liberty ends where another's begins, and society has a legitimate interest in preventing one person from injuring another.
4. Persuasion, remonstrance, and social criticism are not compulsion, and remain available for conduct that harms no one.

**Conclusion:** The only purpose for which power may rightfully be exercised over a member of a civilized community, against their will, is to prevent harm to others.

**Objection:** The principle is indeterminate, because almost everything a person does affects others in some way. If "harm" includes offense, bad example, or erosion of shared morals, it permits nearly any interference; if it excludes all of those, it forbids interferences most would accept, such as regulating degrading public conduct. Mill's chapter 4 concessions — compelling testimony, education, and fair shares of burdens — show the boundary doing more work than the slogan admits.

**Reply:** Mill can grant that the boundary needs judgment while insisting the principle still disciplines it: it shifts the burden of proof onto whoever proposes compulsion, and rules out one whole class of justifications — "for your own good" and "because we disapprove." The chapter 4 cases are applications, not counterexamples: compelling testimony enforces a duty owed to others, not paternalism. Whether that saves determinacy in hard cases (offense, risk, nudging) remains a live dispute, and the right question to carry into chapter 5.

### (b) The argument for freedom of discussion (*On Liberty*, ch. 2)

**Premises:**

1. Suppressing an opinion assumes the suppressor is infallible about that opinion — an assumption no human institution can justify.
2. If the silenced opinion is true, society loses the opportunity to exchange error for truth.
3. If the silenced opinion is false, its collision with truth keeps true belief alive and understood, rather than a dead dogma repeated without comprehension.
4. Most commonly, received and dissenting opinions are each partly true, and each supplies what the other lacks; only their conflict brings the whole truth into view.

**Conclusion:** Freedom of thought and discussion must be protected even for opinions believed false, because silencing discussion harms society in every case.

**Objection:** Premise 1 overstates the point: a society can suppress an opinion while admitting it might be wrong, on the ground that the risk of allowing it — incitement, panic, the corruption of public judgment — outweighs the epistemic benefit. We suppress instructions for wrongdoing without claiming infallibility about them. Mill's own corn-dealer example concedes that expression can lose immunity when it becomes instigation, so the argument needs a theory of harmful speech, not just a theory of fallibility.

**Reply:** Mill's point is not that suppressors claim literal infallibility, but that suppression acts as if the question were settled, removing the process by which errors are corrected. The corn-dealer concession is consistent: it distinguishes discussion, which contributes to truth-seeking, from instigation, which bypasses it. The reply concedes the core, though — the line between discussion and instigation must be drawn by something beyond the infallibility argument, and drawing it is where modern free-speech theory still struggles.

### (c) The higher/lower pleasures argument (*Utilitarianism*, ch. 2)

**Premises:**

1. Happiness is pleasure and the absence of pain, but pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity.
2. The test of a pleasure's quality is the preference of competent judges — those who have experienced both the higher and the lower pleasure in question.
3. Competent judges, Mill claims, invariably prefer the higher pleasures (of intellect, imagination, feeling, and moral sentiment) to lower pleasures of mere sensation, even at the cost of greater discontent.
4. A preference formed by experience of both kinds is evidence of greater value, not merely of taste.

**Conclusion:** Higher pleasures are more valuable than lower pleasures, so the utilitarian standard must weigh quality as well as quantity — and a life rich in higher pleasures can be better than a life richer in lower pleasures alone.

**Objection:** The argument is circular, or it abandons hedonism. If competent judges are defined as those who prefer higher pleasures, premise 3 is true by definition. And if higher pleasures are preferred regardless of the amount of pleasure they contain, then pleasure is no longer the sole standard of value — something else (dignity, nobility, the exercise of human faculties) is doing the work, and Mill has smuggled in a perfectionist value while calling himself a hedonist.

**Reply:** Mill defines competence by experience of both kinds, not by the verdict reached, so premise 3 is empirical and falsifiable: experienced judges could prefer the lower life, and Mill insists they do not. On the second charge, the preference of competent judges is itself a fact about what pleasures are like to those who know them — higher pleasures are preferred because of the kind of pleasure they are, not some separate value. Whether that reconciles quality with hedonism, or merely restates the problem, is one of the most debated questions in Mill scholarship.

### (d) The "proof" of the principle of utility (*Utilitarianism*, ch. 4)

**Premises:**

1. First principles cannot be proved by reasoning from more certain premises; the most that can be offered is evidence of the kind the subject admits.
2. The only evidence that something is visible is that people see it, and the only evidence that something is audible is that people hear it; by parallel, the only evidence that something is desirable is that people desire it.
3. Each person desires their own happiness, and desires nothing else except as a means to happiness or as part of happiness (virtue, for example, comes to be desired for itself once it is associated with happiness).
4. Each person's happiness is therefore a good to that person, and the sum of all persons' happiness — the general happiness — is a good to the aggregate of all persons.

**Conclusion:** The general happiness is the proper standard of right action, and the principle of utility is established as far as a first principle can be.

**Objection:** The argument commits the naturalistic fallacy, as G. E. Moore charged: it moves from people *do* desiring happiness to happiness *ought* to be desired, and from "desirable" as "capable of being desired" to "desirable" as "worthy of being desired." The visible/audible parallel exposes the slide: "visible" means "can be seen," but "desirable" in a moral argument must mean "ought to be desired," and no grammatical parallel bridges that gap. Premise 4 also slides from each person's happiness being good *for them* to the general happiness being good *simpliciter*, without showing the aggregate inherits the goodness of its parts.

**Reply:** Defenders argue Mill offers no deductive proof, and says so himself: chapter 4 offers considerations capable of determining the intellect, of the kind appropriate to a first principle. On this reading, "desirable" is not smuggled between senses; desire is our only access to value, and a standard no one desired could not be a standard for human conduct. And the move from individual to general happiness is not an inference but a restatement of impartiality: if each person's happiness counts as a good, none may be discounted in the aggregate. This defense has a price: it concedes chapter 4 persuades rather than proves — which is why it is widely regarded as fallacious in deductive form and suggestive in looser form. Do not settle the debate until you have read Moore's criticism and at least one defense.


## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check yourself against the answers at the end of this guide.

1. State the harm principle exactly as Mill states it in *On Liberty* chapter 1, and explain the difference between compulsion and persuasion in his account.
2. Why does Mill say he fears the tyranny of the majority exercised through opinion more than political despotism?
3. In chapter 2, what happens, on Mill's view, when a true opinion is held without understanding its grounds — and why is that a loss?
4. What are the three cases Mill considers for a silenced opinion, and what does society lose in each?
5. Explain the corn-dealer example: why is the same speech protected discussion in one context and punishable instigation in another?
6. What is an "experiment in living," and why does Mill think society needs experiments it cannot predict the outcome of?
7. Why does Mill think custom is the enemy of individuality, and what faculties does choosing a life develop?
8. In chapter 4, what duties may society compel a person to perform, and why are those compulsions consistent with the harm principle?
9. Give one example from chapter 5 where Mill permits regulation, and one where he forbids it, and explain the principle behind each.
10. State the greatest-happiness principle as Mill states it in *Utilitarianism* chapter 2. Whose happiness counts, and how?
11. What is the "doctrine worthy of swine" objection, and how does the higher/lower pleasures distinction answer it?
12. Who counts as a competent judge of pleasures, and why does Mill think their verdict settles questions of quality?
13. Why does Mill think utilitarianism does not require us to calculate consequences before every action?
14. In chapter 3, what is the ultimate sanction of utility, and how does Mill's account of conscience differ from a supernatural one?
15. Reconstruct Mill's "proof" of utility in chapter 4 in three steps, and state Moore's naturalistic-fallacy objection to it.
16. In chapter 5, why does Mill say security is the most vital of all interests, and how does that explain why rights feel inviolable?

## Common misreadings

**1. "The harm principle means anything goes, so long as no one is physically hurt."**
Harm includes definite damage to others' interests — fraud, defamation, failing in a duty, creating unreasonable risk — not merely physical injury, and the principle permits compulsion where duties are owed. But harm is not offense, dislike, or moral disapproval; hurt feelings do not trigger it. The hard cases — offense, risk, example — test the principle rather than abandoning it, and scholars disagree about how far it reaches. What it rules out categorically is compulsion for a person's own good or for morality's sake alone.

**2. "Higher pleasures are just snobbery — Mill prefers opera to pushpin because he is an intellectual."**
The doctrine is not a list of approved tastes imposed from above; it is a test. A pleasure counts as higher if those who have experienced both kinds prefer it, even at the cost of discontent. Mill's examples — Socrates dissatisfied versus a fool satisfied — are deliberately provocative, but the test is experiential, not class-based, and falsifiable: if experienced judges preferred the lower life, Mill's claim would fail. Whether it works, and whether it smuggles in a non-hedonistic value, is debated (see reconstruction (c)); but it is an argument, not a prejudice.

**3. "Chapter 4 proves utilitarianism."**
Mill himself says first principles cannot be proved in the ordinary sense; chapter 4 offers evidence of the kind the subject admits, not a deduction. It is widely regarded as fallacious in deductive form — Moore's charge that it slides from "desired" to "desirable" has never been fully answered — and suggestive in looser form, as an appeal to the fact that happiness is what people actually pursue. Treat it as an invitation to judge what support a first principle can have, not as a proof to accept or refute wholesale. The debate is live, and this guide presents both sides without settling it.

**4. "Utilitarianism is egoism — it tells you to maximise your own happiness."**
Mill's standard is the happiness of all concerned, counted impartially: each counts for one, none for more than one. The agent's own happiness counts, but no more than anyone else's. The confusion comes from chapter 4, which begins from what each person desires for themselves; but its conclusion is the general happiness, not self-interest. Sacrificing a stranger's happiness for one's own, without greater total gain, violates the theory rather than applying it.

**5. "*On Liberty* is a theory of absolute free speech with no exceptions."**
Mill allows limits: expression becoming positive instigation to a mischievous act (the corn-dealer case) loses immunity, and chapter 4 permits compulsion for definite duties and harms. He also restricts the principle to adults in civilized communities — a limitation modern readers rightly find troubling, flagged as contested in the companion entry. What he defends absolutely is thought and discussion as a process: opinions may not be suppressed because they are believed false. That is narrower, and stronger, than "say anything, anywhere."

**6. "Mill is just Bentham with better prose."**
Mill inherits Bentham's principle but changes it in ways that matter: qualitative pleasures replace a purely quantitative calculus; rights and justice gain a central, explained place in chapter 5 rather than dismissal as "nonsense upon stilts"; liberty is defended as a permanent human interest, not a policy preference. Bentham is the foil the learning path pairs with him, but the differences are the substance of Mill's originality. Readers who collapse them will miss why Mill, not Bentham, became the canonical liberal.

**7. "Mill's defence of liberty has nothing to do with his utilitarianism."**
It is tempting to read *On Liberty* as rights-based and *Utilitarianism* as a separate theory, and some scholars argue the two cannot be reconciled. But Mill grounds liberty in utility: chapter 1 appeals to "the permanent interests of man as a progressive being," and chapter 3 defends individuality because it serves progress. Whether that grounding succeeds — whether utility-based liberty can be as strong as the language of sovereignty suggests — is the central interpretive dispute about Mill, and should be held open rather than resolved by reading one work and ignoring the other.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **The principle (ch. 1):** "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." **Compulsion** is coercion by law or overwhelming social pressure; **persuasion** — reasoning, remonstrating, entreating — remains always permitted, even for harmless conduct.
2. **Social tyranny** penetrates more deeply into life than political despotism, leaves fewer means of escape (there is no frontier to flee across from opinion), and enforces itself through constant pressure of respectability and custom rather than occasional acts of state power.
3. **A true opinion held without understanding its grounds is a dead dogma,** not a living belief: repeated as a formula, undefended when challenged, doing no intellectual work. Truth ceases to be understood, and so ceases to guide conduct intelligently.
4. **If true,** society loses the chance to exchange error for truth. **If false,** it loses the collision keeping true belief alive and understood. **If partly true** (the most common case), it loses the part of the truth the dissenting opinion contains and the received opinion lacks.
5. **The corn-dealer example** turns on context: telling a newspaper reader that corn dealers starve the poor is discussion; telling the same thing to an excited mob outside a corn dealer's house is positive instigation to a mischievous act. The words are the same; what changes is whether speech functions as argument or as trigger to action.
6. **An experiment in living** is a new way of life tried by an individual, whose value cannot be known in advance. Society needs such experiments because progress depends on discovering better ways of living than the received ones, and no one can know which will succeed without trying them.
7. **Custom** replaces choice with imitation: people ask what is done, not what suits their character. **Choosing** develops perception, judgment, discrimination, mental activity, and moral preference — faculties conformity leaves unused, whose exercise is itself part of well-being.
8. **Society may compel** a fair share of common burdens, testimony, contribution to the common defense, and children's education. These enforce **duties owed to others**, not paternalism for the agent's own good.
9. **Permitted:** regulating drink sales, or punishing drunkenness when it causes harm (e.g. a soldier drunk on duty), because others are affected. **Forbidden:** prohibiting drinking as such, or criminalising private vice on moral grounds alone, because only the agent's own good is at stake.
10. **The greatest-happiness principle:** actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse — happiness meaning pleasure and the absence of pain. **Everyone's happiness counts equally:** the standard is the greatest happiness of all concerned, impartially counted.
11. **The swine objection** charges that a pleasure-based ethics reduces human beings to animals. **Higher pleasures answer it** by distinguishing pleasures of intellect, imagination, feeling, and moral sentiment from mere sensation, and claiming the former are more valuable — so a human life is not best measured by quantity of sensation.
12. **A competent judge** has experienced both the higher and the lower pleasure in question. Their verdict settles quality, on Mill's view, because experience of both kinds is the only evidence about which is more valuable — as only someone who has tasted two wines can judge between them.
13. **Because mankind has learned general rules** from experience — do not lie, do not steal, keep promises — whose observance tends to promote happiness. Follow those rules rather than calculating afresh before every act; calculation is for the rare case, and for the legislator designing rules, not everyday conduct.
14. **The ultimate sanction is internal: conscience,** the pain of violating an internalized standard and the pleasure of living up to it. Mill's conscience is **naturalistic** — built by sympathy, education, and association — not a supernatural voice, so it can be educated toward utility rather than merely obeyed.
15. **Three steps:** (i) first principles admit no direct proof, only evidence of the kind the subject admits; (ii) the only evidence that something is desirable is that people desire it (as visible is proved by seeing); (iii) each person desires their own happiness, so the general happiness is a good to the aggregate. **Moore's objection:** the argument slides from "desired" (fact) to "desirable" (value), committing the naturalistic fallacy; "visible" means "can be seen," but "desirable" in a moral argument must mean "ought to be desired."
16. **Security is the most vital interest** because without assurance that person, property, and expectations will not be arbitrarily violated, no other good can be securely enjoyed. **Rights feel inviolable** because they protect exactly these essential utilities — the groundwork of existence — so violating them threatens the conditions of happiness itself, not merely one pleasure among others.
