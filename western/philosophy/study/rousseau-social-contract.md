# Study Guide: Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762)

**Work:** *The Social Contract* (*Du contrat social; ou, Principes du droit politique*) · **Author:** Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) · **First published:** 1762 · **What this guide covers:** all four books of *The Social Contract*, with argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings. The [learning path](../learning-path.md) assigns Books I–II as the core (read after Part II of the *Discourse on Inequality*); Books III–IV are covered here as the completion, and the reading plan below marks which is which.

## About the work

*The Social Contract* appeared in the spring of 1762 — the same season as *Émile* — and was condemned and burned in Paris and Geneva within the year. Rousseau described it as an extract from a much larger projected work, *Political Institutions*, which he had abandoned; what survived is a short, dense treatise of principles, not a program. Its question is announced in the first chapter: man is born free and everywhere is in chains — how did that come about is a historical question he sets aside; "what can make it legitimate?" is the question the book answers. The answer is a single idea pursued relentlessly: authority over human beings is legitimate only when it rests on a convention in which each person, obeying, obeys only himself — the social pact, whose laws express the **general will**.

The book needs its predecessor. The *Discourse on Inequality* (which the path reads first, Part II) tells the diagnostic story: natural man moved by *amour de soi* (self-preservation) and pity, corrupted through comparison, dependence, and *amour-propre* into the chained creature of the opening line. *The Social Contract* does not continue that history; it changes the subject from what happened to what could be justified. A reader who skips the *Discourse* will misunderstand the *Contract*'s tone: Rousseau is not designing a state for angels but asking what legitimacy could possibly mean for beings already social, already corrupted, and unable to go back.

One warning before the walkthrough: Book IV contains long antiquarian chapters on Roman institutions and a famous, troubling chapter on civil religion. Read them — the path's Books I–II core is where the theory is built, but Book IV is where Rousseau tests whether a people formed by his principles could actually endure, and IV.viii is among the most debated chapters in political philosophy.

The book's central term has a prehistory worth knowing. Rousseau had already used the *general will* in his article on political economy for the *Encyclopédie* (1755), where the concept does fiscal and administrative work; the *Contract* radicalizes it into the sole title of legitimate sovereignty. An early draft of the *Contract* itself — the so-called Geneva Manuscript, unpublished in his lifetime — shows him working the argument out, and scholars use it to test readings of the published text: where the two differ (notably on natural religion and on the general will of mankind), the published book is the considered position. The fate of the published book completes the context: condemned in Paris in June 1762 and burned at Geneva days later, it drove Rousseau into exile and made its author, for the authorities of two states, exactly what his theory said no citizen could be — a man whose obedience they demanded without his authorship of the laws. He would spend years answering them, in the *Letter to Beaumont* and the *Letters Written from the Mountain* (1764), the latter of which returns to the civil-religion chapter and matters again at IV.8 below.

## How to use this guide

Read each book or chapter-cluster of *The Social Contract* first, then the corresponding entry below. The argument reconstructions in §2 — the founding problem and its solution, the general will, and unrepresentable sovereignty — are the three arguments to master; they are also the three on which the "totalitarian Rousseau" controversy turns, and the guide presents that controversy as the open debate it is. Work the checkpoint questions book by book, covering the answers (in §5) until you have attempted them.

Rousseau's French is precise in ways translations strain to keep: *volonté générale*, *souverain*, *gouvernement*, *dépôt*. When an argument seems to turn on a word, it usually does — the notes below flag the places (sovereignty vs. government above all) where keeping the distinction straight is the whole game.

Read the book comparatively, as the path's Stage 3 thread intends. Rousseau wrote against Hobbes's authorization (a sovereign you may not judge) and against Locke's consent (a people that reserves its rights) and against Montesquieu's separated powers (which II.2 calls a dismemberment of sovereignty). After each book, ask which of the three he is answering: Book I is largely Hobbes and Grotius, Book II Locke by contrast and Montesquieu by name, Book III the English constitution Montesquieu praised. The comparisons are not decoration — Rousseau's positions are defined by what they refuse, and the thread's payoff in the learning path is seeing the refusals line up.

## 1. Book-by-book guide

### Book I: the pact

**Chapter 1 — the subject of the book.** The famous opening: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains." Rousseau declines to explain how subjection arose and sets the book's question: what can make subjection *legitimate*? Everything after is an answer to that one question.

**Chapters 2–4 — clearing false titles.** Chapter 2 denies that the family models political authority (children owe obedience only while they need preservation) and denies Aristotle's natural slaves their alleged nature. Chapter 3: force creates no right — "the strongest is never strong enough to be always the master, unless he transforms strength into right, and obedience into duty." Chapter 4, on slavery, makes the logic explicit: to renounce liberty is to renounce being human; a convention that establishes absolute authority on one side and unlimited obedience on the other is "empty and contradictory." Against Grotius, a people cannot alienate itself to a king; against Aristotle, men are slaves not by nature but by force — Aristotle "took the effect for the cause."

**Chapter 5 — the need for a first convention.** Even a legitimate majority rule presupposes one unanimous act: the pact by which a multitude becomes a people capable of willing at all. Majority voting binds only because the pact established it.

**Chapter 6 — the social pact.** The *Contract*'s center. The problem, stated as an engineering specification: "Find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before." The solution is total alienation, structured so as to cancel itself out: each associate gives himself, with all his rights, to the whole community — and "each man, in giving himself to all, gives himself to nobody," receiving back the equivalent of everything he surrenders, plus the force of the whole to preserve it. What emerges is a moral and collective body — the republic or body politic — whose members as sovereign are *citizens* and as law-subjects are *subjects*.

**Chapter 7 — the sovereign; "forced to be free."** The act of association creates a double obligation: each individual contracts both as member of the sovereign toward subjects and as member of the state toward the sovereign. Whoever refuses to obey the general will "shall be compelled to do so by the whole body: this means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free." The sentence is the book's most notorious; read it with its context attached — the compulsion holds a citizen to laws he has co-authored in his public capacity, and its defense and its danger are taken up in §2(a) and §4.3.

**Chapter 8 — the state of nature to the civil state.** The passage transforms the person: justice replaces instinct, actions gain morality, and man acquires *moral liberty* — "obedience to the law one has prescribed for oneself" — in place of the mere "impulse of appetite," which Rousseau flatly calls slavery. Note the candor: what man loses is natural liberty (limited only by his strength); what he gains is civil liberty, property, and a self.

**Chapter 9 — real property.** The pact converts mere possession into right: what each held by force he now holds under the general will's guarantee, and the community's right over all grounds each member's secure title. Rousseau states the appropriation conditions soberly — the land must be unoccupied, no more than one needs, taken by labor and cultivation — and warns that the right of the first occupant is everywhere subordinate to the community's right.

### Book II: sovereignty and the general will

**Chapters 1–2 — sovereignty inalienable and indivisible.** Sovereignty is the exercise of the general will, and will cannot be transferred: a people that promises simply to obey a master dissolves itself as a people (ch. 1). Nor can sovereignty be divided into legislative, executive, taxing, and judicial "parts": those are emanations of it, and theorists who dismember it mistake the machinery for the authority (ch. 2). Both chapters are load-bearing for III.15's attack on representation.

**Chapter 3 — whether the general will can err.** The general will "is always right and always tends to the public advantage" — but the people judging can be deceived, and then what they will is not the general will. Here stands the book's most important distinction: the **will of all** is "merely a sum of particular wills," while the general will "regards only the common interest." Remove from the particular wills the pluses and minuses that cancel each other out, and the general will "remains as the sum of the differences." Hence the institutional corollaries: citizens must be adequately informed, and there should be no partial associations (factions) within the state — or, if there are, their number should be multiplied so none dominates. Voting, on this account, is not the expression of preference but a judgment about the common good; §2(b) reconstructs the argument.

**Chapter 4 — the limits of sovereign power.** The sovereign cannot burden subjects with useless chains or target individuals: acts of sovereignty must be general, because the general will loses its nature if it pronounces on a particular person or fact. The same generality that empowers the sovereign confines it.

**Chapters 5–6 — life, death, and law.** Chapter 5 grounds punishment and pardon in the pact (the lawbreaker becomes a public enemy; pardon belongs to the sovereign alone, and frequent pardons signal decay). Chapter 6 defines **law**: when the whole people decrees concerning the whole people, will and object are both general — "I give the name Republic to every State that is ruled by laws." Laws need a guide in their drafting, because the people "always wills the good, but does not always see it" — the bridge to the Legislator.

**Chapter 7 — the Legislator.** The strangest figure in the book. The Legislator drafts the fundamental laws for a people not yet formed to receive them, yet holds no office: his function "is no part of the constitution," neither magistracy nor sovereignty, and he can neither command (he has no authority) nor persuade (a raw people cannot yet follow the reasoning). He must therefore appeal to divine sanction — Moses, Lycurgus, Numa put their laws in the mouths of the gods — while Rousseau insists the Legislator's greatness is proved by the wisdom of the laws, not the trick. The paradox is structural: for a people to will good laws, the effect (a formed citizenry) would have to become the cause. Rousseau does not hide this; he displays it, and §4.7 notes the standing worry.

**Chapters 8–12 — the people, and the kinds of law.** A people fit for legislation is one bound by some union but not yet corrupted by deep-rooted custom — small enough to know itself, neither rich nor poor, self-sufficient. Corsica is named as Europe's one candidate. The system's two aims are liberty and **equality** — not sameness of wealth, but that "no citizen shall ever be wealthy enough to buy another, and none poor enough to be forced to sell himself" (II.11). II.12 closes the book by classifying laws: political (fundamental) laws, civil laws, criminal laws — and a fourth kind, "morals, customs, and above all public opinion," on which the rest silently depend.

### Book III: government

**Chapters 1–3 — what government is.** Government is an intermediary body between sovereign and subjects, charged solely with executing the laws: a *commission*, not a contracting party. Rousseau gives the relation a quasi-mathematical form (sovereign : government :: government : subjects), and one durable consequence: as the people grows, government must grow relatively stronger and liberty shrinks — his standing argument that the system suits small states. Book III's classification (ch. 3) counts magistrates: government by all or most is *democracy*, by the few *aristocracy*, by one *monarchy* — three forms of *government*, none of them forms of sovereignty, which is one and popular throughout.

**Chapters 4–8 — the forms assessed.** Democracy as government — the people itself executing the laws — would suit "a people of gods"; for men it demands impossible conditions (a tiny state, simple manners, great equality, little luxury). Elective aristocracy is "the best and most natural arrangement," the wisest governing in the people's name under law. Monarchy concentrates force and corrupts most easily: the court's interest is a standing conspiracy against the general will, and royal education forms men to rule badly. Mixed governments and climate (chs. 7–8) fill out the taxonomy; the climate chapter can be read quickly.

**Chapters 9–11 — health and decay.** The sign of a good government is brutally empirical: the population grows (ch. 9). But every body politic carries its death within it (chs. 10–11): the particular will of the government acts incessantly against the general will; executive power usurps; the state dissolves into tyranny — which for Rousseau means not rule by a bad man but the breakdown of law's empire altogether.

**Chapters 12–18 — assemblies, deputies, and the people in session.** The sovereign maintains itself only by assembling: when the people is legitimately assembled as sovereign, "all jurisdiction of the government ceases" (chs. 12–14). Chapter 15, "Deputies or Representatives," draws the consequence the book is famous for: sovereignty "cannot be represented, for the same reason that it cannot be alienated"; deputies are mere agents; a law the people has not ratified in person is null; and "the people of England regards itself as free; but it is grossly mistaken; it is free only during the election of members of parliament. As soon as they are elected, slavery overtakes it." Chapters 16–17 deny that instituting a government is a contract between people and rulers (it is a law plus an executive act), and ch. 18 prescribes periodic assemblies opening with two questions: does it please the sovereign to preserve the present form of government, and does it please the people to leave the administration to those now charged with it?

### Book IV: the working institutions — and civil religion

**Chapters 1–3 — voting.** While citizens still deliberate as citizens, the general will is nearly indestructible (ch. 1); it falls silent when private interest and faction capture the assembly. Chapter 2 grades unanimity: the graver the matter, the nearer the vote should approach unanimity; the original pact alone requires it absolutely, all later majorities binding only because the pact says so. Elections (ch. 3) divide between choice (for offices needing talent) and lot (for burdens properly democratic).

**Chapters 4–7 — the Roman machinery.** Chapter 4, on the Roman comitia, is a long antiquarian proof that a numerous people really can assemble and legislate; skim it for that function. The tribunate (ch. 5) is a preserving magistracy outside the constitution, able to *prevent* everything and *do* nothing — powerful and dangerous in proportion. The dictatorship (ch. 6) is Rousseau's concession to emergency: in a crisis that laws cannot foresee, a supreme chief may silence the laws for a strictly limited term — the Roman six months — and the chapter's warning is that extending the term is how republics die (Sulla, Caesar). The censorate (ch. 7) publicly declares the judgment of public opinion, useful for preserving morals, powerless to restore them.

**Chapter 8 — civil religion.** The book's most debated chapter, and it must be read factually. Rousseau surveys three religions in their political effects: the religion of *man* (pure, inward Christianity) — true, he says, but politically dissolving, since it detaches citizens' hearts from the state; the religion of the *citizen* (a national cult) — binding but intolerant and founded on error; and the religion of the *priest* (dual authority, as in Rome's church) — simply bad. His remedy is a purely **civil profession of faith**, whose articles the sovereign fixes "not exactly as religious dogmas, but as sentiments of sociability": the existence of a powerful, intelligent, beneficent divinity; the life to come; the happiness of the just; the punishment of the wicked; the sanctity of the social contract and the laws. To these positive dogmas he adds one negative one — the rejection of intolerance — and the chapter states its teeth plainly: whoever does not believe the dogmas may be banished, "not for impiety, but as an unsociable being"; and whoever, having publicly professed them, acts as if he does not believe them, "has lied before the laws" and may be put to death. Christianity as actually preached he judges worse than useless for the state ("it preaches only servitude and dependence"), while allowing that the Gospel's own religion of man is holy — a tension commentators have pressed ever since, not least because Rousseau elsewhere (the *Letter from the Mountain*, 1764) defends himself differently. §4.6 and the checkpoints take up how this sits with toleration.

**Chapter 9 — conclusion.** Having laid down the principles of political right, Rousseau turns at the end to what remains — external relations, federations, the law of nations — and defers it to the larger work the *Contract* was extracted from. The treatise closes where its problem closes: legitimacy is possible; the conditions are now on the table.

## 2. Argument reconstructions

### (a) The founding problem and the pact's solution (I.6–I.8)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. Authority over human beings is legitimate only if it does not destroy the freedom of those subject to it: men give up their natural liberty only for something that preserves them, and a convention of absolute obedience is self-contradictory (I.4).
2. The required form of association must therefore meet a specification: it must defend each associate with the whole common force while each, "uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before" (I.6).
3. Total alienation meets it. If each gives himself wholly, without reserve, the condition is equal for all, so no one has an interest in making it burdensome; and since each gives himself to the whole community and not to any person, "each man, in giving himself to all, gives himself to nobody."
4. The act creates a collective moral body whose will — directed by the common interest — is the general will; its laws are the acts of that will, hence laws the citizens prescribe to themselves.
5. A citizen compelled to obey such a law is therefore held to his own legislation in his public capacity — in Rousseau's phrase, "forced to be free" (I.7) — and the freedom he now has is moral liberty: obedience to self-prescribed law, replacing the slavery of appetite (I.8).
6. Therefore, a social pact of total, equal alienation to the whole community makes authority legitimate without costing freedom: what is surrendered (natural liberty) is exchanged for civil and moral liberty under laws of one's own co-authorship.

**Conclusion.** Legitimacy is possible — conditionally. The whole construction stands or falls with premise 4: the laws must actually be acts of the general will, general in form and co-authored by all, or the "forced to be free" of premise 5 becomes the fraud its critics allege.

**One standard objection.** The phrase in premise 5 gave the twentieth century its indictment: Isaiah Berlin classed Rousseau's freedom among the dangerous "positive" liberties, and Jacob Talmon read the *Contract* as a charter of "totalitarian democracy" — a power claiming to know the citizen's real will better than he does can coerce him and call the coercion his freedom. Total alienation (premise 3) leaves no reserved-rights foothold from which to resist, and premise 4's "general will" is exactly the sort of abstraction a party or leader can claim to embody.

**One reply.** Rousseau's defenders answer from the constraints built into the text itself. The general will cannot be embodied: sovereignty cannot be represented (III.15) or alienated (II.1), so any leader claiming to incarnate it is a usurper by the book's own criteria. Its acts must be general — laws from all, applying to all, naming no one (II.4, II.6) — so persecution of particular persons cannot take legal form. And "forced to be free" governs compliance with laws one has co-authored, in a polity whose conditions (small scale, rough equality, no dominant factions) Rousseau states as preconditions and warns will usually fail. Whether these constraints suffice is the live question — the reply shows the abuse requires breaking Rousseau's rules, not following them; critics answer that rules a theory cannot enforce against its own devotees are frail protection. The debate is presented here as the library's entry presents it: open.

### (b) The general will and the will of all (II.3)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. When citizens vote on a proposed law, the question before each is not "do I prefer this?" but "is this conformable to the general will?" — the common interest, which each citizen shares qua citizen (II.3; IV.2).
2. Each citizen also has a particular will, as a private person, which may diverge from the common interest.
3. Across a whole assembly, the particular deviations are not coordinated: the pluses and minuses of private interest cancel one another out, leaving "the sum of the differences" as the general will.
4. The cancellation requires conditions: citizens adequately informed, deliberating without the distortion of partial associations; if factions exist, they must be many and none dominant, or the cancellation fails and the result is the will of the strongest partial association.
5. Therefore, a majority vote taken under these conditions *declares* (does not constitute) the general will — and a voter in the minority learns from the outcome not that his preference lost, but that he was mistaken about the common good.

**Conclusion.** Voting in Rousseau's republic is epistemic, not aggregative: it aims at a fact of the matter — the common interest — which is why the general will is "always right" while the people's judgment of it can err. The theory stands or falls with premises 1 and 4: that citizens can ask the common-good question, and that its answer survives aggregation.

**One standard objection.** The model is fragile to the point of emptiness. Real voters ask the preference question, not the common-good question; deviations do not cancel randomly but correlate — wealth, media, and party line them up, which is precisely premise 4's failure case, and it is the normal case. Worse, the epistemic reading licenses the losing minority to be told their defeat proves their error — a rhetorical machine for manufacturing consent: if the vote goes against the "general will," redefine the vote as corrupted.

**One reply.** Defenders reply that Rousseau knows all of this and prices it in: the conditions in II.3, II.8–11 (informed citizens, no dominant factions, rough equality, small polity) are stated as the regime's *preconditions*, not as afterthoughts, and IV.1–2 describes exactly the descent into faction and private interest as the body politic's death. On this reading the argument is a standard against which actual democracies are measured and found wanting — a critical tool, not an apology. Whether a standard so rarely instantiated can do political work, or merely consoles, remains debated — as does the modern deliberative-democracy attempt to rebuild its conditions institutionally.

### (c) Sovereignty cannot be represented (II.1; III.15)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. Sovereignty is the exercise of the general will: the people's legislative willing of the common good.
2. A will cannot be transferred or represented: either a supposed representative's will is the people's will (then it is the people willing, not a representation of it) or it is another will (then it is not sovereignty).
3. Therefore sovereignty is inalienable and cannot be represented; deputies can be the people's *agents* (commisaires) carrying out settled business, but they cannot conclude anything definitively in the people's name.
4. A law the assembled people has not ratified in person is therefore null; the English system — surrendering legislative willing to Parliament between elections — delivers freedom only at election time and slavery in between.
5. Therefore, legitimate legislation requires the people's own ratification; representation may administer, but it may not legislate.

**Conclusion.** Modern representative *legislatures* are, by Rousseau's criteria, usurpations of sovereignty unless their acts return to the people for ratification; what survives legitimately is representative *government* — executive delegation under laws the people itself has made.

**One standard objection.** Scale makes the conclusion unusable: a state of millions cannot assemble, and Rousseau concedes his system suits small polities — so for actual modern states the argument issues in either irrelevance or a nostalgia with no institutional translation. Worse, the one obvious translation — plebiscites and referendums — hands lawmaking to exactly the uninformed, faction-driven voting II.3 warns against, on questions framed by executives.

**One reply.** Defenders answer that the argument's target was never assembly mechanics but the locus of authority: laws bind because the people is their author, and any institutional device — ratification requirements, constitutional referendums, citizen assemblies — honors the principle insofar as law's *authorship* stays popular while its drafting and administration are delegated. Read this way, III.15 is the standing critique of purely electoral legitimacy ("free only during elections") that democratic theory from the Federalist's critics to contemporary participatory democrats has had to answer — less a blueprint than a test no modern system passes cleanly, which Rousseau would have regarded as the point.

## 3. Checkpoint questions

Attempt these before reading the answers in §5.

1. State the problem of I.6 exactly: what must the form of association achieve, in Rousseau's own formulation?
2. Why does Rousseau argue that force can never create right (I.3), and what transformation does he say the strongest must perform?
3. What does each associate alienate in the pact, and why does "giving himself to all" amount to "giving himself to nobody"?
4. Explain "forced to be free" (I.7) in its context: who compels whom, to obey what, and on what ground is the compulsion called freedom?
5. Distinguish the general will from the will of all, and explain the "pluses and minuses" passage (II.3).
6. On Rousseau's account, what question is a citizen answering when he votes — and what does it mean for a minority voter to be told he was "mistaken"?
7. Why can sovereignty be neither alienated (II.1) nor represented (III.15)? What role remains legitimate for deputies?
8. What is the difference between the sovereign and the government (III.1), and why is government a commission rather than a contracting party?
9. Who is the Legislator (II.7)? What is his paradox, and why does he appeal to the gods?
10. What are the sovereign's limits in II.4? Why must law be general in both its object and its source?
11. Why does Rousseau say the English are free only during elections (III.15)?
12. List the positive dogmas of the civil religion (IV.8) and its one negative dogma. What penalties attach to refusing or to betraying the profession?
13. How does the civil religion sit with toleration — where exactly is the tension, stated in Rousseau's own terms?

## 4. Common misreadings

1. **"The general will is whatever the majority votes for."** No: the general will is defined by its object — the common good, in laws general in source and application — and majorities can err about it, be deceived, or be captured by factions (II.3, IV.1). A vote total is at best a declaration of the general will under stated conditions, never its definition.
2. **"The totalitarian-Rousseau verdict is settled scholarship."** It is a famous twentieth-century reading (Talmon 1952; Berlin on positive liberty) and it identifies real dangers in "forced to be free" and total alienation. But it is contested, not settled: defenders cite the generality constraints, the ban on anyone embodying sovereignty, and the stated preconditions. Present the debate; do not deliver either side as the verdict.
3. **"'Forced to be free' licenses any coercion done in the people's interest."** In its I.7 context the phrase covers compelling a citizen to obey general laws he co-authors as sovereign — self-legislation enforced against private appetite. That is narrower than the slogan's afterlife, though whether the narrowing holds in practice is exactly the controversy of §2(a). Quote it with the context or not at all.
4. **"Rousseau's natural man is the 'noble savage.'"** The phrase is Dryden's, not Rousseau's, and his natural man is not noble: he is pre-moral — good in the sense of harmless, moved by self-preservation and pity, without virtue, vice, or comparison. Nobility would require the moral development only society brings.
5. **"The social contract is a historical event Rousseau believes happened."** Like the state of nature in the *Discourse*, the pact is a normative standard: the form an association *would* have to take to be legitimate. Rousseau is asking what could justify obedience, not reporting a founding ceremony.
6. **"Rousseau opposes representation, period."** He opposes representative *sovereignty*. Executive government is by design delegated to magistrates and agents (III.1), and his later writings for Poland contemplate deputies bound by instructions — mandates, not free legislative wills. The distinction between legislating and administering is the whole point.
7. **"The Legislator is Rousseau's dictator, or his philosopher-king."** The Legislator holds no office and no power: he can neither command nor (for a raw people) persuade; he drafts, proposes, and must persuade the people to ratify — his recourse to divine sanction is an admission of powerlessness, not a seizure of it. The figure is genuinely troubling (the people must ratify what they cannot yet judge), but the trouble is the paradox of founding, not a license to rule.
8. **"The civil religion is a state theocracy, full stop" — or its mirror image, "mere ceremonial deism."** Neither flattening survives IV.8. The creed is minimal and civic in content — five sentiments of sociability plus the ban on intolerance — yet it is enforced with banishment and, for the lying professor, death. Rousseau's own aim was toleration between sects; his instruments are coercion. Holding both halves at once is the reading the chapter demands, and the standing scholarly problem.

## 5. Answers to checkpoint questions

1. "Find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before" (I.6). Total security for persons and goods, purchased without any loss of self-government.
2. Force is a physical power, not a moral one: yielding to it is necessity, not duty, so no obligation and no right arise from it. To rule durably the strongest must "transform strength into right, and obedience into duty" — convert mere power into legitimate authority, which is the conversion only a valid convention can perform (I.3).
3. Each alienates himself totally — all his rights, without reserve — to the whole community. Because the gift is made to all equally and to no private person, and because he receives the equivalent of everything he gives plus the whole's force to defend it, he "gives himself to nobody": no master is created by the act (I.6).
4. The whole body compels the individual citizen (each as subject) to obey the general will — that is, the laws — which he has co-authored in his capacity as citizen (each as sovereign). The compulsion is called freedom because the law obeyed is self-prescribed: the citizen is held to his own public legislation against his private appetite. Whether that identity of author and subject is real enough to dignify the compulsion is the contested point of §2(a).
5. The will of all is the sum of particular wills — private preferences aggregated. The general will regards only the common interest. Take the particular wills and remove the pluses and minuses of private interest that cancel one another across the assembly, and "the sum of the differences" that remains is the general will (II.3). Aggregation counts preferences; the general will is what survives when divergent private interests neutralize each other — under conditions of information and no dominant factions.
6. He is answering "is this proposal conformable to the general will?" — a judgment about the common good, not an expression of his preference. If the contrary opinion prevails, that proves (on Rousseau's account) that he was mistaken about what the general will required: his defeat is treated as the correction of an error, not the frustration of a desire. This is why voting is epistemic in §2(b)'s reconstruction.
7. Sovereignty is the exercise of the general will, and a will cannot be transferred: an alienated or "represented" will is either the people's own (no representation) or someone else's (no sovereignty). Deputies remain legitimate as *agents* — commissioners executing settled mandates — but any law they purport to conclude definitively without the people's ratification is null (II.1; III.15).
8. The sovereign is the people as lawmaker — the source of law expressing the general will. The government is an intermediary body charged with executing the laws: magistrates applying what they did not make. It is a commission because the people creates, instructs, and can revoke it by law; a contract would set the government up as a co-equal party with rights against the sovereign, which II.1 forbids (III.1; III.16).
9. The Legislator is the founding law-drafter: an extraordinary outsider figure (Moses, Lycurgus, Numa) who frames the fundamental laws for a people not yet able to will them understandingly. His paradox: he has a task requiring authority he cannot possess — legislation belongs to the sovereign people; persuasion requires the very civic capacity the laws are meant to create — so the effect must become the cause. He appeals to the gods because a raw people will obey laws as divine commands that it could not yet follow as its own reasoned will; Rousseau insists the appeal honours only legislators whose laws prove wise (II.7).
10. The sovereign's acts must be general: the general will "loses its natural rectitude when it is directed to some particular and determinate object," so law cannot name individuals, single out persons, or impose burdens that serve no common purpose. Generality in source (from all) and object (applying to all) is what keeps sovereignty from decaying into the rule of particular wills — the majority's included (II.4; II.6).
11. Because sovereignty cannot be represented, entrusting legislation to Parliament transfers the people's willing to others; the people wills only in the act of electing, and between elections its deputies' wills rule in its place. "As soon as they are elected, slavery overtakes it" (III.15).
12. Positive dogmas: the existence of a powerful, intelligent, and beneficent divinity; the life to come; the happiness of the just; the punishment of the wicked; and the sanctity of the social contract and the laws. The one negative dogma is the rejection of intolerance. Penalties: banishment for the one who will not profess them — "not for impiety, but as an unsociable being"; death for the one who professes them publicly and then acts as if he does not believe them — he has "lied before the laws" (IV.8).
13. The tension is structural. Rousseau's aim is toleration between religions: no sect may claim exclusive salvation, and the state professes only civic sentiments. But the instrument is coerced profession with penal teeth — banishment and death — which is intolerance of the non-professor by any ordinary standard; and his judgment that historical Christianity "preaches only servitude and dependence" puts the majority religion of his readers on the wrong side of the civic test. Critics press the incoherence (a toleration enforced by death); defenders reply that the dogmas are the minimal conditions of citizenship, not a theology. The chapter is presented here, as in the library's entry, as a live problem, not a settled one.
