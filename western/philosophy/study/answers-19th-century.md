# Worked Answers: 19th-Century Study Questions

Model answers to the Study questions at the end of the 19th-century core entries. These are study aids, not substitutes for your own attempt: work each question book-closed first, as the [learning path](../learning-path.md) directs, then compare. Where a question asks you to judge a debate, the answer gives the strongest considerations on each side rather than a verdict — if your judgment differs and is argued, it can be equally valid.

## [Hegel](../eras/04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)

**1. The master–slave dialectic, step by step.**
(1) Self-consciousness cannot confirm its own freedom alone; it needs recognition from another self-consciousness. (2) When two meet, each demands that recognition, and the encounter becomes a struggle in which one risks death and the other submits through fear. (3) The victor — the master — receives recognition only from the bondsman, someone he himself treats as a thing and deems unfree. Recognition satisfies only when it comes from a consciousness recognized as free and independent, so the master's prize is worthless: he is confirmed by a witness he has already disqualified. (4) The bondsman, meanwhile, is transformed twice over: absolute fear has shaken his whole being loose, and formative work on resistant material disciplines desire and lets him see his own mind made durable in what he shapes. He thus achieves a self-relation — a mind of his own — deeper than the master's stagnant consumption. The conclusion is structural: one-sided domination defeats its own aim, and recognition must become mutual.

**2. Speculative anthropology, or conceptual proof?**
The objection: read as an account of how selfhood historically arose, the passage offers no evidence — no archaeology of a primal life-and-death struggle issuing in slavery and self-forming labor. It narrates where it should prove. Hegel's best reply, on the standard defense, is that the *Phenomenology* is not empirical history but a reconstruction of the logic of self-consciousness: whatever our actual prehistory, domination *structurally* cannot supply the recognition it seeks, because recognition counts only from someone recognized as free. That conceptual point is what launched Kojève's lectures and modern recognition theory. Judgment: the reply succeeds if the passage is read on its own terms — it concedes the historical reading and wins the conceptual one. The remaining vulnerability is whether a "logic of self-consciousness" can float free of all empirical claims about how selves actually form; critics who think it cannot will find the defense a retreat. A balanced verdict: stronger as phenomenology than as anthropology, and most readers now take it that way.

**3. *Sittlichkeit* against *Moralität*.**
For Kant, duty is tested by an abstract procedure: can the maxim be willed as universal law? Hegel objects that the test underdetermines content — it yields no concrete duties without quietly borrowing them from the surrounding social world. Ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*) supplies the determinacy: in the family, fidelity and care are specified by the roles themselves; in civil society, honesty, professional responsibility, and contract take definite shape within markets, professions, and law; in the state, duty becomes citizenship in institutions one can rationally endorse. Freedom is then not mere capacity to will but actually *finding oneself* in these institutions. The risk is exactly as the question suggests: if duties are read off existing institutions, philosophy may end up sanctifying them — the conservative reading pressed since Marx. Hegel's guard is his criterion (see Q4): institutions count as rational only insofar as they actualize freedom. Whether his own Prussian-era applications honor that criterion is a separate, open scholarly question.

**4. "The real is rational" and *Wirklichkeit*.**
The slogan sounds like a blessing on everything that happens. Hegel's distinction cuts it down: *existence* is whatever happens to be there; *actuality* (*Wirklichkeit*) is what embodies its rational structure — what a thing genuinely is when it realizes its concept. A corrupt, failing state exists, but is not fully actual *as a state*, because a state just is freedom organized, and this one fails at being what it purports to be. So "what is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational" licenses criticism of merely existing arrangements in the name of their own concept; it does not pronounce them good. How far does this answer the status-quo charge? Considerably, as a matter of doctrine — the distinction is real and does work in the *Philosophy of Right*. The residual worry is practical: the distinction gives no neutral test for which institutions are "really" rational, and Hegel's own applications leaned toward the arrangements of his time. The defense answers the crude charge; the subtler one — who judges actuality, and from where — remains debated.

**5. Application: recognition at work.**
The manager wants recognition *as* a superior, freely given — but she wants it from juniors she defines as interchangeable, i.e. from people whose judgment she has already discounted. By the dialectic's logic this cannot satisfy: being praised by someone you treat as a thing confirms nothing, because confirmation requires a recognizer whose recognition counts, and it counts only from an equal whose freedom you acknowledge. The emptiness she feels is therefore structural, not a personal failing, and no quantity of deference fixes it — an exact workplace analogue of the master's position. Mutual recognition would require changes in both directions: the juniors must be treated as independent centers of judgment whose assessment can genuinely be withheld — which means real voice, security to dissent, and work in which they too form something durable and see themselves in it (the bondsman's route to selfhood, now to be made general rather than servile); and the manager must accept recognition from people she recognizes, accepting the vulnerability that it can be refused. Other workplace examples can be analyzed equally well in these terms.

## [Kierkegaard](../eras/04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md)

**1. The teleological suspension of the ethical.**
The ethical — the universal moral order, voiced by society's norms and philosophy's systems — demands of Abraham what it demands of everyone: do not kill, above all not your innocent son; a father who raises the knife is a murderer. Faith demands instead absolute obedience to a singular divine command that cannot be justified in universal terms. Abraham is a knight of faith because, "by virtue of the absurd," he believes he will nevertheless receive Isaac back in this life, even while carrying out the command. De Silentio can neither *mediate* the act — mediation would translate Abraham back into the universal, where he is simply a criminal or insane — nor explain the *silence*: Abraham cannot speak, because anything he said (to Sarah, to Isaac) would state his act in the universal's language and falsify it. The paradox is the point: the single individual, in absolute relation to the absolute, stands higher than the universal, and this cannot be made intelligible to the ethical standpoint he has suspended.

**2. The Kantian scandal — and the missing check.**
For Kant, a supposed divine command that violates universal moral law must be rejected precisely *as* not God's: Abraham should have answered the voice that, since murder contradicts the moral law God himself authored, the command could not be divine. Certainty about morality outranks certainty about any putative revelation. Kierkegaard's Abraham has, frankly, no such check — no public criterion distinguishes God's voice from his own desire, and de Silentio's presentation embraces rather than repairs this: a check would be another mediation, re-subordinating faith to the universal. Is the absence a defect or the point? Defenders say the point: faith that could be verified would be knowledge, and the terror of the category is its singularity — Abraham is admirable, on this telling, exactly where he cannot be imitated or generalized into a licence. Critics, including the theologians the entry notes who dispute whether the suspension licenses fanaticism, reply that a category with no check is indistinguishable from a permission structure for fanatics. The debate is live, and Kierkegaard's own insistence on the horror — Abraham is no model for Sunday imitation — is the defenders' strongest textual support.

**3. Judge William and the knight of faith.**
Judge William *chooses himself*: he takes up marriage, vocation, and duty — the universal norms — with earnest repetition, renouncing the aesthete's evasion and endless possibility. What he gains is a self: continuity, responsibility, a life that can be narrated and owned. The knight of faith outwardly resembles him (he may look like a contented bourgeois), but he has made a further movement the Judge cannot: resignation of what he loves most, and then, by virtue of the absurd, receiving it back — a double movement resting on an absolute relation to God that no ethical reasoning generates. The religious is not a higher rung reachable by climbing the ethical harder, because the transition is a *leap*: a qualitative reorientation, not a deduction. Ethical reasoning, perfected, arrives at the universal — and the paradox of faith is defined precisely as the individual's standing beyond it. Hence de Silentio, who understands everything about Abraham, cannot take the step himself.

**4. Application: the broken promise.**
The *aesthetic* standpoint asks what the situation offers me: it would break or keep the promise by mood, interest, and the story I want to inhabit, and would call the self-serving outcome honesty. The *ethical* standpoint treats the promise as a universal obligation: promises bind because the practice of promising is a shared good; grave harm to the promisee might enter ethical deliberation itself (the Judge's world has room for weighing duties against each other), but an exception made because *I* find keeping it costly is exactly the aesthetic in ethical dress. The *religious* standpoint could in principle suspend the ethical — but only under the Abrahamic structure: an absolute duty to God, sustained in fear and trembling, with no universal justification available and no appeal to the harm calculation as the real reason. That last point is the distinguishing test the question asks for: a genuine suspension, in Kierkegaard's frame, cannot be cashed out as "the consequences were better" — that is ethical reasoning again. A suspension that conveniently coincides with my interests, costs me nothing, and can be explained to everyone's satisfaction is, by these criteria, self-serving exception-making, whatever it calls itself. (Different hard cases will weight the standpoints differently; the map of them is the answer's content.)

## [Mill](../eras/04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)

*The study guide [Mill, On Liberty and Utilitarianism](mill-liberty-utilitarianism.md) reconstructs these arguments in full; what follows is the compressed version.*

**1. The harm principle, precisely.**
"The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant." Society — by law *or* by the social tyranny of opinion, which Mill fears more — may coerce only conduct that harms others. "Harm" excludes mere offense, dislike, moral disapproval, and the distress others feel at knowing you live badly; it means damage (or definite risk of damage) to others' interests. A person's own good is not a sufficient warrant because the individual is sovereign over his own body and mind: he is the person most interested in his own well-being, and outsiders' judgments about it are likelier wrong than his. The line falls between *self-regarding* conduct — what affects only the agent (or others only with their free consent) — and *other-regarding* conduct, where compulsion may be justified. Mill concedes the boundary generates hard cases (his Chapters 4–5 work through fair-share burdens, testimony, education), but insists the burden of proof always lies on coercion.

**2. Two moves in *Utilitarianism*.**
The Chapter 4 "proof": the sole evidence that anything is *desirable* is that people actually desire it, just as the sole evidence that something is visible is that people see it; each person's happiness is a good to that person, so the general happiness is a good to the aggregate. It answers the objection that utilitarianism has no foundation — that its first principle is mere assertion — by offering the only kind of "proof" Mill thinks ultimate ends admit. The quality-of-pleasures test: pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity, and the verdict belongs to *competent judges* — those who have experienced both and persistently prefer the higher (intellect, imagination, moral feeling) even knowing it brings more dissatisfaction: "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." This answers the "doctrine worthy of swine" objection — that a pleasure-maximizing ethic fits pigs, not humans — by showing the theory can rank a life of the mind above mere sensation without leaving hedonism, or so Mill intends.

**3. The two objections pressed.**
*Moore's charge*: the proof equivocates — "desirable" means *able to be desired* in the premise (like "visible") but *ought to be desired* in the conclusion; sliding between them commits the naturalistic fallacy, deriving value from fact. Mill's strongest reply: he is not defining "good" as "desired" but offering evidence for an ultimate end, which by its nature cannot be deduced from anything prior; desirability, like visibility, can only be shown by the capacity's exercise. Judgment: the reply rescues the argument as practical evidence but concedes the logical gap Moore identified — most readers now treat the "proof" as the weakest plank. *Higher pleasures*: if quality ranks pleasures independently of felt amount, the standard is no longer pleasure — dignity or perfection has been smuggled in. Mill's reply: quality is still judged *by* preference among experiencers, so the currency remains felt experience, not an external ideal. Judgment: this succeeds only if competent preference is itself a hedonic fact rather than evidence of non-hedonic value — a point critics and defenders still contest, and the act/rule dispute in the entry grows from the same soil.

**4. Mill against Bentham: gains and losses.**
Bentham's calculus treats pleasures as homogeneous quantities and rights as "nonsense upon stilts." Mill gains two things. Grounding rights in utility — above all in *security*, the interest on which all other goods depend — explains why rights feel inviolable while keeping them inside the theory: they are the utilities too important to be traded case by case. Ranking pleasures qualitatively lets him answer the swine objection, honor the evident preference of experienced judges for a Socrates-life, and connect utility to human development (the *On Liberty* themes of individuality and experiments in living). The loss is theoretical purity: Bentham's single metric promised mechanical decision; Mill's version needs judges, qualitative rankings, and rule-like protections whose justification (act or rule utilitarianism?) his text leaves genuinely ambiguous, as the entry notes. He buys humanity and defensibility at the price of the calculus's clean determinacy — most readers judge the trade worth making; purists on both sides still protest.

**5. Application: the false health post.**
*For "harm"*: Mill's principle covers not only realized damage but conduct creating definite risk of damage to others; a viral false claim foreseeably lowers vaccination and foreseeably produces infections in identifiable classes of victims, though not identifiable individuals. His Chapter 2 epistemic argument also cuts against suppression: false views are best defeated in open collision, which keeps true ones alive — and the claim's falsity is the sort of thing discussion is good at exposing. *For "offence/risk only"*: no one's rights are directly violated by a post; the harm runs through others' voluntary choices to believe and act, and Mill's framework is wary of counting harms mediated by persuasion, else all unpopular speech becomes coercible by predicting bad uptake. Where the principle runs out: Mill wrote for pamphlets and platforms of soapboxes, not algorithmic amplification; his text gives no metric for how probability × scale × mediation converts speech into other-regarding harm, and his exceptions (incitement, the corn-dealer mob passage in Chapter 3) point both ways. A Millian can reach either a narrow takedown rule for demonstrably false, high-risk health claims or a counterspeech-only rule — the principle frames the argument more clearly than it settles it. Other applications of the same distinction are equally arguable.

## [Marx](../eras/04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

**1. Alienation: the four dimensions.**
Start from the premise: labor is the distinctively human activity — in free, conscious production we realize our *species-being*, the capacity to create deliberately and in common. Wage labor under private property systematically separates the worker at four points, and each separation follows from the structure — the worker sells labor-power to an owner of the means of production — not from any employer's cruelty. (i) *From the product*: what she makes belongs to another and confronts her as an alien power; the more she produces, the more powerful the world that dominates her. (ii) *From the activity*: labor is forced and external, experienced as a means of survival rather than self-expression — she is at home when she is not working. (iii) *From species-being*: creative production, which should be life's expression, is degraded into a mere instrument for staying alive. (iv) *From other people*: others appear as owners, managers, and competitors rather than fellow producers. A cheerful employee on a good wage is still alienated in all four senses: the analysis is of an objective structure, not a mood.

**2. Commodity fetishism.**
A commodity's value presents itself the way weight does — as if it were a natural property of the thing, measurable in the object itself. Marx's claim is that value is not in the coat or the phone at all: it is a *social* relation — the fact that independent producers' labors are connected only through exchange, so their mutual dependence can appear only as a relation between their products. Because producers meet as strangers in a market, the social character of their labor has nowhere to show itself except in prices; relations among people thus take "the fantastic form of a relation between things." What the appearance conceals is precisely that web: the labor embodied, the organization of production, the dependence of each producer on all the others. Fetishism is therefore not a subjective error consumers make but an appearance the system itself generates — which is why merely knowing about it does not dissolve it, any more than knowing optics dissolves a mirage.

**3. One objection to each account, with replies.**
*Alienation*: the standard of species-being looks like an essentialist human nature the later Marx abandoned — Althusser's "epistemological break" (c. 1845) casts the 1844 manuscripts as pre-scientific ideology, leaving alienation without its yardstick. Marx's best reply, via the continuity reading: the critique survives in *Capital* in altered form, as fetishism and the domination of workers by their own products; the vocabulary changed more than the charge. The break thesis is itself contested scholarship, not a settled division. *Fetishism*: the objection says prices are fully explained by supply, demand, and marginal utility — no hidden labor-essence is needed, so the "concealed" labor is a theoretical ghost. The reply distinguishes value from price: the theory is an account of the *social form* labor takes under capitalism, not a price formula — though the transformation problem (prices systematically diverging from values in Marx's own later volumes) keeps the pressure on, and analytical Marxists like Roemer restate exploitation through property relations alone. Both debates remain open, as the entry presents them.

**4. What Marx takes from Hegel, and what he inverts.**
From the master–slave dialectic Marx takes the engine: selfhood is made, not given, and it is made through *labor* — the bondsman's formative work on resistant things, in which mind becomes objective and a self is achieved; and the insight that recognition and freedom are social achievements, not private states. He inverts the setting and the motor. For Hegel the drama plays out in consciousness and its institutions, resolved when Spirit recognizes itself; for Marx the same drama is material: human beings produce their world and themselves under definite *relations of production* — who owns the means of production, who must sell labor-power to whom. What Hegel saw as the bondsman's path to self-consciousness becomes, in Marx, the proletariat's formation through production itself: a class formed by capital, positioned by that formation to abolish the relations that formed it. Where Hegel's resolution is reconciliation within ethical life, Marx's is transformation of the relations of ownership — philosophy of consciousness turned into critique of political economy.

**5. Application: the app-managed gig driver.**
*Alienation*: from the product — the completed trip, the rating, the data trail belong to the platform, and the accumulated data confronts him as the algorithm that prices and disciplines him; from the activity — pace, route, acceptance, and rest are dictated by the app, labor as pure means to the next payout; from species-being — driving, a skilled social activity, is narrowed to executing instructions whose rationale is opaque; from others — passengers are ratings, fellow drivers competitors for surge zones, and there is no shared workplace in which solidarity could form. *Fetishism*: the fare and the driver's "score" appear as natural properties — what the ride "is worth," what he "is worth" — concealing the social relations (platform's commission structure, labor supply, regulatory choices) that set them; the algorithm presents political decisions as technical facts. Unalienated work, on Marx's terms, would require the relations to change, not the driver's attitude: producers collectively owning or governing the platform, setting its rules, appropriating the product, and organizing the activity as their own — perks and friendlier notifications leave all four separations intact. (Other gig arrangements map onto the same four points with different emphases.)

## [Nietzsche](../eras/04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md)

*The study guide [On the Genealogy of Morals](nietzsche-genealogy.md) works through all three essays in detail; these answers follow it and the entry.*

**1. The First Essay: how slave morality is created.**
Noble valuation comes first and is spontaneous: the noble, powerful, and well-constituted call themselves and their like "good," and only secondarily, as a pale contrast, call the low and common "bad" — an afterthought of contempt, not hatred. The slave revolt begins when *ressentiment* — the stored, unexpressed vengefulness of those who cannot act — becomes creative. Unable to answer strength with strength, the powerless invent a new table of values: the noble's strength is redefined as "evil" — a culpable choice by a free agent who *could* have been meek — while their own involuntary weakness (humility, patience, pity, inability to retaliate) is redescribed as "good," a chosen virtue. What is creative about the revolt is exactly this: ressentiment, normally sterile, here *gives birth to values*; it fabricates the fiction of a neutral subject behind the deed (the strong man's strength becomes blameworthy only if he could have done otherwise) so that weakness can count itself merit. Nietzsche's evidence is partly etymological (the Latin and Greek pedigrees of "good" and "bad"), partly psychological — the priestly type he traces from Judaism into Christianity, whose victory over Rome he takes as the historical fact to be explained.

**2. The Second Essay: bad conscience.**
Step one: human beings are enclosed within society and peace; the semi-animal freedom to discharge aggression outward is walled off. Step two: the instincts of cruelty, aggression, and domination do not vanish — denied an external object, they turn *inward*, discharging against the self. Step three: this self-cruelty hollows out an interior — a "soul" — as the animal learns to torture itself; this is bad conscience in its raw form. Step four: religion, above all the Christian God as maximal creditor, gives the inner wound an interpretation: the suffering is *guilt*, a debt before God that can be made unpayable and therefore permanent. The most vulnerable step is arguably the second-to-third transition: the hydraulic picture of instincts — blocked pressure that must discharge somewhere — is an asserted psychology, not a demonstrated mechanism, and it does much of the argument's work. A critic can grant the phenomenology (civilized people do turn aggression on themselves) while denying that internalization, rather than socialization, empathy-training, or institutions, explains it. Nietzsche's narrative is most exposed exactly where it is most metaphorical.

**3. The genetic-fallacy objection.**
The objection is clean: origins do not settle worth. Pity might have resentful ancestors and still be, now, a genuine good; condemning a value by its pedigree commits the genetic fallacy, and a value's present function can differ wholly from its origin. The defender's reply, which the entry's signature-argument section gives: genealogy's aim is not refutation by origin. By exhibiting a contingent, interested, and rather ignoble history, it destroys morality's *aura* — its pretension to be timeless, pure, and self-evident — and thereby forces the question morality never asks itself: what is the *value of these values*? Once humility and pity can no longer coast on their halo, they must be assessed like anything else, by what they do to life. Judgment: the reply succeeds against the crude version of the objection — Nietzsche is not (only) arguing "resentful origin, therefore bad." It succeeds less completely as positive critique: stripping an aura shows a value *may* be questioned, not that it fails the questioning, and Nietzsche's own standard for the further assessment (life-affirmation, strength) is itself contestable. Genealogy clears the ground; whether it also condemns is, as scholars debate, undecided by the method alone.

**4. Perspectivism against the relativism reading.**
"Nietzsche thinks morality is just relative" misstates the critique because perspectivism is a thesis about *knowledge*, not a surrender of ranking. All knowing is from a perspective — structured by drives, affects, and interests; there is no view from nowhere and no "immaculate perception." But Nietzsche draws the opposite moral from the relativist's: since one perspective is partial, objectivity consists in employing *more* eyes — "the more eyes, different eyes, we are able to use for the same thing, the more complete will our 'concept' of the thing be." Perspectives can still be compared, and some are wider, more honest, and more inclusive of others than others. By what standard, then, does he rank master above slave morality? By life: whether a valuation is an expression of ascending, self-affirming strength that creates values spontaneously, or of declining life that must first negate an enemy to feel itself good — and by honesty, whether a morality can survive knowing its own genealogy. Slave morality, on his account, fails his standard not because it is *a* perspective but because it is a reactive and self-deceived one. Whether "life" is a standard he is entitled to is a further, fair question.

**5. Application: public apology rituals.**
Take the celebrity or institutional apology cycle: offense, denunciation, a scripted public confession, visible self-flagellation, and an audience that consumes the contrition. *Cruelty turned inward*: the apologizer's aggression and shame, denied any honorable outward form, are discharged against the self in public — the confession performs self-punishment as spectacle, and the audience's own ressentiment enjoys a safe, moralized outlet in watching it. *Meaning for suffering*: the ritual tells everyone involved what the pain is *for* — it is guilt, and guilt can be confessed, priced, and (sometimes) discharged; suffering with an interpretation is bearable in a way meaningless suffering is not. Nietzsche's classification: the apology as institutionalized form is closest to *bad conscience* given an ascetic script — the priestly function is now performed by the audience and the platform, which, like the priest, both produce the wound's interpretation and sell the cure, keeping guilt permanently available. It is the ascetic ideal in its modern, therapeutic dress rather than something new: note his own test — does the practice make anyone stronger or more honest, or does it train everyone to enjoy self-accusation? A different practice (say, private restitution without confession theater) would map differently, and the classification follows the analysis, not the label.
