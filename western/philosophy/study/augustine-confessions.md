# Study Guide: Augustine's *Confessions*

**Work:** *Confessions* (*Confessiones*) · **Author:** Augustine of Hippo (354 – 430 CE) · **Composed:** c. 397–400 (conventional date)
**Covers:** Books I–VIII and XI only, with a reading plan, book-by-book guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings. Books IX, X, and XII–XIII are noted briefly at the end of the book-by-book guide, not covered in full.
**Companion entry:** [Augustine of Hippo](../eras/02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Read the *Confessions* in a modern translation with book and chapter numbers in the margins. The plan in this library names Maria Boulding's translation (New City Press) — clear, readable, and well annotated — with F. J. Sheed's translation (Hackett) as a respected alternative, valued for its crispness and long classroom use. The companion entry also names Henry Chadwick's translation (Oxford World's Classics) as a first read; any of the three serves for this guide. Either will serve for this guide, since citations here are by book and chapter, the standard way of referring to the *Confessions* across all translations. Avoid older Victorian translations as a first read: their diction can obscure Augustine's plainest sentences, and his argument is often carried by short, plain clauses rather than by rhetoric.

Book and chapter numbers (e.g. VIII.12, XI.14) work the way Stephanus numbers work for Plato: every serious secondary source cites them, and they let you compare translations passage by passage. Learn to navigate by them.

A note on quotations in this guide: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows the Boulding wording unless noted. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so that your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

## How to use this guide

The *Confessions* is not a treatise arranged by topic, and it is not a diary. It is a sustained prayer addressed to God, written in the second person, in which Augustine retells his life up to his conversion as evidence for a theological argument: that the human heart is made for God, wanders when it loves lesser goods in God's place, and is healed only by grace. Read it as an argument in narrative form, not as a sequence of anecdotes.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a book, read its section below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each book, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed. Do this before reading the reconstruction sections in this guide — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

The learning path places the *Confessions* in Stage 2 (Medieval Foundations) and recommends the route covered here — Books I–VIII and XI — for a first pass. Books I–VIII tell the story from infancy to conversion; Book XI steps back from narrative into philosophy, asking what time is and what it means for God to create. Read them in order: Book XI's argument about time and the soul is far easier to follow once you have watched Augustine's mind work its way out of Manichaean materialism in Books V–VII.

## Reading plan

The *Confessions* in the recommended route runs to roughly 250 pages in a modern paperback. At the learning path's pace of about two hours of primary reading per week, this route takes roughly five to six weeks.

| Week | Books | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | I–II | Infancy, childhood, and the pear theft: what is evil? |
| 2 | III–IV | Carthage, the Manichaeans, and grief |
| 3 | V–VI | Faustus, Ambrose, and the unravelling of materialism |
| 4 | VII | The Platonists, incorporeal reality, and evil as privation |
| 5 | VIII | The divided will and the garden conversion |
| 6 | XI | Time, creation, and the distension of the soul |

Do not rush Book VIII: it is the emotional and argumentative climax of the narrative, and its account of the divided will is the hinge on which the whole work turns. Do not skip Book XI either — it is easy to treat the conversion as the ending, but Augustine deliberately moves from story to philosophy, and Book XI reframes the search of Books I–VIII as a question about the nature of time and of the God who stands outside it.

## Book-by-book guide

### Book I (I.1–I.20): The restless heart

The work opens with the sentence that states its whole argument: God has made human beings for himself, and the heart is restless until it rests in God (I.1). Augustine then asks how a finite creature can praise, invoke, or even know an infinite God — whether God is to be invoked before he is known, or known before he is invoked. The questions set the method of the entire work, in which understanding follows upon address, and the search for God is itself conducted in God's presence.

From there he turns to infancy (I.6–I.8). He cannot remember his own infancy, and reconstructs it from observation of other babies: the infant cries, demands, and rages when its will is thwarted. The point is not sentimentality about babies. It is that disordered self-love appears before any teaching could have caused it — an early, experiential hint of the doctrine of original sin that the later Augustine would state systematically. Childhood and schooling follow (I.9–I.19): he hated Greek, loved Latin and Virgil, was beaten at school, and preferred games to study. He notes, with some shame, that he wept for the fictional Dido while remaining dry-eyed about his own distance from God — an early version of the theme, developed in Book III, that art can move the emotions without moving the will toward truth.

**Key passages:** the opening prayer and the restless heart, I.1; infancy and the will of the child, I.6–I.8; schooling, beatings, and Virgil, I.9–I.19.

**Main argument:** The human heart is oriented toward God by nature, and its restlessness — visible from infancy onward — is the evidence. The life that follows is the story of that restlessness misdirected.

### Book II (II.1–II.10): Adolescence and the pear theft

Book II covers Augustine's sixteenth year, the year of his father's death and of his idleness in Thagaste while his family saved for his studies. Its center is the famous pear theft (II.4). With a gang of friends, Augustine stole pears from a neighbor's tree at night, not because he was hungry — he had better pears at home — and not for gain, since he threw most of them to the pigs. He stole, he says, because the theft itself, done in company and against the law, was pleasurable. The episode is small, and deliberately so: Augustine wants an evil act with nothing else in it — no need, no profit, no passion — so that he can isolate what evil is.

The surrounding chapters widen the point: lust confused love with desire (II.2–II.3), and friendship became a snare when valued above truth (II.5–II.8). The closing question drives the next five books: if every nature God made is good, where did the evil in the act come from?

**Key passages:** lust and the confusion of love with desire, II.2–II.3; the pear theft, II.4; friendship and complicity, II.5–II.8.

**Main argument:** Evil is not a thing added to the world from outside, but a disordered act of will — loving a lower good in a way that turns from the highest good. The reconstruction below states this formally.

### Book III (III.1–III.12): Carthage, Cicero, and the Manichaeans

At seventeen Augustine went to Carthage to study rhetoric, and the city — famous for its theaters, its schools, and its temptations — gave him everything he wanted and nothing he needed. He fell in love with love itself, took a concubine with whom he lived faithfully for years, and wept at tragedies in the theater, enjoying the pain of pity as a pleasure (III.2). The theater passage matters: Augustine is already asking why the emotions enjoy their own agitation, a question that returns in Book X's examination of the passions.

Then came the intellectual turning point of his youth: reading Cicero's *Hortensius*, a lost exhortation to philosophy, "changed his affections" and set him seeking wisdom rather than rhetorical success (III.4). But the Bible, when he turned to it, repelled him — its style seemed crude beside Cicero, and its stories, read literally, seemed unworthy of God (III.5). Into that gap stepped the Manichaeans. Their dualism offered what Augustine wanted: a rational, apparently scientific account of evil that blamed an evil power of darkness, not God, and that excused the human agent as a fragment of light trapped in matter. He became a "hearer" of the sect — a junior adherent, not one of its ascetic elect — and stayed for about nine years (III.6–III.10).

**Key passages:** the theater and the pleasure of pity, III.2; Cicero's *Hortensius*, III.4; the Bible's apparent crudeness, III.5; Manichaean dualism, III.6–III.10; Monica and the bishop, III.12.

**Main argument:** The search for wisdom has begun, but it is blocked twice over: by an aesthetic prejudice against Scripture, and by a dualist theory of evil that flatters the seeker by removing his responsibility.

### Book IV (IV.1–IV.16): Friendship, grief, and the failure of Manichaean science

Book IV opens with Augustine's years as a teacher of rhetoric in Thagaste and Carthage, still a Manichaean hearer, and with his close friendship with a young man of his own age. When the friend died suddenly, Augustine was shattered (IV.4–IV.7). He found his home town hateful because the friend was absent from it, and hated everything except grieving itself; he asked himself why he feared death so much when the friend he mourned was already dead. The grief passage is the work's first sustained piece of philosophical psychology: love fixed on a mortal object, he argues, necessarily ends in loss, and the pain teaches what the intellect had not yet grasped — that the soul that loves only what can die will itself be torn.

The book's second half records the intellectual collapse of his Manichaeism (IV.8–IV.16). He wrote a treatise on beauty and proportion, tried to reconcile it with Manichaean cosmology, and found that the sect's astronomy failed against the predictions of the philosophers: eclipses could be calculated years ahead by astronomers, while Manichaean myth could not match them. He also began to see that the Manichaeans' materialism — their picture of God as a luminous substance extended through space — made God a body among bodies, and therefore divisible and corruptible.

**Key passages:** the friend's death and the analysis of grief, IV.4–IV.7; beauty and proportion, IV.13–IV.15; the failure of Manichaean astronomy, IV.3 and IV.16.

**Main argument:** Love of mortal things, loved as if they were final, ends in grief; and a materialist picture of God cannot survive contact with the sciences of nature. Both lessons push Augustine toward an incorporeal God he does not yet know how to conceive.

### Book V (V.1–V.14): Faustus, Rome, Milan, and Ambrose

Book V is the book of disappointments that clear the ground. The great Manichaean bishop Faustus finally arrived in Carthage, and proved eloquent, charming, and shallow: he could not answer Augustine's questions about astronomy and cosmology, and honestly admitted it (V.3–V.7). Augustine respected the honesty and left the sect — not yet for the Church, but for a skeptical suspension of judgment. The move matters for the argument: he leaves Manichaeism on intellectual grounds, by its own failure to explain the world, not by conversion.

He sailed for Rome, taught rhetoric there, and grew disgusted with students who conspired to leave without paying their fees; then in 384 he won the municipal chair of rhetoric in Milan, the imperial court's official speechwriter (V.8–V.13). There he heard Bishop Ambrose preach, and two things changed. First, Ambrose's allegorical reading of Scripture answered the objection that had blocked Augustine since Book III: the Old Testament passages that seemed crude literally could be read spiritually (V.14). Second, Ambrose's preaching — heard at first only for its rhetoric, since Augustine attended as a critic of style — planted the possibility that the Catholic faith was intellectually defensible after all.

**Key passages:** Faustus' failure, V.3–V.7; the move to Rome and Milan, V.8–V.13; Ambrose and allegorical reading, V.14.

**Main argument:** Manichaeism fails its own test of rational explanation, and the literalist objection to Scripture collapses once allegory is understood. The intellectual obstacles are falling; what remains is a problem of will, not of intellect.

### Book VI (VI.1–VI.16): The divided life

Book VI is the least dramatic and, for the argument, one of the most important books. Monica followed Augustine to Milan, and there she found Ambrose and the Catholic community; Augustine, meanwhile, lived in a state of suspended decision. He describes the pressures on him with unusual candor: ambition for a career and a rich marriage, the plan (hatched with friends) of a communal life of philosophy that collapsed over the question of wives, the dismissal of his concubine of many years so that he could marry respectably — and the taking of another mistress in the meantime — and the prayer he later reports with shame, "give me chastity and continence, but not yet" (VIII.7).

The book's philosophical point is stated plainly at its close. Augustine no longer doubted that God is incorporeal, or that the Catholic faith was more credible than Manichaeism; he was held back, he says, not by argument but by the weight of habit — the "chains" of sexual habit and ambition that he both hated and clung to. The stage is set for Book VII's metaphysics and Book VIII's crisis of will: knowing the good and doing it are two different things, and philosophy alone has not bridged the gap.

**Key passages:** Monica in Milan, VI.1–VI.2; Alypius and the games, VI.7–VI.8; the failed philosophical community and the dismissal of the concubine, VI.11–VI.15; the prayer "but not yet," VIII.7.

**Main argument:** Intellectual conviction is not enough. The will can be divided against itself — commanding the body easily, and failing to command its own wanting — and that division, not ignorance, is the obstacle to conversion.

### Book VII (VII.1–VII.21): The Platonists, incorporeal being, and evil as privation

In Book VII Augustine reads "the books of the Platonists" — Plotinus and Porphyry in Latin translation, put into his hands by friends in Milan — and they give him the conceptual breakthrough Manichaeism never could (VII.9). From them he learned to conceive of something incorporeal: God not as a luminous body extended through space, but as Being itself, unchangeable and present everywhere without division. In a moment of intellectual vision he describes rising from bodily images to "that which is" (VII.10), then falling back, unable to sustain the vision. The Platonists gave him the goal; they did not give him the road.

The book then solves, at least in outline, the problem that has run since Book II. If God is incorporeal and supremely good, and made all things good, evil cannot be a substance opposed to God — for then there would be two ultimate principles, and God would not be supreme. Evil must therefore be a privation: a corruption or falling-short in a good nature, as blindness is the absence of sight in an eye that should see, not a thing in its own right (VII.12–VII.16). Augustine also finds, in the Platonists, the doctrine of the Word — that all things were made through a divine reason — but not the doctrine that the Word became flesh: the philosophy lacked humility, incarnation, and grace, and so left the reader knowing the destination but unable to travel (VII.9, VII.18–VII.21).

**Key passages:** the books of the Platonists, VII.9; the vision of Being, VII.10; evil as privation, VII.12–VII.16; what the Platonists lacked, VII.18–VII.21.

**Main argument:** God is incorporeal Being; evil is not a rival substance but a privation of good; and the will's turning toward lower goods is its own act, for which it is responsible. The reconstruction below states both the privation argument and the anti-Manichaean argument formally.

### Book VIII (VIII.1–VIII.12): The garden

Book VIII opens with Augustine telling his story to Simplicianus, Ambrose's successor-to-be, who answers with the story of Victorinus, the famous Roman rhetorician who converted late in life and publicly professed a faith he had long held privately (VIII.2). Then Ponticianus, a fellow African at the imperial court, told Augustine and Alypius about the Egyptian monk Antony, whose life of renunciation had moved two courtiers to abandon their careers on the spot (VIII.6). The stories work on Augustine the way the theater worked in Book III, but in reverse: where tragedy moved the emotions without moving the will, these examples move the will by showing that the life he wants is actually being lived, by people no better equipped than himself.

The crisis follows (VIII.5–VIII.11). Augustine describes it as a struggle of two wills in one person: the old will, formed by habit, pulling toward the familiar life, and the new will, formed by his convictions, pulling toward continence. He could command his body to rise, but could not command his soul to want what it knew to be good. The famous scene in the garden in Milan resolves it (VIII.12): weeping under a fig tree, he heard a child's voice chanting from a neighboring house, "take up and read" (*tolle lege*). He opened Paul's letter to the Romans and read the first passage his eyes fell on — the injunction to put on Christ and make no provision for the flesh (Romans 13:13–14).

**Key passages:** Victorinus, VIII.2; Ponticianus and Antony, VIII.6; the two wills, VIII.5 and VIII.8–VIII.11; the garden and *tolle lege*, VIII.12.

**Main argument:** Conversion is not the acquisition of a new belief — Augustine already believed — but the healing of a divided will, accomplished by grace working through an external occasion. Knowledge without grace leaves the will split; grace makes the will whole.

### Book XI (XI.1–XI.31): What is time?

Book XI abandons narrative and asks a question that looks like a riddle: what is time, and what was God doing before he created the world? The second question, Augustine says, rests on a mistake (XI.10–XI.13). To ask what God was doing "before" creation assumes time existed before creation, but time itself is a creature: God created the world with time, not in time, and eternity is not endless duration but a timeless present in which all times are present at once. The question dissolves once the assumption is removed.

The first question is harder (XI.14–XI.28). The past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no duration — if the present were always present, it would be eternity, not time. Yet we measure time: we say one syllable is longer than another, one life longer than another. What, then, are we measuring? Augustine's answer relocates time in the soul. Time is a distension of the mind (*distentio animi*): the present has three aspects — the present of past things, which is memory; the present of present things, which is attention; and the present of future things, which is expectation (XI.20). When he recites a psalm, expectation of the whole drains through attention, syllable by syllable, into memory; the passage he measures is the impression left in the mind, not a stretch of something outside it (XI.27–XI.28).

**Key passages:** creation "with" time, not "in" time, XI.10–XI.13; "what, then, is time?", XI.14; the threefold present, XI.20; the recited psalm, XI.27–XI.28; the gathering of the soul, XI.29–XI.31.

**Main argument:** Time is not a container in which events sit, nor a thing outside the mind, but the mind's own distension across memory, attention, and expectation. The reconstruction below states this formally.

### A brief note on the books not covered here

Book IX completes the narrative: Monica's death at Ostia on the journey home, the famous shared vision at Ostia in which mother and son briefly touched eternal Wisdom together, and Augustine's life in the months after baptism, including the death of his son Adeodatus. Book X turns from past to present: a long analysis of memory and a present-tense examination of Augustine's temptations as bishop. Books XII–XIII return to Genesis, reading the creation account allegorically: the "heaven of heavens," formless matter, and the days of creation become a meditation on how a mutable creature receives form from an immutable God.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The pear theft and the nature of evil (Book II, II.4–II.10)

**Premises:**

1. Every nature, insofar as it exists, is good, since it was made by a supremely good God (developed in VII.12–VII.16 and applied retrospectively to Book II).
2. In the pear theft, nothing external explains the act: Augustine was not hungry, gained nothing, and threw most of the pears away (II.4).
3. What he loved in the act was the act itself — doing what was forbidden, in company, for the thrill of transgression.
4. The evil of the act therefore lies not in any thing involved (the pears, the tree, his companions, all good in themselves) but in the will's disordered love: preferring a lower good — the pleasure of defiance — to the higher good of obedience to God.
5. An evil will has no efficient cause outside itself; it is a deficient movement, a turning away, not the product of a rival substance.

**Conclusion:** Evil is a privation — a disorder or falling-short in a good nature — not a substance, and the will is responsible for its own turning.

**Objection:** The privation account redescribes evil rather than explaining it. Calling cruelty or the theft's malice a "lack" of goodness seems to miss its felt positivity: the thieves positively enjoyed the wrongdoing, and cruelty positively delights in suffering. A lack cannot delight, and a purely negative account leaves the worst evils looking like mere absences.

**Reply:** Augustine can grant that the experience of evil feels positive while insisting that what is positive in it belongs to the good nature that is being misused: the pleasure, the companionship, the cleverness of the theft are all real goods, and the evil consists in their disordered arrangement — goods loved in the wrong order and to the wrong degree. The account's work is metaphysical: it explains how evil can exist in a world made wholly good, by locating it in the ordering of the will rather than in a second creator. Whether that fully answers the felt positivity of cruelty remains a live debate.

### (b) The argument against Manichaean materialism (Books V–VII, V.3–V.14, VII.9–VII.16)

**Premises:**

1. The Manichaeans claim God is a luminous substance, extended through space, locked in combat with an equally real substance of darkness — and that this dualism explains evil without blaming God (III.6–III.10).
2. A substance extended through space is divisible: it has parts, and what has parts can be diminished, corrupted, and acted upon from outside.
3. The supreme good cannot be divisible, corruptible, or acted upon; nothing can be greater than it, and a being that can suffer loss is not supreme.
4. Manichaean cosmology also fails its own empirical test: its predictions about the heavens are outclassed by astronomy, and its great teacher Faustus cannot answer the questions the theory raises (IV.3, V.3–V.7).
5. The Platonists supply the missing conception: a being that is incorporeal, unchangeable, and present everywhere without division (VII.9–VII.10).

**Conclusion:** God is not a material substance and evil is not a rival substance; Manichaean dualism fails both metaphysically and empirically, and an incorporeal God plus privation explains what dualism could not.

**Objection:** Premise 3 assumes the conclusion: it defines the supreme good as incorporeal and impassible, then finds materialism wanting for failing to meet that definition. A Manichaean could reply that their picture of God is driven by a different datum — the manifest reality of evil and suffering — and that a God who cannot be opposed or wounded is too remote to explain why the world looks like a battlefield. The argument wins only if you already accept the Platonic conception of perfection.

**Reply:** The argument does not rest on definition alone. It has an independent empirical leg (premise 4): Manichaeism presented itself as a rational, scientific account of the cosmos, and its cosmology failed where astronomy succeeded. And it has an independent metaphysical leg: if two ultimate substances exist, neither is ultimate, since each limits the other.

### (c) The theory of time (Book XI, XI.10–XI.28)

**Premises:**

1. Time was created with the world, not before it: to ask what God did "before" creation is to assume time existed independently of creation (XI.10–XI.13).
2. The past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present, if it had duration, would be divisible into past and future parts and so would not be present (XI.14–XI.15).
3. Yet we measure time: we compare the lengths of syllables, songs, and lives, and judge some longer than others (XI.16–XI.21).
4. What we measure cannot be what no longer exists or does not yet exist; it must be something present in the mind — the impression left by things as they pass.
5. The mind therefore holds time in three present aspects: memory of the past, attention to the present, and expectation of the future (XI.20).
6. Reciting a psalm shows the mechanism: the whole song is held in expectation, passes syllable by syllable through attention, and settles into memory; the measured passage is the distension of the soul across these three (XI.27–XI.28).

**Conclusion:** Time is the distension of the soul (*distentio animi*) — the mind's extension across memory, attention, and expectation — and God, who is eternal, stands outside this distension in an unchanging present.

**Objection:** The argument proves too much about measurement and too little about time. Measuring an impression in the mind presupposes that something passed in an order outside the mind: the psalm's syllables occur in sequence whether or not anyone remembers them, and two people can agree on a song's length without sharing a mental impression. If time were only in the soul, it is unclear what makes one person's measure of a day correct and another's mistaken, or what time would be in a world with no minds in it.

**Reply:** Augustine need not deny that events occur in sequence outside the mind; his claim is about what measurement measures, not about whether events happen. The syllables pass, but the passing is not itself an object that can be held up and measured — only the impression it leaves can be compared with another impression. The agreement between two listeners is then explained by their shared exposure to the same sequence, not by direct access to a stretch of time outside both minds. Whether that answer satisfies depends on a further question Augustine leaves open: whether time requires a mind at all, or whether minds merely measure a passage that exists independently.

### (d) The soul's restlessness and God as interior (Books I, VII, VIII, I.1, VII.10, VIII.12)

**Premises:**

1. The heart is restless until it rests in God (I.1): every love Augustine pursued — praise, sex, friendship, truth, honor — satisfied briefly and left the desire unsatisfied.
2. The things loved are mutable: friends die, praise fades, pleasures pass, and a love fixed on a mortal object as if it were final ends in grief (IV.4–IV.7).
3. Only an immutable good could satisfy a desire that no mutable good satisfies; and the search itself points beyond every finite object to something the soul already, obscurely, seeks.
4. That good is not found by going outward — through the senses, through the heavens, through reputation — but by turning inward: in the vision of Book VII, Augustine rose above bodily images to Being itself, present within the soul yet above it (VII.10, VII.17).
5. The soul's failure to rest is therefore not ignorance but disordered love: it loves lower goods as if they were highest, and is healed only when grace reorders its loves (VIII.5–VIII.12).

**Conclusion:** God is the soul's proper rest, found interiorly — more inward than the soul's own inwardness, in the phrase the tradition takes from this work — and the whole narrative of Books I–VIII is the demonstration that every other resting place fails.

**Objection:** The argument moves from a psychological fact to a metaphysical conclusion without a bridge. That no finite good has satisfied Augustine shows only that he has not yet found satisfaction, not that an infinite good exists or that it is God. Restlessness might be a permanent feature of human psychology — desire renewing itself with each satisfaction — rather than evidence of a divine object. The interior "vision" of Book VII could equally be described as an intense experience, not an encounter with Being.

**Reply:** Augustine's argument is cumulative, not deductive, and it is presented as a life, not a syllogism, for that reason. The force lies in the pattern across eight books: the same structure repeats with every good — enjoyment, then loss, then renewed seeking — and the pattern itself, rather than any single experience, is offered as evidence that the desire has an object proportionate to it. A critic can still reply that a pattern of disappointment proves only that satisfaction is elusive, not that it is divine; and scholars debate whether Augustine himself intended a proof here or a confession — a report of what he found, offered as an invitation to test it in one's own experience rather than as a demonstration.

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check yourself against the answers at the end of this guide.

1. What does Augustine mean by saying the heart is "restless until it rests in God" (I.1), and what evidence does Book I offer for the claim?
2. Why does Augustine choose the pear theft (II.4) as his central example of evil, and what does it show that a theft for gain would not?
3. What attracted Augustine to Manichaeism, and why did he remain only a "hearer" rather than becoming one of the elect?
4. How did Cicero's *Hortensius* change Augustine's ambitions, and why did the Bible initially repel him?
5. What does the grief for his friend in Book IV teach Augustine about loving mortal things?
6. Why did Faustus' failure matter more to Augustine than a defeat in debate would have, and what did he do after leaving the Manichaeans?
7. How did Ambrose's allegorical preaching remove Augustine's intellectual objection to Scripture?
8. What is the difference, in Book VI, between Augustine's intellectual convictions and his will — and what is the prayer that exposes it?
9. What did the "books of the Platonists" give Augustine that Manichaeism could not, and what did they fail to give him?
10. State the privation theory of evil, and explain how it preserves both God's goodness and human responsibility.
11. In Book VIII, what is the "divided will," and why could Augustine command his body but not his own wanting?
12. What role do the stories of Victorinus and Antony play in Augustine's conversion, and how do they differ from the theater's effect in Book III?
13. What happened in the garden (VIII.12), and why does Augustine present the conversion as healing rather than as a change of belief?
14. Why is the question "what was God doing before creation?" a mistake, on Augustine's account?
15. Explain the threefold present (XI.20) and the example of the recited psalm (XI.27–XI.28).
16. How does Book XI's theory of time connect with Book I's claim about restlessness?
17. Why does Augustine say the will's evil movement has no efficient cause, and what problem does that claim create?
18. Augustine says God is found by turning inward. Does that make his God merely a feature of the soul? Give the strongest reason on each side.

## Common misreadings

**1. "The *Confessions* is just an autobiography."**
The narrative of Books I–IX is real — the places, the people, and the sequence are historical — but the work is arranged as a theological argument, not a diary. Events are selected and interpreted to demonstrate a thesis about restlessness, disordered love, and grace, and Books X–XIII abandon narrative altogether for philosophy. Scholars debate how much the conversion story is shaped retrospectively by the bishop Augustine had become by c. 397; some read the garden scene as a crafted theological climax, others as a faithful report. Either way, reading the work as mere memoir misses its argumentative structure.

**2. "Augustine hated the body, sex, and the world."**
Augustine affirmed creation's goodness against the Manichaeans throughout his life: bodies, pleasures, and friendships are good in themselves, and the problem is disordered love, not the objects loved. His struggle with sexual habit in Book VI is presented as a struggle with habit and ordering, not as a claim that the body is evil — that was the Manichaean position he rejected. Whether his later doctrine of concupiscence nonetheless burdened Western attitudes to the body is a separate, much-debated question.

**3. "The pear theft shows Augustine thought small sins are as bad as great ones."**
The theft is chosen precisely because it is small: with no need, no gain, and no passion in it, it isolates the will's movement in its purest form. Augustine is not grading the theft against murder or claiming all sins are equal; he is using a minimal case as a laboratory, the way a scientist uses a simple experiment. The disproportion between the trivial act and the long analysis is the point — the analysis is of the will, not of the pears.

**4. "Original sin means Augustine thought infants are wicked."**
Book I's account of the infant's raging will is an observation about disordered self-love appearing before teaching, offered as early evidence for a wounded condition of the will, not as a claim that babies commit sins. The mature doctrine concerns an inherited bias of the will, and its implications — above all for unbaptised infants — troubled Augustine himself and were moderated by later councils.

**5. "Augustine's theory of time means time is unreal, or only in your head."**
Book XI does not deny that events occur in sequence, or that the world changes; it asks what measurement measures, and answers that the measurable passage is the mind's distension across memory, attention, and expectation. That is compatible with a realist account of temporal order outside the mind, and scholars divide over whether Augustine intended a full psychology of time, a step toward realism, or something between. What is not in the text is the claim that time is an illusion.

**6. "The conversion in the garden was a sudden, purely emotional event."**
The garden scene is sudden, but it crowns years of intellectual movement — the failure of Manichaeism, Ambrose's preaching, the Platonists, the stories of Victorinus and Antony — and Augustine presents it as the healing of a will whose convictions were already formed, not as a leap in the dark. Readers who treat it as mere emotion miss the eight books of argument that precede it; readers who treat it as mere intellect miss the fact that Augustine insists the final step was grace, not reasoning.

**7. "Augustine abandoned reason when he converted."**
Augustine held that faith and understanding interpenetrate — believe so that you may understand — and the *Confessions* is intensely argumentative throughout, including after the conversion: Book XI's theory of time is philosophy, not piety. What he abandoned was the claim that reason alone, without grace, could heal the will.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **The heart is made for God and finds no final rest in finite goods (I.1).** Book I's evidence is developmental: even the infant's rage at a thwarted will, and the schoolboy's tears for Dido alongside indifference to his own state, show a desire that overshoots every object offered to it.
2. **The theft isolates the will (II.4).** A theft for gain could be explained by need or profit; this theft had neither — Augustine threw the pears away — so the only explanation left is love of the transgression itself.
3. **Manichaeism offered a rational account of evil that excused God and the agent (III.6–III.10).** Augustine remained a hearer — a junior adherent — because the elect's strict ascetic discipline was a commitment he did not make.
4. ***Hortensius* redirected him from rhetorical success to the search for wisdom (III.4).** The Bible repelled him because its style seemed crude beside Cicero and its stories, read literally, seemed unworthy of God (III.5).
5. **Love fixed on a mortal object as if it were final ends in grief (IV.4–IV.7).** His home town became hateful because his friend was absent from it; the pain taught that a soul loving only what can die will itself be torn by loss.
6. **Faustus was the sect's great teacher, and his honest admission that he could not answer the cosmological questions showed the system itself had no answers (V.3–V.7).** Augustine left the sect on intellectual grounds and suspended judgment, rather than converting immediately.
7. **Allegory showed the Old Testament passages that seemed crude literally could be read spiritually (V.14).** The text was not the crude document he had assumed.
8. **By Book VI he no longer doubted that God is incorporeal or that Catholicism was more credible than Manichaeism; he was held back by habit, not argument (VI.11–VI.15).** The prayer "give me chastity and continence, but not yet" exposes a will that wants the good and simultaneously wants to go on wanting otherwise.
9. **The Platonists gave him an incorporeal conception of God as Being itself, and the privation account of evil (VII.9–VII.16).** They failed to give him humility, incarnation, and grace (VII.18–VII.21).
10. **Evil is a privation: a lack, corruption, or disorder in a good nature, as blindness is absence of sight (VII.12–VII.16).** God's goodness is preserved because he authors no evil substance; responsibility is preserved because the will's turning toward lower goods is its own act.
11. **The divided will is one will split against itself: the old will of habit pulling toward the familiar life, the new will of conviction pulling toward continence (VIII.5, VIII.8–VIII.11).** He could command his body because the body obeys a single command; he could not command his wanting because the command itself was divided.
12. **Victorinus (VIII.2) and Antony, via Ponticianus (VIII.6), show that the life Augustine wants is actually being lived, by people no better equipped than himself.** The theater (III.2) moved his emotions without moving his will; these examples move his will.
13. **In the garden he heard a child's voice chanting "take up and read," opened Romans, and read the injunction to put on Christ (VIII.12; Romans 13:13–14).** He presents it as healing because his beliefs were already formed; what changed was the will, made whole by grace working through an external occasion.
14. **Time was created with the world, not before it (XI.10–XI.13).** "Before" creation assumes a time in which God waited; but eternity is a timeless present, and creation includes time itself, so the question rests on a false presupposition.
15. **The threefold present (XI.20): the present of past things is memory, of present things attention, of future things expectation.** Reciting a psalm (XI.27–XI.28), expectation of the whole drains through attention, syllable by syllable, into memory; the passage measured is the soul's distension across the three.
16. **Book XI restates Book I as a philosophy of time.** The soul distended across past and future is the restless heart in temporal form; its gathering into God's eternal present is the rest it sought from the first sentence.
17. **An evil will is a deficient movement, a turning away, and a deficiency has no efficient cause the way a positive act does — nothing makes the will turn except the will itself.** The problem this creates is explanatory: if there is no cause, the turning looks unintelligible.
18. **For the "merely interior" reading:** the vision of Book VII is reported as an experience of the soul, and everything Augustine cites as evidence is inward. **Against it:** Augustine insists the Being encountered is above the soul, not a part of it — present within, but unchangeable where the soul is changeable.
