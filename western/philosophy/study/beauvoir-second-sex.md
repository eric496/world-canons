# Study Guide: Beauvoir's *The Second Sex*

**Work:** *The Second Sex* (*Le Deuxième Sexe*, 1949), two volumes · **Author:** Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986) · **Composed:** 1949
**Covers:** The Introduction in full; Volume I in summary; Volume II's opening — "Childhood" and "The Young Girl" — in full, with the rest of Volume II in summary — with a reading plan, a section-by-section guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Simone de Beauvoir](../eras/05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Read the translation by Constance Borde and Sheila Malovany-Chevallier (Vintage, 2010; paperback 2011). It is the first complete English translation of the book, and it is the translation this guide quotes and navigates by. Do not use the older English translation by H. M. Parshley (1953): it abridged the text by roughly 15%, cutting whole passages on women's history, philosophy, and sexuality, and mistranslated key terms, flattening her argument. Before you begin, check the translator's name on the title page of any copy you pick up: if it says Parshley, put it down and find the Borde and Malovany-Chevallier.

Two practical notes on the text itself. First, *The Second Sex* is long — roughly 800 pages in the complete translation — and uneven by design: Beauvoir assembles biology, psychoanalysis, history, economics, literature, and lived testimony, and tests each against the others. Expect a case built from many kinds of evidence, not a single linear argument. Second, the book's famous sentence — "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" — opens Volume II, not the Introduction. The Introduction prepares it, and this guide follows that order.

On quotations: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows the Borde and Malovany-Chevallier wording. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

## How to use this guide

Beauvoir's prose is the opposite trap from Mill's clarity: it is rich, digressive, and full of evidence, and it is easy to finish a chapter feeling moved or convinced without being able to state what was argued. The Introduction alone moves between philosophy, biology, psychoanalysis, and economics in a few pages, and each move does argumentative work. Resist the urge to agree or disagree until you can say what the claim is.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a section, read its entry below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each section, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

The learning path places Beauvoir in Stage 5, after Sartre. This guide follows the companion entry's order: the Introduction first, then the opening of Volume II, then Volume I on myth and history, then the rest of Volume II according to interest. Read in that order or in book order; what matters is not skipping the Introduction, because everything in Volume II depends on the distinctions it draws.

One further caution, stated at the outset because it colors the whole book: Beauvoir writes in 1949, about the France she knows, drawing on the science and psychoanalysis of her time. Some of her biology and psychoanalytic sources are dated, and her picture of "women" is, as later critics have argued, too often a picture of bourgeois European women. That does not dissolve her argument, which is structural rather than statistical — but separate the argument from its evidence as you read, and ask at each point whether the evidence she gives is the evidence the argument needs.

## Reading plan

At about two hours of primary reading per week, the program below takes about eight weeks. The Introduction and the opening of Volume II get full weeks; Volume I is read as a summary except for the chapters on myth, which repay a slower pass.

| 2 | Volume I, Part One (Destiny) as summary | Biology, psychoanalysis, historical materialism — and what each misses |

| 4 | Volume II, Part One, ch. 1: Childhood | How a girl is made: dolls, passivity, the body as object |
| 5 | Volume II, Part One, ch. 2: The Young Girl | Puberty, the crisis of the body, the renunciation of transcendence |

| 7 | Volume II, Part Two as summary | The married woman, the mother, social life, prostitutes and hetaeras, maturity to old age |
| 8 | Volume II, Parts Three–Four as summaries; re-read the Introduction | Justifications (narcissist, woman in love, mystic); the independent woman and the conclusion |

Do not rush week 1: the Introduction contains the whole method in miniature. Do not skip week 5 either — the young girl's chapter is where "becomes a woman" is shown rather than asserted.

## Section-by-section guide

### Introduction — "What is a woman?"

Beauvoir opens by confessing hesitation: the subject is irritating, especially to women, and a book on woman risks either repeating the platitudes of the eternal feminine or provoking the charge that its author is not a real woman. Her first move is to turn that irritation into the question itself. What does "woman" mean? Biology alone will not answer it, she argues, because a female mammal is not thereby "a woman" in the sense that matters — the sense in which a society organizes lives, duties, and possibilities around femaleness. Nor will the "eternal feminine," the timeless essence invoked by poets and moralists, because it explains nothing: it merely restates the mystery it claims to solve. The book's question is therefore posed existentially, not essentially: given that women exist, what is it to be a woman, and what has made her what she is?

The answer begins with the Other. Beauvoir borrows from Hegel the drama of two consciousnesses, each seeking recognition, and generalizes it: no group defines itself as the One without setting up an Other against which it is defined. Men, she argues, have made themselves the Subject and the Absolute, and woman the inessential counterpart — the Other. Her famous formulation: "He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other." But the asymmetry is the point. In Hegel, master and slave at least confront each other reciprocally, and the slave can turn the relation by work. Woman's otherness is different in kind: she does not stand over against men as a separate group at all, but lives dispersed among them — bound to fathers, brothers, husbands, sons — divided by class and race, with no shared past, no common religion, no institutions of her own through which to forge solidarity. That dispersion, not any natural inferiority, is one reason the relation has proved so durable, and Beauvoir states it as historical analysis, not destiny.

The Introduction then tests three candidate explanations of woman's situation, and rejects each as insufficient — not as false, but as partial. Each rejection matters, because each becomes a reconstruction below.

**Biology.** The facts of sexual dimorphism are real: the female bears the heavier burden in reproduction, and in many species the male is the more active partner. But biology describes a body, not a destiny. Nothing in the physiology of pregnancy entails that women should be excluded from public life, paid less, or taught to experience themselves as relative beings. As Beauvoir puts it, the body is one of the essential elements of the situation a woman occupies in the world — but that alone does not define her. The error of biological explanation is to treat a condition as a cause.

**Psychoanalysis.** Freud's account makes the girl's development turn on penis envy and the supposed inferiority of the female body; Adler, more usefully, treats the "masculine protest" — the child's revolt against inferiority — as central, but still frames woman as a deviation from a masculine norm. Beauvoir's objection is structural: psychoanalysis describes how a child experiences her situation, but it takes that situation as given rather than explaining it. It tells us what it feels like to be raised as a girl in a patriarchal world; it mistakes that feeling for a law of the psyche. The categories of psychoanalysis are themselves shaped by the civilization they claim to explain.

**Historical materialism.** Engels' account — that woman's oppression arose with private property, when men seized control of production and reduced women to domestic servitude — explains something real about the economics of dependence. But it reduces woman's situation to a single economic cause, and Beauvoir argues the historical record does not fit: women's subordination predates private property in the form Engels needs, and it survives in societies where property relations have changed. Materialism, like biology and psychoanalysis, captures a dimension and mistakes it for the whole.

Having cleared the ground, Beauvoir states her own method. She will treat woman as a *situation*: a human freedom situated in a body and a world, whose possibilities are shaped — opened and closed — by the civilization around her. This is her application, and her criticism, of the existentialist thesis that existence precedes essence. The thesis is applied because woman has no fixed essence to be discovered; she is what her situation makes possible. It is criticized because existence does not precede essence in a vacuum: a girl does not choose her situation freely, and the freedom she exercises is always situated freedom, unequal in its conditions. Beauvoir's advance on early Sartrean existentialism is exactly here — freedom is real, but it is not abstract, and the conditions under which it is exercised are the proper subject of philosophy.

The Introduction closes with the book's most uncomfortable theme: complicity. Women are not merely victims of the order Beauvoir describes; they are, in part, its accomplices. Surrendering freedom is tempting, because freedom is risky and immanence — existence confined to repetition, maintenance, and the safety of being protected — is comfortable. A woman who accepts the role of the Other gains material advantages, social approval, and exemption from the risks of transcendence. Beauvoir insists on this not to blame women, but to refuse two false pictures at once: the picture of women as pure passive victims, which denies their freedom, and the picture of women as freely choosing their subordination, which denies their situation. The double refusal — structural analysis without victimology, freedom without voluntarism — is the book's spine, and it is what makes the later chapters on justification (the narcissist, the woman in love, the mystic) intelligible rather than cruel.

**Main argument:** Woman is not an essence to be defined but a situation to be analyzed: constituted as the Other by a civilization that disperses her among men, explained by no single cause, and maintained partly by the temptations of complicity.

### Volume I, Part One — Destiny (summary)

Volume I is subtitled "Facts and Myths," and its first part tests the three explanations of the Introduction at length. The biology chapter surveys sexual reproduction across species, noting that the female's reproductive burden is real but that no biological fact entails social subordination; its conclusion is the Introduction's point made concrete — biology is a situation, not a verdict. The psychoanalysis chapter works through Freud and Adler in detail, granting psychoanalysis its descriptive power over childhood experience while denying it explanatory authority over woman's condition. The historical-materialism chapter takes Engels seriously — the economics of private property and the division of labor genuinely matter — and then shows its limits: it cannot explain why subordination takes the forms it does, nor why it persists where property relations change. Read these chapters as evidence for the Introduction's rejections, not as freestanding surveys; their point is methodological, that no single discipline owns the question.

**Main argument:** Each of the three great explanations of woman's condition is true in part and false as a whole; the question needs a method that holds them together.

### Volume I, Part Two — History (summary)

The history chapter is a rapid survey from prehistory to the nineteenth century, and its thesis is negative: at no point did women form a separate group with a history of their own. Unlike a conquered people or a slave class, women have no golden age to look back to, no collective past, and no institutions through which resistance could accumulate. Their history is the history of their dispersion — attached to men of every class, sharing the fortunes of their fathers and husbands rather than the fortunes of women as such. The chapter also traces the material gains that did arrive: the slow opening of education, work, and legal rights in the nineteenth century, which Beauvoir treats as real progress and as incomplete — legal emancipation without economic independence leaves the situation largely intact.

**Main argument:** Women have never constituted a separate historical group, and that dispersion is a structural cause of the durability of their subordination.

### Volume I, Part Three — Myths (summary)

The myths section asks what men have dreamed woman to be. The "eternal feminine" is not one myth but a cluster: woman as Nature, as flesh, as mother, as virgin, as temptress, as muse — each image making her simultaneously exalted and debased, essential and inessential. Beauvoir's key move is to show that these myths are not descriptions but projections: man makes woman the Other in imagination in order to avoid confronting his own ambiguity — his own bodiliness, mortality, and dependence. The chapter on the myth of woman in six authors (Montherlant, D. H. Lawrence, Claudel, Breton, and Stendhal, among others) tests the claim against literature. Stendhal is the exception Beauvoir praises: he treats his heroines as free subjects with projects of their own, rather than as symbols of something else. Myth is not harmless poetry: a girl raised on these images learns what she is supposed to be before she learns who she is. That is the bridge into Volume II.

**Main argument:** The myths of woman are projections of male ambiguity, and they function socially by teaching girls their supposed nature in advance of experience.

### Volume II, Part One, Chapter 1 — Childhood

Volume II opens with its famous sentence: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." The childhood chapter shows the becoming at its earliest stage. No one is born with a feminine destiny, Beauvoir argues; the girl is made, from the first, by a thousand small differences in treatment. She is dressed differently, handled differently, praised for different things. Her doll is the emblem: where a boy's toys invite action on the world, the doll invites identification with a passive object — the girl learns to see herself as something to be dressed, admired, and looked at, and is punished for the roughness and initiative encouraged in her brother.

The chapter follows the girl's own experience. At first, Beauvoir insists, the little girl is not inferior — as active, as curious, as desirous of mastery as any boy. The difference is imposed from outside, through the body: she discovers early that her body is not simply hers to use but an object others look at and judge. She learns to experience herself as seen. This is the origin, in Beauvoir's account, of the structure that will govern the rest of the book: transcendence — projects, creation, risk — is trained out of her, and immanence — repetition, appearance, pleasing — is trained in.

Two cautions keep the chapter honest. First, Beauvoir describes a pattern in the bourgeois France she knows; its details — dolls, dresses, religious instruction — are historically specific even when the structure they illustrate is general. Second, the girl is not passive clay. She resists, she protests, she envies her brother's freedom — and that resistance is evidence for the chapter's thesis, not against it: a nature would not need to be enforced.

**Main argument:** Femininity is not present at birth but is produced in childhood, above all by training the girl to experience her body as an object for others rather than an instrument of her own projects.

### Volume II, Part One, Chapter 2 — The Young Girl

Adolescence is where the making of woman becomes a crisis, and this chapter is the book's emotional and argumentative center. Puberty arrives as a rupture: the girl's body, which she had begun to use as her own, becomes flesh — heavy, bleeding, exposed to the gaze of others in a new way. Menstruation, for Beauvoir, is not merely a biological event; it is experienced as a monthly reminder that her body has a destiny independent of her will, a destiny that will be read by others as her meaning. Where the boy's puberty confirms his growing power, the girl's puberty announces her object-status.

The young girl now faces the demand that she renounce transcendence. She is told, in a hundred ways, that her future lies not in projects of her own but in being chosen — by a man, for marriage, for a life whose meaning will be given to her rather than made by her. Her education, her reading, her friendships, and the whole apparatus of courtship teach her that to be feminine is to be passive, to wait, to please, to be looked at rather than to look. She oscillates between two poles: moments of revolt, in which she claims her freedom and refuses the role, and moments of surrender, in which she embraces it — through coquetry, through daydreams of romantic rescue, through the cultivation of her own beauty as her chief project.

Three forms of escape structure the chapter's second half, and they prefigure Volume II's later section on justifications. The young girl may turn to narcissism, making her own body and image her project and finding in the mirror the admiration the world withholds from her deeds. She may turn to love, investing a man with the absolute value her own projects are denied, and waiting for him to confer meaning on her life. Or she may turn to mysticism, addressing to God the absolute devotion the world refuses to her own freedom. Beauvoir treats all three sympathetically and critically at once: intelligible responses to a situation that blocks transcendence, and evasions of freedom that reproduce the situation they flee.

The chapter's thesis, stated against both essentialism and voluntarism, is that the young girl does not choose to become a woman in the sense of freely selecting an identity from a menu. She becomes a woman because her situation — her body as others treat it, her education, her economic prospects, the marriage market awaiting her — progressively closes every other road while opening this one, and because the road itself offers real rewards: admiration, protection, love, a place. "Becomes" is a verb of formation, not of decision. That is why the chapter is the best test of the Introduction: read it as free choice and you have missed the situation; read it as programming and you have missed the freedom.

**Main argument:** Adolescence is the crisis in which a girl's situation — her body as others treat it, her education, and the marriage market awaiting her — progressively closes the road of transcendence and opens the road of becoming a woman, a formation she neither simply chooses nor merely suffers.

### Volume II, Part One, Chapters 3–4 — Sexual initiation and the lesbian (summary)

Sexual initiation, for Beauvoir, is asymmetrical in a way that follows from the young girl's chapter: where the boy's first sexual experience confirms his agency, the girl's is structured as being taken, and the cultural scripts around virginity and reputation make her first experience a trial of her value rather than an exploration of her desire. The chapter on the lesbian is the book's most important methodological case study: Beauvoir refuses both the pathologizing accounts of her time and any simple celebration, treating lesbian existence as one possible response to the same situation — a refusal of the feminine destiny that, like every response, is shaped by the conditions it refuses. Read it as a control case for the whole book's method: the same situation, a different formation.

**Main argument:** Sexual initiation and lesbian existence are not exceptions to the book's thesis but tests of it — the same situation, differently lived.

### Volume II, Part Two — Situation (summary)

The long middle of Volume II follows woman through the situations the first part prepared her for. The married woman discovers that marriage, as an institution, assigns her immanence as a vocation: housework that undoes itself daily, the maintenance of a household whose meaning belongs to her husband's projects, and a legal and economic dependence that makes revolt costly. The mother discovers that maternity is treated as her destiny rather than one project among others, and that the culture simultaneously exalts and confines her. Social life, prostitutes and hetaeras, and the passage from maturity to old age each show the same structure in a new setting: independence is made difficult, dependence comfortable. The hetaera and the prostitute are not moral warnings but case studies in what trading on one's otherness costs.

**Main argument:** Marriage, maternity, and social life are not natural destinies but situations that reproduce the Other-position by making independence costly and dependence comfortable.

### Volume II, Part Three — Justifications (summary)

The justifications chapters return to the young girl's three escapes, now as settled adult strategies. The narcissist makes her image her project and lives for admiration. The woman in love makes a man her absolute and lives through his projects. The mystic makes God her absolute and lives through devotion. Beauvoir shows each strategy as an intelligible attempt to live as a subject within a situation that denies subjectivity, and as a failure mode of freedom — a way of willing oneself as object. The point is diagnosis, not condemnation: these are what transcendence looks like when its ordinary roads are closed.

**Main argument:** Narcissism, romantic love, and mysticism are intelligible strategies for living as a subject under blocked transcendence — and evasions of freedom that reproduce the block.

### Volume II, Part Four — Toward Liberation (summary)

The final part asks what liberation would require, and Beauvoir's answer is deliberately twofold. Economic independence is necessary — a woman who cannot support herself cannot be free — but it is not sufficient: the independent woman of the book's penultimate chapter discovers that work alone, inside a civilization that still trains her to be Other, leaves her torn between two worlds, exhausted by a double burden and judged by both. Liberation requires a moral conversion as well: men and women recognizing each other as equals, as subjects, in a world where transcendence is open to both. The conclusion is famously sober rather than triumphant: the free woman, Beauvoir writes, is just being born. The book ends not with a program but with a condition: a world in which woman's situation no longer makes her the Other, made by women and men together.

**Main argument:** Liberation requires both material independence and a transformation of the relations between the sexes — neither alone suffices.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The Other argument (Introduction)

**Premises:**

1. No group defines itself as the One without setting up an Other against which it is defined; the categories of Subject and Other are a fundamental structure of human consciousness.
2. In the patriarchal order Beauvoir analyses, man occupies the position of Subject and Absolute, and woman is constituted as the inessential Other — the counterpart against whom he defines himself.
3. Unlike other oppressed groups, women do not stand over against men as a separate group: they live dispersed among men, divided by class and race, with no shared past, institutions, or religion of their own through which to forge solidarity.
4. This dispersion means the Other-position cannot be overturned by the usual means of group resistance — there is no separate collective to rise, and no separate history to reclaim.

**Conclusion:** Woman's subordination is not a natural fact but a historically produced constitution as Other, made unusually durable by women's dispersion among men.

**Objection:** The argument overstates the asymmetry. Women have formed collective movements — suffragism, feminism itself — and shared institutions, from convents to women's organizations, through which solidarity was forged. If dispersion were as complete as Beauvoir claims, the feminist movement that made her book possible would be inexplicable. Moreover, many societies recognized women's distinct powers and roles rather than simply constituting them as inessential.

**Reply:** Beauvoir can grant the objection's facts while holding the thesis. Feminist movements are recent and partial, and themselves evidence of the difficulty: they had to be built against the grain of dispersion. The claim is comparative, not absolute — women are *more* dispersed than other oppressed groups, not absolutely without solidarity — and the Hegelian frame describes a structure, not a claim that every society realized it identically. Whether the comparative claim survives the historical record in full is a live question; the book's contribution is to have posed dispersion as an explanatory factor at all.

### (b) The argument that biology, psychoanalysis, and history do not explain woman's situation (Introduction; Volume I, Part One)

**Premises:**

1. Biology describes real facts — sexual dimorphism, the female's heavier reproductive burden — but no biological fact entails social subordination; a condition is not a cause, and the body is an element of a situation, not a verdict on it.
2. Psychoanalysis describes how a child experiences her situation — penis envy, the masculine protest — but it takes the patriarchal situation as given and mistakes the experience of that situation for a law of the psyche.
3. Historical materialism explains the economics of dependence under private property, but women's subordination predates private property in the form the theory needs and survives where property relations have changed.
4. A complete explanation must therefore be none of these alone: it must hold the biological, psychological, and economic dimensions together within an account of lived experience as actually lived.

**Conclusion:** No single discipline explains woman's situation; the question requires an existential-phenomenological method that treats woman as a situation formed across a lifetime.

**Objection:** The rejections are too quick, and they trade on a double standard. Beauvoir uses biological and psychoanalytic evidence freely when it suits her, while denying those disciplines explanatory authority when their conclusions embarrass her thesis. A defender of any one of the three can reply that Beauvoir has shown only that each explanation is incomplete, not that it is wrong — and incompleteness is compatible with that discipline being the fundamental one.

**Reply:** Beauvoir's point is not that these disciplines say nothing true but that each, taken alone, converts a dimension into a destiny — biology into nature, psychoanalysis into psychic law, economics into historical necessity. Her use of their evidence is consistent with that: evidence can be borrowed without accepting the framework that produced it. The reply concedes the objection's core, though — the book shows the need for a synthetic method more conclusively than it demonstrates that no single-factor account could ever succeed, and later scholarship has continued the argument on all three fronts.

### (c) The "becomes a woman" argument (Volume II, Part One)

**Premises:**

1. The female body is a biological fact, but femininity — the meanings, behaviors, dress, ambitions permitted and forbidden, and sense of self attached to a female body — is not given with it.
2. A girl is formed across childhood and adolescence by a thousand small differences in treatment: dolls and dress, praise and correction, stories and myths, schooling, and the economic prospect of marriage as her future.
3. These influences do not merely add customs to a pre-existing nature; they train the girl to experience her own body as an object for others and to renounce transcendence in favor of immanence.
4. The formation is neither a free choice — the roads are closed before she chooses — nor a mechanical programming — she resists, protests, and envies her brother's freedom, and that resistance proves the formation is enforced, not natural.

**Conclusion:** One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman: femininity is a situation produced across a lifetime, not an essence and not a voluntary identity.

**Objection:** The argument proves too much or too little. If "becomes" means only that socialization shapes behavior, it is trivially true of every human trait and says nothing distinctive about woman. If it means that femininity is entirely made, it contradicts Beauvoir's own retention of biological sex as a real element of the situation, and it sits uneasily with the experience of women who report their femininity as deeply felt rather than imposed. Later readers divide on whether she opens the door to a full sex/gender distinction or keeps a firmer view of biological sex than that reading allows.

**Reply:** The distinction between situation and essence answers the first charge: the claim is not that everything is socialized but that this particular formation — being constituted as Other — is produced by a specific, analysable situation rather than following from biology. On the second charge, Beauvoir's position is deliberately double: biology is real and it matters, but what matters about it is what a civilization makes of it, and a deeply felt femininity is exactly what a lifetime of formation would produce. Whether that double position is stable — whether it can hold biology and construction together without collapsing into one or the other — is the central interpretive debate about the book, and it remains genuinely unresolved.

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check yourself against the answers at the end of this guide.

1. Why does Beauvoir say the question "What is a woman?" cannot be answered by biology alone, or by appealing to the "eternal feminine"?
2. What does Beauvoir mean by woman as the Other, and how does her otherness differ from that of other oppressed groups?
3. Why does Beauvoir argue that women's dispersion among men makes their subordination unusually durable?
4. State Beauvoir's objection to biological explanations of woman's situation in one sentence.
5. What is her objection to psychoanalysis — what does it describe well, and what does it mistake?
6. What does historical materialism explain about woman's situation, and where does Beauvoir say it fails?
7. What is a "situation" in Beauvoir's sense, and how does it apply — and criticize — the thesis that existence precedes essence?
8. What is the difference between transcendence and immanence, and why is the distinction central to the book?
9. What does Beauvoir mean by "complicity," and why does she insist on it rather than treating women as pure victims?
10. What is the role of the doll in the childhood chapter, and what does it show about how femininity is formed?
11. Why is puberty a crisis for the young girl in Beauvoir's account, and how does it differ from the boy's puberty?
12. What are the three escapes the young girl turns to, and what do they prefigure in Volume II?
13. In what sense is "becomes a woman" a claim about formation rather than about choice — and why does the girl's resistance support, rather than undermine, the claim?
14. What does the chapter on the lesbian show about Beauvoir's method, and why is it a useful control case?
15. Why does Beauvoir argue that economic independence is necessary but not sufficient for liberation, and what else does liberation require?

## Common misreadings

**1. "Beauvoir was simply applying Sartre to women."**
The influence ran both ways, and on some questions Beauvoir's formulation is earlier or stronger. Her situated account of freedom — freedom exercised under unequal conditions — is an advance on the more individualist early Sartre, not an application of him. The companion entry sets out the direction-of-influence debate; the current consensus rejects the disciple picture while leaving precise priorities contested. Judge the Sartre relation as a dialogue, not a derivation.

**2. "'One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman' means femininity is purely socially constructed, or freely chosen."**
Neither. Beauvoir does not deny biology — the body is an essential element of the situation — and she does not claim girls choose femininity from a menu. "Becomes" names a formation: a situation progressively closes some roads and opens others, while offering real rewards for taking the open one. A purely constructionist reading loses the body; a voluntarist reading loses the situation. Both miss the argument, which is precisely that formation is neither essence nor choice.

**3. "The book is only about biology, or only about 1940s France."**
The biology chapter is one part of Volume I, and its conclusion is that biology does not settle the question. The book's argument is structural — about how a civilization constitutes a group as Other and maintains that constitution across a lifetime — and its evidence is historically specific precisely so the structure can be seen at work. Later critics have rightly pressed Beauvoir on whose women she describes, and that criticism refines the argument; it does not reduce the book to a period piece.

**4. "Complicity means Beauvoir blames women for their own oppression."**
Complicity is Beauvoir's refusal of a comforting picture, not an accusation. She insists women participate in maintaining the order because freedom is risky and immanence is safe, and because the order offers real advantages — protection, approval, exemption from risk. To call that blame is to miss the double refusal it serves: women are neither pure passive victims, which would deny their freedom, nor freely choosing subjects, which would deny their situation. The justifications chapters are diagnosis, not condemnation.

**5. "The Other is a symmetrical concept — men are women's Other too."**
Beauvoir's point is the asymmetry. In principle each consciousness is Other to the other, but in the patriarchal order she analyses, man occupies both terms at once: he is the Subject and the Absolute, and woman is the inessential counterpart. Woman is not reciprocally Other in the same way, because she does not stand over against men as a separate group with its own institutions and history. Flattening the concept into symmetry dissolves the very phenomenon it was introduced to explain.

**6. "Beauvoir judges women's lives by a masculine standard, devaluing care and domestic work."**
This is a serious criticism, developed by care ethicists, and it should be weighed rather than dismissed — but it is not quite Beauvoir's position. Her target is compulsion, not the activities themselves: housework and care are devalued because they are imposed as a destiny and confined to immanence, not because they are worthless. Whether her language of transcendence can fully accommodate that distinction is a live debate; read the justifications chapters with it in mind.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **Biology** describes a body, not a destiny: nothing in pregnancy or sexual dimorphism entails exclusion from public life or the experience of oneself as a relative being. **The eternal feminine** explains nothing: it restates the mystery — that women are somehow essentially this way — as if it were an answer.
2. **Woman as Other** means she is constituted as the inessential counterpart against whom man defines himself as Subject and Absolute. **Her otherness differs** from other oppressed groups because she does not stand over against men as a separate group; she lives dispersed among them, without a shared past, religion, or institutions of her own.
3. **Dispersion blocks solidarity:** without a separate collective, a shared history, or institutions of their own, women cannot easily forge the group consciousness through which oppressed groups typically resist — and they share the fortunes of their fathers and husbands rather than the fortunes of women as such.
4. **Biology gives a condition, not a cause:** the body is an element of woman's situation, but no physiological fact entails the social meanings and limits built on it.
5. **Psychoanalysis describes well** how a child experiences her situation — the feelings, conflicts, and identifications of growing up as a girl. **It mistakes** that experience for a law of the psyche, taking the patriarchal situation as given instead of explaining it.
6. **Historical materialism explains** the economics of dependence: private property and the division of labor made women economically dependent on men. **It fails** because women's subordination predates private property in the form the theory needs and survives where property relations change — economics is a dimension, not the whole.
7. **A situation** is a human freedom located in a body and a world, whose possibilities are shaped — opened and closed — by the civilization around it. It **applies** existence precedes essence because woman has no fixed essence; it **criticizes** the thesis because freedom is never exercised in a vacuum, and its conditions are unequal.
8. **Transcendence** is the reach beyond the given: projects, creation, risk. **Immanence** is existence confined to repetition and maintenance. The distinction is central because Beauvoir's charge is that women have been systematically confined to immanence while transcendence is reserved to men — and then blamed for the narrowness the confinement produces.
9. **Complicity** means women partly maintain the order that subordinates them, because surrendering freedom is tempting: immanence is safe, freedom is risky, and the role of the Other brings real advantages. Beauvoir insists on it to refuse both pictures — pure victimhood, which denies freedom, and free choice, which denies situation.
10. **The doll** teaches identification with a passive object: where a boy's toys invite action on the world, the doll invites the girl to dress, admire, and look at something — and thereby to experience herself as something to be looked at. It shows femininity being formed through the body and play long before any explicit teaching.
11. **Puberty is a crisis** because the girl's body becomes flesh — heavy, bleeding, exposed to the gaze of others — and is read by others as her meaning, announcing a destiny independent of her will. **The boy's puberty** confirms his growing power; the girl's announces her object-status.
12. **Narcissism** (making her own image her project), **love** (investing a man with absolute value), and **mysticism** (addressing absolute devotion to God). They **prefigure Volume II's justifications chapters** — the narcissist, the woman in love, and the mystic as settled adult strategies.
13. **Formation, not choice,** because the roads are closed before she chooses: her body as others treat it, her education, and the marriage market progressively narrow her future while offering rewards for the open road. **Her resistance supports the claim** because a nature would not need to be enforced — protest and envy of her brother's freedom show the formation is imposed, not innate.
14. **The lesbian chapter is a control case:** it treats lesbian existence as one possible response to the same situation — a refusal of the feminine destiny shaped by the conditions it refuses — without pathologizing it or simply celebrating it. It shows the method working on a case that fits neither the usual condemnation nor the usual celebration.
15. **Economic independence is necessary** because a woman who cannot support herself cannot be free. **It is not sufficient** because work alone, inside a civilization that still constitutes her as Other, leaves her torn between two worlds. **Liberation also requires** a moral transformation: men and women recognizing each other as equals and subjects, with transcendence open to both.
