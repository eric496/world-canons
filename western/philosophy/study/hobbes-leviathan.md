# Study Guide: Hobbes's *Leviathan*

**Work:** *Leviathan* (1651) · **Author:** Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) · **Covers:** Chapters 13–17 in full, with chapters 18–21 in summary, and chapters 14–15 as needed for the laws of nature — with a reading plan, chapter-by-chapter guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Thomas Hobbes](../eras/03-early-modern/thomas-hobbes.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Read *Leviathan* in a modern edition with the original chapter divisions intact, since this guide navigates by chapter rather than by page. The Cambridge edition edited by Richard Tuck is a widely used scholarly text, and it conveniently modernizes spelling while keeping Hobbes's chapter structure; any comparable modern edition will serve equally well. A public-domain text is also usable for these chapters, but beware of older printings that preserve seventeenth-century spelling inconsistently and of editions that renumber or merge chapters — if your chapter 13 does not begin with the state of nature, you have the wrong text.

Quotations here are short and follow Hobbes's own wording, stable across modern editions; the guide otherwise paraphrases, so your reconstruction work is in your own words.

On dates and context: *Leviathan* was published in 1651, the year Hobbes returned to England from eleven years of exile in Paris, as the English Civil Wars were reshaping who held power and on what authority. It was written against a background of actual civil war, not abstract speculation — and that urgency explains both its argument and its tone. The first half of the book builds a materialist psychology (chapters 1–12) and then the political theory covered here; the second half (chapters 32–47) argues at length from Scripture that the church must be subordinate to the civil sovereign. This guide concentrates on the political core, chapters 13–21, but do not mistake that core for the whole book: for Hobbes, subordinating clerical power was part of the same argument against divided sovereignty.

## How to use this guide

Hobbes's prose is plain by seventeenth-century standards, and that plainness is a trap of its own kind: the sentences are short, the definitions are crisp, and it is easy to finish a chapter feeling you understand it without being able to state why one step follows from the last. His argument is a chain — psychology to state of nature, state of nature to laws of nature, laws of nature to covenant, covenant to sovereignty — and a broken link anywhere breaks the whole. If you cannot say what work "authorisation" does that "consent" alone does not, you have not yet got chapter 16.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a chapter, read its section below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each chapter, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

Two cautions before you start. First, keep Hobbes's definitions in front of you: he defines his terms — right, law, liberty, person, author — with unusual care, and most misreadings of *Leviathan* come from importing ordinary meanings of those words. Second, remember the question Hobbes is answering. It is not "what government is best?" but "what arrangement could rational people, who fear violent death above all, agree to in order to escape a condition where no one is safe?"

## Reading plan

Chapters 13–21 run to roughly 100 pages in a modern paperback. At about two hours of primary reading per week, the program below takes about five weeks; the first pass can be compressed into four by reading the summary chapters as summaries and folding week 5 into week 4.

| Week | Reading | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ch. 13, then chs. 6 and 11 as background | The state of nature and the war of all against all |
| 2 | Chs. 14–15 | Right versus law, and the laws of nature |
| 3 | Chs. 16–17 | Persons, authorization, and the generation of the commonwealth |
| 4 | Chs. 18–21 as summaries, with ch. 21 read closely | Rights of the sovereign, liberty of subjects, and the retained right of self-preservation |
| 5 | Re-read chs. 13 and 17; *De Cive* Preface and chs. 1–3 as a check | The whole chain, tested against the shorter restatement |

Do not rush chapter 13: it contains the whole problem in miniature. Do not skip chapter 16 either — authorization is the book's most original idea, and without it the covenant of chapter 17 is just a promise, not the creation of a new artificial person.

## Chapter-by-chapter guide

### Chapter 13 — Of the Natural Condition of Mankind as Concerning Their Felicity and Misery: the state of nature

Chapter 13 opens with a claim about equality that is easy to misread. Hobbes does not deny that people differ in strength and wit. His point is that the differences do not matter for the argument: even the weakest can kill the strongest, by secret machination or by confederacy with others in the same danger. Nature has made people "equal" in the specific sense that no one is so far above the rest that they can count on prevailing — and therefore no one can count on being safe.

From equality, together with scarcity and the absence of a common power, Hobbes derives three causes of quarrel. **Competition** drives people to invade for gain: if two people desire the same thing, which both cannot enjoy, they become enemies. **Diffidence** — by which Hobbes means distrust, not shyness — drives people to invade for safety: anticipating attack, the rational move is to strike first, by force or wiles, until there is no power left large enough to endanger them. **Glory** drives people to invade for reputation: people value being esteemed by others, and will fight over trifles — a word, a smile, a difference of opinion — because reputation is itself a form of power, a sign that others will defer rather than challenge.

The result is not constant battle. Hobbes is explicit that "war" here means a known disposition to fight, a tract of time during which the will to contend is sufficiently known, just as bad weather means a tract of time disposed to foulness, not perpetual rain. In such a condition there is no place for industry, because its fruit is uncertain; no navigation, trade, building, arts, or letters; and "the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." There is also no justice or injustice, because those names presuppose law, and there is no law where there is no common power. What exists is force and fraud, which Hobbes calls, in war, the two cardinal virtues — a deliberately shocking phrase, and a sign of how completely morality, on his account, depends on institutions.

Two questions to carry with you. First, is the state of nature a historical claim? Hobbes says it was never generally so over the whole world, but offers approximations — the condition of "savage people" in America (a passage modern readers rightly find tendentious and poorly evidenced) and, more tellingly, sovereigns toward one another, who live in a state of nature with respect to each other, maintaining forts and spies. The important reading, stressed in the companion entry, treats the state of nature chiefly as an analytical device: remove the common power and ask what follows. Second, is the argument about human nature or about structure? Hobbes's agents need not be villains. Even moderate, fearful people, uncertain of others' intentions and unable to trust a promise no power enforces, are driven to pre-emptive action. That structural character is what makes the argument hard to dismiss by pointing to human kindness: kindness does not solve the trust problem.

**Main argument:** Without a common power to keep all in awe, rational and roughly equal people, facing scarcity and uncertainty about others' intentions, will fall into a condition of war — and in that condition there is no security, no industry, and no justice.

### Chapter 14 — Of the First and Second Natural Laws, and of Contracts: right versus law

Chapter 14 begins with definitions, and the chapter's whole argument hangs on them. A **right of nature** (*jus naturale*) is the liberty each person has to use their own power as they will, for the preservation of their own nature — that is, their own life. In the state of nature this right extends to everything, even to another's body, because nothing forbids it. A **law of nature** (*lex naturalis*) is a precept or general rule, found out by reason, by which a person is forbidden to do what is destructive of their life, or to omit what they think may best preserve it. The distinction is the point: right is liberty, the absence of obligation; law is obligation, a bond. Right and law differ as much as liberty and obligation differ.

The first law of nature follows: every person ought to endeavor peace, as far as they have hope of obtaining it; and when they cannot obtain it, they may seek and use all helps and advantages of war. This law contains both a duty (seek peace) and a permission (use war when peace fails), and Hobbes immediately draws its consequence, the second law: a person should be willing, when others are so too, to lay down their right to all things, and be contented with so much liberty against other people as they would allow other people against themselves. The laying down of a right happens either by renouncing it or by transferring it — giving it to someone who then has the right, and who acquires an obligation not to hinder the giver's use of it. Renunciation and transfer are the matter of contracts and covenants, and Hobbes pauses to define them carefully: a contract is a mutual transferring of right; a covenant is a contract where one party performs later, trusting the other; a gift is a transfer without return. He also insists that words alone, without a sign of the will, do not transfer right — a covenant must be a sufficient sign that the party intends to be bound.

The chapter closes with the problem that drives the rest of the book. Covenants made in the state of nature, where no power enforces them, are void in practice whenever reasonable suspicion arises — keeping your side alone, when others will not, is self-destruction. Words are too weak to bridle ambition, avarice, and anger without fear of a coercive power. Reason tells people what to do; only a common power can make them do it.

**Main argument:** Reason forbids self-destruction and therefore commands seeking peace; peace requires mutual laying down of the unlimited right to all things; but such covenants cannot hold without a coercive power to enforce them.

### Chapter 15 — Of Other Laws of Nature: the third law, justice, and the Foole

Chapter 15 enumerates the remaining laws of nature — Hobbes lists nineteen in all, though the number matters less than the logic. The **third law** is the pivot: people must perform their covenants made. Without it, covenants are in vain, and the state of nature continues. From it Hobbes derives **justice**: where covenants have been made, keeping them is just, and breaking them is unjust. Note the order, which reverses the usual moral picture. Justice is not a standard standing outside and above agreement; it is defined by agreement. Before covenants, there is neither just nor unjust, because there is nothing to keep or break. After them, injustice is precisely the breach of covenant — and, Hobbes adds, it is the same thing as injury done to the other party.

The famous objection comes from the **Foole**, who "hath said in his heart, there is no such thing as justice" — that is, no reason to keep a covenant when breaking it secretly serves one's interest. If breaking a covenant brings gain and no one finds out, why keep it? Hobbes's reply turns on the difference between a single act and a rule of conduct. The Foole reasons about one act in isolation; but to break a covenant is to adopt the principle that covenants may be broken when advantageous, and a person who adopts that principle cannot reasonably expect to be received into any society that requires keeping covenants. Their error, Hobbes says, is a failure of reasoning: they cannot be received into society, and therefore they cannot live securely, which is the very thing they wanted. Whether this answer succeeds — it seems to assume the Foole cares about being received into society, and that secret breach will be discovered or generalized — is a live question, and one of the most productive to argue about after reading.

The remaining laws — gratitude, complaisance (accommodating oneself to others), pardon of past offenses, revenge aimed only at future good, and others down to the rules against pride, arrogance, and partiality in judgment — elaborate the dispositions peace requires. They are best read as a catalogue serving the first law's end, self-preservation through peace, and all of them, Hobbes insists, bind only in conscience (*in foro interno*) until a commonwealth exists. Reason tells you they are right, but acting on them unilaterally in the state of nature, where others will exploit your compliance, is forbidden by the same reason.

**Main argument:** Justice is keeping covenants, and keeping covenants is rational even for the self-interested, because a policy of breach destroys the trust on which one's own security depends.

### Chapter 16 — Of Persons, Authors, and Things Personated: authorization

Chapter 16 is the book's hinge, and its vocabulary is technical. A **person** is someone whose words or actions are considered either as their own or as representing the words or actions of another. When they are considered as their own, the person is a **natural person**; when they represent another, an **artificial person**. The one represented is the **author**: the author owns the words and actions, and the representative is the **actor**, who acts by the author's authority. The actor's act is the author's act — "as if" the author had done it themselves. Hobbes's examples run from legal representatives and actors on a stage to, crucially, a multitude united into one person.

Two consequences matter for what follows. First, authorization is stronger than mere consent to a particular act. To authorize someone is to give them authority to act in your name generally, within the scope of the authorization, so that their acts count as yours — including acts you would not have chosen yourself. Second, a multitude can be a person only when it is represented by one person or assembly: "a multitude of men are made one person, when they are by one man, or one person, represented." Without a representative, a crowd is many persons, not one; with one, it can speak, act, and be held to its acts as a unity. This is the conceptual machinery chapter 17 will use to build the commonwealth, and it explains why Hobbes's sovereign is not merely a powerful ruler but the representative of a new artificial person — the state itself.

**Main argument:** A multitude becomes one person only through representation: when each authorizes a single representative, the representative's acts count as the acts of each author, and the multitude can act as a unity.

### Chapter 17 — Of the Causes, Generation, and Definition of a Commonwealth: the covenant

Chapter 17 gathers the chain. The final cause of submitting to a common power is self-preservation: people seek to get themselves out of the miserable condition of war, which necessarily follows from natural passions when there is no visible power to keep them in awe and tie them by fear of punishment to their covenants. Concord among people who merely agree is not enough, because human beings compete for honor and glory, and because agreement without enforcement collapses at the first suspicion. A common power is therefore needed, and it must overawe all parties alike.

The generation of the commonwealth is a covenant of every person with every other, in words to this effect: "I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man, or to this assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy right to him, and authorise all his actions in like manner." The multitude so united in one person is called a **commonwealth**, in Latin *civitas*; this is the generation of the great **Leviathan**, or mortal god, to whom we owe, under the immortal God, our peace and defense. The sovereign is that person or assembly in whom the commonwealth's power resides, and each subject thereby owns the sovereign's acts as their own — which is why, Hobbes will argue, a subject cannot accuse the sovereign of injury: to do so would be to accuse themselves, since the sovereign acts by their authority.

Two points in this chapter are essential and easily missed. First, the covenant is among subjects only: the sovereign is not a party to it, makes no promises under it, and therefore cannot breach it. There is no contract to appeal against the sovereign, and no judge above the sovereign to hear the appeal — any such judge would itself be a sovereign, and the problem would recur. Second, the sovereign may be one person or an assembly. Hobbes prefers monarchy in practice, but the theory requires only that sovereignty be unified: the artificial person must have one representative, not several competing ones. A commonwealth can also arise by **acquisition** — by force, when a conqueror's subjects covenant out of fear of death — as well as by **institution**, the mutual agreement described here; Hobbes insists the rights of sovereignty are the same in both cases, since in both the subjects' consent, motivated by fear of death, is the ground of obligation. Scholars debate whether acquisition really carries the same weight as institution, and the debate is worth having: it tests whether Hobbes's theory rests on consent at all, or on submission to any power capable of protecting.

**Main argument:** Rational people escape the state of nature by mutually authorizing a single sovereign, thereby creating an artificial person — the commonwealth — whose acts each subject owns as their own.

### Chapters 18–21 in summary — the rights of the sovereign and the liberty of subjects

**Chapter 18** derives the rights of sovereignty from the act of institution. Because subjects have authorized the sovereign and own its acts, they cannot lawfully change the form of government, cannot transfer sovereignty to another, and cannot accuse the sovereign of injustice — for the sovereign's acts are their own. The sovereign is sole legislator, judge of controversies, maker of war and peace, chooser of counsellors and ministers, and judge of what doctrines may be publicly taught, since opinions are a cause of war and must be governed if peace is to hold. Sovereignty is, by its nature, indivisible: to divide it is to dissolve the commonwealth into competing powers, which is civil war by institutional means.

**Chapter 19** classifies the forms of commonwealth — monarchy, aristocracy, democracy — by the representative: one person, an assembly of some, or an assembly of all. Tyranny and oligarchy are not distinct forms, Hobbes insists, but names of disapproval applied to monarchy and aristocracy by those who dislike them. The chapter also argues monarchy's practical advantages, but the classification point is the theoretical one: legitimacy does not depend on the form, and a democracy is no less absolute than a king.

**Chapter 20** treats dominion by acquisition and paternal dominion, and the rights of subjects under conquest. Its importance for this guide is the claim, noted above, that sovereignty by acquisition carries the same rights as sovereignty by institution, because the subjects' submission — motivated by fear of violent death — is still their act, and protection is the ground of obedience: "the obligation of subjects to the sovereign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth by which he is able to protect them." That sentence is the hinge of Hobbes's theory of obligation, and of its limits.

**Chapter 21** turns to the liberty of subjects, and corrects the crudest reading of absolutism. Liberty, for Hobbes, is the absence of external impediments to motion — not a moral ideal but a physical fact, applying to water in a channel as much as to people. In a commonwealth, subjects are free where the sovereign's laws are silent: where the law says nothing, subjects may act as they judge best. More importantly, the covenant cannot alienate certain rights, because no one can be understood to have covenanted away what the covenant exists to protect. A subject retains the liberty to resist immediate violence to their own body, to refuse to kill or wound themselves, to refuse to incriminate themselves without pardon, and to disobey commands that endanger their life. This right is narrow and individual: it licenses no collective resistance, no right of revolution, no standing mechanism for judging the sovereign. But it is real, and it shows the theory's foundation: obligation rests on self-preservation throughout, and a command that defeats that purpose defeats its own authority. Where the sovereign can no longer protect, obligation ends — not because subjects judge the sovereign unjust, but because the reason for obedience has ceased.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The state-of-nature argument for war (ch. 13)

**Premises:**

1. People are roughly equal in the sense that matters: even the weakest can kill the strongest, by stealth or by alliance with others in the same danger, so no one can count on prevailing or on being safe.
2. People desire many of the same scarce goods, and where two desire what both cannot enjoy, they become enemies (competition).
3. People cannot know others' intentions and cannot trust promises no common power enforces, so each rationally anticipates attack and strikes first for safety (diffidence).
4. People value reputation as a form of power, and will fight over slights and differences of opinion that threaten their standing (glory).
5. In the absence of a common power to keep all in awe, there is no law, and therefore no justice or injustice, and no security for industry, trade, or knowledge.

**Conclusion:** Without a common power, rational and roughly equal people will fall into a condition of war — a known disposition to fight, not constant battle — in which life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

**Objection:** The argument proves too much, because its premises do not hold universally. Anthropology offers many examples of stateless societies that are orderly, cooperative, and long-lived, sustained by kinship, custom, and reputation without a sovereign; and people plainly cooperate without enforcement in families, friendships, and small groups. If cooperation without a common power is common, the move from uncertainty to universal war fails, and Hobbes has described one possible condition, not the necessary consequence of removing authority.

**Reply:** Hobbes need not claim that every stateless society is at war, and the analytical reading of the state of nature concedes the historical point: the argument is about what follows when trust cannot be secured at scale, not a survey of prehistory. Small groups sustain cooperation through repeated interaction, kinship, and reputational knowledge that do not scale to strangers; and where those mechanisms fail — between sovereigns, between strangers, in civil war — the Hobbesian dynamic appears. The reply concedes something real, though: the more cooperation without sovereignty you admit, the more the argument depends on the scale and anonymity of the society in question, and the less it works as a universal proof.

### (b) The argument from the laws of nature to covenant and authorization (chs. 14–17)

**Premises:**

1. Reason forbids self-destruction and therefore commands seeking peace where peace can be had (first law of nature).
2. Peace requires each person to lay down their unlimited right to all things, on condition others do the same (second law), and to perform covenants made (third law), since justice consists in keeping covenants.
3. Covenants made without a coercive power to enforce them are void in practice whenever reasonable suspicion arises, because keeping one's side alone when others will not is self-destruction.
4. A multitude can act as one person only through representation: when each authorizes a single person or assembly, that representative's acts count as the acts of each author.
5. Mutual authorization of a single sovereign, by a covenant of each subject with every other, creates such a representative and therefore a commonwealth.

**Conclusion:** Rational people, following the laws of nature, must covenant with one another to authorize a single sovereign — creating the Leviathan — because only a common power can make covenants hold and peace possible.

**Objection:** The argument smuggles in what it needs. Premise 3 assumes covenants without enforcement always fail, but people keep promises for reasons other than fear — conscience, reputation, love, habit — and Hobbes himself appeals to such motives elsewhere. More fundamentally, the move from authorization to ownership is suspect: authorizing a representative for the sake of peace is not the same as owning everything the representative later does, including acts against your interests or your life. Locke's later charge captures this: no one can be understood to have given a representative authority to destroy them, since the authorization was given for preservation.

**Reply:** Hobbes can grant that some covenants hold without enforcement while insisting that a whole society cannot rest on them: the question is not whether any promise holds, but whether enough hold, reliably, among strangers who cannot monitor each other. On ownership, Hobbes's reply is that authorization is precisely the act of making the representative's acts your own within its scope, and that scope is set by the purpose — preservation. That is why chapter 21's retained rights are not an exception to the theory but its boundary: you cannot have authorized your own destruction, because authorization for preservation cannot include it. Whether that boundary is stable — whether a sovereign who systematically endangers subjects has exceeded its authorization or merely governs badly — remains the theory's most contested edge.

### (c) The argument for absolute (undivided) sovereignty (chs. 17–18)

**Premises:**

1. The sovereign is created by a covenant among subjects only; the sovereign is not a party to it, makes no promises under it, and therefore cannot breach it.
2. The purpose of sovereignty is to end the state of nature by providing a common power that overawes all parties alike; a power that can be overruled, resisted by right, or divided among rivals cannot overawe all parties alike.
3. Whoever judges whether the sovereign has overstepped a limit, or settles disputes between divided powers, is effectively sovereign — so a limited or divided sovereignty merely relocates sovereignty to the judge or the strongest rival.
4. Divided sovereignty is therefore civil war by institutional means: the condition the commonwealth exists to escape, reproduced inside it.

**Conclusion:** Sovereignty must be absolute and undivided — final, unlimited by any lawful superior or rival power, and not lawfully resistible by subjects as a body — whatever its form (monarchy, aristocracy, or democracy).

**Objection:** Absoluteness is self-defeating, because a sovereign with no lawful check has no reason to serve the subjects' preservation, and subjects have no lawful remedy when it does not. Hobbes's own retained right of self-preservation (chapter 21) shows the tension: if subjects may resist commands that endanger their lives, sovereignty is already limited in the one case that matters most, and the limit will be judged by each subject — which is exactly the private judgment Hobbes says must be surrendered. Either the retained right is empty, or sovereignty is not absolute in the sense Hobbes claims.

**Reply:** Hobbes can distinguish two kinds of limit. Sovereignty is unlimited in the sense that no subject, court, or assembly has lawful authority over it or a right to depose it; it is limited in the sense that its authority rests on, and lasts only while it serves, its purpose — protection. The retained right is not a legal check but a boundary of authorization: a command to destroy yourself was never within the authority you gave, because you gave it for preservation. The reply has a cost, which Hobbes accepts: subjects may judge in the extreme case whether a command endangers their life, but they may not generalize that judgment into a right of revolution, because the moment private judgment becomes a standing political right, the common power dissolves. Whether that distinction can hold in practice is a question Hobbes leaves uncomfortably open.

## Common misreadings

**1. "Hobbes is a crude psychological egoist — he thinks everyone is selfish and nothing but selfish."**
Hobbes's argument does not need the claim that people care only about themselves, and chapter 13's agents need not be villains: fearful, moderate people under uncertainty are driven to conflict by structure, not by wickedness. Hobbes does ground motivation in appetite and aversion, and he is skeptical of disinterested benevolence as a political foundation — but the state-of-nature argument turns on distrust and the absence of enforcement, not on universal selfishness. A world of partly benevolent people who cannot trust each other's promises still generates his problem.

**2. "The state of nature is Hobbes's history of early humans."**
It is chiefly an analytical device: remove the common power and ask what follows. Hobbes does gesture at historical approximations, including the condition of sovereigns toward one another and peoples living without common government, but the argument's force does not depend on those examples being accurate history — and some of them, notably his remarks on peoples in America, are poorly evidenced by modern standards. Treat the state of nature as a model to be tested, not a chronicle to be believed, and judge it by whether its mechanism — uncertainty plus unenforceable promises — explains conflict where it appears.

**3. "Sovereignty is unlimited in every sense — the sovereign may do anything, and subjects must obey everything."**
Sovereignty is unlimited in one precise sense: no subject, court, or rival assembly has lawful authority over it, and subjects have no collective right to depose it. But the covenant cannot alienate the right of self-preservation: a subject may resist immediate violence to their body, refuse self-destruction, and disobey commands that endanger their life. And obligation lasts only as long as protection does — where the sovereign can no longer protect, obedience ends, not by rebellion but because its reason has ceased. An absolutism with a retained right of self-defense at its center is a subtler doctrine than "obey everything."

**4. "The sovereign signed a contract with the people and can be fired for breaking it."**
The covenant is among subjects only. The sovereign is not a party, makes no promises under it, and cannot breach it; there is no judge above the sovereign to hear a complaint, because any such judge would itself be sovereign. This is the deliberate break with later contract theory: Locke will make government a trust that can be dissolved, and Rousseau will make the people themselves sovereign. Hobbes's point is that a contract with an umpire above the sovereign merely moves the problem one step up. Whether that makes his theory consistent or merely authoritarian is the debate to have — but it is not a contract the sovereign can break.

**5. "'Hobbesian' means cynical, brutal, or totalitarian."**
The word has become a slur, used for any politics of fear or force, and it obscures Hobbes's actual project: a secular, artificial, consent-based account of authority, in which the state is made by people rather than handed down by God, and in which an assembly or a democracy can be sovereign as legitimately as a king. Hobbes was accused of atheism in his lifetime; modern scholarship increasingly reads him as a theorist of the modern secular state rather than a philosopher of total power. The slur also hides his egalitarian starting point: authority rests on roughly equal individuals authorizing a representative, not on natural hierarchy.

**6. "Hobbes thought morality is just whatever the sovereign commands."**
Half true, and the half matters. Justice, for Hobbes, is defined by covenant: keeping covenants is just, breaking them unjust, and there is no justice before covenants. But the laws of nature — seek peace, keep covenants, show gratitude, pardon offenses — are precepts of reason that bind in conscience before any sovereign exists, and they are what tell people to create a sovereign in the first place. The sovereign does not invent the reason to seek peace; it supplies the enforcement without which that reason cannot be acted on safely. Hobbes's position is better described as: reason discovers the laws of nature, and sovereignty makes them effective — not as: might makes right.

**7. "Hobbes is a theorist of monarchy, so his theory is irrelevant to democracies."**
Hobbes preferred monarchy in practice and argued its practical advantages in chapter 19, but his theory requires only a unified representative. A democratic assembly can be Leviathan, and its sovereignty is as absolute as a king's. That is why later democratic theorists could borrow his machinery — popular authorization creating a single artificial person — while rejecting his conclusion that the people cannot then judge or replace their representative.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **Equality** means no one is so far above the rest that they can count on prevailing: even the weakest can kill the strongest by stealth or alliance. It matters because it removes the escape route of natural superiority — no one is safe by being strong, so everyone faces the same insecurity.
2. **Competition** (gain), **diffidence** (safety), and **glory** (reputation).
3. **War** means a known disposition to fight over a tract of time, as bad weather means a disposition to foulness, not perpetual rain. Actual fighting may be intermittent; what defines the condition is that no one can count on peace.
4. A **right of nature** is the liberty to use one's power as one will for self-preservation — in the state of nature, a right to all things. A **law of nature** is a precept of reason forbidding what destroys life or omitting what preserves it. They differ as **liberty** (absence of obligation) differs from **obligation** (a bond on action).
5. **First law:** seek peace as far as peace can be had; otherwise use the advantages of war. **Second law:** lay down your right to all things when others will do the same, keeping only as much liberty as you allow others. The second follows because peace is impossible while everyone retains an unlimited right to everything, including each other's bodies.
6. Because keeping your side alone, when the other party will not keep theirs, is self-destruction — contrary to the reason for covenanting. Words without fear of a coercive power are too weak to bind, so a covenant collapses at the first reasonable suspicion of non-performance.
7. **Third law:** perform covenants made. **Justice** is keeping covenants; injustice is breaking them. Before covenants there is neither just nor unjust, because there is nothing to keep or break.
8. The **Foole** says there is no justice: breaking a covenant secretly, when it pays, is rational. **Hobbes replies** that breach adopts a policy of breach, and a person known to break covenants when advantageous cannot be received into any society — destroying their own security, the very thing they sought. **Weakness:** the reply assumes the breach is known or generalized; a truly secret breach, never discovered, seems to escape it.
9. A **person** is one whose words or actions are considered as their own (natural person) or as representing another (artificial person). The **author** owns the words and actions; the **actor** performs them by the author's authority. A multitude needs a representative because, without one, it is many persons, not one: only when one person or assembly represents it can it speak and act as a unity.
10. **The covenant:** each person says to each other, in effect, "I authorise and give up my right of governing myself to this man or assembly, on condition you do the same." The **sovereign is not a party** because the covenant runs between subjects; the sovereign receives authority from it but promises nothing under it, and so cannot breach it.
11. Because in both cases subjects submit out of fear of violent death, and protection is the ground of obedience: obligation lasts as long as the power to protect lasts. Institution and acquisition differ in how sovereignty arises, not in the rights it carries — a claim scholars debate, since consent under conquest looks thinner than mutual agreement.
12. **Rights include:** sole legislation; judging controversies; making war and peace; choosing counsellors and ministers; judging what doctrines may be publicly taught. Subjects **cannot accuse the sovereign of injustice** because the sovereign acts by their authority — its acts are their own acts, so accusing it is accusing themselves.
13. Because **tyranny and oligarchy are names of disapproval**, not distinct forms: monarchy disliked is called tyranny, aristocracy disliked is called oligarchy. Forms are distinguished by the representative (one, some, all), not by whether the ruler is liked.
14. **Liberty** is the absence of external impediments to motion. Subjects are **free where the law is silent**: where the sovereign has not legislated, they may act as they judge best. Liberty is thus a space left by law, not a moral power against it.
15. Subjects retain the liberty to **resist immediate violence to their body, refuse self-destruction or self-wounding, refuse self-incrimination without pardon, and disobey commands endangering their life**. This is **not a right of revolution** because it is individual and defensive: it licenses no collective judgment, no deposition, and no standing mechanism against the sovereign — only refusal in the extreme case where obedience would defeat the covenant's purpose, preservation.
