# Study Guide: Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*

**Work:** *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (*Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten*) · **Author:** Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) · **Composed:** 1785
**Covers:** All three sections, with a reading plan, section-by-section guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, common misreadings, and a guide to approaching the *Critique of Pure Reason*.
**Companion entry:** [Immanuel Kant](../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)
**Focus study:** This guide is the Week 5 companion of the [Kant focus-study dossier](../focus-studies/immanuel-kant/README.md), whose eight-week plan builds the whole critical system around the *Groundwork* — the first *Critique* before it, the second *Critique* and *Religion* after it.

## Edition note

Read the *Groundwork* in a modern translation with Akademie (Ak) page numbers in the margins. The Akademie edition is the standard German scholarly edition, and Ak numbers (for example, 4:421) are how Kant is cited across translations, so learn to navigate by them. The plan in this library names the translation by Mary Gregor, revised by Jens Timmermann, with an introduction by Christine Korsgaard (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy) — clear, widely used on university syllabi, and well supported by the introduction. Allen Wood's translation (Yale) is a respected alternative. The older Ellington translation (Hackett) is serviceable if it is the copy you already own, though its wording is less current than Gregor's.

For the *Critique of Pure Reason*, which the closing section of this guide prepares you for, the edition to use is Paul Guyer and Allen Wood (Cambridge). Do not use Kemp Smith (1929) or Meiklejohn for the *Critique* as a first read: both are older translations whose terminology can mislead a beginner, and cheap reprints of them often lack the A/B pagination that every secondary source relies on.

A note on quotations in this guide: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows the Gregor wording. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so that your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

## How to use this guide

The *Groundwork* is short — roughly a hundred pages — and dense in a way that rewards slow reading rather than speed. Kant is not writing a list of moral rules. He is trying to find, and then to justify, the supreme principle of morality: the single principle from which particular duties could be derived, and which would bind any rational being, not merely human beings with our particular desires and history.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a section, read its part below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each section, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed. Do this before reading the reconstruction sections in this guide — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

The learning path places the *Groundwork* in Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant), after Hume, with Roger Scruton's *Kant: A Very Short Introduction* alongside. The sequence within the Kant block is the whole game: first "What is Enlightenment?" (1784, a few pages), then the *Groundwork*, then the *Prolegomena* (1783), and only then selections from the *Critique of Pure Reason*. Do not invert that order. The *Groundwork* comes before the *Critique* in this plan even though the *Critique* was published first (1781), because the *Groundwork*'s ethics can be followed without the *Critique*'s machinery, and because the *Groundwork*'s third section is the gentlest available introduction to the two-standpoint thinking the *Critique* will demand of you.

One warning, stated plainly: **Section 3 is the hardest section in the book, and it is the section most readers are tempted to skim or skip. Do not skip Sections 1–2 to get to it, and do not skip it either.** Sections 1 and 2 build the principle; Section 3 asks whether that principle is anything more than an attractive idea. If you read only Sections 1–2, you will have Kant's ethics without his answer — or his admitted failure to fully give an answer — to the question of why it binds. If you jump straight to Section 3, you will not know what principle is being justified. Read all three sections, in order, and allow Section 3 twice the time you give either of the others.

## Reading plan

The *Groundwork* runs to roughly a hundred pages in a modern paperback. At the learning path's pace, a first pass with this guide takes about three weeks; a second pass, after the *Prolegomena*, will take considerably less time, because you will already know where the argument is going.

| Week | Section | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Preface and §1 | Good will, duty, and the move from ordinary moral thought to philosophy |
| 2 | §2 | The categorical imperative: its formulations and its four illustrations |
| 3 | §3 | Freedom, autonomy, and the deduction — read twice, slowly |

Read "What is Enlightenment?" in the same week you begin the Preface, if you have not already read it. Its theme — thinking for oneself rather than under the direction of another — is the practical ancestor of the autonomy doctrine that Section 3 will defend.

Do not rush Section 2's four illustrations. They are the only place in the book where Kant shows the categorical imperative at work on concrete cases, and they are the evidence you will need when you come to judge whether the imperative is a real test or an empty formula.---

## Section-by-section guide

### Preface (4:387–392): What kind of book is this?

Kant opens by explaining the division of labor he has in mind. A metaphysics of morals, as he conceives it, is a system of moral principles that is pure — derived from reason alone, without borrowing anything from human nature, psychology, or experience. Empirical observations about what people happen to do, or what makes them happy, cannot yield a law that binds unconditionally; at most they yield advice. The *Groundwork* is therefore only a groundwork: it seeks the supreme principle of morality and justifies it, but it does not yet work out the full system of duties that a later *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797) would attempt. Kant's route is analytic in Sections 1–2 — ascending from ordinary moral cognition to the principle — and synthetic in Section 3, returning from the principle to ordinary cognition.

**Main argument:** Morality, if it is unconditional, cannot rest on experience; it needs a principle derived from reason alone, and finding that principle is this book's task.

### Section 1 (4:393–405): Good will, duty, and ordinary moral thought

Section 1 begins from what Kant takes ordinary reason already to know, and tries to make it explicit. Its famous opening claim is that nothing can be called good without limitation except a good will. Intelligence, courage, wealth, and even happiness can be misused or can corrupt their possessor; a good will, Kant argues, is good in itself, whatever its success. He compares it to a jewel that shines by its own light even if it is of no use — a striking image, but note what it does not claim: Kant is not saying that results do not matter to us, but that the worth of a will does not depend on its results.

From the good will, Kant turns to duty. He distinguishes acting merely in conformity with duty from acting from duty. A shopkeeper who charges fairly only because honesty pays is acting in conformity with duty, but his action, Kant says, has no moral worth: change the incentives and the behavior changes. An action has moral worth, on this account, only when it is done from duty — from respect for the moral law itself — rather than from inclination, however admirable the inclination may be. Kant's hardest illustration here is the person who has lost all love of life and yet preserves it from duty: such a person acts with moral worth precisely because inclination contributes nothing and duty does all the work. Readers often find this example forbidding, and it is worth asking whether Kant is claiming that feeling must be absent for an action to be moral, or merely that the moral worth lies in the motive of duty rather than in the feeling. The text supports the second reading more comfortably than the first.

Kant then extracts three propositions about duty. First, an action has moral worth when done from duty, not from inclination. Second, the moral worth of such an action lies not in its aim or expected effect but in the maxim — the subjective principle — on which it is done. Third, duty is the necessity of acting from respect for the law. Respect here is not admiration; it is a feeling produced by reason's recognition of the law's authority, and it is the only feeling Kant allows to be morally motivating in this strict sense. The section closes by pointing to the law itself: ordinary reason already operates with a principle it cannot yet state — act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law — and Section 2 will make it explicit.

### Section 2 (4:406–445): The categorical imperative

Section 2 is the center of the book, and the section you should be able to reconstruct from memory. Kant begins with a distinction between kinds of imperative — commands of reason. A hypothetical imperative says: if you want some end, take the necessary means. It binds only conditionally, on your having the end. A categorical imperative commands unconditionally: it tells you what to do regardless of what you happen to want. Morality, if it is unconditional, must take the form of a categorical imperative. The question is what its content can be.

Kant's answer proceeds by elimination. A categorical imperative cannot command any particular end drawn from inclination, since that would make it conditional. It can therefore command only the form of lawfulness itself: act only on a maxim that you could at the same time will to become a universal law. This is the **formula of universal law** (4:421). A maxim is your act's subjective principle — roughly, "in circumstances of this kind, I will do this, for this purpose." A maxim can fail the test in two ways: it may be inconceivable as a universal law, or conceivable but impossible to will — contradiction in conception versus contradiction in the will.

The four illustrations (4:422–423) apply the formula to four cases. A person contemplating suicide from self-love asks whether a maxim of ending life when it promises more pain than pleasure could be a universal law of nature; Kant argues it could not, since self-love's function is to preserve life. A person needing money considers a lying promise; universalized, promises would no longer be believed, so the maxim destroys the very practice it exploits (see the reconstruction below). A person with a talent considers letting it rust in idleness; Kant argues that while a world of universal idleness is conceivable, a rational being cannot will it. And a person comfortably off considers refusing all aid to others; again, a world without beneficence is conceivable, but Kant argues one cannot will it, because one might one day need the aid of others oneself. The first two cases are presented as perfect duties — duties admitting no exception — and the last two as imperfect duties, which leave latitude about how and when they are fulfilled. Scholars debate whether all four arguments succeed, and whether the suicide and talents cases are as strong as the lying-promise case; judge them case by case rather than accepting or rejecting the formula wholesale.

From the universal-law formula Kant derives two further formulations, which he presents as different ways of expressing the same law. The **formula of humanity** (4:429) runs: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means." Humanity here means rational nature — the capacity to set ends and to reason — not the human species as such; Kant's principle would apply to any rational being. Using a person as a means is ordinary and unavoidable: you use a taxi driver's services, a colleague's expertise, a shopkeeper's labor. What is forbidden is using them *merely* as a means — treating them as a tool only, in a way that bypasses their rational consent. Deception and coercion are Kant's paradigm cases, because they work precisely by preventing the other person from consenting to how they are being used.

The **formula of autonomy** (4:431) states that the will of every rational being is a will that gives universal law: the moral law is not imposed from outside, by God, custom, or nature, but is a law the rational will gives itself. From autonomy Kant derives the **formula of the kingdom of ends** (4:433): a systematic union of rational beings, each treated as an end, under laws they give themselves in common — an ideal standpoint for judging actual conduct, and the bridge to Section 3, where autonomy becomes the key to morality's justification.

Section 2 closes by distinguishing autonomy from heteronomy: a will is heteronomous when its law comes from outside reason — from inclination, from authority, from the desire for happiness, from divine command as externally imposed. All previous moral theories, Kant charges, failed because they were heteronomous: they looked for the ground of obligation somewhere other than the will's own lawgiving. Whether that charge is fair to, say, Aristotle or the sentimentalists is a question worth pressing; Kant's claim is programmatic, not a piece of historical scholarship, and it should be read as such.

**Main argument:** Morality's supreme principle is the categorical imperative, expressible as universal law, as respect for humanity as an end, and as autonomy — and its test is applied to maxims, not directly to acts in the abstract.

### Section 3 (4:446–463): Freedom, autonomy, and the deduction

Section 3 is the book's hardest and most compressed argument, and it is where most readers stall. Read it twice, and read it after a break, not immediately after Section 2. Its question is new: Sections 1 and 2 have shown what the moral law would be, if there is one; Section 3 asks why it binds us, and whether we are entitled to believe we are the kind of beings it addresses.

Kant's starting point is that every rational being with a will must act under the idea of freedom. He then argues for an identity: a free will and a will under moral laws are one and the same. A free will is not a will that does whatever it pleases; it is a will whose determining ground is not alien to it, and a will determined by nothing alien is determined by the law it gives itself — the moral law of Section 2.

But then comes the difficulty, which Kant states himself: the argument looks circular, since freedom is assumed to derive morality and morality is then used to establish freedom. His response turns on two standpoints: as a member of the world of sense I appear determined by natural causality, while as an intelligence I must regard myself as free when I deliberate. That practical necessity, not a theoretical proof, is what Kant offers as the moral law's justification — a "deduction" in his technical sense, meaning a vindication of an entitlement, not an ordinary derivation.

Kant closes the *Groundwork* by acknowledging the limits of this result with unusual candor. We cannot explain how freedom is possible, or how a purely rational law can move a being who is also sensibly affected; we can only show that morality and freedom stand or fall together, and that we must act as if we are free. The *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) will later recast this argument, presenting the moral law itself — the "fact of reason" — as the starting point from which freedom is inferred, reversing the order of presentation here. Scholars debate whether that later version repairs Section 3 or quietly abandons its strategy; you do not need to settle that on a first reading. What you need is to see where the argument's weight rests: on the claim that deliberation commits us to regarding ourselves as free, and on the distinction between the standpoints of sense and intelligence.

**Key passages:** freedom as a property of the will of all rational beings, 4:447–448; the circle, 4:450; the two standpoints, 4:451–453; the limits of the deduction, 4:458–463.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The good-will argument (§1, 4:393–394)

**Premises:**

1. Intelligence, wit, courage, wealth, and happiness can all be used badly or can corrupt their possessor; their goodness depends on how they are used and on the will that uses them.
2. A good will is good not because of what it achieves or effects, but by its own willing: even if it accomplishes nothing, it retains its full worth.
3. Therefore nothing else can be called good without limitation, since everything else is good only conditionally on the will that directs it.

**Conclusion:** The good will is the only thing good without limitation, and moral evaluation must begin from the will rather than from results or traits.

**Objection:** Premise 2 seems to make consequences irrelevant, and that looks morally perverse: a well-intentioned person who causes disaster through negligence seems worse, not equal, to a well-intentioned person who succeeds. And premise 1 overstates the case: some things, like pleasure or knowledge, may be good even when misused in a particular instance.

**Reply:** Kant need not deny that consequences matter for other kinds of evaluation — for prudence, for policy, for regret — only that they do not determine moral worth. The negligent agent is criticized, on Kant's account, for failing in the care that a good will would exercise. Whether that distinction fully answers the objection is debated, but it shows the argument is narrower — and therefore harder to refute — than the slogan "consequences do not matter" suggests.

### (b) The lying-promise application of the universal-law formula (§2, 4:422)

**Premises:**

1. The agent's maxim is: when I believe I need money, I will borrow it while promising to repay, though I know I cannot repay.
2. The universal-law test asks whether the agent can consistently will this maxim as a universal law — that is, as a law everyone acts on in the same circumstances.
3. If everyone acted on this maxim, promises would cease to be believed, since no one could distinguish sincere promises from insincere ones; the practice of promising would collapse.
4. The maxim therefore depends for its success on others not acting on it, and it destroys the very institution it exploits.

**Conclusion:** The lying promise fails the universal-law test: it is contrary to duty, not because of its bad consequences in this instance but because its maxim cannot be willed as a universal law.

**Objection:** The argument proves too little or too much depending on how the maxim is described. Describe the maxim more specifically — "when in desperate circumstances of exactly this kind, make a false promise" — and universalizing it may not destroy promising, since such circumstances are rare. Describe it more generally and almost any useful practice can be made to collapse. The test's result seems to depend on a level of description Kant does not justify.

**Reply:** Kant's test is applied to the maxim as the agent actually conceives it, and the lying-promiser's maxim, honestly stated, is the general one: borrow on a false promise when you need money. Agents cannot rescue a maxim by adding ad hoc qualifications designed to evade universalization; the qualifications would themselves have to be part of the principle the agent is willing to see universalized. Whether this reply fully fixes the level of description is a live debate in Kant scholarship — the "false positives and false negatives" problem — but the lying-promise case remains the strongest illustration precisely because the maxim, stated without evasion, defeats itself.

### (c) The humanity-formula argument (§2, 4:429)

**Premises:**

1. Rational nature — the capacity to set ends and to deliberate — is the condition under which anything can be an end in itself, since ends have worth only for beings who can value them.
2. A person who deceives or coerces another uses that person in a way that bypasses their rational consent: the victim could not agree to the treatment if they understood it, since understanding it would defeat the deception or the coercion.
3. To use a person in a way they could not rationally consent to is to treat them merely as a means — as a tool — rather than also as an end.

**Conclusion:** Deception and coercion violate the formula of humanity, and rational nature must always be treated as an end in itself, never merely as a means.

**Objection:** The consent criterion is too strict and too vague at once. Many ordinary interactions — advertising, negotiation, persuasion — involve influencing people in ways they have not fully consented to, and it is unclear what "could consent" means: actual consent, hypothetical informed consent, or consent under ideal conditions give different verdicts. Kant's own notorious application — that one must not lie even to a murderer at the door — shows how strict the prohibition becomes when deception is treated as always bypassing rational agency.

**Reply:** Kant's criterion targets the structure of the interaction, not its outcome: the question is whether the other person's rational agency is engaged or circumvented. Advertising that persuades by reasons engages agency; deception circumvents it, because the deceived person acts on a false belief the deceiver has deliberately created. As for the murderer at the door, many Kantians argue Kant's application was a mistake even on his own principles — one may refuse to answer, or argue that the murderer's wrongful threat changes what counts as treating him as an end — while others defend Kant's strictness as the honest price of a universal prohibition.

### (d) The §3 deduction: from freedom to morality, and the circularity problem (§3, 4:447–453)

**Premises:**

1. A free will is a will whose determining ground is not alien to it — not imposed by inclination, nature, or any external cause.
2. A will determined by nothing alien to it is determined by a law it gives itself; and a law a rational will gives itself universally is the moral law of Section 2.
3. Therefore a free will and a will under moral laws are one and the same.
4. Every rational being with a will must act under the idea of freedom: deliberation itself presupposes that I can determine myself by reasons rather than merely being pushed by causes.
5. A rational being can regard itself from two standpoints: as a phenomenon in the world of sense, subject to natural causality, and as an intelligence in the intelligible world, free from that causality.

**Conclusion:** Morality is vindicated practically: we must regard ourselves as free when we act, and a free will is a will under moral law, so the moral law binds every being that must act under the idea of freedom.

**Objection:** The argument is circular, and Kant admits the circle before answering it. Freedom is introduced to derive morality, but freedom itself seems established only by appeal to morality: we know we are free because we are under moral law, and we are under moral law because we are free. The two-standpoints move does not obviously break the circle; it relocates it. If the standpoint of intelligence is merely a way of regarding ourselves, not a piece of knowledge, then the deduction gives us a necessary presupposition of agency, not a proof that we are in fact free — and a presupposition is weaker than the justification Kant seems to promise.

**Reply:** Kant's aim in Section 3 is more modest than a theoretical proof, and recognizing that modesty is the key to reading it fairly. A deduction, for Kant, vindicates an entitlement; here the entitlement is to regard ourselves as bound by the moral law when we act. The two standpoints are not two worlds we must choose between but two perspectives on one agent, and the practical standpoint is not optional: an agent cannot deliberate without presupposing freedom, whatever metaphysics it professes. Whether that practical vindication is enough — and whether the later "fact of reason" doctrine improves on it — remains debated.

## How to approach the Critique of Pure Reason

This section is a bridge, not a summary of the *Critique*. Do not attempt the *Critique* before you have finished the *Groundwork* and the *Prolegomena*; the plan's sequence exists because the *Critique* is the book most likely to make a self-taught reader stop. When you do approach it, approach it as selections, not as a cover-to-cover march. The learning path prescribes selections only: the Prefaces, the Introduction, the Transcendental Aesthetic, the core of the Analytic, and the Paralogisms and Antinomies in summary. That is a design feature, not a compromise.

Begin with the two Prefaces, and read them as statements of intent rather than as arguments. The A Preface (1781) presents the *Critique* as a tribunal of reason: reason judging its own claims, deciding which metaphysical questions it may legitimately answer and which it must abandon. The B Preface (1787) restates the project with the famous Copernican analogy: just as Copernicus explained the heavens better by moving the observer, Kant proposes that objects of experience conform to our cognition rather than cognition conforming to objects. If you grasp that reversal — that the mind contributes the form of experience, not its content — you have the key to everything that follows. If it still sounds like the claim that the mind invents the world, you have misunderstood it: Kant is an empirical realist, as the companion entry stresses, and the world of experience remains public, law-governed, and real.

The Introduction then poses the *Critique*'s driving question: how are synthetic a priori judgments possible? A judgment is analytic if its predicate is already contained in its subject, synthetic if it adds something; a priori if it does not depend on any particular experience. Arithmetic, geometry, and the principle that every event has a cause look both informative and necessary, and Hume's skepticism about causation showed that experience alone cannot supply necessity. The *Critique* is Kant's attempt to explain how such judgments can be both informative and necessary: they articulate the structure our faculties contribute to experience. Keep Hume's *Enquiry* in mind throughout: Kant is answering Hume, not ignoring him.

The Transcendental Aesthetic is the most accessible stretch of the *Critique*, and the right place to build confidence. Its argument is that space and time are not things we perceive among other things, nor concepts we abstract from experience, but the a priori forms of our sensibility: space is the form of outer sense, time the form of inner sense. Nothing can appear to us except in space and time, which is why geometry and arithmetic seem both necessary and informative — they articulate these forms. The Aesthetic's conclusion, transcendental idealism, is also its most misread doctrine: space and time are transcendentally ideal, meaning they belong to the conditions of our cognition rather than to things in themselves, but empirically real, meaning they genuinely structure the world we experience. Hold both halves of that sentence together.

The Analytic is the *Critique*'s core and its hardest stretch; this is where a guided edition and secondary support earn their keep. Its central claim is that the understanding contributes twelve categories — pure concepts including unity, substance, and causality — which are the rules by which impressions are unified into experience of objects. The Transcendental Deduction argues that these categories legitimately apply to anything that can be an object for a self-conscious mind, because the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations. Do not try to master the Deduction on a first pass. Read it for its conclusion and its shape — that self-conscious experience requires conceptual unity, and that the categories are the form that unity takes — and return to its detail only if the *Prolegomena* has left you wanting more. The Schematism, which explains how pure concepts apply to sensible intuitions through time, may be skimmed on a first pass without guilt; its conclusion matters more than its mechanism at this stage.

Finally, take the Dialectic's Paralogisms and Antinomies in summary, as the plan directs. The Paralogisms are Kant's critique of rational psychology: arguments for the soul as a simple, persisting substance mistake the unity of consciousness — the "I think" that accompanies experience — for knowledge of a thing. The Antinomies are pairs of apparently rigorous proofs for contradictory conclusions about the world as a whole: that it is finite and that it is infinite, that everything is determined and that there is freedom. Kant's diagnosis is that both sides assume the world as a completed totality is a knowable object, which transcendental idealism denies. The third antinomy, on freedom versus determinism, is the one that matters most for your *Groundwork* reading: it is where the two-standpoints idea of Section 3 comes from, and it shows why Kant thinks theoretical reason can neither prove nor disprove freedom, leaving the question to practical reason. Read the Antinomies for that diagnosis, not for the proofs themselves.

Three practical rules for the whole undertaking. First, read with a secondary guide open: the plan recommends the [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)'s opening section before you start and the full entry afterwards, and Guyer on Kant in selection if the *Prolegomena* stalls. Second, keep an argument card for each major move — the Copernican reversal, the Aesthetic's argument, the Deduction's conclusion, the third antinomy — rather than notes on Kant's terminology: the terminology will follow the arguments, not the reverse. Third, accept incompleteness: a selections-only reading that leaves you able to state transcendental idealism, the synthetic a priori, and the third antinomy's diagnosis in your own words is a successful first pass, and the *Critique* is a book to return to, not a book to finish.---

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check yourself against the answers at the end of this guide.

1. What does Kant mean by saying that nothing is good without limitation except a good will, and why does he think wealth or intelligence fails that test?
2. What is the difference between acting in conformity with duty and acting from duty, and why does Kant think only the second has moral worth?
3. What is a maxim, and why does Kant's moral theory test maxims rather than acts in the abstract?
4. What is the difference between a hypothetical imperative and a categorical imperative, and why must morality take the second form?
5. State the formula of universal law. What are the two ways a maxim can fail its test?
6. Apply the universal-law formula to the lying-promise case: why does the maxim defeat itself when universalized?
7. In the four illustrations, which cases does Kant present as perfect duties and which as imperfect duties, and what is the difference between those two kinds of duty?
8. State the formula of humanity. What does Kant mean by "humanity," and what is the difference between using someone as a means and using them merely as a means?
9. Why does Kant think deception and coercion are paradigm violations of the humanity formula?
10. State the formula of autonomy and the formula of the kingdom of ends. How are they related?
11. What is heteronomy, and why does Kant charge all previous moral theories with it?
12. In §3, what does Kant mean by saying a free will and a will under moral laws are one and the same?
13. What is the circularity problem in §3, and how does Kant's distinction between two standpoints attempt to answer it?
14. What is the difference between the analytic route of §§1–2 and the synthetic route of §3?
15. Why does Kant say we cannot explain how freedom is possible, and what does he offer instead of such an explanation?
16. How does the *Groundwork*'s §3 argument relate to the third antinomy of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, as summarized in this guide's closing section?

## Common misreadings

**1. "The categorical imperative is just the Golden Rule."**
The Golden Rule — do as you would be done by — is indexed to what you happen to want: it tells you to treat others as you would wish to be treated. Kant explicitly distinguishes his principle from it, and the companion entry notes his reason: a principle indexed to contingent desires cannot ground duties to oneself, or strict duties of justice, since a person might wish to be treated in ways morality should not endorse. The categorical imperative tests whether your maxim could be willed as a universal law, not whether you would like its application to yourself. The resemblance is superficial; the logic is different.

**2. "Kant is indifferent to consequences."**
Kant denies that consequences determine moral worth, which is not the same as denying that they matter. A good will, on his account, is criticized for negligence, and his later system of duties plainly cares about the effects of actions — beneficence aims at others' happiness, and the kingdom of ends is a social ideal. The claim is narrower: that an action's moral worth lies in its maxim and motive, not in its outcome.

**3. "Autonomy means doing whatever you choose."**
Autonomy, for Kant, is obedience to a law your own reason gives universally — the opposite of following inclination or whim. A will that merely does what it pleases is heteronomous, governed by desires that come from outside reason. Autonomy is demanding precisely because the law it gives is the same for every rational being; it is self-legislation, not self-indulgence, and it is what grounds dignity on Kant's account rather than licensing arbitrary choice.

**4. "Section 3 straightforwardly proves that we are free."**
Section 3 offers no theoretical proof of freedom, and Kant says as much. What it offers is a practical vindication: deliberation commits us to acting under the idea of freedom, and a free will is a will under moral law. Whether that practical argument is enough, and whether the later "fact of reason" doctrine improves on it, is debated among scholars.

**5. "Kant's ethics is a rulebook: never lie, never break a promise, no exceptions."**
The *Groundwork* derives a principle and illustrates it; it does not publish a code. Kant's own strict applications — notoriously on lying — are his applications of the principle, and they are debated even by Kantians, as the reconstruction of the humanity formula above notes. The theory's center is the test applied to maxims, and much of the interesting work lies in stating maxims honestly and seeing what the test yields.

**6. "The good will must be free of all feeling."**
Kant's claim is that moral worth lies in acting from duty, not that feeling must be absent. The example of the person who preserves life from duty though they have lost all love of life is meant to isolate the motive of duty for analysis, not to recommend emotional desolation. Kant elsewhere encourages cultivating feelings — sympathy, for instance — that support dutiful action.

**7. "Kant's kingdom of ends is a prediction about the future."**
The kingdom of ends is an ideal, a standpoint for judging conduct and institutions, not a forecast that such a community will come into being. Like the city in speech in Plato's *Republic*, it functions as a standard: act as if you were legislating for a community of ends. Reading it as a utopian program mistakes its role.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **Only the good will is good without limitation (4:393–394)** because everything else — intelligence, courage, wealth, happiness — can be misused or can corrupt its possessor; its goodness depends on the will directing it. A good will is good by its willing alone, whatever it achieves.
2. **Acting in conformity with duty** matches what duty requires but from another motive, such as profit; **acting from duty** is done from respect for the moral law itself. Only the second has moral worth, because only it would remain if the incentives changed.
3. **A maxim is the subjective principle of an act** — roughly, in circumstances of this kind, I will do this, for this purpose. Kant tests maxims because the same outward act can be done on different principles, and moral evaluation concerns the principle the agent acts on.
4. **A hypothetical imperative** binds conditionally: if you want an end, take the means. **A categorical imperative** commands unconditionally, regardless of your wants. Morality must take the categorical form, since a morality binding only those who happened to want something would not be unconditional.
5. **"Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (4:421).** A maxim fails by contradiction in conception (it cannot even be conceived as a universal law) or by contradiction in the will (it can be conceived but cannot be willed by a rational being).
6. **The lying promise (4:422)** universalized destroys promising: if everyone borrowed on false promises when short of money, promises would no longer be believed, so the liar's own maxim could not succeed. The maxim exploits a practice it would destroy.
7. **Perfect duties:** not committing suicide and not making lying promises — duties admitting no exception. **Imperfect duties:** developing one's talents and helping others — duties of a kind that leave latitude about when and how they are fulfilled.
8. **"Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means" (4:429).** Humanity means rational nature, the capacity to set ends. Using someone as a means is ordinary — employing their services — but using them merely as a means bypasses their rational consent and treats them as a tool only.
9. **Deception and coercion** work by circumventing rational agency: the deceived person acts on a belief deliberately created for them, and the coerced person is compelled rather than persuaded. Both bypass the consent that treating someone as an end requires.
10. **Autonomy (4:431):** the will of every rational being gives universal law to itself. **The kingdom of ends (4:433):** a systematic union of rational beings, each an end, under self-given common laws — autonomy made social.
11. **Heteronomy** is a will whose law comes from outside reason — inclination, authority, happiness, or externally imposed divine command. Kant charges previous theories with heteronomy because they grounded obligation somewhere other than the will's own lawgiving.
12. **A free will is one determined by nothing alien to it;** a will determined by nothing alien is determined by a law it gives itself; and a law a rational will gives itself universally is the moral law (4:447). Freedom and subjection to moral law are therefore the same property viewed two ways.
13. **The circle (4:450):** freedom is assumed to derive morality, and morality is then appealed to in order to establish freedom. Kant's answer is the two standpoints (4:451–453): as a phenomenon I am determined, as an intelligence I must regard myself as free, and practical deliberation requires the second standpoint.
14. **The analytic route (§§1–2)** ascends from ordinary moral cognition to the supreme principle. **The synthetic route (§3)** descends from the principle back to ordinary cognition, asking whether it genuinely binds beings like us.
15. **We cannot explain how freedom is possible** because explanation would trace it to causes in the world of sense, where freedom by definition does not operate (4:458–463). What Kant offers instead is a practical vindication: we must act under the idea of freedom, and that standpoint suffices for morality's authority.
16. **The third antinomy** shows theoretical reason can prove both determinism and freedom, and therefore neither — a stalemate. Section 3 presupposes that stalemate: since theory cannot settle freedom, the question is handed to practical reason, which requires us to act under the idea of freedom.
