# Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*: A Study Guide

**Work:** *Nicomachean Ethics* (*Ethica Nicomachea*)
**Author:** Aristotle (384–322 BCE)
**Composed:** c. 340 BCE (conventional date; the treatise took shape during Aristotle's mature period, and its exact date of composition is not securely known)
**What this guide covers:** A book-by-book guide to all ten books of the *Ethics*, with argument reconstructions, checkpoint questions, and common misreadings. It is designed to be read alongside the primary text, not instead of it.
**Related entries:** [Aristotle](../eras/01-ancient/aristotle.md) · [Learning path](../learning-path.md)

## Suggested edition

Read the *Ethics* in a modern student edition with notes: Terence Irwin's translation (Hackett) or C. D. C. Reeve's translation (Hackett) are both well suited to a first reading, with extensive notes and a glossary of key Greek terms. The learning path names these two for exactly that reason, and warns against starting with W. D. Ross's translation: Ross is accurate but terse and largely unhelped by notes, so it is harder going for a first reader.

The W. D. Ross translation is in the public domain, which makes it useful as a freely available reference text. Where this guide quotes Aristotle at all, it quotes briefly and attributes the translation to Ross; everywhere else it paraphrases. Quotation is kept short deliberately: the aim is to help you reconstruct Aristotle's arguments in your own words, which is the skill the learning path asks of every core author.

A note on references: citations in this guide use Bekker numbers (the standard page, column, and line references to the Berlin Academy edition of Aristotle, e.g. 1097a15). Every modern translation prints them in the margin, so you can find a passage in any edition from the numbers given here.

## How to use this guide

1. **Read the primary text first, in the order below.** Use the book summaries here as orientation before each book and as a check afterwards — not as a substitute for reading Aristotle.
2. **Reconstruct before you criticize.** After each book, close the book and write the main argument in your own words, premises first, conclusion last. Only then turn to the objections noted here.
3. **Use the checkpoint questions book-closed.** The questions in §3 are for self-testing; the answers are in §5, collected at the end so you cannot accidentally read them first.
4. **Keep the Greek terms in view.** Several of Aristotle's key words — *eudaimonia*, *aretē*, *phronēsis*, *theōria* — have no exact English equivalent. This guide keeps them alongside their translations so you notice when a translation is doing interpretive work.

### Reading plan

The learning path assigns the *Ethics* selectively on a first pass: Books I–II, selections from III, VII, and VIII, and selections from X, before moving on to the *Politics*. This guide covers all ten books so that you can fill in the gaps (notably IV–VI and IX) on a second pass or in a buffer week, without needing a second book.

A workable pacing, at roughly the learning path's rate of two hours of primary reading per session:

| Week | Reading | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Book I | The good, eudaimonia, the function argument |
| 2 | Book II | Virtue of character, habituation, the mean |
| 3 | Books III–IV | Voluntary action, choice, courage, temperance, and the particular virtues |
| 4 | Book V | Justice |
| 5 | Book VI | Intellectual virtues and practical wisdom |
| 6 | Book VII | Akrasia, pleasure (first discussion) |
| 7 | Books VIII–IX | Friendship |
| 8 | Book X | Pleasure (second discussion), contemplation, and the turn to politics |

If time is short, protect Books I, II, VI, and X.7–8: they carry the theory. Books IV and IX repay a slower read on a second pass; Book V is the most technical and can be read selectively the first time through (V.1–2 and V.5 for the core distinction between kinds of justice).

### A note on the *Eudemian Ethics*

Aristotle wrote — or is associated with — a second ethical treatise, the *Eudemian Ethics*, which overlaps the *Nicomachean Ethics* substantially: three books (on justice, intellectual virtue, and related topics) appear in both treatises in nearly identical form, and scholars debate which treatise they originally belonged to and how the two works relate. The debate matters for fine points of interpretation — for example, whether the shared books should be read primarily in a *Nicomachean* or a *Eudemian* context — but it does not change the overall shape of the theory presented here. This guide follows the standard practice of treating the *Nicomachean Ethics* as the primary text and notes the overlap where it affects a reading, rather than settling the debate.

## 1. Book-by-book guide

### Book I — The good and the function argument (1094a1–1102a33)

**Summary.** Aristotle begins from a method: start from reputable opinions (*endoxa*) — what people already say about the good — and test them. Every craft, inquiry, and action aims at some good, and goods form hierarchies: bridle-making serves horsemanship, which serves generalship, which serves politics. The highest good must be final (sought for its own sake, never for something else), self-sufficient, and the most complete end. Candidates are examined and found wanting: pleasure (the life of the many), honor (the political life — too dependent on others), wealth (merely useful), and Plato's Form of the Good (rejected: a single Form cannot cover the many ways "good" is said, and it would be useless to a practitioner). The answer is *eudaimonia*: living well and doing well, achieved through activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a whole life.

**Key passages:** the hierarchy of ends (1094a1–1094b11); the three lives (1095b14–1096a10); the critique of the Form of the Good (1096a11–1097a14); the function argument (1097b22–1098a20); eudaimonia and a complete life, including the Priam discussion (1098a16–1101b32).

**Main argument.** The function argument (I.7) moves from the thought that each kind of thing has a characteristic activity — a flautist's is flute-playing, an eye's is seeing — to the claim that the human function is activity of the soul in accordance with reason, and that the human good is performing that activity well, that is, in accordance with virtue. A full reconstruction is given in §2(a) below.

### Book II — Virtue, habituation, and the mean (1103a14–1109b26)

**Summary.** Virtues divide into virtues of thought (*dianoetic* virtues: wisdom, understanding, practical wisdom), which are taught, and virtues of character (*ethical* virtues: courage, temperance, generosity), which are acquired by habit. We become just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts — as a builder learns by building. This raises the puzzle: how can we do just acts before we are just? Aristotle's answer is that beginners act justly in the sense of doing the same *kind* of act, but without yet having the settled disposition, knowledge, and choice for its own sake that the virtuous person has.

Character virtue is then defined as a state concerned with choice, lying in a mean relative to us, determined by reason and by the standard the practically wise person would use. The mean is flanked by two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency: courage between recklessness and cowardice, generosity between wastefulness and stinginess. Hitting the mean is hard, and Aristotle gives practical advice: steer away from the extreme you are most drawn to, and beware of pleasure, which distorts judgment.

**Key passages:** habituation (1103a14–1103b25); the puzzle of becoming virtuous (1105a17–1105b12); the definition of virtue (1106b36–1107a27); the table of virtues and vices (1107a28–1108b10); practical advice on hitting the mean (1109a24–1109b26).

**Main argument.** The doctrine of the mean (II.6) is reconstructed in §2(b) below. Its central subtlety is that the mean is relative *to us* — relative to the agent and the situation — not an arithmetic midpoint. Ten pounds of food may be too much for a beginner athlete and too little for a champion; likewise the right amount of anger depends on the occasion, the person, and the reason.

### Book III — Voluntary action, choice, courage, and temperance (1109b30–1119b18)

**Summary.** The first half (III.1–5) is about responsibility: which actions are voluntary, and therefore praiseworthy or blameworthy? Actions done under compulsion or through ignorance of the particular circumstances are involuntary; actions done from ignorance *of what one ought to know* are not excused. Choice (*prohairesis*) is deliberate desire for what is in our power, reached through deliberation about means, not ends. We deliberate about how to achieve health, not about whether to be healthy. Character, Aristotle insists, is itself voluntary in the sense that we are responsible for the habits that formed it — the person who became unjust by repeatedly acting unjustly cannot now plead that he cannot help it.

The second half (III.6–12) begins the survey of particular virtues: courage (the mean concerning fear and confidence, paradigmatically fear of death in battle — civilian courage, endurance, and spiritedness are distinguished from genuine courage) and temperance (the mean concerning bodily pleasures, especially touch and taste).

**Key passages:** the voluntary and involuntary (1109b30–1111b3); choice and deliberation (1111b4–1113a14); character as voluntary (1113b30–1115a3); courage (1115a6–1117b22); temperance (1117b23–1119b18).

**Main argument.** Aristotle's account of responsibility turns on two conditions: the origin of the action must be in the agent, and the agent must know the particular circumstances. Ignorance excuses only when it is ignorance of particulars (who, what, with what instrument) and is regretted afterwards. This framework underwrites his later treatment of akrasia in Book VII and of justice in Book V.

### Book IV — The remaining virtues of character (1119b19–1128b35)

**Summary.** Book IV works through the virtues that govern wealth, honor, and social life: generosity and magnificence (giving well, at the right scale), magnanimity (greatness of soul — claiming the honor one deserves, neither over- nor under-claiming), the virtue concerned with small honors (ambition rightly ordered), gentleness (anger rightly felt), truthfulness, wit, and friendliness. Several of these chapters are brief and culturally specific — magnanimity in particular presupposes a social world of public honor that looks remote today — but the book matters because it shows the mean operating across the whole texture of social life, not only in dramatic cases like battlefield courage.

**Key passages:** generosity (1119b19–1122a17); magnificence (1122a18–1123a33); magnanimity (1123a34–1125a35); gentleness (1125b26–1126b10); truthfulness, wit, friendliness (1126b11–1128b35).

**Main argument.** The recurring pattern is that each virtue names a *way of being well-disposed* toward some domain of feeling and action, and the vices name failures that come in pairs. The book also quietly introduces a difficulty: some of these virtues seem to presuppose good fortune (magnificence requires wealth; magnanimity requires a public standing worth claiming), which raises the question — pressed again in Book I's discussion of Priam — of how far eudaimonia depends on external goods.

### Book V — Justice (1129a3–1138b14)

**Summary.** Justice is treated in two senses. Universal justice is lawfulness: the whole of virtue as it bears on relations with others, expressed in law. Particular justice divides into distributive justice (fair shares of honor, wealth, and other divisible goods, allocated in proportion to merit) and rectificatory or corrective justice (restoring the balance when one party has wronged another, treating the parties as equals before the law regardless of status). Aristotle adds a discussion of reciprocity, of equity (*epieikeia*) — the correction of law where a general rule fails in a particular case — and of the distinction between acting unjustly and being an unjust person.

**Key passages:** universal vs. particular justice (1129a3–1130b29); distributive justice and geometric proportion (1130b30–1132b20); rectificatory justice and arithmetic proportion (1132b21–1133b28); reciprocity (1132b31–1134a16); equity (1137a31–1138b14).

**Main argument.** The famous proportions are worth getting right. Distributive justice uses *geometric* proportion: shares should stand in the same ratio as the merits of the recipients (if A deserves twice what B deserves, A should receive twice the share). Rectificatory justice uses *arithmetic* proportion: the judge removes the wrongdoer's gain and restores the victim's loss, treating the parties as equal for this purpose. Disagreement about what counts as merit — Aristotle lists freedom, wealth, birth, and virtue as rival criteria — is itself a political question, which is why Book V leads naturally into the *Politics*.

### Book VI — The intellectual virtues and practical wisdom (1138b18–1145a11)

**Summary.** Book VI turns from character to thought. The soul's rational part divides into the scientific part, which grasps what cannot be otherwise, and the calculative part, which deliberates about what can be otherwise. Corresponding virtues: scientific knowledge (*epistēmē*), art or craft (*technē*), practical wisdom (*phronēsis*), theoretical wisdom (*sophia*), and understanding (*nous*). Practical wisdom is the virtue of deliberating well about what conduces to living well as a whole — not merely about means to a given end, but about ends themselves as they appear in a concrete situation. It requires experience, which is why Aristotle says the young can be clever at mathematics but cannot yet possess practical wisdom.

The book's central question is the relation between practical wisdom and character virtue: neither is possible without the other. Practical wisdom without virtue would be mere cleverness in service of bad ends; virtue without practical wisdom would be a natural disposition (a child can be naturally brave) rather than virtue in the full sense.

**Key passages:** the two parts of the rational soul (1138b18–1139a17); the five intellectual virtues (1139b14–1141b23); practical wisdom distinguished from art and science (1140a24–1140b30); natural virtue vs. virtue proper (1144b1–1145a11).

**Main argument.** Aristotle's claim that virtue and practical wisdom are mutually dependent is one of the theory's load-bearing joints. If it holds, then ethics cannot be reduced to a set of rules a person could follow without judgment, and moral education cannot be separated from the cultivation of perception — seeing what a situation calls for. If it fails, the mean threatens to become empty: the "right amount" would be whatever the practically wise person says, with no independent grip on what makes it right. This circularity worry is a live scholarly debate, not a settled refutation; see §2(b) and §4.3.

### Book VII — Akrasia and pleasure (1145a15–1154b34)

**Summary.** Book VII takes up three problems: akrasia (acting against one's better judgment), pleasure, and the brutish or bestial state. The akrasia discussion starts from Socrates' puzzle: how can a person who *knows* the good act against it? Aristotle rejects the Socratic answer that this is impossible and mere ignorance. His account distinguishes knowing in the abstract (having knowledge) from knowing in the active sense (using it in the moment); the akratic person has the relevant knowledge but, under the pressure of appetite, fails to bring it to bear — like a person asleep, drunk, or reciting lines without understanding them. He also distinguishes the akratic person from the self-indulgent person: the former acts against a judgment he endorses and regrets it; the latter endorses his appetites and feels no conflict.

The book then turns to pleasure, arguing against both the view that pleasure is the good and the view that no pleasure is good. Pleasure is the activity of a healthy state, unimpeded — more like the bloom on a healthy activity than a separate thing added to it.

**Key passages:** the Socratic puzzle stated (1145b21–1146b5); the two senses of knowing (1146b31–1147b19); akrasia vs. self-indulgence (1150a9–1152a7); pleasure as unimpeded activity (1152b33–1153a35).

**Main argument.** Aristotle's solution preserves both halves of the phenomenon: the akratic person genuinely judges the better course (so he is not simply ignorant, as Socrates claimed) and yet genuinely fails to act on it (so he is not simply a hypocrite). Whether the two-senses-of-knowing account fully explains this remains debated — see §4.4.

### Books VIII–IX — Friendship (1155a3–1172a15)

**Summary.** Friendship (*philia*) is treated at greater length than any other single topic in the *Ethics*, which itself tells you how central Aristotle takes it to be: he opens by saying no one would choose to live without friends, even with all other goods. There are three kinds of friendship, distinguished by what is loved: the useful, the pleasant, and the good. Friendships of utility and pleasure dissolve when the utility or pleasure ends; friendship between good people, based on mutual recognition of character, is stable and complete. Later chapters discuss friendship between unequals (parent and child, ruler and subject), the claims friends have on each other, self-love (the good person is a friend to himself, and that is why he can be a friend to others), and whether the happy person needs friends — Aristotle's answer is yes, since a flourishing life is an active life shared with others, and friends make both virtuous activity and self-knowledge possible.

**Key passages:** the three kinds of friendship (1155b17–1157a36); friendship of the good as complete (1156b7–1157a36); friendship between unequals and proportion (1158b11–1163b28); self-love (1168a28–1169b2); why the happy person needs friends (1169b3–1172a15).

**Main argument.** The treatment of friendship is where Aristotle's ethics most visibly depends on a social world: eudaimonia is not a solitary achievement but an activity carried on with others. This is also the book that most rewards reading against the modern tendency to treat friendship as a private sentiment: for Aristotle, friendship is partly a civic institution, and the best friendships are relationships of mutual moral formation.

### Book X — Pleasure, contemplation, and the turn to politics (1172a19–1181b23)

**Summary.** Book X revisits pleasure (arguing, against the hedonist Eudoxus's critics and against the anti-hedonists, that pleasure is not itself the good but accompanies and completes good activity) and then crowns the treatise with the famous claim about contemplation. The happiest life, Aristotle argues, is the life of *theōria* — contemplative activity, the exercise of our highest capacity, intellect, on the highest objects. It is the most continuous, self-sufficient, and leisurely activity, and the one we share with the divine. The life of ethical virtue is happy too, but secondarily: it is human happiness, bound up with the body, with others, and with external goods, whereas contemplation touches something divine in us.

The book closes by turning to politics: ethics alone cannot make people good, since most people are moved by compulsion rather than argument, so the inquiry must continue into laws and constitutions — the bridge to the *Politics*.

**Key passages:** pleasure revisited (1172a19–1176a29); the contemplative life (1177a12–1178a8); the life of ethical virtue as second-best (1178a9–1179a32); the transition to politics (1179a33–1181b23).

**Main argument.** The contemplative peak of Book X is reconstructed in §2(c) below. It also creates the *Ethics*' most discussed internal tension: how does a life devoted to contemplation fit with the practically engaged, socially embedded account of flourishing in Books I–IX? Competing readings — that contemplation is one ingredient among others, that it is the dominant end, or that the two accounts reflect different strata of the text — are all defended by serious scholars. This guide presents the tension as unresolved; see §2(c) and §4.7.

## 2. Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction states the argument in numbered premises, then the conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. The replies are Aristotle's or a standard Aristotelian defense as noted — they are not presented as decisive refutations of the objections.

### (a) The function argument (I.7, 1097b22–1098a20)

1. For anything with a function (*ergon*), its good lies in performing that function well. (A flautist's good is playing the flute well; an eye's good is seeing well.)
2. If a part of a person (eye, hand, foot) has a function, then the person as a whole presumably has a function too — it would be strange if the parts had functions but the whole did not.
3. The human function cannot be mere nutrition and growth (shared with plants) or mere perception (shared with animals).
4. What remains, distinctive to humans, is activity of the soul in accordance with reason — both obeying reason and exercising reason in thought.
5. The good of a thing is performing its function in accordance with the relevant excellence (virtue): a good flautist plays well, not merely plays.
6. Therefore, the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue — and, since a single swallow does not make a spring, over a complete life.

**Conclusion:** Eudaimonia is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a lifetime.

**Objection.** The move from "having a function" to "having a good" seems to trade on an ambiguity. Artefacts have functions because someone designed them for a purpose; eyes have functions in a biological sense. But even granting those, why should a human being *as such* have a single function, rather than many capacities with no one of them privileged? The argument seems to assume the teleological worldview it needs to establish.

**Reply (Aristotelian).** Aristotle's notion of function is not primarily about design. It is about what a thing characteristically does when it is functioning well — the activity that explains why it has the structure it does. An eye is for seeing because seeing is what explains the eye's structure. Likewise, reason is the capacity that structures distinctively human life: our social, deliberative, and moral activities are all exercises of it. The argument's force therefore depends on accepting Aristotle's broader teleology — that natural things can be understood by their characteristic activities — which is exactly where a modern reader may reasonably part company with him. That is a legitimate disagreement about the premises, not a confusion in the argument's form.

### (b) The doctrine of the mean (II.6, 1106a26–1107a27)

1. Virtue of character is concerned with feelings and actions, in which excess and deficiency are both bad (too much fear is cowardice, too little is recklessness) while the intermediate is praiseworthy.
2. The intermediate is not an arithmetic midpoint but a mean *relative to us* — relative to the agent, the occasion, the object, and the reason.
3. Virtue is a settled state (*hexis*) that disposes us to choose this intermediate, as determined by reason and by the standard the practically wise person would apply.
4. Therefore, virtue is a state concerned with choice, lying in a mean relative to us, determined by reason.

**Conclusion:** Each virtue of character is a mean between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency.

**Objection.** The doctrine looks either trivial or empty. Trivial, if "the mean" just means "the right amount" — then it tells us nothing we did not already know. Empty, if the only way to identify the mean is to ask what the practically wise person would do, since we then need an independent account of practical wisdom to avoid circularity.

**Reply (Aristotelian).** The doctrine is not meant to be a decision procedure; it is a structural claim about what virtue *is*, which does real work. It rules out the idea that more of a good trait is always better (more courage is not always better — beyond a point it becomes recklessness), and it explains why virtues come in triples rather than pairs. The content comes from elsewhere in the theory: from the detailed accounts of each virtue in Books III–V, from the account of practical wisdom in Book VI, and from the habituated perception of the virtuous person. The circularity worry is genuine but not fatal: Aristotle's view is that ethical knowledge is not codifiable, so a theory that left a remainder for judgment would be a vice, not a virtue, in his eyes. Whether that satisfies a reader who wants rules is a matter of philosophical temperament — and a live debate.

### (c) The contemplative life is happiest (X.7–8, 1177a12–1179a32)

1. Happiness is activity in accordance with the highest virtue, and the highest virtue belongs to the best thing in us — intellect (*nous*).
2. Contemplation (*theōria*) is the activity of intellect, directed at the highest objects (truths that do not change).
3. Contemplation is the most continuous activity (we can contemplate longer than we can act), the most self-sufficient (it needs least from outside), the most leisurely, and the only activity loved for its own sake without qualification.
4. The gods, who are supremely happy, do not perform just or brave actions (it would be absurd to imagine gods making contracts or facing dangers); their activity must be contemplation. A life that shares in this activity shares in divine happiness.
5. Therefore, the life of contemplation is the happiest life, and the life of ethical virtue — though happy — is happy in a secondary way, as the life of a human being rather than of something divine in us.

**Conclusion:** The most eudaimōn life is the contemplative life; the practically virtuous life is happy secondarily.

**Objection.** This seems to contradict the rest of the *Ethics*. Books I–IX present flourishing as an active, social, practically engaged life, dependent on friendship, external goods, and the virtues of character. If contemplation is the happiest life, the ethical virtues look demoted to a consolation prize, and the practically wise person — the hero of Book VI — looks like someone who has settled for second best. Some scholars conclude the two accounts cannot both be Aristotle's considered view.

**Reply (Aristotelian, and one scholarly line).** One standard reply is that the two accounts are compatible if contemplation is the *dominant* end of a life that also includes ethical virtue, not a replacement for it: the contemplative person still needs friends, still acts justly, and still exercises character — contemplation crowns such a life rather than cancelling it. Another line, defended by other scholars, is that the tension is real and reflects different layers of the treatise. This guide does not adjudicate between these readings; what matters for study is to see why each is motivated. Note also that Aristotle himself qualifies the claim: contemplation is the happiest life *for a human being insofar as something divine is present in us*, and the life of ethical virtue remains "happy" in its own right — he does not call it unhappy.

## 3. Checkpoint questions

Answer book-closed where possible. Answers are in §5.

1. (Book I) What three conditions must the highest good satisfy, and why does wealth fail them?
2. (Book I) State the function argument in three sentences, and name the step a critic is most likely to attack.
3. (Book I) Why does Aristotle say a single swallow does not make a spring — and what does the Priam example show about the limits of that point?
4. (Book II) Explain the difference between virtues of thought and virtues of character, and how each is acquired.
5. (Book II) Give one example of a virtue as a mean, naming both vices, and explain why the mean is not an arithmetic midpoint.
6. (Book III) What two conditions make an action involuntary, and why does ignorance of what one ought to know not excuse?
7. (Book III) What is choice (*prohairesis*), and how does it differ from wish and from opinion?
8. (Book IV) Why does magnanimity seem to require good fortune, and what problem does that raise for the theory?
9. (Book V) Distinguish distributive from rectificatory justice, and state which proportion each uses.
10. (Book VI) What is practical wisdom (*phronēsis*), and why does Aristotle say the young cannot yet have it?
11. (Book VI) Why is virtue impossible without practical wisdom, and practical wisdom impossible without virtue?
12. (Book VII) How does Aristotle's account of akrasia differ from Socrates' claim that no one acts against their knowledge?
13. (Books VIII–IX) Name the three kinds of friendship and explain why only one is stable.
14. (Book X) Why does Aristotle argue that contemplation is more self-sufficient than ethical activity?
15. (Whole work) State one way in which the contemplative peak of Book X creates a tension with Books I–IX, and one scholarly response to that tension.

## 4. Common misreadings

**1. "The mean is mediocrity, or a simple midpoint."**
It is neither. The mean is the *right* response — the right amount, at the right time, toward the right person, for the right reason — and it is relative to the agent and the situation. Sometimes the right amount of anger is a great deal; sometimes it is none. And some acts, Aristotle insists, admit no mean at all: murder, theft, and adultery are simply bad, not "excessive" versions of something good. A doctrine that recommends moderation in everything would be a different, much duller theory.

**2. "Eudaimonia means happiness in the modern subjective sense."**
It does not. Eudaimonia is flourishing — living well and doing well over a whole life through excellent activity. A person can feel cheerful and still fail to be eudaimōn (a life of passive pleasure would not count), and a person can be eudaimōn while experiencing grief. Translating the word as "happiness" is conventional but misleading, and most of the confusion about Book X comes from reading modern connotations into it.

**3. "Virtue ethics is rule-free intuition."**
Aristotle denies both halves. He denies that ethics can be reduced to a code — that is why practical wisdom, not a rulebook, is the center of Book VI. But he does not replace rules with gut feeling: the practically wise person's judgment is trained by habituation, informed by experience, and answerable to reasons. Whether that trained judgment can be made fully explicit is debated (see the circularity worry in §2(b)), but "do what feels right" is not Aristotle's view.

**4. "Akrasia is just weakness of will in the modern sense."**
Only with qualification. Modern discussions of weakness of will often concern a conflict between a judgment and a desire where the agent acts on the desire. Aristotle's akrasia is narrower and more structured: it involves a specific failure to bring knowledge to bear in the moment, and he distinguishes it carefully from self-indulgence (where there is no conflicting judgment) and from brutishness. Reading Book VII as if it were a modern self-help chapter on willpower flattens those distinctions.

**5. "Aristotle's views on slavery and women can be set aside as products of his time."**
They cannot be airbrushed, and they should not be. Aristotle argues explicitly — in the *Politics* and by implication in the *Ethics* — for natural slavery and for women's subordination, and these are argued positions, not passing remarks. Scholars debate whether they are detachable from his ethics or whether they expose a flaw in his method of trusting reputable opinion: if his theory of human flourishing excludes whole classes of humans from full flourishing, either the theory or the exclusion must give. Read these passages critically, not apologetically, and notice the tension with his own claim that reason is the distinctively human capacity.

**6. "The *Ethics* is a self-help book."**
It is not. It is a work of philosophical theory about what the good life *is*, written in the compressed style of lecture material, not a program for personal improvement. Its advice is often indirect (the practical tips in II.9 are the exception, not the rule), and its central claims — about function, virtue, and contemplation — are meant to be argued with, not followed as instructions. Treating it as self-help also obscures its real difficulty: it asks you to reconsider what counts as a good life, not merely to live your current one more efficiently.

**7. "Book X shows Aristotle changed his mind and abandoned ethics for mysticism."**
This overstates the case, and scholars divide on it. Some read Book X as a genuine tension with the earlier books; others read contemplation as the crown of a life that still includes ethical virtue, friendship, and practical wisdom. What is not in doubt is that Aristotle does not retract the earlier books: the life of ethical virtue is still called happy in Book X, and the treatise ends by pointing forward to politics, not by dissolving into theology. Present the tension as a debate, not as a verdict.

**8. "Aristotle was simply Plato's pupil who systematised him."**
The *Ethics* is in constant, respectful, critical conversation with Plato — the critique of the Form of the Good in I.6 is the clearest example — but its method (starting from reputable opinions and observed human life rather than from transcendent Forms) and its conclusions (virtue as habit, not knowledge alone) are distinctively Aristotle's. The Socratic inheritance is real; so is the break.

## 5. Answers to checkpoint questions

1. The highest good must be final (sought for its own sake), self-sufficient (lacking nothing when possessed), and most complete. Wealth fails because it is useful — sought for the sake of what it buys — not final.
2. The human function is activity of the soul in accordance with reason; the good of a thing is performing its function well; so the human good is such activity in accordance with virtue over a life. The most attacked step is the move from parts having functions to the whole person having one function, and from function to good — see §2(a).
3. Eudaimonia is an achievement over a complete life, not a momentary state, so one good day (a swallow) does not make a flourishing life (a spring). Priam shows the converse limit: great misfortune late in life can mar flourishing however virtuous the person, so virtue may be necessary but not sufficient for eudaimonia.
4. Virtues of thought are taught (through instruction and time); virtues of character are acquired by habit (by repeatedly acting as the virtuous person acts, until the disposition is settled).
5. Example: courage is the mean between recklessness (excess of confidence, deficiency of fear) and cowardice (deficiency of confidence, excess of fear). It is not an arithmetic midpoint because the right amount depends on the danger, the agent's capacities, and the reason for acting — the same action can be brave for one person and reckless for another.
6. An action is involuntary when done under compulsion (the origin is external) or through ignorance of particular circumstances, and regretted afterwards. Ignorance of what one ought to know — of general moral truths, or of facts one is responsible for knowing (as with drunkenness) — does not excuse, because the ignorance itself is blameworthy.
7. Choice is deliberate desire for what is in our power, reached through deliberation about means. It differs from wish, which can concern impossibles and ends (wishing for health, or for immortality), and from opinion, which concerns what is true or false rather than what to do.
8. Magnanimity concerns claiming great honors one deserves, and magnificence concerns large-scale giving — both seem to require wealth and public standing. The problem: if some virtues require good fortune, then eudaimonia depends partly on luck, which sits uneasily with the idea that virtue is up to us.
9. Distributive justice allocates divisible goods (honor, wealth) in proportion to merit, using geometric proportion. Rectificatory justice corrects wrongs by removing gain and restoring loss, treating the parties as equals, using arithmetic proportion.
10. Practical wisdom is the virtue of deliberating well about what conduces to living well as a whole. The young cannot yet have it because it requires experience of particular situations over time — unlike mathematics, which can be grasped young.
11. Virtue without practical wisdom is mere natural disposition (a child can be naturally brave without knowing why or when bravery is called for); practical wisdom without virtue is mere cleverness, which can serve bad ends. Each needs the other to be virtue in the full sense.
12. Socrates claimed no one acts against their knowledge — apparent akrasia must be ignorance. Aristotle replies that the akratic person *has* the knowledge but fails to use it in the moment (knowing in the abstract vs. knowing actively), so he acts against a judgment he genuinely holds and regrets it afterwards.
13. Friendships of utility (based on mutual advantage), of pleasure (based on mutual enjoyment), and of the good (based on mutual recognition of character). Only the last is stable, because character is stable while utility and pleasure change; the first two dissolve when their basis ends.
14. Contemplation needs least from outside: it can be pursued alone, with fewer resources, and for longer stretches than ethical activity, which requires other people, external goods, and occasions for action. It is also loved purely for its own sake, whereas ethical actions, though choiceworthy in themselves, also serve further ends.
15. Tension: Books I–IX present flourishing as practically engaged and socially embedded, while Book X crowns a solitary, intellectual activity. Responses include: (a) contemplation is the dominant end crowning a life that still includes ethical virtue; (b) the tension reflects different strata of the treatise; (c) the two accounts describe two kinds of happiness, the divine and the human, both genuinely available. Scholars defend all three.
