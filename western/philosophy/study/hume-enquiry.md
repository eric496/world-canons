# Study Guide: David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748)

**Work:** *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* · **Author:** David Hume (1711–1776) · **First published:** 1748 · **What this guide covers:** all twelve sections of the *Enquiry*, with argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.

## About the work

Hume published this book in 1748 under the title *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding*. It was retitled *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* in the 1758 edition, and that is the title by which it is now known. It is a recasting of Book I of Hume's earlier *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), rewritten in a clearer, more essayistic style, with new material — most famously the essay on miracles (Section X) — added. It is not a mere abridgement: Hume drops some *Treatise* material (notably the long discussion of personal identity and the Appendix), sharpens several arguments, and presents his philosophy in a form he evidently preferred for general readers. The *Treatise* remains the fuller statement; the *Enquiry* is the better entry point, and it is the work the study plan assigns first.

The *Enquiry* is Hume's central statement of empiricism and mitigated skepticism. Its driving question is simple to state and hard to answer: what can the human understanding actually do, and on what authority does it do it? Hume's answer, developed section by section, is that reason handles relations of ideas (mathematics, logic) with certainty, but that everything we believe about matters of fact beyond present perception rests not on reason but on experience, custom, and feeling. The book's closing section draws the moral: philosophy should be modest, practical, and alert to the limits of reason — academic or mitigated skepticism rather than despair.

## Suggested edition

Hume wrote in English, and his original text is in the public domain, so any complete modern edition will serve and short quotations in this guide give section numbers so they can be located in any edition. For a first read, use either of the two editions named in the study plan:

- **Tom L. Beauchamp (ed.), *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1999)** — the standard scholarly edition, with a full critical apparatus and introduction.
- **Eric Steinberg (ed.), *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (Indianapolis: Hackett, 2nd ed. 1993)** — a reliable, affordable student edition.

Avoid relying on the older Selby-Bigge/Nidditch text as a first read; it has been superseded by Beauchamp's edition. Section numbers (§) are stable across editions, so all references in this guide use them.

## How to use this guide

Read each section of the *Enquiry* first, then read the corresponding entry below. The section entries summarize the argument, flag the key passages, and point out where Hume is doing something subtler than a first reading suggests. The argument reconstructions in §2 below (on induction, necessary connexion, and miracles) are the three arguments to master thoroughly; they are the ones most often examined and most often misunderstood. Work through the checkpoint questions at the end of each reading session, covering up the answers until you have attempted them.

Hume's prose is unusually clear for an eighteenth-century philosopher, but it rewards slow reading: his conclusions often look more radical — or less radical — than they are, and the difference usually turns on a single qualifying clause. Where scholars disagree about what Hume meant, this guide says so rather than settling the question for you.

## Reading plan

The *Enquiry* is short — roughly 130 pages in a modern edition — and can be read whole in a week at a comfortable pace. A suggested schedule:

| Session | Read | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sections I–III | The kinds of philosophy; impressions, ideas, and the copy principle; association |
| 2 | Sections IV–V | The problem of induction and the skeptical solution: custom |
| 3 | Sections VI–VII | Probability; necessary connexion |
| 4 | Sections VIII–IX | Liberty and necessity; the reason of animals |
| 5 | Sections X–XI | Miracles; providence and a future state |
| 6 | Section XII | Academic philosophy; mitigated skepticism |
| 7 | Re-read §§IV, VII, X | The three reconstructed arguments; checkpoint questions |

**Core sections:** II–VII and X–XI carry the philosophical weight of the book, and Sections IV, VII, and X are essential. If time is short, read Sections II, IV, V, VII, X, and XII in full, and skim III, VI, VIII, IX, and XI — but read the whole book if you can: Sections VIII and XI matter for understanding what Hume thinks his skepticism *permits* us to believe, not only what it forbids.

## 1. Section-by-section guide

### Section I: Of the different species of philosophy

**Summary.** Hume opens by distinguishing two kinds of philosophy. "Easy" philosophy — the moral essay, the character sketch, the literature of common life — pleases taste and reforms conduct, but lacks precision. "Abstract" or "abstruse" philosophy — metaphysics, logic, the science of human nature — aims at exactness but risks becoming obscure, unintelligible, and useless. Hume refuses to choose between them outright: he wants an accurate philosophy of human nature that is also accessible and useful, and he warns that much metaphysics is not merely difficult but empty — disputes conducted in words to which no clear idea is attached.

**Key passages.** §I.1–7 (the two species); §I.11–14 (the plea for a science of man as the foundation of the other sciences, and the warning against obscure metaphysics).

**Main argument.** The science of man is the only solid foundation for the other sciences, because every science bears some relation to human nature; but metaphysics that cannot be traced to experience deserves suspicion, not reverence. This sets up the method of the whole book: test an idea by asking what impression it copies.

### Section II: Of the origin of ideas

**Summary.** Here Hume lays the foundation of his empiricism. All perceptions of the mind divide into **impressions** — the lively perceptions of sensing, feeling, and emotion — and **ideas** — the fainter copies of impressions in thinking, remembering, and imagining. The **copy principle** states that every simple idea is copied from a corresponding simple impression. Complex ideas (a golden mountain, a virtuous horse) are built by compounding, transposing, augmenting, or diminishing materials supplied by impressions, but no new *simple* idea can be invented without an impression to copy.

**Key passages.** §II.3–5 (impressions versus ideas); §II.6–9 (the copy principle and its proof by the blind man who has no idea of color); §II.9 (the missing shade of blue).

**Main argument.** The copy principle is both a psychological claim and a critical instrument. Whenever a philosophical term is in dispute — substance, self, necessary connexion — Hume will ask: from what impression is this idea derived? If no impression can be produced, the idea is suspect. Note the exception Hume himself raises: a man who has seen every shade of blue except one could, Hume concedes, form the idea of the missing shade without a corresponding impression. He calls the case "so singular" that it scarcely overturns the principle — a candor worth remembering when the principle is later applied.

### Section III: Of the association of ideas

**Summary.** Ideas do not follow one another at random. Three principles associate them: **resemblance** (a picture leads us to think of the original), **contiguity** in time or place (mention of one room leads us to think of the next), and **cause and effect** (thinking of a wound leads us to think of the pain that followed it). Hume calls these "a kind of attraction" in the mental world, comparable — cautiously — to gravitation in the natural world.

**Key passages.** §III.1–3 (the three principles); §III.6–8 (association as the bond of the imagination and the explanation of why trains of thought differ between people).

**Main argument.** Association explains the order of thought without invoking a rational faculty that deliberately arranges ideas. It also prepares the ground for Sections IV–VII: cause and effect is not discovered by reason inspecting ideas, but is one of the associative principles by which the mind moves from one idea to another. The section is sometimes skimmed; it should not be, because Hume's account of causal inference in Section V is an application of it.

### Section IV: Skeptical doubts concerning the operations of the understanding

**Summary.** This is the heart of the book. Hume divides the objects of human reason into two kinds, later known as **Hume's fork**: **relations of ideas** (geometry, algebra, arithmetic, logic), which are intuitively or demonstratively certain and whose denial implies a contradiction; and **matters of fact**, whose contrary is always conceivable and which are known only through experience. All reasoning about matters of fact beyond present perception and memory rests on the relation of cause and effect. But what is the foundation of causal reasoning itself? Not reason: the inference that the future will resemble the past — that bread will nourish tomorrow because it nourished yesterday — cannot be justified by demonstration (a changed course of nature involves no contradiction) or by experience (appealing to past experience assumes the very uniformity in question). This is the **problem of induction**; a full reconstruction is given in §2(a) below.

**Key passages.** §IV.1–2 (the fork); §IV.4–9 (cause and effect as the basis of factual reasoning); §IV.10–16 (why the inference to the future is not founded on reason); §IV.19–21 (the fork applied to books: works containing neither abstract reasoning nor experimental reasoning "commit to the flames").

**Main argument.** Reason cannot justify its own most important everyday inference. The famous closing passage, recommending suspect books to the flames, is often quoted as a slogan; read in context, it is the conclusion of the fork, not a program for book-burning — and Hume himself immediately qualifies it in the next section.

### Section V: Skeptical solution of these doubts

**Summary.** If reason does not carry us from past to future, what does? Hume's answer: **custom or habit**. After experiencing constant conjunction — A followed by B, repeatedly — the mind is determined by custom to expect B on seeing A, without any reasoning process. This is the "sceptical solution": skeptical, because it concedes that the inference has no rational foundation; a solution, because it explains why we nevertheless make the inference, and why we are right to go on making it in practice. Nature, not argument, keeps skepticism from paralyzing us.

**Key passages.** §V.2–5 (custom as the guide of life); §V.8–10 (the child, the peasant, and the philosopher all reason the same way here); §V.21–22 (the conclusion: philosophy should be modest, and "be sceptical" in its pretensions rather than in its practice).

**Main argument.** The solution is naturalistic, not justificatory. Hume is not saying induction is unreliable or that we should stop using it; he is saying that its authority is not rational demonstration but a principle of human nature as universal as the senses. A burned child fears fire without any argument — and so, in the end, do we all. This distinction between *justification* and *practice* is the key to Hume's mitigated skepticism, developed in Section XII.

### Section VI: Of probability

**Summary.** Most of life is not a matter of certainty but of probability, where experience has been mixed: some events of a kind were followed by one outcome, some by another. Hume explains probability psychologically: when we have seen A followed by B in, say, three cases out of four, the mind is carried toward B with a force proportioned to the number of past instances, but the contrary instances check and weaken that movement. Probability is therefore a felt balance of associative force, not a calculation — though Hume's picture anticipates, in rough form, later ideas about weighing evidence.

**Key passages.** §VI.1–4 (probability as arising from contrariety of experience); §VI.5 (the mind's "survey" of past instances).

**Main argument.** Probability belongs on the same footing as certainty in Hume's system: both are products of custom operating on experience, differing only in the uniformity of the experience. The section matters chiefly because Section X's argument about miracles turns on exactly this weighing of contrary evidence.

### Section VII: Of the idea of necessary connexion

**Summary.** We say A *causes* B, and mean more than that B followed A: we mean B *must* follow, that there is a necessary tie between them. But where does the idea of necessity come from? Hume runs his Section II test. It is not copied from any impression of a single instance: looking at one billiard ball striking another, we see contiguity and succession, but no power, force, or tie. It is not copied from reflection on the mind's own operations, as Locke had suggested — the will's command over the body is itself mysterious and experienced only through repeated conjunction. The source, Hume argues, is internal: after repeated conjunctions, custom determines the mind to pass from the impression of A to the lively idea of B, and *this felt determination of the mind* is the impression from which the idea of necessity is copied. Necessity is therefore in the mind's transition, not observed in the objects.

**Key passages.** §VII.4–8 (the search for the impression, in single instances and in the will); §VII.11–16 (the mind's determination after repetition); §VII.25–30 (the two definitions of cause).

**Main argument.** Hume then gives two definitions of cause: (1) "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second" (regularity); and (2) "an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" (the mind's customary transition). Whether these two definitions are meant to be equivalent, complementary, or in tension — and whether Hume is denying objective necessities or merely saying we can form no idea of them beyond regularity — is the subject of the "New Hume" debate, discussed in §2(b) and §4.7 below. The secure point is Hume's negative one: our evidence never goes beyond constant conjunction plus the mind's habitual transition.

### Section VIII: Of liberty and necessity

**Summary.** The dispute about free will, Hume argues, has been largely verbal, because the disputants have not agreed on what "liberty" and "necessity" mean. Necessity, as established in Section VII, means constant conjunction and the mind's customary inference — and human actions display exactly that regularity: motives, character, and circumstances are followed by actions as uniformly as physical causes are followed by effects. Liberty, as actually understood, means "a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" — the absence of external constraint, not the absence of causation. The two are compatible, and necessity is even required for morality: an action unconnected with character could not be praised or blamed, since it would reveal nothing about the agent.

**Key passages.** §VIII.4–7 (necessity in human action); §VIII.21–23 (liberty defined; the "hypothetical" sense); §VIII.27–30 (why necessity is essential to moral responsibility).

**Main argument.** This is the founding text of modern **compatibilism**. Note the move Hume is making: he is not redefining the words arbitrarily, but appealing to what people actually mean and presuppose in everyday moral judgment — that actions show character — and showing that the metaphysical dispute dissolves once those meanings are fixed.

### Section IX: Of the reason of animals

**Summary.** A short section with a sharp point. Animals, Hume argues, reason in the same way humans do in matters of fact: not by argument, but by custom. A dog learns to avoid fire and to expect food at a familiar sound through repeated experience, exactly as a child does. If human causal inference were the work of a special rational faculty, animals — which plainly lack it — could not learn from experience as they do. The analogy confirms the Section V account: reasoning about matters of fact is a kind of instinct, shared across species.

**Key passages.** §IX.1–5 (the whole section is brief; the dog and horse examples carry the argument).

**Main argument.** The section functions as a touchstone for the book's naturalism. It also quietly lowers the barrier between human and animal minds, a move with implications Hume does not develop here but which later readers have pressed.

### Section X: Of miracles

**Summary.** A miracle, Hume defines, is a violation of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent. A law of nature is established by firm and unalterable experience — the strongest possible evidence for a matter of fact. Testimony is itself a form of experience, but its force depends on the usual conjunction between testimony and truth, and testimony is notoriously weakened by credulity, love of the marvellous, religious enthusiasm, and the tendency of miracle reports to cluster among "ignorant and barbarous nations" and to cancel each other across rival religions. A wise man therefore proportions his belief to the evidence, and when testimony for a miracle collides with uniform experience against it, the testimony cannot prevail: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish."

**Key passages.** §X.4–5 (the definition of a miracle and of proof); §X.12–13 (the maxim); §X.16–24 (the four considerations weakening miracle testimony); §X.35–36 (the closing reflection on faith and reason).

**Main argument.** The argument concerns **evidence**, not metaphysical possibility — Hume never claims miracles cannot occur. A full reconstruction, with objection and reply, is given in §2(c) below. Whether the argument is an a priori barrier or a strong evidential presumption, and how it fares when recast in modern probabilistic terms, is actively debated; the guide presents the argument as Hume states it and notes the debate rather than settling it.

### Section XI: Of a particular providence and of a future state

**Summary.** In the form of a dialogue between a skeptic and a friend of Epicurus, Hume argues that the moral attributes of God and a future state of rewards and punishments cannot be established by reason from the observed course of nature. We may infer a cause proportioned to its observed effects, but the world as we find it — mixed, imperfect, full of suffering — does not warrant inferring a perfectly just and benevolent governor, nor a life to come designed to redress earthly injustice. To suppose otherwise is to go beyond the evidence, adding attributes to the cause that the effect does not show.

**Key passages.** §XI.1–10 (the dialogue form and the rule that the cause must be proportioned to the effect); §XI.18–25 (the imperfections of the world as evidence against a moral providence); §XI.27–30 (the conclusion: reason alone cannot establish a future state).

**Main argument.** Section XI is the *Enquiry*'s quiet companion to the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*: it applies the same evidential modesty to theology that Sections IV and X applied to induction and miracles. Note what Hume does *not* do: he does not deny providence or a future state, but argues that they are not conclusions reason can draw from experience — a distinction his religious critics often collapsed.

### Section XII: Of the academical or skeptical philosophy

**Summary.** Hume closes by distinguishing two kinds of skepticism. **Pyrrhonism** (extreme, total doubt) is unliveable: nature forces us to believe, reason, and act, and no one can sustain universal doubt outside the study. **Academic philosophy** (mitigated skepticism), named for the Academy of Plato's later followers, accepts the force of skeptical arguments but confines them to their proper sphere: it doubts abstruse metaphysical speculation, insists that enquiries be limited to subjects within the capacity of human understanding, and recommends modesty about conclusions — especially in theology and metaphysics — while leaving common life untouched. The book ends with the fork applied once more: enquiries containing neither abstract reasoning concerning quantity and number nor experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence should be committed to the flames, "for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion."

**Key passages.** §XII.3–5 (the critique of popular and philosophical skepticism); §XII.15–24 (mitigated skepticism and its advantages: modesty, limitation of enquiry, avoidance of dogmatism); §XII.33–34 (the closing passage).

**Main argument.** Section XII is Hume's answer to the charge that his skepticism destroys knowledge and morality. It does not: it destroys only dogmatic pretension. The skeptic still dines, plays backgammon, and believes that fire burns — but does so knowing that custom, not demonstration, is the ground of the belief. That combination — doubt about foundations, confidence in practice — is the stance the whole book has been building toward.

## 2. Argument reconstructions

### (a) The problem of induction (Section IV)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. All reasoning about matters of fact beyond present perception and memory is founded on the relation of cause and effect.
2. Causal reasoning assumes that the future will resemble the past — that instances of which we have had no experience will resemble instances of which we have had experience (the uniformity principle).
3. The uniformity principle cannot be established by demonstrative reasoning, because its contrary — a changed course of nature — is conceivable without contradiction, and whatever is conceivable without contradiction is possible.
4. The uniformity principle cannot be established by probable (experiential) reasoning, because all such reasoning itself presupposes the uniformity principle; arguing that experience has been uniform in the past, and therefore will be uniform in the future, assumes the very point at issue.
5. Therefore, the uniformity principle — and with it all causal reasoning about the unobserved — has no foundation in reason.

**Conclusion.** Reason cannot justify induction. (Hume's further claim, in Section V, is that custom, not reason, is what actually carries us from past to future — a naturalistic explanation, not a rational justification.)

**One standard objection.** The argument sets an unfair standard: it demands deductive certainty for an inference that never claimed it. Ordinary inductive reasoning may be justified in a weaker, non-demonstrative sense — for example, by its track record, or by pragmatic vindication (we cannot do better, and it works). On this view, Hume shows only that induction is not deduction, which no one denied.

**One reply.** Hume's defender can concede that induction is not deduction — that is precisely Hume's point — but insist the objection misses the dialectic. The question Hume poses is not "is induction deductively valid?" but "what justifies the move from observed to unobserved at all?" A track-record argument still reasons from past success to future success, which is itself an inductive move, and so presupposes what it is meant to establish. Pragmatic vindication changes the subject from justification to usefulness: it tells us why we *use* induction, not why induction is *rationally warranted*. Whether that is enough is exactly what remains debated — and the debate is the problem of induction's afterlife, not its refutation.

### (b) The analysis of necessary connexion (Section VII)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. By the copy principle (Section II), every legitimate idea must be copied from a corresponding impression; an idea with no impression-source is empty.
2. We have an idea of necessary connexion — we mean more by "A causes B" than "B followed A."
3. No impression of necessity is found in any single instance of causation: observing one billiard ball strike another, we perceive contiguity and succession, but no power, force, or tie.
4. No impression of necessity is found in reflection on the mind's own operations: the will's apparent power over the body is known only through repeated experience, and its mechanism is unknown to us.
5. After repeated instances of constant conjunction, however, custom determines the mind to pass from the impression of A to a lively idea of B; this felt determination of the mind *is* an impression — an impression of reflection.
6. Therefore, the idea of necessary connexion is copied from this internal impression: necessity is the mind's felt determination, not a quality observed in objects.

**Conclusion.** Our idea of necessity originates in the mind's customary transition, and causal judgments rest on constant conjunction plus that transition — not on any perceived power in objects.

**One standard objection.** The argument proves too little or too much. Too little: even if we never *perceive* a power, powers may still exist in objects; Hume's conclusion about the origin of our *idea* does not settle whether objects really are necessarily connected. Too much: if the felt determination is itself a genuine impression, then we do after all have an impression of necessity — so why should the resulting idea not represent something real?

**One reply.** Hume's defender can distinguish the two complaints. Against the first: Hume's claim is deliberately about what we can *know and mean*, not a metaphysical denial — on the traditional reading, he shows that necessity as we conceive it has no basis in experience of objects, and therefore that causal claims exceeding regularity are unwarranted, whatever may exist beyond our ideas. Against the second: the impression of determination is an impression of the mind's own activity, not of a tie between objects; copying it gives us an idea of a feeling, and projecting that feeling onto objects is exactly the mistake Hume diagnoses. Whether this reply fully succeeds is the heart of the "New Hume" debate (see §4.7).

### (c) The argument against belief in miracles on testimony (Section X)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. A wise man proportions his belief to the evidence.
2. A miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is established by firm and unalterable experience — the strongest possible evidence for a matter of fact, amounting to proof.
3. The evidence for a reported miracle is testimony, whose force rests on experience of the usual conjunction between testimony and truth.
4. Testimony is an inherently weaker and more variable form of evidence than uniform experience: it is weakened by human credulity, love of the marvellous, religious enthusiasm, and the tendency of miracle reports to arise among the uneducated and to contradict one another across rival religions.
5. When testimony for a miracle is weighed against uniform experience against it, the evidence for the law is always greater; belief should follow the stronger evidence.
6. Therefore, no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it endeavors to establish — a condition that, in practice, is never met.

**Conclusion.** Belief in a miracle on the basis of testimony is never rationally warranted. (Note the scope: the argument targets *belief on testimony*, not the possibility of miracles themselves.)

**One standard objection.** Hume's weighing is rigged: he treats the evidence for the law of nature as maximal and the evidence from testimony as necessarily weak, but testimony can in principle be extremely strong — many independent, disinterested, well-placed witnesses — and a single well-attested exception does not so much "violate" a law as reveal that the law was incompletely stated. Modern probabilistic (Bayesian) reconstructions argue that the posterior probability of a miracle given testimony depends on the prior probability of the miracle and the reliability of the witnesses, and need not always come out against the miracle.

**One reply.** Hume's defender can accept the Bayesian framing and still hold the argument's force: the prior probability of a violation of a law confirmed by uniform experience is extremely low, so the testimony would have to be extraordinarily reliable to overcome it — and actual miracle testimony, Hume argues, is never of that quality, for the empirical reasons given in premise 4. The Bayesian objection shows that Hume's conclusion is not a logical impossibility but a very strong evidential presumption; Hume's own maxim is already stated conditionally ("unless the testimony be of such a kind..."), so the objection refines rather than refutes the argument — though whether Hume himself intended a mere presumption or something stronger remains debated.

## 3. Checkpoint questions

Attempt these before reading the answers in §5.

1. What is the difference between "easy" and "abstruse" philosophy in Section I, and which does Hume favor?
2. State the copy principle, and explain why Hume raises the case of the missing shade of blue.
3. What are the three principles of association of ideas, and which one matters most for the rest of the book?
4. What is Hume's fork, and why does the denial of a relation of ideas imply a contradiction while the denial of a matter of fact does not?
5. In Section IV, why can the uniformity principle ("the future will resemble the past") not be proved by demonstration?
6. Why can it not be proved by experience either? What is the circularity Hume alleges?
7. What does Hume mean by the "sceptical solution" in Section V, and how does custom solve — and fail to solve — the problem of induction?
8. In Section VII, why does Hume reject the suggestion that the idea of necessity comes from reflection on the will's power over the body?
9. Give Hume's two definitions of cause. Why might they seem to be in tension?
10. What does Hume mean by "liberty" in Section VIII, and why does he think necessity is required for moral responsibility?
11. Why does Hume discuss animals in Section IX? What work is the section doing in the book's overall argument?
12. State Hume's maxim about testimony and miracles. Does his argument show that miracles cannot occur? Why or why not?
13. In Section XI, why does Hume argue that reason cannot establish a future state of rewards and punishments from the observed world?
14. What is the difference between Pyrrhonian and Academic (mitigated) skepticism in Section XII, and why does Hume say the former is unliveable?

## 4. Common misreadings

1. **"Hume says induction is irrational, so we should stop using it."** Hume says induction has no *rational foundation* — it cannot be demonstrated or non-circularly justified. He does not say it is unreliable, useless, or to be abandoned; custom compels us to use it, and Section V presents that as the solution, not a counsel of despair. The target is justification, not practice.
2. **"The copy principle admits no exceptions."** Hume himself notes the missing shade of blue: a person who has seen every shade of blue except one could form the idea of the missing shade without a corresponding impression. Hume concedes the case but judges it too singular to overturn the principle. A reading that ignores the exception misrepresents both the principle and Hume's candor about it.
3. **"Hume's fork is Hume's own term."** "Hume's fork" is a later label for the distinction in Section IV between relations of ideas and matters of fact. Hume himself does not use the phrase, and the label can mislead if it suggests a formal doctrine rather than a distinction Hume draws to frame the problem of induction. Relatedly, the fork's self-application — which prong does the fork itself fall under? — is a genuine difficulty raised by critics, not a settled refutation.
4. **"Hume proved that miracles cannot occur."** Section X argues about *evidence and testimony*, not metaphysical possibility. Hume's claim is that testimony can never rationally warrant belief in a miracle, given the weight of uniform experience against it. Whether the argument amounts to an a priori barrier or a strong evidential presumption is debated, but on no reading is it a proof that miracles are impossible.
5. **"Hume's scepticism is Pyrrhonian paralysis."** Section XII explicitly rejects total, Pyrrhonian doubt as unliveable and psychologically impossible. Hume's skepticism is Academic or mitigated: it doubts dogmatic metaphysics and insists on modesty, while leaving common life — dining, playing backgammon, trusting fire to burn — entirely intact.
6. **"The *Enquiry* is simply a summary of the *Treatise*."** It is a recasting, not a summary. Hume drops *Treatise* material (personal identity, the Appendix), adds new material (miracles), and restates arguments in a different form and sometimes with different emphasis. Where the two works differ, scholars debate which represents Hume's considered view; neither should simply be read through the other.
7. **"Hume denied causation, or denied that we know anything about causes."** Hume denied that we *perceive* a necessary tie in objects, and argued that causal inference rests on constant conjunction and custom. He did not deny that we can know regularities, make causal judgments, or rely on them — his science of human nature depends on doing exactly that. The "New Hume" debate — whether Hume believed in objective necessities we cannot conceive, or reduced causation to regularity and projection — remains open, and both sides can cite passages in their favor.
8. **"Reason being the slave of the passions means Hume recommends irrationality."** The slogan (from the *Treatise*, not the *Enquiry*) concerns motivation: reason alone cannot move us to act; only a passion can supply an end. Reason retains full authority over truth and over correcting false beliefs. Hume's point is about what moves us, not a licence to ignore evidence.

## 5. Answers to checkpoint questions

1. "Easy" philosophy treats of common life and manners, pleases taste, and reforms conduct; "abstruse" philosophy aims at precise, systematic knowledge of human nature but risks obscurity. Hume wants an accurate philosophy that is also accessible — he criticizes abstruse metaphysics when it becomes unintelligible, not precision itself.
2. Every simple idea is copied from a corresponding simple impression. The missing shade of blue is Hume's own counterexample: someone who has seen all other shades could form the idea of the missing one without an impression. Hume concedes it but calls it too singular to overturn the principle.
3. Resemblance, contiguity in time or place, and cause and effect. Cause and effect matters most, because Sections IV–VII analyze causal inference, which operates through association and custom.
4. Relations of ideas (mathematics, logic) are demonstratively certain; denying one implies a contradiction. Matters of fact are known only by experience, and their contrary is always conceivable without contradiction — a world where the sun does not rise tomorrow is thinkable.
5. Demonstration establishes what cannot be otherwise without contradiction. A changed course of nature involves no contradiction, so the uniformity principle cannot be demonstrated.
6. Experiential reasoning itself presupposes uniformity: to argue from past experience to future uniformity is to assume that the past is a guide to the future, which is the very principle in question. The argument is circular.
7. The skeptical solution concedes that induction has no rational foundation, but explains that custom or habit — the mind's determination after repeated conjunction — is what actually carries us from past to future. It solves the psychological question (why do we infer?) but not the justificatory one (what warrants the inference?).
8. The will's power over the body is itself known only through experience of constant conjunction, and its mechanism is unknown to us; it therefore cannot supply an original impression of power or necessity. Appealing to it would presuppose what is to be explained.
9. (1) An object followed by another, where all similar objects are followed by similar objects (regularity). (2) An object whose appearance conveys the mind, by custom, to the idea of the other (the mind's transition). They may seem in tension because the first locates causation in objects and their regularities, the second in the mind's habitual movement — whether Hume intended them as equivalent or complementary is debated.
10. Liberty is "a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" — freedom from external constraint, not from causation. Necessity is required for moral responsibility because an action unconnected with character and motives would reveal nothing about the agent and could not be praised or blamed.
11. Animals learn from experience — a dog avoids fire, expects food at a familiar sound — without any rational faculty. This confirms that human causal inference is likewise the work of custom and instinct, not of reason, and supports the naturalism of Section V.
12. "No testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish." No: the argument concerns whether testimony can rationally warrant belief in a miracle, not whether miracles are possible.
13. Reason can infer a cause only in proportion to its observed effects. The world as observed is mixed and imperfect, so it does not warrant inferring a perfectly just and benevolent governor or a future state designed to redress earthly injustice; those attributes go beyond what the effect shows.
14. Pyrrhonian skepticism is total doubt, which Hume says nature makes unliveable — we cannot sustain it outside the study. Academic or mitigated skepticism accepts skeptical arguments but confines them: it doubts abstruse speculation, limits enquiry to what human understanding can handle, and recommends modesty, while leaving common life untouched.
