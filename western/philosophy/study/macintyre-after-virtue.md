# Study Guide: MacIntyre's *After Virtue*

**Work:** *After Virtue: A Study in Moral Theory*, 1981; 3rd edition, University of Notre Dame Press, 2007 · **Author:** Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025) · **Covers:** the whole book — Prologue, chapters 1–18, and the Postscript to the second edition (chapter 19 in the third edition) — with a reading plan, section-by-section guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Alasdair MacIntyre](../eras/05-20th-century/alasdair-macintyre.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Read the third edition of 2007, not the 1981 original or 1984 second edition. Its new prologue, "After Virtue after a Quarter of a Century," is part of the assignment, not an optional extra: MacIntyre answers a quarter-century of critics, corrects his own errors — notably conceding his Aristotle needed Aquinas — and restates the thesis. Read it first, and again after chapter 18.

Chapter numbers are stable across editions; material was added around the argument — the second edition's Postscript and the third edition's Prologue — rather than inserted into it. Navigate by chapter.

A note on quotations: short quoted phrases follow MacIntyre's own wording; the guide otherwise paraphrases, so your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

On dates: *After Virtue* first appeared in 1981; its sequels are discussed under Next steps and misreading 6.

## How to use this guide

MacIntyre's prose is lucid and historical — the trap: it reads like a story, so you can finish a chapter with an impression and no statable argument. But the parts interlock: the catastrophe hypothesis of chapter 1 is tested against chapter 2's debates, explained by chapters 4–5's history, and answered by chapters 14–15.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before a chapter, read its part of this guide: know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each chapter, write its main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read each reconstruction's objection and reply, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second.

## Reading plan

Unlike most guides in this library, this one covers a book meant to be read whole: the diagnosis of the first half earns the constructive chapters, which are unintelligible without it. The whole book, with Prologue and Postscript, runs to roughly 300 pages. At about three hours a week, the program takes nine weeks.

## Section-by-section guide

### Prologue and chapter 1 — A disquieting suggestion: the catastrophe hypothesis

MacIntyre opens with an imagined catastrophe in the natural sciences: laboratories burned, books destroyed, then a revival reassembling the fragments without the whole that gave them sense — experiments without knowledge of what they test, theorems without proofs, rival schools arguing past one another in a language that still *sounds* like science. His disquieting suggestion: this is not a fantasy about science. It has already happened — to morality.

On the hypothesis, moral language now consists of fragments of a once-coherent scheme: *virtue*, *duty*, *rights*, and *utility* survive, but the contexts that made them intelligible together — a shared account of human purpose — have been lost, and largely lost unnoticed. We go on using the words seriously, which is what makes the disorder hard to see.

His supporting analogy is *taboo*. When Kamehameha II abolished the taboos in early nineteenth-century Hawaii, later Polynesians could still use the word but could no longer say what a taboo was or why anything had been one: the practice giving the concept its point had gone. Key moral terms, MacIntyre claims, stand to their lost schemes as "taboo" stood to a vanished order — except that no single abolition can be dated, and we flatter ourselves our confusion is sophistication.

Note its status: chapter 1 offers the hypothesis as a suggestion to be tested by the history that follows, not as a conclusion. Hold him to that standard — the objection to reconstruction (a) turns on whether the test can ever come out against him.

**Main argument:** Modern moral language is best explained as the fragments of older, coherent schemes surviving without the contexts that made them intelligible — which is why our moral arguments use serious words to no settling effect.

### Chapters 2–3 — Moral disagreement today and the claims of emotivism

Chapter 2 begins with three specimen debates — over a just war, over abortion, and over public provision of schooling and medical care — and identifies three characteristics of contemporary moral argument. First, **interminability**: the arguments do not conclude; no rational way of securing agreement seems to exist. Second, the rival arguments are **conceptually incommensurable**: each may be logically valid, but their premises invoke rival concepts — rights against utility, justice against innocence — with no shared standard for weighing them, so each is asserted against the other. Third, paradoxically, the debaters **purport to appeal to impersonal rational criteria**: nobody says "this is merely my preference," while the premises derive, historically, from incompatible sources — fragments of different schemes, carried into the debate unexamined.

**Emotivism** claims to explain this: the doctrine that all moral judgments are nothing but expressions of preference, attitude, or feeling, neither true nor false and incapable of rational adjudication. As sociology about *our* culture, MacIntyre argues, it is substantially true: it describes how moral utterances are *used* among us — to express attitude and manipulate others', the distinction between manipulative and non-manipulative relations effectively obliterated. But as a theory of the *meaning* of moral sentences, emotivism is false: expressing a feeling is a function of how a sentence is *used* on an occasion, not of what it *means* — the same sentence can be uttered angrily, ironically, or in a seminar — and emotivism cannot distinguish reporting one's approval from passing judgment. Emotivism is thus the philosophy a fragmented culture produces about itself, mistaking a truth about its own use of moral language for a timeless theory of meaning.

**Main argument:** Contemporary moral debate is interminable because its premises are incommensurable fragments, yet it masquerades as impersonal rational argument — and emotivism is true as a description of that culture's use of moral language, false as a theory of moral meaning.

### Chapter 3 (continued) — The characters: the Manager, the Therapist, and the Aesthete

Chapter 3 shows emotivism socially embodied through MacIntyre's notion of a **character**: a social role in which a culture's norms achieve recognisable embodiment — a fusion of social type and moral philosophy, whose occupant *is*, for that culture, a moral exemplar legitimating a whole mode of life.

Emotivist culture has three central characters. **The Manager** embodies bureaucratic authority: his claim to power rests on alleged expertise in matching means to ends — manipulating people and resources toward given goals whose value he does not evaluate. That expertise, chapters 7–8 argue, is fictitious in its strong form: social science supplies no law-like generalizations reliable prediction would require. **The Therapist** plays the parallel role in private life: he too treats ends as given, converting moral evaluation into technique — adjusting the individual to function, without asking whether the functioning is worth having. Neither engages in moral argument; both replace it with expert adjustment. **The Aesthete** — of Henry James's and Edith Wharton's world — is the consumer counterpart: life as an arena of aesthetic experience, others as means to interesting sensations, commitment itself a failure of taste. In public, manipulation by expertise; in private, the self absorbed in experience — judgment dissolved in both.

**Main argument:** A culture's characters show what it actually honors — and emotivism's characters, the Manager, the Therapist, and the Aesthete, all treat other persons as means to given ends, replacing moral argument with technique or taste.

### Chapters 4–5 — The predecessor culture and the Enlightenment project that had to fail

Chapters 4–5 supply the history the hypothesis requires. The scheme whose fragments we inherit was **teleological and threefold**: *man-as-he-happens-to-be* (human nature untutored), *man-as-he-could-be-if-he-realized-his-essential-nature* (human nature realizing its *telos*), and the precepts bridging them. Within that scheme moral claims could be factual: whether an action was right depended on what it contributed to a recognisable human end. The scheme survived into theistic form — Aristotle completed by divine law in Aquinas — and the early modern period kept that morality's *content* while discarding its teleology.

The **Enlightenment project of justifying morality** was the attempt, from roughly the seventeenth to the nineteenth century, to found that content on human nature as it is, without the *telos*. It **had to fail**: precepts designed to correct untutored human nature cannot be derived from a description of that same nature. Each attempt exhibits the failure in a different key. **Hume, Diderot, and Adam Smith** appealed to the passions and sympathy: but passions conflict, and selecting which shall rule requires a criterion the passions cannot supply — Hume's choices quietly import his social order's standards. **Kant** appealed to pure practical reason and universalizability: but the test is too permissive — suitably formulated immoral maxims pass it — and too empty to generate Kant's content. **Kierkegaard**, inheriting Kant's failure, relocated the foundation in **radical choice**: the ethical is what an individual chooses, criterionlessly, to make it — but a choice made for no reason can confer no authority. The utilitarian sequel, from Bentham to **Mill** and Sidgwick (chapter 6), fares no better: "the greatest happiness" names no single measurable quantity; pleasures differ in kind and the calculus never acquired a unit.

**Main argument:** The Enlightenment inherited moral precepts designed for a teleological scheme, discarded the teleology, and therefore could not re-found them on passion (Hume), reason (Kant), choice (Kierkegaard), or utility (Mill) — the project's failure was necessary, not accidental.

### Chapters 6–8 — Consequences: rights, protest, and the fiction of expertise

Chapter 6 traces moral life after the failure. **Rights** become trump cards appealed to precisely where justification has run out: MacIntyre's notorious verdict is that belief in natural rights, in the sense his targets require, is on a par with belief in fictions — no self-evident entitlements are discoverable outside a teleological, social context, though rights *within* practices and traditions are another matter. And **protest** becomes a characteristic stance: shrill assertion against assertion, indignation itself treated as an argument — chapter 2's incommensurability in public.

Chapters 7–8 turn the same lens on the social sciences. Against prising "facts" cleanly apart from values, MacIntyre contends central social concepts are internally evaluative, and the fact/value dichotomy is itself a fragment. Chapter 8 argues social-scientific generalizations systematically lack predictive power: radical innovation is unpredictable in principle; agents' knowledge of predictions alters outcomes; genuine contingency intervenes. If so, the Manager's claim to law-like knowledge is misconceived — theater legitimating power.

**Main argument:** A post-Enlightenment culture gets characteristic fictions — rights asserted as trumps, protest as argument, and managerial expertise claiming a predictive science that chapter 8 argues cannot exist.

### Chapters 9–13 — Nietzsche or Aristotle, and the historical recovery

Chapter 9 poses the book's famous fork. If the Enlightenment project failed, two honest responses remain. **Nietzsche** drew the radical conclusion: the whole vocabulary of morality is a mask for the will to power, and the honest person will abandon moral talk and create values in sovereign self-assertion. **Aristotle** represents the road not taken: morality intelligible within a teleological account of human life, the virtues being the qualities that realize our end. MacIntyre's claim: Nietzsche's critique succeeds *against Enlightenment morality* — the fragments — but has no force against the Aristotelian tradition in its strong form; and Nietzsche's alternative, the solitary will inventing values from nothing, is a fantasy, since the would-be superman's concepts are inherited and his solitude parasitic on the social world he disowns.

Chapters 10–13 trace the virtues through successive social orders. In **heroic societies** (chapter 10), known through epic, courage and fidelity are the virtues of a world where identity is one's deeds and place in household and story. At **Athens** (chapter 11), rival accounts — Sophists, Plato, the tragedians — contest the virtues between the city's standpoint and the good as such. **Aristotle's account** (chapter 12) gives the tradition its classic form: the human *telos*, virtues acquired by habituation, practical wisdom (*phronesis*), friendship as a central good. The **medieval transformation** (chapter 13) is no mere preservation: Aquinas synthesises Aristotle with Augustine and biblical law, relocates the *telos* beyond this life, and supplies the metaphysics of human nature Aristotle's biology gestured at. The tour is argumentative, not antiquarian — a preview of the concept of tradition chapter 15 will define.

**Main argument:** Nietzsche's unmasking defeats only the Enlightenment's fragments, not the Aristotelian alternative — and the history of heroic, Athenian, Aristotelian, and medieval accounts shows that alternative as a living, self-revising tradition rather than a museum piece.

### Chapter 14 — Practices and internal goods

Chapter 14 gives the book's most influential constructive concept. A **practice** is a coherent, complex, socially established cooperative activity through which goods internal to it are realized, in trying to achieve the standards of excellence partly definitive of it — with the result that human powers, and conceptions of the ends involved, are systematically extended. A practice is *socially established* and *cooperative*: you cannot invent one alone. It has *standards of excellence* the beginner must accept as authoritative over her own preferences — your judgment of your performance may be wrong by standards you do not yet command. And it *develops*: great practitioners extend what it can be.

The key distinction is between **goods internal** and **goods external** to a practice. MacIntyre's example is teaching a child chess: you may bribe the child with sweets — sweets, like money, prestige, and power, are external goods, achievable in many ways, and objects of competition where one person's gain is another's loss. But what the child who comes to love chess finds in it — the analytical skill, strategic imagination, and intensity *this* game makes possible — are internal goods: achievable only by playing chess (or something relevantly like it), fully recognisable only by participants, and enriching the whole community of players when achieved. His other example is portrait painting: what a Rembrandt achieves can be judged only from within the practice's history; from outside, only fame and price are visible.

**Institutions** — chess clubs, universities, hospitals, firms — sustain practices, and are necessary yet dangerous: organized around external goods (money, status, survival), they threaten to corrupt the practice they house. The **virtues** enter with MacIntyre's first definition: a virtue is an acquired human quality whose exercise enables us to achieve goods internal to practices, and whose lack prevents us achieving them. **Justice** (giving each their due within the practice, regardless of liking), **courage** (risking harm for the practice's goods), and **honesty** or truthfulness (refusing to fake achievement) are indispensable precisely because without them practices collapse into vehicles for external goods. The definition is deliberately incomplete: chapters 15–16 must add the whole life and the *telos*.

**Main argument:** Practices define goods achievable only from inside, judged by standards the novice must submit to — and justice, courage, and honesty are the qualities without which practices corrupt into mere machinery for money, prestige, and power.

### Chapter 15 — The narrative unity of a human life

The practice account alone leaves the virtues fragmented across a life: chess player, parent, citizen, worker — nothing yet says what makes these one life with one good. Chapter 15's answer is **narrative**. Actions are intelligible only within stories: to identify what someone is doing — gardening, signalling, plotting — you must place the episode in a narrative history, hers and her community's. Each of us lives an enacted narrative, author and actor both, constrained by inherited stories and others' parts in our own.

The unity of a human life is thus the unity of a **narrative quest**: not any journey, but a search for a good itself partly understood only through the searching — the quest educates the quester about the goal. Two features follow. **Intelligibility**: my actions are intelligible to others only through the story in which they make sense. **Accountability**: I am answerable to others entitled to ask for that story — the subject of a history that is mine, unable to step into a purely episodic self. The modern tendency to segment a life into compartments — work self, family self, leisure self, each with its own norms — is, on this account, not liberation but loss of the unity that makes virtues, dispositions across a whole life, possible.

**Main argument:** A single human life is intelligible only as a narrative quest — actions make sense within stories, we are accountable as the subjects of those stories, and only a life with that unity can be the bearer of the virtues.

### Chapters 15–17 — Traditions, and justice as a virtue

Chapter 15 scales narrative up to enquiry itself. A **tradition** is a historically extended, socially embodied argument — in part about the very goods constituting it. Living traditions argue with themselves, revise their standards, and sometimes enter **epistemological crisis**, when their own methods can no longer resolve their own problems. A tradition vindicates itself rationally when it can resolve its own crisis and explain, in terms the rival must recognize, why the rival could not resolve its own comparable problems — Aquinas's synthesis is the standing example. This outlines what *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* later develops as **tradition-constituted rationality**: rationality is not a neutral, tradition-free tribunal — the Enlightenment's fiction — but neither is it sealed inside incommensurable worlds, since traditions can genuinely defeat one another. Critics judge this the least finished part of the system; treat it as a program sketched, not a doctrine completed.

Chapter 16 guards against a relativising reading: differing local lists of virtues do not reduce virtue to whatever a society prizes, because the three-stage account — practices, narrative unity, tradition — disciplines any local list by the goods of practices, the good of a whole life, and a tradition's argument about the human good. Chapter 17 applies this to **justice**: rival conceptions of desert, need, and entitlement belong to rival traditions — Aristotle's, Aquinas's, Hume's, the liberal one — so "justice" in debate is not one concept contested but several inherited from different wholes.

**Main argument:** Rationality lives inside traditions — historically extended, socially embodied arguments — but traditions can rationally defeat one another in epistemological crisis, so tradition-constituted reason answers both the Enlightenment's neutral tribunal and relativism.

### Chapter 18 and the Postscript — Aristotle recovered, and waiting for St. Benedict

The conclusion gathers the three stages: virtues defined by practices (chapter 14), by their place in a narratively unified life (chapter 15), and by their sustenance of traditions pursuing the human good across generations. The Aristotle recovered is Aristotle **through Aquinas**: the *telos* restored, the virtues — **justice, courage, and honesty** at the core — again excellences of a being with an essential nature and a final end. This is recovery with differences: no going back to the *polis*, and the modern state — bureaucratic, emotivist in its self-understanding — is not a possible home for the virtues as the ancient city was.

Hence the famous ending: not a program for capturing the state, but local forms of community — households, schools, parishes, workplaces ordered to practices — within which civility and the moral and intellectual life can survive the new dark ages he believed were already upon us. We are, in his closing words, "waiting for another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict." The allusion is to Western monasticism's founder, who in another dark age preserved civilized life in small communities, not empires. In the Postscript, MacIntyre answers his first critics, conceding local points while holding the central line.

One flag must be planted firmly here. MacIntyre was grouped with the **communitarians** in the 1980s debate, and he **explicitly rejected the label**. He is not a communitarian theorist of the state: he did not argue that a political community should enforce a shared conception of the good, he regarded political appeals to "community" as mostly vacuous or worse, and his allegiance is to Thomist Aristotelianism — practices, traditions, local forms of life.

**Main argument:** The recovered Aristotle is Aquinas's Aristotle — virtues ordered to a human *telos*, sustained in local communities rather than states — and its author is a Thomist Aristotelian awaiting "another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict," not a communitarian theorist of the state.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction follows the learning path's discipline: premises, conclusion, one standard objection, one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The catastrophe and emotivism argument

**Premises:**

1. Contemporary moral debates — war, abortion, distributive justice — are interminable: no rational procedure could settle them, yet they continue as assertion and counter-assertion.
2. Their rival premises are conceptually incommensurable: rights, utility, justice, and innocence are invoked as weights on a nonexistent scale, yet every party purports to appeal to impersonal rational criteria.
3. The best explanation of that combination is that the concepts are fragments of older, coherent schemes whose unifying contexts are lost, as "taboo" survived its order.
4. Such a culture will *use* moral utterances as expressions of preference and instruments for manipulating others' attitudes — exactly what emotivism describes.

**Conclusion:** Emotivism is false as a theory of the meaning of moral language, but true as sociology: modern moral disagreement is interminable because modern moral concepts are fragments, and emotivism is the philosophy a fragmented culture generates about itself.

**Objection:** The catastrophe hypothesis is an unfalsifiable just-so story: any recalcitrant evidence — moral agreement, successful argument, creative early modern philosophy — can be absorbed as another "fragment"; and historians such as J. B. Schneewind argue Kant and the utilitarians were self-aware reformers, not curators of debris. Emotivism's sociology is also contested: much moral practice — medicine, law, ordinary life — proceeds by shared standards and reaches conclusions.

**Reply:** MacIntyre can answer that the hypothesis is not unfalsifiable: chapter 1 presents it as a suggestion the history must confirm, with checkable claims — chapter 2's debates do exhibit incommensurable premises, and the Enlightenment attempts failed for chapter 5's structural reason. Local agreement inside a surviving practice (a clinic, a courtroom) is what his theory predicts — fragments work inside surviving wholes; interminability appears where rival wholes collide. Whether the history is as schematic as critics charge remains disputed.

### (b) The argument from practices and internal goods

**Premises:**

1. Some activities are practices: coherent, complex, socially established cooperative activities whose partly definitive standards of excellence participants must accept as authoritative over their own judgment.
2. Practices have goods internal to them — excellences, like those of a fine chess combination or a great portrait, achievable only by engaging in the practice, and fully recognisable only by those with experience of participating in it.
3. External goods — money, prestige, power — are achievable in many ways and are objects of zero-sum competition; internal goods enrich the whole community of practitioners when anyone achieves them.
4. Institutions, which practices need to survive, are organized around external goods, and will corrupt practices unless participants possess the qualities that resist corruption.

**Conclusion:** Justice, courage, and honesty are virtues in a non-arbitrary sense: the acquired qualities without which internal goods cannot be achieved and practices cannot be sustained — and some excellences can be judged only from inside a practice, because the standard of judgment is itself internal to it.

**Objection:** Practices can be evil. If a practice is defined by internal standards and goods, a socially established, complex, cooperative activity of torture — or expert burglary — would seem to qualify, with its own internal excellences and its own demand for courage and honesty among practitioners. The definition gives no standpoint for condemning a flourishing but wicked practice, since the condemning standards are internal to some *other* practice.

**Reply:** The practice definition is only the first of three stages, never meant to bear the whole weight. A putative practice is further judged by its place in a narratively unified life ordered to a good, and in a tradition's argument about the human good as such. Torture fails those tests: its "goods" cannot be integrated into a defensible Aristotelian-Thomist account of flourishing, and the virtues are ultimately defined by their contribution to that flourishing, not by any practice's self-endorsement. Whether the later stages supply the standpoint, or merely relocate the question, is a standard pressure point.

### (c) The argument for the narrative unity of a human life

**Premises:**

1. Actions are identifiable and intelligible only within settings: to say what an agent is doing is to place a movement within a narrative history — the agent's, and her communities'.
2. A human life therefore has the form of an enacted narrative: each agent is co-author and actor, constrained by inherited stories, but accountable for the story as hers.
3. The virtues are dispositions across a whole life, not isolated episodes: courage or honesty in a single act means something only against a life's pattern.
4. Therefore the good for a person cannot be specified episode by episode, or role by role, but only as the good of a whole, unified life.

**Conclusion:** The unity of a human life is the unity of a narrative quest: a life ordered by the search for its good, in which the virtues sustain the quester in the search and in the self-knowledge the search produces.

**Objection:** Narrative unity imposes a literary form on lives that do not have it. In Galen Strawson's form: some people experience themselves *episodically*, not diachronically — past and future selves are not characters in a story continuous with the present — and are not thereby deficient or unintelligible. Narrativity is neither a universal psychological truth nor an ethical requirement; making it the condition of a virtuous life mistakes one temperament's self-experience for human nature.

**Reply:** MacIntyre can reply that his claim concerns accountability and intelligibility, not felt experience: the episodic agent is still answerable for yesterday's promises, must render her actions intelligible in narrative terms when challenged, and lives inside stories she did not write. The quest need not be consciously dramatized. Strawson's defenders answer that minimal accountability does not deliver MacIntyre's strong conclusion — a life *ordered to a telos discoverable only through the quest*.

## Common misreadings

**1. "MacIntyre is a communitarian."**
He explicitly rejected the label, repeatedly and in print. Communitarianism as a political theory holds that the political community should embody a shared conception of the good; MacIntyre held no such view of the modern state, which he regarded as irredeemably bureaucratic and emotivist, and treated political appeals to "community" as usually vacuous. His units are the practice, the unified life, and the tradition — not the political community. "Thomist Aristotelian" is his description; use it.

**2. "The book is nostalgia for the Middle Ages."**
The medieval chapters argue about a tradition's development; they are not a restoration program. There is no way back — the *polis* is gone, and the last page's St. Benedict is "doubtless very different" from the first. The task is local construction under modern conditions.

**3. "MacIntyre rejects modernity wholesale — science, rights, prosperity and all."**
The critique is targeted: emotivism as a culture's self-understanding, the fact/value dichotomy, managerial expertise, morality detached from teleology. His practices include physics and modern medicine; internal goods are achieved in modern universities and hospitals, however endangered; rights conferred within practices and traditions are real — what he denies is one foundational story about rights as self-evident trumps.

**4. "For MacIntyre, a virtue is whatever a community happens to prize."**
That is the relativist reading the three-stage account blocks. A local list (practices) is disciplined by the good of a whole life (narrative) and by a tradition's argument about the human good, in which traditions can be defeated by their own crises. Communities can simply be wrong about the virtues.

**5. "Emotivism is MacIntyre's own theory of morality."**
Emotivism is his diagnosis of modern moral *culture*; as a theory of meaning he argues it is false. His own theory is the second half's Aristotelian one: virtues defined by practices, unified lives, and traditions, ordered to a *telos*. Confusing diagnosis with cure inverts the book.

**6. "*After Virtue* is MacIntyre's last word on its subjects."**
He did not treat it as one. *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988) supplies the full theory of tradition-constituted rationality chapter 15 only sketches; *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (1990) defends Thomism against encyclopaedic and genealogical rivals; *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999) grounds the virtues in vulnerability and dependence, correcting the earlier book's silence about the body; and the 2007 Prologue concedes its Aristotle needed Aquinas. Read it as the first statement of a project revised over forty years.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **The disquieting suggestion:** moral language is in the state science's would be after catastrophe and fragmentary revival — fragments of older schemes, used without the whole that made them intelligible. The **taboo analogy** exhibits a real case — a term surviving the practice that gave it sense — as a model for *virtue*, *duty*, and *rights*.
2. **The three characteristics:** (i) interminability — no rational procedure settles the debates; (ii) conceptual incommensurability — rival, individually valid arguments invoke rival concepts, with no shared weighing standard; (iii) the purported appeal to impersonal rational criteria. The third qualifies the second because the incommensurability is masked — premises descend from different historical sources, so the impersonal tribunal is an illusion.
3. **Emotivism** holds all moral judgments to be nothing but expressions of preference, attitude, or feeling — neither true nor false. It is **true** as sociology: it describes how moral utterances are used in our culture, to express attitude and manipulate others. It is **false** as a theory of meaning: expressing feeling is a function of a sentence's use, not its meaning, and emotivism cannot distinguish expressing approval from passing judgment.
4. **A character** is a social role embodying a culture's norms — a fusion of social type and moral exemplar. **The Manager**: bureaucratic authority claiming means–ends expertise. **The Therapist**: technique applied to private life, adjusting persons to given ends. **The Aesthete**: consumption of experience, others as means to aesthetic interest. All replace moral argument with manipulation or taste.
5. **The threefold scheme:** man-as-he-happens-to-be; man-as-he-could-be-if-he-realized-his-essential-nature (the *telos*); and the precepts as the bridge between them. Discarding the middle term left precepts designed to *transform* human nature with only untutored nature to found them on; precepts whose point was correction cannot be derived from what they exist to correct.
6. **Passions (Hume):** passions conflict, and choosing which shall rule needs a criterion outside them; Hume's choices smuggle in his order's standards. **Reason (Kant):** universalizability is a formal test immoral maxims can pass, and cannot generate the inherited content. **Radical choice (Kierkegaard):** a criterionless choice confers no authority. **Utility (Mill):** "the greatest happiness" is not one measurable quantity; pleasures differ in kind, the calculus has no unit.
7. **Managerial expertise** would require law-like social-scientific generalizations with predictive power. Chapter 8 argues these systematically fail — radical innovation is unpredictable in principle, knowledge of predictions changes outcomes, contingency intervenes — so the Manager's authority rests on knowledge nobody possesses.
8. **The fork:** either follow Nietzsche — morality unmasked as will to power, values created by sovereign self-assertion — or recover Aristotle. Nietzsche's critique demolishes only the Enlightenment's fragments; against the Aristotelian tradition in its strong form the unmasking has no purchase, while Nietzsche's alternative presupposes the inheritances it claims to transcend.
9. **A practice** is a coherent, complex, socially established cooperative activity with standards of excellence partly definitive of it, through which goods internal to it are realized. In the **chess** example, the sweets bribing a child to play are an **external good** — obtainable many ways, competitively possessed; the analytical skill and strategic imagination later found in chess itself are **internal goods** — achievable only through the practice, fully recognisable only by participants, and enriching the whole community of players.
10. **Institutions** — clubs, universities, hospitals, firms — house practices, distributing the external goods they need to survive. They threaten them because their logic is external goods, tending to subordinate internal excellence to revenue, prestige, and survival, unless the virtues resist.
11. **Chapter 14's definition:** a virtue is an acquired human quality whose possession and exercise enables us to achieve goods internal to practices, and whose lack prevents us achieving any such goods. The three indispensable virtues: **justice, courage, and honesty**.
12. **A narrative quest** is a life whose unity is that of a story ordered by a search for the good — a good partly understood only through the searching. **Intelligibility** follows because an action makes sense only located in a life's story and its settings; **accountability** follows because the agent is that story's subject, answerable to others entitled to demand an account of it.
13. **A tradition** is a historically extended, socially embodied argument, in part about the goods constituting it. It can **defeat a rival** in epistemological crisis: when it resolves problems its own methods generated but could not solve, *and* explains — in terms the rival must recognize — why the rival was bound to fail comparably, the defeat is rational without any neutral standpoint. Aquinas is the example.
14. **The label is rejected** because his is not a political theory of community: he does not propose the modern state embody a shared good (he thinks it cannot), he distrusts political invocations of "community," and his framework is practices, narratives, and traditions ordered to a Thomist human good. He is a **Thomist Aristotelian**.
15. **St. Benedict proposes** constructing local forms of community — ordered to practices, civility, and the moral and intellectual life — within which those goods can survive a dark age. It does **not** propose capturing the state, restoring a medieval order, or waiting passively: the waiting is a building, and the Benedict is "doubtless very different."
