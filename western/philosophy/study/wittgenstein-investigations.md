# Study Guide: Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*, Part I

**Work:** *Philosophical Investigations* · **Author:** Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) · **Composed:** Part I essentially complete by 1946; published posthumously 1953
**Covers:** Part I selections — the Augustinian picture (§§1–38), language-games and family resemblance (§§65–77), method (§§109–133), rule-following (§§185–242), private language (§§243–315), with aspect-seeing from Part II — with a reading plan, section-by-section guide, argument reconstructions, and self-test questions.
**Companion entry:** [Wittgenstein (late)](../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)
**Focus study:** This guide is the companion for Weeks 3–6 of the [Wittgenstein focus-study dossier](../focus-studies/ludwig-wittgenstein/README.md), whose seven-week plan reads the *Tractatus* whole before these *Investigations* selections and closes with *On Certainty*.

## Edition note

Read the *Philosophical Investigations* in G. E. M. Anscombe's translation. Anscombe — Wittgenstein's student, literary executor, and translator — produced the classic English text, and her wording is still the one most commentary quotes. The current standard is the revised fourth edition (2009), edited by P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim Schulte: it revises Anscombe in places, prints German and English on facing pages, and is the edition the learning path names for Stage 5. An older Anscombe paperback is entirely usable for a first reading, but check any wording you intend to quote against the fourth edition.

Cite by section number, not page. Part I consists of 693 numbered remarks, cited as §§1–693, stable across editions and languages in a way pagination never is.

Two structural facts matter. First, Part I is the work Wittgenstein himself prepared for publication: revised repeatedly, offered in 1946, withdrawn, and left essentially finished. Part II is later material (1946–49), assembled by his editors; in the fourth edition it is retitled *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*, which signals that lesser status honestly. This guide is therefore a guide to Part I, with one excursion into Part II for aspect-seeing. Second, the book is not a treatise but an album of remarks, dialogues, examples, and aphorisms — in an order Wittgenstein chose, but not as a linear proof.

A note on quotations: the *Investigations* in translation is in copyright, so this guide paraphrases throughout, quoting only very short fragments — "meaning is use" (§43) is the model case — always with Wittgenstein's hedges attached.

## How to use this guide

The *Philosophical Investigations* looks easy and is not — the most deceptive combination in this library. The sentences are mostly short, the examples come from shops, building sites, and classrooms, and there is almost no technical vocabulary. Readers regularly finish fifty sections feeling they have understood everything and discovered nothing. That feeling is the first trap. Wittgenstein is not hiding a difficult theory behind simple language. He is dismantling, example by example, pictures you almost certainly hold: that words mean by standing for things, that understanding is an inner process accompanying words, that a sensation is a private object observed inwardly, that following a rule is consulting an inner formula. When a remark seems obvious, ask which temptation it removes. When it seems perverse, ask what assumption it refuses.

Work in the library's usual three passes:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before each block, read its part below. Know which picture is under pressure and what example does the work.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** No more than one block per primary session. After each block, write its main move in your own words, book closed, before reading this guide's reconstructions — a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction. Here the third pass needs special care: many famous "Wittgensteinian positions" are his interpreters' positions. This guide flags the two largest cases — Kripke's rule-following reading and the community view — rather than letting them pass as the text's doctrine.

The learning path places the *Investigations* in Stage 5A (Analytic), after *Tractatus* selections and Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic*, with an explicit rule: **not before those predecessors** — without them you are reading one side of a phone call. The path's minimum is §§1–38, 89–133, and 243–311. This guide covers that minimum and adds family resemblance (§§65–77), rule-following (§§185–242), and aspect-seeing from Part II. Do not try to march through all 693 remarks on this pass.

## Reading plan

The selections amount to roughly 200 of the 693 remarks, plus a short Part II excerpt. At the learning path's pace, allow about four weeks, in the order given: private language presupposes rule-following.

| Week | Sections | Focus |

| 1 | Preface; §§1–38 | The Augustinian picture, the builders, ostensive definition |
| 1–2 | §§65–77; §§109–133 | Games and family resemblance; the method remarks |
| 2–3 | §§185–242 | Rule-following: the series example, the §201 paradox, practice and agreement |

A session covering fifteen sections that leaves you able to reconstruct one example — the builders, the thread, the diary — is a good session; one covering sixty that leaves only underlined pages is a failure.

## Section-by-section guide

### The Preface and the shape of the book

The Preface (1945) gives you three things. First, the origin: Wittgenstein says he had been forced to recognize grave mistakes in the *Tractatus*, crediting criticism — Frank Ramsey's, and conversations with Piero Sraffa among others — with loosening the old picture's grip. Second, the form: sketches approaching the same points from different directions, an album rather than a treatise. Third, the aim: not to spare others the trouble of thinking, but to stimulate thought.

### §§1–38: The Augustinian picture and the builders

The book opens with a passage from Augustine's *Confessions* (I.8): Augustine describes learning language as a child — elders named objects and moved toward them, and he grasped that the sounds designated the things. Wittgenstein finds in it a particular picture of the essence of language: every word has a meaning; the meaning is the object the word stands for; words name objects, and sentences are combinations of names. Note his care, and copy it: this is not an accusation against Augustine, who was plausibly describing one real strand in learning a first language. The picture is the target — a template that feels like common sense and governs theories of meaning far beyond Augustine, including the *Tractatus* itself.

Against it, §1 sets a shopping example: a slip marked with a word is taken to a shopkeeper, who opens a drawer, matches a color sample, and counts out items. Even here, words do work that "standing for an object" does not describe — counting, matching, and acting are part of what the words do. Then §2 gives the builders: a builder, A, and an assistant, B, working with blocks, pillars, slabs, and beams. A calls a word; B brings the stone he has been trained to bring at that call. Conceive this, Wittgenstein asks, as a complete primitive language. The point is not that English is like this — it is not — but that here language functions while the Augustinian description gets no grip: the calls are orders woven into building, and their meaning is their role in the activity. If one coherent case works without naming in the picture's sense, the picture cannot be language's essence.

The following sections widen the crack. Different words do different jobs, as tools in a toolbox do (§11): hammer, glue, and ruler have no uniform function, and the uniform look of written words tricks us into expecting one. Philosophical confusion arises when language is like an engine idling rather than doing work — when language "goes on holiday" (§38). Section 19 introduces the organizing term: to imagine a language is to imagine a *form of life*. Section 23 coins the working vocabulary — speaking is part of an activity, a *language-game* — and lists the motley: giving orders, describing, reporting, speculating, making up a story, play-acting, guessing riddles, asking, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying. The list is the argument: no one activity on it is the essence of the others.

**Main argument:** The Augustinian picture describes at most one region of language. A primitive but complete counter-example (the builders) and the diversity of actual uses show meaning must be sought in use within activities, not in a uniform naming relation.

### §§27–38 (within the opening block): Ostensive definition and samples

Naming looks like the picture's strongest case, so Wittgenstein examines how naming is taught: by *ostensive definition* — pointing and uttering a word, "this is called —". His question (§§28–30) is what makes the pointing succeed. Point at a pencil and say a word: has the learner been given the name of the shape, the color, the material, the number, or the pencil? The gesture underdetermines its lesson. Ostension works only because the learner already commands a stage-setting — some grasp of what kind of thing is being named. Ostensive definition therefore presupposes a language-game already in place; it cannot be the foundation on which all language rests. This is not skepticism about teaching children words, which succeeds daily. It is a point about what the success consists in: training into a practice, not a link forged between sound and object by pointing or inner attention.

Samples belong here too. In the shopping case a color sample serves as an *instrument* of the language — part of the means of representation — rather than as one more object the word names. Keep that distinction: it returns in §258, where the diarist tries to make a private sensation serve as his own sample, and the stage-setting is exactly what he lacks.

**Main argument:** Ostensive definition is not self-interpreting. It works only inside an established practice, so it cannot ground meaning from outside language.

### §§65–77: Language-games and family resemblance

Section 65 stages the central confrontation. An interlocutor objects: you talk about language-games but never say what is common to them all — what language *is*. Wittgenstein turns the challenge into a test case. Consider, §66 says, the proceedings we call *games*: board-games, card-games, ball-games, Olympic games, ring-a-ring-a-roses. Do not *say* there must be something common — *look and see*. Winning and losing occur in chess and patience, but a child throwing a ball at a wall has amusement without competition; skill matters in chess and tennis, but in roulette luck displaces it; patience is a game for one. Similarities crop up and disappear. What remains, §67 says, is "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing" — *family resemblances*, after the way build, eye color, gait, and temperament run through a family without any single feature appearing in every member.

His image (§67) deserves slow reading: a thread's strength does not lie in one fiber running through its whole length, but in many fibers overlapping. A concept held together by family resemblance is not defective, awaiting definition; the thread is strong as overlapping fibers make it strong. Sections 68–71 press the consequence. Tennis has no rule for how high the ball may be thrown, and is a game all the same; a boundary drawn for a special purpose does not show the concept was unusable before, or that the drawing discovered a line already there. "The concept 'game' is a concept with blurred edges" (§71) — and a blurred photograph is still a picture, usable where sharpness is not needed. Section 77 adds: a sharp boundary we draw may be the one we always wished to draw — but the wish was ours, not the concept's confession.

The target is not definition as such — Wittgenstein defines constantly, for purposes — but *essentialism* as a method: Socrates' demand, renewed by Frege and the *Tractatus*, that a concept is understood only when necessary and sufficient conditions are produced. Family resemblance is a counter-example to that demand, and a diagnosis of why it feels compulsory.

**Main argument:** At least some central concepts — *game*, and by extension *language* — are united by family resemblance, not common essence. Usability does not require sharp definition, and the craving for essence is a picture to be examined, not a standard to be met.

### §§109–133: Method — philosophy as therapy

Read these remarks as the book's operating manual, twice: here, and again after rule-following. Section 109: philosophy is not a theory competing with the sciences; its problems are solved not by new information but by arranging what we have always known — reminders of how words are actually used. Section 116 states the program: bring words back from their metaphysical to their everyday use. Section 122 names the goal, a *perspicuous representation* — a surveyable arrangement of uses that lets us see the source of a confusion. Section 124 is the quietist crux: philosophy may in no way interfere with the actual use of language; it can only describe it, and it "leaves everything as it is". Section 133 gives the success condition: not a single method but methods, like different therapies; the real discovery is the one that gives philosophy peace, so it is no longer tormented by questions it brings upon itself.

Two cautions, because these are the book's most sloganised remarks. First, "leaves everything as it is" is a claim about *language*: philosophy does not reform grammar as a legislature reforms law. Inflating it into general political conservatism — as critics in Ernest Gellner's line (*Words and Things*, 1959) did — extends it beyond its text; whether the extension is fair is debated. Second, the therapy metaphor (§133; compare §255, treating a question like an illness) is an aim, not an excuse for vagueness. The therapy works by concrete means: invented cases and reminders checkable against your own use of the words. A "therapeutic" reading that cannot point to the example doing the work is an incantation, not the method.

**Main argument:** Philosophical problems are confusions generated by our grammar, dissolved — not solved — by arranging reminders of ordinary use until the confusion loses its grip. Whether that conception is liberation or abdication is a standing debate about the book.

### §§185–242: Rule-following

Wittgenstein's own example (§185) is a pupil learning number series. Teach him the +2 series — 0, 2, 4, 6, up to 1000 — with training and correction. Now tell him to go on beyond 1000, and he writes 1004, 1008, 1012, insisting he *is* going on in the same way: he took the instruction to change the step after 1000. Our reaction is that he has misunderstood. Wittgenstein's question is what the misunderstanding consists in. Everything we gave him was finite, and any finite course of past behavior fits more than one rule, diverging only on cases not yet reached. What fixes *the* rule he was meant to follow?

Section 201 states the paradox, paraphrased: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course can be made out, on some interpretation, to accord with it — and therefore also to conflict with it, leaving neither accord nor conflict. Wittgenstein's response is immediate, and is the sentence interpreters most often omit: there is a way of grasping a rule which is *not an interpretation*, but which is exhibited in what we call "obeying the rule" and "going against it" in actual cases. Interpretations are signs — formulae, pictures, tables — and a sign does not interpret itself; an interpretation behind every grasp generates a regress, each needing another to fix its application. The regress stops not at an inner item but at practice: training, custom, techniques in which "going on" is shown rather than further interpreted. Section 202 draws the privacy conclusion in advance: obeying a rule is a practice, and thinking one is obeying a rule is not obeying a rule — so one cannot obey a rule "privately", because obeying and merely seeming to obey would coincide. Section 217 gives the stopping-point image: if I have exhausted the justifications, I have reached bedrock and my spade is turned — this is simply what I do. Sections 241–242 locate the bedrock socially without making it a vote: agreement in language is not agreement in *opinions*, which can be false, but agreement in *judgments* and in a form of life — the shared concord in reactions without which even disagreement, truth, and falsity could not start.

Now the warning this guide must give prominently. In *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982), Saul Kripke recast §§185–242 as a *skeptical paradox* about meaning, illustrated by his invented "quus" — a function agreeing with *plus* on all numbers so far encountered but diverging on larger ones, so no fact about past use or inner state fixes whether I meant plus or quus — and offered a *skeptical solution*: correctness is understood through the conditions under which a community licenses assertions about meaning. Kripke's book is brilliant and influential, and, on the majority view among Wittgenstein scholars (Gordon Baker and P. M. S. Hacker prominently), not faithful exegesis. On the rival view, §201's paradox is to be *dissolved* by rejecting its premise — that grasping a rule must be an interpretation — not answered by a new theory; Wittgenstein offers no theory of meaning at all. "Kripkenstein" is the standard label for the possibly distinct philosopher of Kripke's book. Likewise the *community view* — rule-following essentially requires a community of checkers — is one contested reading of §§202 and 241–242; individualist readings argue a solitary agent in a practice can sustain the seeming-right/being-right distinction, and that the appeal is to practice, not head-counting. Quus is Kripke's illustration, not Wittgenstein's: the text's case is §185's pupil, and you should be able to run the whole argument from that case alone.

**Main argument:** A rule does not determine its applications through interpretations, formulae, or inner items, since each can itself be variously taken. Grasp of a rule is shown in practice — trained, shared techniques of going on — and where following and seeming to follow collapse into one, there is no rule.

### §§243–315: Private language

Section 243 poses the question precisely: can we imagine a language whose words refer to what can be known only to the speaker — his immediate private sensations — so that another person cannot understand it, not as a matter of fact (an uncracked code) but in principle? The target is a *picture*: the Cartesian mind as a private theater, sensation-words as names given to what is observed there. The sections test whether that picture can work as an account of a language.

The hinge is §258, the diary case. I keep a diary about the recurrence of a certain sensation, writing "S" on each day I have it, fixing the sign by attending to the sensation and, as it were, pointing to it inwardly — an inner ostensive definition. But a definition fixes a sign's use only if a use is established; here nothing is established beyond a ceremony of concentration. Tomorrow, what makes it *correct*, rather than merely sincere, to write "S" again? No criterion was laid down, because the only candidate check — my impression that this sensation is the same again — is the very thing needing a check. Wittgenstein's compressed conclusion: here, whatever seems right to me *is* right — and that only means we cannot talk about "right". A language requires logical space for error; a practice in which error is impossible in principle is not rigorous but the picture of rigor.

Two supporting cases sharpen it. Section 265 blocks memory as an independent check: justifying one memory impression by another is like buying several copies of today's newspaper to confirm what the first copy said — the copies are not independent witnesses. Section 293 gives the beetle in the box: everyone has a box only they can look into, and we call its contents a "beetle". The point is not that the contents are unreal, or the same, or different: the thing in the box *cancels out* as irrelevant to the language-game — it could change constantly, or the box be empty, and the word's use would proceed unchanged. The private object, posited as the word's *meaning*, drops out of the account of meaning. The positive sketch is §244: sensation-words are connected with the natural expressions of sensation and used in their place — a child hurts himself and cries; adults teach him exclamations and later sentences. Sensation-language grows from behavior and circumstance, not private naming ceremonies.

Scope: the argument's reach is debated. At minimum it refutes a necessarily private language as defined in §243 and the inner-ostension model of sensation-words. Whether it refutes stronger Cartesian claims is contested. It does not deny that pains exist or settle other minds.

**Main argument:** A sign fixed only by private ostension has no criterion of correct reuse, because seeming right and being right coincide — and where they coincide, "right" has no application. Sensation-words get their meaning from public practices built on natural expression, not from naming private objects.

### Part II selections: Aspect-seeing — the duck-rabbit

Part II (the *Philosophy of Psychology* fragment) applies Part I's method to perception, in the discussion conventionally cited as Part II, section xi. Its emblem is a drawing that can be seen as a duck or as a rabbit — the duck-rabbit, adapted from a puzzle picture popularized by the psychologist Joseph Jastrow. The lines do not change, yet everything changes when the aspect flips: the beak becomes ears, the facing direction reverses. Is *seeing-as* part of perception, or a kind of thinking that accompanies it? Wittgenstein distinguishes *seeing that* something is the case — reportable, checkable — from *seeing* an aspect, which dawns, can be described only by comparison ("now it's a rabbit"), and obeys the will as ordinary seeing does not: you can be told to see it as a rabbit, and try, and succeed, as you cannot be told simply to see the page.

He distinguishes continuous seeing of an aspect (you have only ever seen the drawing as a duck) from the *dawning* of an aspect (the flip), and coins *aspect-blindness* for a hypothetical inability to experience the dawning. Two easy theories are refused. Against pure *interpretation*: when the aspect dawns you do not see neutral lines and infer "rabbit" — you see a rabbit, immediately; the experience is genuinely perceptual, and an interpretation-report ("I take it as a rabbit") falsifies what happened. Against an extra inner object: no second picture in the mind changed while the first stayed fixed; positing one repeats, for perception, the private-object picture the language sections dismantled. The payoff for Part I is direct: aspect-seeing is the perceptual analogue of rule-following. In both, we insert an inner mechanism — an interpretation, a mental picture — between the drawing or sign and its life in use; in both, describing the phenomenon fully shows the inserted mechanism explaining nothing.

**Main argument:** Seeing-as is neither plain seeing nor detached interpretation, and no changing inner picture explains it. It is a concept with blurred edges, straddling perception and thought — and noticing that dissolves the puzzle rather than failing it.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction follows the learning path's discipline: premises, conclusion, one standard objection, one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) Meaning-as-use against the Augustinian picture (§§1–43)

**Premises:**

1. The Augustinian picture holds that a word's meaning is the object it stands for, and sentences are combinations of such names.
2. The builders' language (§2) functions completely — orders given, understood, obeyed — yet its words work as calls within an activity, not as names whose meaning is a correlated object.
3. Across actual language, words perform irreducibly diverse jobs (ordering, describing, joking, calculating, praying — §23), as diverse as the tools in a toolbox (§11).
4. Ostensive definition, the picture's favored mechanism, presupposes an established practice and cannot fix meaning by itself (§§28–30).

**Conclusion:** Meaning is not, in general, a naming relation. For a large class of cases — "though not for all" (§43) — a word's meaning is its use in the language.

**Objection.** The argument refutes a straw man. No sophisticated theory identifies meaning with the bare object named; Frege distinguished sense from reference a generation earlier. And "use" is left so broad the conclusion risks vacuity.

**Reply.** The target is a picture, not a named opponent, and pictures survive sophisticated theories: Frege still demands that every well-formed name secure a reference, and the *Tractatus* built a metaphysics on the naming model. The §43 hedge answers the vacuity charge in advance — this is a redirection of attention, from hidden correlates to observable practice, not a rival theory. Whether a redirection is enough is the quietism debate, and it is open.

### (b) Family resemblance against essentialism (§§65–77)

**Premises:**

1. If the essentialist method is correct, a concept such as *game* is understood only when a property common to all and only games is stated.
2. Surveying the games (§66) — competitive and non-competitive, skilled and luck-based, solitary and social — reveals no such property; similarities appear, overlap, and drop out.
3. The concept nevertheless functions: speakers apply it, extend it to new cases, and correct misuse, without necessary and sufficient conditions.
4. A thread's strength lies in overlapping fibers, no one running throughout (§67); likewise a concept's unity can lie in overlapping similarities.

**Conclusion:** Some concepts are united by family resemblance, not essence. Definition is a tool for purposes, not the precondition of understanding.

**Objection.** The argument generalizes from one conveniently messy case. *Game* may work this way while *knowledge* or *number* do not — and "anything goes" is the standing undergraduate moral, dissolving analysis altogether.

**Reply.** The claim is methodological, not universal: it removes the *a priori* guarantee that an essence awaits discovery, and shifts the burden — in each case, look and see. Borderline cases are handled by decision and analogy to paradigms, which is how the concept lives. "Anything goes" misreads the thread: fibers overlap in constrained patterns, and misuse remains corrigible. Stipulative sharpness for a purpose (§69) is fully compatible with this.

### (c) The rule-following paradox and its dissolution (§§185–201)

**Premises:**

1. Finite training — examples, formulae, explanations — appears to fix how a rule applies in indefinitely many new cases (§185).
2. Yet any finite set of examples fits more than one rule, diverging on unencountered cases; the pupil continuing 1004, 1008 beyond 1000 can claim accord with *some* interpretation of what he was given.
3. If grasping a rule consisted in having an interpretation, the interpretation — itself a sign — would need a further interpretation to fix its application, and so on without end (§201).
4. Therefore grasp of a rule cannot consist in interpretation all the way down.

**Conclusion:** There is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation, exhibited in practice: training, custom, and shared techniques of going on (§201). Justifications run out at bedrock practice (§217), and that terminus is the concept's foundation, not its failure.

**Objection.** This is Kripke's opening, not his refutation. Press premise 2 skeptically: if no fact — past use, dispositions, inner states — constitutes my having meant one rule rather than a deviant alternative (the quus case), then "practice" merely relocates the mystery, and the honest conclusion is Kripke's: meaning-attributions are licensed by community assertibility conditions, not grounded in facts about the individual.

**Reply.** The Wittgensteinian reply (Baker and Hacker among others) is that the skeptical press assumes what §201 rejects: that unless an interpretation or meaning-constituting fact mediates, nothing has been grasped. Within a practice, "this is the same" has criteria — training succeeded, corrections converge, the technique meshes with counting and measuring — and demanding a further fact behind all criteria at once is demanding to step outside every practice while still using its words. Kripke presented his reading as Wittgenstein's argument *as it struck him*, not as commentary. Treat "Kripkenstein" as a major development *from* the text, contested *as* exegesis; do not cite the skeptical solution as Wittgenstein's own view.

### (d) The private language argument via §258 (§§243–293)

**Premises:**

1. Suppose a language whose words refer to sensations knowable only to the speaker (§243), and a diarist who fixes "S" by inner ostension — concentrating on a sensation and resolving to call that sensation "S" (§258).
2. A sign has a use only if using it correctly is distinct from merely seeming to use it correctly; a rule without possible error is not a rule.
3. In the diarist's set-up, the only check on a later "S" is his impression of sameness, and that impression is itself what needs checking (§265: memory cannot independently verify memory).
4. Therefore here whatever seems right is right — which means the distinction required by premise 2 does not exist.

**Conclusion:** The supposed private language has no criterion of correct use and is not a language. Sensation-words get their meaning another way: from public practices built on natural expression (§244).

**Objection.** A lifelong castaway keeping a sensation-diary in a language learned in society seems a counter-example: his "S" has criteria. And critics since Ayer's contemporary review argue Wittgenstein smuggles in verificationism: why must seeming-right collapse into being-right, unless correctness is defined as public checkability from the start?

**Reply.** The castaway concedes the point: his diary works because it is *not* private in §243's sense — it borrows criteria from a public practice, which Wittgenstein's stipulated case strips away. On verificationism, defenders in the Baker–Hacker line reply that no theory of verification is assumed: the premise is grammatical, earned in the rule-following sections — a rule requires a technique of application, and a "technique" that one private impression both constitutes and certifies is a picture of one, not one. The argument's scope beyond sensation-language remains debated.

## From the *Tractatus* to the *Investigations*: what is overcome

The *Investigations* is, among other things, a book-length argument with the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921/1922) — the book its author now judged to contain grave mistakes.

**The picture theory is abandoned.** In the *Tractatus*, a proposition with sense pictures a possible state of affairs, sharing logical form with what it depicts; names mean simple objects, and their combination mirrors a possible combination of objects. The *Investigations* dismantles each storey. The builders (§2) exhibit functioning language with no picturing in sight. Ostensive definition (§§28–30) shows the name–object link presupposing a practice rather than founding language. The simples — never given a settled example even in the early work — are traced to a demand of the theory: determinacy of sense was *required*, and simples postulated to meet the requirement. Color-exclusion, Ramsey's problem — "red all over" and "green all over" exclude each other, contradicting the requirement that elementary propositions be independent — is the kind of ordinary fact the early system could not house, and the later method is built to house such facts by describing them.

**Analysis is abandoned, and replaced by description.** The early book held that everyday language disguises logical form, and clarification means analysis down to the fully analyzed proposition. The later book holds that nothing is hidden in that sense: confusion arises because we are bewitched by analogies between surface forms — expecting every word to work like a name because words look alike on the page (§11's toolbox). Hence §109's arrangement of what we have always known, and §124's description-only philosophy. Where the *Tractatus* sought crystalline purity, the *Investigations* turns the examination around (§108, in paraphrase): the crystalline is a requirement we impose, and the friction we need is the rough ground of actual practice (§107).

**The ladder is the hinge.** The *Tractatus* ends by indicting itself: its propositions are elucidatory — whoever understands their author finally recognizes them as nonsensical, having climbed out through them; the ladder of 6.54 is to be thrown away after the climb, before the silence of proposition 7. How literally to take that is the early work's central dispute: on a *traditional* (ineffability) reading, the book conveys genuine truths that can be shown but not said; on a *resolute* reading (Cora Diamond, James Conant), there are no ineffable truths — the propositions are plain nonsense, and therapy succeeds only when the reader stops salvaging a doctrine from them. Flag it as a debate; this guide takes no side. But notice its effect on continuity. If the resolute reading is right, the *Investigations*' method is no break but the *Tractatus*' own method applied consistently — philosophy as activity, not doctrine, was already declared at *Tractatus* 4.112. If the traditional reading is right, the break is deeper: a metaphysics of logical form genuinely surrendered. The Preface supports holding both emphases at once — new published with old, later thoughts seen "in the right light" against it — presupposing real contrast *and* real continuity. What survives on either reading: philosophy as a struggle against bewitchment by language, by clarification rather than theory. What does not: the picture theory, logical atomism, the saying/showing distinction as a metaphysical doctrine, and the idea that philosophy's problems were solved once, in 1921.

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check yourself against the answers at the end of this guide.

1. What is the Augustinian picture of language, and why does Wittgenstein begin with Augustine's *Confessions* rather than a named philosophical opponent?
2. What do the builders (§2) show, and what do they not show, about ordinary language?

## Common misreadings

**1. "Meaning is use — so words mean whatever anyone does with them."**
The slogan is hedged in the text: "for a large class of cases … though not for all" (§43). And use is not individual whim: it is use in a practice, with training, correction, and criteria. A speaker whose word is answerable to nothing is §258's diarist, whose seeming-right has collapsed into right and whose word has lost its use.

**2. "Wittgenstein is a behaviourist."**
He repeatedly denied the label, and the text supports the denial. He does not claim sensations are behaviors, or that the inner does not exist. His claim is about *meaning*: sensation-words are not names fixed by private ostension, and their grammar is bound up with natural expression (§244) and public criteria. A behaviorist theory of mind is a theory; the private language sections attack a picture of how words mean.

**3. "The private language argument settles other minds — solipsism is refuted."**
It does nothing of the kind, in either direction. The argument targets a model of sensation-language — private ostensive definition — not the traditional problem of other minds, and it supplies no bridge from behavior to inner states. Enlisting §258 against skepticism, and enlisting it as a denial of inner life, are the same mistake in opposite directions.

**4. "Family resemblance means anything goes."**
A concept with blurred edges still has edges for most purposes (§71): you can misapply "game", be corrected, and learn. Family resemblance denies that usability requires a common essence; it does not deny paradigms, correction, or purpose-relative sharp boundaries (§69) — it explains how correction works without essences.

**5. "Kripke's sceptical solution is Wittgenstein's solution."**
Kripke said in his own preface that the argument should be thought of as Wittgenstein's as it struck Kripke — a presentation, not commentary. The paradox as a conclusion to be answered, the quus construction, and the community-assertibility solution are Kripke's architecture. Most scholars who address the question (Baker and Hacker prominently) hold that §201's paradox is stated to be dissolved by rejecting its interpretive premise, and that Wittgenstein offers no solution because he offers no theory of meaning. Cite "Kripkenstein" for Kripke's construction.

**6. "§124 makes Wittgenstein politically quietist — philosophy must never criticise anything."**
Section 124 concerns philosophy's relation to *language*: it does not legislate grammar or reform usage from theory. Extending it into a general ban on criticism adds a premise the text does not contain — and Wittgenstein held strong convictions, fiercely expressed outside the philosophy. Whether the therapeutic conception nonetheless *tends* toward conservatism is a legitimate open question (Gellner pressed it), but it is a question, not a quotation.

**7. "The *Investigations* refutes the *Tractatus*, so the early book can be skipped."**
Wittgenstein wanted the two published together, and the later book's opening moves address positions a reader who has not met the *Tractatus* does not yet hold, and so cannot feel the argument against. Skipping the early book removes the target, not the difficulty.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **The Augustinian picture (§1):** every word names an object, meaning is the object named, sentences are combinations of names. Augustine presents it innocently, as a description of learning language — a pre-philosophical temptation, not one school's doctrine, and one that governed the *Tractatus*.
2. **The builders (§2)** show a complete language whose words work as calls within an activity, not as names of correlated objects — so naming cannot be language's essence. They do not show ordinary language is that simple, or naming unimportant within it.
3. **Ostensive definition underdetermines:** point at a pencil and utter a word, and shape, color, material, number, and object are all candidates. The lesson succeeds only given stage-setting — a practice already in place.
4. **§43:** "For a large class of cases — though not for all — … the meaning of a word is its use in the language." The hedge matters: Wittgenstein redirects attention rather than legislating a new essence.
5. **A language-game** is language woven into an activity. The §23 list's motley diversity is itself the evidence against a single essence of language.
6. **Family resemblance (§§66–67):** games share no one common property; similarities overlap and criss-cross, as family features do. A thread's strength lies in many fibers overlapping, not one running throughout.
7. **Blurred edges (§71):** usability is relative to purpose. A boundary can be drawn when needed (§69), but the concept guided speakers and corrected misuse before any drawing — as a blurred photograph is still a picture.
8. **§185:** the pupil, trained to 1000 in the +2 series, continues 1004, 1008, insisting he goes on the same way. Every finite set of examples he was given fits more than one rule; on some interpretation his continuation accords.
9. **§201:** no course of action could be determined by a rule, since every course can be made out to accord with it — and to conflict with it. Wittgenstein's response: there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation, exhibited in obeying and going against it. An interpretation is another sign, and a sign does not fix its own application.
10. **Quus** is Kripke's function agreeing with plus on past cases but diverging on new ones: no fact, he argues, fixes which function I meant. The "sceptical solution" is Kripke's construction ("Kripkenstein"), rejected as exegesis by many scholars, for whom §201 is to be dissolved, not solved.
11. **Bedrock (§217):** where justifications run out — "this is simply what I do". Not defeat: justification was always *within* a practice; demanding one of the practice itself repeats §201's regress.
12. **§258:** the diarist fixes "S" by inner attention, with no independent criterion for its next use. The only check is his impression of sameness — what needed checking — so whatever seems right is right, and "right" has no use. The diary lacks not privacy but correctness.
13. **The beetle (§293):** a private object posited as a word's meaning cancels out — the box's contents could change or be absent while the word's use proceeds unchanged. It does not show sensations do not exist, or that pain is behavior.
14. **Aspect-dawning** is the flip — duck to rabbit, drawing unchanged — while **continuous seeing** is having only ever seen it one way. Seeing-as is not plain seeing (the object is unchanged, the experience transforms) and not interpretation (the rabbit is *seen*, not inferred).
15. **Abandoned:** the picture theory, and logical atomism with its simple objects and fully analyzed propositions. **Surviving:** philosophy as clarification rather than doctrine, already declared at *Tractatus* 4.112.
16. **§124:** philosophy does not interfere with the actual use of language; it describes grammar rather than legislating usage. It does not mean philosophy must never criticize anything else — that extension is a further, debated claim.
