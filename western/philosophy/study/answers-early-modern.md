# Answer Key: Early Modern Study Questions

Worked model answers to the Study questions in the early modern entries. Attempt each question in writing first — the learning path's rule is reconstruction before comparison — and treat these as one defensible answer, not the only one: where scholarship is divided, the main positions are given rather than a verdict, and application questions in particular admit other equally valid workings.

## [Machiavelli](../eras/03-early-modern/niccolo-machiavelli.md)

**1. *Virtù* against *fortuna*: the river.** In *The Prince* XXV Machiavelli estimates that fortune governs roughly half of human actions, leaving the other half to us — and the estimate itself is the anti-fatalist move: if fortune governed all, counsel would be pointless, and if none, his book would be unnecessary. The river image gives the mechanism: fortune is like a flooding river — irresistible in spate, but in calm weather men can build dikes and embankments that change what the flood does when it comes. Preparation does not abolish fortune; it converts her from destiny into a variable whose damage depends on prior *virtù*. This is not moral virtue: *virtù* is the repertoire of capacities — boldness, adaptability, willingness to use cruelty or fraud when the times demand — by which a founder seizes the *occasione*; a morally good man with no *virtù* is simply washed away. Nor is it fatalism, because the half left to us is causally decisive at the margins that matter: the same flood ruins the unprepared province and spares the diked one. The doctrine's pressure point, which Machiavelli concedes, is temperament: men prosper while their mode of proceeding matches the times and fail when times change, because they cannot change their natures — the dikes are built by character as much as by policy.

**2. *The Prince* versus the *Discourses*.** The objection: the *Prince* advises one man to make himself feared, to learn how not to be good; the *Discourses* praise the multitude as wiser and more constant than a prince and make the people the guard of liberty. Same author, apparently opposite politics. The coherence reply — the strongest standard one, which the entry endorses as a reading rather than a certainty — distinguishes situations, not principles. Founding and *re*founding are moments of corruption and emergency, where one person must act with undivided energy, even terribly (Romulus, Cesare); *maintaining* a free order is a different problem, solved by distributing power among many whose conflicts, channelled by institutions, protect liberty. The constant throughout is the goal — a strong, self-sustaining political body — and the variable is the regime-appropriate means. Testing against the texts: the *Prince*'s last chapter calls for a redeemer of Italy, a founder; the *Discourses* repeatedly insist founders must yield to orders and laws, and that a republic outlives principalities precisely because it can adapt through many citizens. Residual tension survives the reply — the *Discourses* also praise founders' solitary violence, and the *Prince* also analyses republics — so the honest verdict is coherence of project with real friction of emphasis, which is why the "one Machiavelli or two" debate persists.

**3. Effectual truth versus Plato's imagined republic.** In *The Prince* XV Machiavelli announces his break: he will follow the *verità effettuale*, the effectual truth of the thing, rather than imagined republics and principalities "that have never been seen or known to exist." Plato's *Republic* asks how a city would be if speech could build it purely from the best principles; its standard is what *ought* to be, and existing cities are measured by their distance from it. Machiavelli's charge is that starting from the ought loses the world: a ruler who professes good in every circumstance "will come to ruin among so many who are not good," so the imagined republic, far from elevating politics, disarms the good and instructs no one who must act. What does each think the other loses? Plato would reply — and the *Republic* itself anticipates it — that effectual truth loses the *standard*: describing how cities survive cannot tell us which survivals are worth wanting, and his paradigm exists so that judgment, not merely success, is possible. Machiavelli loses normativity's independence; Plato loses traction on the actual. The exchange is the founding statement of political realism versus political idealism, still unsettled by design.

**4. Tumults, *Discourses* I.4, and the suppressed protest.** Machiavelli's scandalous thesis: the disunion between the Roman plebs and the Senate produced Rome's liberty, because the people, desiring not to be dominated, forced the creation of the tribunes and laws guarding freedom — "in every republic are two diverse humours," and good laws arise from their regulated clash. Apply to a modern state suppressing noisy protest in the name of order. By his criteria, tumults yield liberty-guarding laws when (i) the protesters' humor is the popular one — resistance to domination, not appetite to dominate; (ii) the conflict vents through, or forces the creation of, institutional channels — courts, assemblies, a tribunate-like office where grievances become law rather than wounds; (iii) the clash stops short of private violence, exile-lists and foreign intervention, which mark Rome's later, corrupt tumults. Suppression fails his test twice: it removes the pressure that generates liberty-guarding law, and it mistakes quiet for health — for Machiavelli the dangerous states are those where grievances have no public outlet and so turn conspiratorial. The channels he would demand are institutionalized accusation and appeal: public procedures where the people's humor can accuse the great without riot. A state that criminalises the noise should expect, on his analysis, not order but corruption deferred.

## [Descartes](../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md)

**1. The cogito as intuition.** Descartes insists (Replies to the Second Objections; *Principles* I.7) that "I think, therefore I am" is not a syllogism: he does not first know the major premise "everything that thinks exists" and then subsume himself — he *sees*, in the single act of doubting, that his thinking and his existing are one self-evident given, as the Meditations study guide reconstructs it. What it secures is deliberately thin: a thinker existing *in the act of thinking* — "a thing that thinks," whose existence is certain exactly while and because it doubts, affirms, imagines, senses-as-it-were. What it does not yet secure: that this thinker is a *substance* persisting between thoughts (duration requires God's conservation, argued later); that it is immaterial or an enduring soul (the real distinction comes only in Meditation VI, and needs the divine guarantee); and anything about body, which remains in doubt. The cogito is a first certainty, not a completed metaphysics — readers who treat it as the whole Cartesian system mistake the foundation stone for the building.

**2. Lichtenberg's "it thinks."** Lichtenberg objected that the evidence licenses only "thinking is occurring" (*es denkt*), as one says "it is lightning": the "I" smuggles in a substantial subject the experience does not present. Descartes' first-person reply begins by denying the impersonal redescription is innocent: doubting, affirming and being deceived are not free-floating events but acts someone performs; a deception with no one deceived is not a thinner datum but an incoherent one. Moreover the cogito's claim is not a metaphysical "I-substance" — it is that *this* thinking cannot be doubted away by the thinker whose thinking it is; first-person givenness is the certainty, not a theory about what underlies it. Judgment: the modest "I" — the subject as presented in its acts — is entitled; Lichtenberg successfully blocks any slide to a simple, enduring soul-substance, which Descartes himself only argues for later and on other grounds. Modern verdicts divide: some read the exchange as a draw at the modest reading (Descartes wins the datum, Lichtenberg wins the metaphysics), which is roughly where the entry leaves it.

**3. The Cartesian circle.** Arnauld's charge (Fourth Objections), premise by premise: (i) Descartes holds we may be certain of nothing until we know God exists and is no deceiver. (ii) The existence of God is itself proved by clear and distinct perception — the causal and ontological arguments of Meditations III–V rely on the rule that what is clearly and distinctly perceived is true. (iii) But by (i), that rule is not yet validated until the proof concludes. So the proof assumes the reliability it is meant to establish: a circle. The standard defense distinguishes *present* intuitions from *remembered* ones: while I am actually attending to a clear and distinct perception, doubt is psychologically and rationally impossible — God is not needed for occurrent certainty, only to guarantee the *standing* of conclusions I am no longer attending to, i.e. the deliverances of memory and the completed system. Does it save or shrink the guarantee? It saves Descartes from formal circularity (the proof of God uses present intuition, whose certainty never awaited the guarantee) at the cost of shrinking what God underwrites: not truth as such, but the stability of a science built over time by a forgetful mind. Critics respond that a deceiver could corrupt even present intuition, which is the deeper doubt; defenders say that doubt, stated, defeats itself. The scholarly verdict remains divided, and the entry presents the memory defense as the strongest available rather than as conclusive.

**4. Elisabeth's objection and the successors.** Princess Elisabeth pressed the mechanical philosophy's own standard: causation between substances, as Descartes defines it, works by contact, shape and motion; an immaterial, unextended soul has no surfaces, no location, no leverage — so how can it *push* the pineal gland, or be pushed by animal spirits? The pineal proposal names a junction but supplies no mechanism intelligible in either vocabulary. Spinoza's parallelism removes interaction entirely: mind and body are one substance under two attributes; the order and connection of ideas is the order and connection of things, so volition and arm-rising correspond without either causing the other across the attribute divide. Cost: the correspondence itself is brute — why these two series march together is answered only by their identity, which some experience as renaming the mystery. Leibniz's pre-established harmony also removes interaction: monads are windowless, and soul and body unfold in synchrony like two perfectly set clocks, by God's initial arrangement. Cost: the harmony is a standing miracle of precision, and real causal influence between created things is denied wholesale. Descartes' successors agree with Elisabeth that interaction is the problem; they pay opposite currencies — Spinoza spends the duality, Leibniz spends causation — to escape it.

## [Spinoza](../eras/03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md)

**1. Substance monism from the definitions.** *Ethics* I builds from definitions: substance is what is in itself and conceived through itself (ID3); an attribute is what the intellect perceives as constituting a substance's essence (ID4); a mode is what is in another and conceived through that other (ID5). Then the machinery turns: two substances cannot share an attribute, for then nothing would distinguish them except their modes — but substance is prior to its modes, so they would be indistinguishable, hence one (IP5). No substance can be produced by another (IP6–7), so existence belongs to substance's nature. God, defined as absolutely infinite, consisting of infinite attributes (ID6), therefore cannot share reality with any second substance: anything that is, is in God (IP15), and God is the one substance — *Deus sive Natura*. The premise Leibniz could reject is IP5's exclusion — more fundamentally, the definition-driven claim that genuinely distinct substances cannot share an attribute or nature: Leibniz's monads are infinitely many substances, each a complete, independent center, distinguished not by attributes but by their entire internal point of view on the universe. What he would owe in exchange is an account of how windowless, independent substances compose one ordered world — which is exactly the bill the pre-established harmony pays — and of individuation, where Spinoza's single-substance economy is stronger.

**2. The objection that modes are illusory.** Press it: if only substance truly is, and finite things are "in" it, then tables, persons and battles seem demoted to appearances — monism purchases unity by dissolving the world we wanted explained, and with it real distinction, agency and change. Spinoza's reply, as the Ethics Part I study guide lays it out: modes are not illusions but *affections* — real, determinate ways the one substance is modified, as a wave is a real modification of water, not a fiction about it. A mode has its own essence and its own causal career: this body really acts on that one; finite things are expressions of God's power in definite form (IP25, IP36), and their reality is graduated by power, not abolished by dependence. Change is real as modification, though substance itself does not change. Is modal distinction distinction enough? For individuation and causal explanation, largely yes — physics and psychology proceed at the modal level, and Spinoza's own Parts III–V do. The residual worry is normative and experiential: if my distinction from you is a wave's distinction from the sea, the standpoint of the mode (suffering, choosing) may still feel under-described. Scholars divide on whether Spinoza's expression-language fully earns real plurality; present the reply as powerful and the assessment as open.

**3. *Conatus* and the counterexamples.** The argument (E IIIP6–7): each thing, insofar as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being; this striving is nothing apart from the thing's actual essence — a thing's power *is* its conatus, and for minds the striving includes appetite and, when conscious, desire. Nothing can be destroyed except by an external cause (IIIP4): no thing's essence involves its own non-existence, so self-destruction, strictly, is impossible. Test suicide and self-sacrifice: Spinoza's answer is systematic — the person who kills himself is *overcome by external causes* contrary to his nature (IVP20 scholium): disordered affects, themselves the products of external forces acting on his imagination, have become stronger than his conatus; the self-sacrifice of a soldier is a mode whose striving now expresses itself through the larger wholes (city, comrades) with which he identifies, or again the effect of affects. Does this answer or immunise? The critic's charge of unfalsifiability is real: if every apparent counter-case is reclassified as external compulsion, no evidence could count against the doctrine. The defense is that the classification is not ad hoc but follows from the definition of essence (IIIP4 is a theorem, not a patch): a thing's essence cannot involve its negation, on pain of the thing being self-contradictory. Whether that definitional move is deep or merely verbal is a live scholarly dispute — flag it, as the entry does.

**4. Anger at a colleague, worked through.** The case: a colleague takes credit for your work in a meeting; anger follows. Step one, analyze it as a passive affect: anger, for Spinoza, is sadness (a passage to a lesser power of acting) accompanied by the idea of an external cause, plus the desire to destroy or injure its object. As passive, it is driven by *inadequate* ideas: you imagine the colleague as a free, originating cause — a little god of malice — and yourself as simply wronged; the affect's intensity tracks the imagination's partiality (you do not see the incentives, fears and habits that determined his act, nor your own contribution to the situation). Step two, form adequate ideas of the causes: understand that he acted necessarily from his nature and circumstances, that your anger arises necessarily from yours, that the institution's credit-structures conditioned both. Spinoza's claim (VP3, VP6): an affect that is a passion ceases to be a passion as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea of it — the anger does not necessarily vanish, but it loses its compulsive, distorting character; you can still act (document your work, raise the matter) but from reason rather than revenge. That counts as greater freedom although the outcome remains necessitated, because freedom for Spinoza is not exemption from necessity but the *quality* of one's necessity: being determined by one's own nature understood, rather than by external causes misunderstood. Descartes, the natural comparison, instead sets the will to *master* the passions — a combat model in which reason governs unruly motions of the animal spirits, generosity its chief weapon. Spinoza finds the combat picture incoherent (VP Preface): an affect can be restrained only by a stronger affect or by understanding that dissolves its passivity — there is no unmoved commander inside.

## [Leibniz](../eras/03-early-modern/gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md)

**1. Sufficient reason and the necessary being.** The argument: by the principle of sufficient reason (PSR), no fact is true and no thing exists without a reason why it is so and not otherwise. Contingent things — the whole series of finite, dependent beings — cannot supply their own ultimate reason: each is explained by a predecessor, but explaining every member by another member leaves the *whole series* unexplained, as copying a geometry book from an eternal chain of copies never explains the geometry. The reason for the totality must therefore lie outside the series of contingent things, in a necessary being whose essence involves existence, which carries the reason of itself and of the series — God. A critic can refuse at two marked points. First, the PSR itself, in its unrestricted form: Hume will ask why the demand for a reason for the *whole* is legitimate when each part has one, and why "brute fact" is not a possible resting place. Second, the application to the whole series: even granting PSR for things and events, treating the totality as one more item needing an external reason may commit a fallacy of composition. Leibniz's strongest ground is the first refusal point: if you accept PSR anywhere near full strength, the cosmological conclusion is very hard to stop; the historical debate therefore concentrates exactly there.

**2. Indiscernibles and the shifted universe.** The identity of indiscernibles follows from PSR: if two things were perfectly alike in every property, God would have no sufficient reason to place one here and the other there, or to create both rather than one — so perfect duplicates are impossible; every substance differs from every other intrinsically. Apply to Newton's absolute space (the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence): suppose God had created the entire universe three feet to the east, or with east and west interchanged, matter and relations unchanged. For Newton, that is a genuinely different state of affairs, since positions in absolute space differ. For Leibniz, it is no difference at all: space is not a container but the order of coexistences among things; a universal shift changes no relation, no perception, no fact — and by PSR God could have no reason to choose the one over the other, which shows the supposed difference is a fiction of the container picture. The argument does double duty: it supports relational space and illustrates how PSR and indiscernibility function as one instrument. Clarke's side can protest that God's mere will is reason enough; Leibniz replies that a will without motive is not a reason but chance — the debate's crux, still recognisable in modern discussions of symmetry and spacetime.

**3. The best of all possible worlds under pressure.** The objection: the scale and distribution of suffering — the Lisbon earthquake was Voltaire's exhibit — or the simpler thought that *any* world could be improved somewhere (remove one agony, keep the rest) makes "best possible" either false or an insult. Leibniz's reply turns on *compossibility*: possible things come in packages; a world is a maximal consistent system, and local improvements may be globally incompossible — removing this evil may unravel the conditions of greater goods, as altering one note can ruin the harmony it seemed to blemish. God, choosing among complete worlds by the principle of the best, *permits* moral and physical evil as a condition or consequence within the optimal whole, without willing it as an end; metaphysical evil (finitude itself) is unavoidable in any created order. Does this answer or relocate? It answers the crude improvement argument — naive one-change comparisons ignore compossibility, and the objection must show a *compossible* better world, which is a far heavier lift. It relocates the difficulty to our epistemic position: since we cannot survey possible worlds, "best" functions as an article of rational faith in God's choice rather than a checkable claim, and the sheer weight of suffering strains exactly that faith. Voltaire's satire and the entry's debate both live in that relocated space; do not award a knockout either way.

**4. Your arm rises: three systems.** The common case: you will to raise your arm; your arm rises. Descartes — *interaction*: the immaterial soul, united to the body at the pineal gland, redirects the animal spirits; the volition genuinely causes the motion and the motion genuinely causes sensations. What coincides is everything, causally, at the junction — at the price Elisabeth identified: no intelligible mechanism for unextended-on-extended causation. Spinoza — *parallelism*: the volition and the neural-muscular process are one and the same event conceived under two attributes, thought and extension; nothing causes anything across the divide because there is no divide in reality, only in conception; the order and connection is identical in both series. What coincides is total, but by identity rather than coordination. Leibniz — *pre-established harmony*: the soul's perceptions unfold from its own internal law, the body's motions from its own mechanical laws, and neither touches the other at all (monads are windowless); they agree as two clocks built to keep perfect time agree, because God constructed each to express the same universe from its point of view. What coincides is correspondence without causation between created things. The three systems are the three exits from Elisabeth's room: pay with mechanism (Descartes), with duality (Spinoza), or with interaction itself (Leibniz).

## [Locke](../eras/03-early-modern/john-locke.md)

**1. Warm to one hand, cold to the other.** Put one hand in hot water, the other in cold, then both in lukewarm water: the same water feels warm to one hand and cold to the other, at the same time. Locke's distinction sorts qualities by this test. *Primary* qualities — solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, number — are inseparable from body itself, present in every particle however divided, and our ideas of them *resemble* what is in the object: the idea of a shape is like the shape. *Secondary* qualities — colors, sounds, tastes, warmth — are nothing in the object itself but *powers* to produce ideas in us, grounded in the texture of the object's insensible primary qualities; our ideas of them resemble nothing in the thing (the water is not itself "warm-and-cold," yet reliably produces both ideas). The water case is the cleanest evidence: one object cannot simultaneously possess two contrary qualities, so warmth-as-felt cannot be in the water the way its volume is; it must be a power relative to a perceiver. Note Locke's modesty: secondary does not mean unreal or arbitrary — the powers are real, law-governed, and shared, which is why color language works.

**2. Berkeley's collapse objection.** Berkeley presses the same variability test against the primaries themselves: extension, figure and motion appear large or small, fast or slow, with the perceiver's position, organs and motion, just as color does — a mite's foot is large to the mite; the same tower is round from here, square from there. Worse, the primaries are *inseparable* from the secondaries: try to conceive extension with no color or tangible quality whatever and nothing remains to conceive. So the resemblance thesis collapses — if variability debars colors from resembling, it debars shapes too — and Locke's material world of primary qualities only becomes an abstraction never perceived. What survives of the distinction? Arguably its *explanatory* skeleton survives its epistemology: the idea that physics describes a perceiver-independent structure while experience presents its effects is exactly the modern scientific picture, and even Berkeley concedes the secondaries are lawfully produced. But it survives as a causal-explanatory distinction, not as Locke's resemblance doctrine, and the "substratum" that bore the primaries is precisely what Berkeley removes next. Judge: Berkeley wins the argument as stated against Locke's stated version; the distinction's ghost governs science anyway.

**3. Prince, cobbler, Butler, Reid.** Locke's criterion: a person is "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places" — personal identity consists in sameness of *consciousness*, extendable backwards to any past action one can appropriate in memory. Hence the prince's consciousness transferred to the cobbler's body makes the cobbler-bodied person the prince: he would answer for the prince's deeds, not the cobbler's. "Person" is a *forensic* term — identity matters because we praise, blame and punish persons — so the criterion is built for accountability, not biology. Butler's circularity charge: consciousness of having done X presupposes that I am the one who did X — memory cannot *constitute* the identity it already assumes. Reid's brave officer: the boy flogged at school becomes the brave officer who remembers the flogging; the officer becomes the old general who remembers the officer's deeds but not the flogging — by transitivity of Locke's criterion the general both is and is not the boy. The psychological-continuity reply (overlapping chains of memory rather than direct memory links — the general connects to the boy *via* the officer) saves consistency against Reid and softens Butler (appropriation need not be infallible recollection of one's *own* acts, circularity is avoided if continuity is causal, not epistemic). Verdict offered by the entry's debate: the reply saves the spirit of Locke by revising the letter — continuity replaces consciousness, and later writers (Parfit) inherit the revised version.

**4. The defendant who cannot remember.** For Locke, the uncomfortable answer is: insofar as his consciousness does not extend to the crime, he is *not* the same person who committed it — Locke himself bites this bullet in the drunkenness and madness cases: human courts punish the sober man for the drunk man's acts because they cannot distinguish real from feigned forgetfulness, but "in the great day" the secrets of hearts are open and no one shall be made answerable for what he knows nothing of. Same *man* (same living organism), different *person* — the forensic identity that punishment targets has lapsed. The criteria answer differently: a body-based (animalist) criterion says unambiguously the same person — the organism that acted is the organism in the dock, memory irrelevant. A soul-based criterion (identity of an immaterial substance) also says the same person, whatever the memory loss, because the substance persists through amnesia — but at the price Locke attacked: sameness of soul is undetectable and does no forensic work, since we cannot know this soul is that one. The case shows why Locke wanted a criterion tied to consciousness: it is the only one that connects punishment to the agent's own appropriation of the deed — and also why courts, unable to verify consciousness, cannot actually use it.

**5. Veined marble versus innate ideas.** Locke's attack (Essay I) targets innate *content*: no proposition — speculative or moral — is universally assented to, children and the unreflective show no trace of supposedly stamped truths, and anything that must be extracted by reason and instruction was never "in" the mind in the relevant sense; the mind's furniture is a general capacity to learn, and calling capacities innate makes "innate" empty (everything we ever learn would be innate). Leibniz replies (*New Essays*, staged as a dialogue with Locke) with the veined marble: the mind is not a blank tablet but a block whose veins already favor Hercules over other figures — truths like those of arithmetic and the principle of contradiction are *virtually* present as dispositions, tendencies and structures, actualized by experience as the sculptor's cuts follow the veins. Is a predisposition an innate idea, or exactly Locke's empty capacity? The discriminating question is specificity: a general capacity to form *some* truth or other is Locke's point and trivially "innate"; a structured disposition toward *these* truths and not others — which Leibniz claims the veins give — is content-bearing and not empty. Whether the mind's actual dispositions are that specific is the empirical-philosophical question the debate bequeaths to Kant (whose a priori forms are Leibniz's veins rebuilt as architecture) and, later, to cognitive science.

## [Berkeley](../eras/03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md)

**1. The master argument and the alleged slide.** Try, Berkeley challenges, to conceive a tree in a park or books in a closet existing unconceived and unperceived by anyone: you find that all the while *you* are conceiving them — the supposedly unperceived objects are present to your mind in the very attempt (*Principles* §23). Berkeley infers that "unperceived existence" for sensible things is a contradiction in terms: their *esse est percipi*, to be is to be perceived, since everything we can mean by their existence is their being perceived or perceivable. The objection locates the slide between two propositions: "I cannot *conceive* a tree that is not conceived" — trivially true, about the act of conceiving — and "a tree cannot *exist* unconceived" — a claim about the world. The first follows from the performance; the second requires the extra premise that conceiving gives us the thing's whole nature, i.e. that sensible things are ideas all the way down, which is what the argument was supposed to prove. Berkeley's defenders reply that the master argument is not freestanding: combined with his earlier results — the likeness principle (an idea can resemble only an idea), the collapse of the primary/secondary distinction — the tree just *is* a bundle of ideas, and for ideas the slide is no slide. Fair assessment: as a stand-alone proof it commits the equivocation charged; as a summary of the system, it dramatizes it. Most scholars treat it as rhetorical compression rather than the load-bearing argument.

**2. The collapse of Locke's distinction, rebuilt.** Berkeley's two instruments are variability and inseparability. Variability: size, shape and speed vary with the perceiver's position and constitution exactly as color and heat do — the same water argument Locke used cuts against the primaries too. Inseparability is the sharper tool: abstract a shape from every secondary quality — a figure that has no color, no texture, no tangible resistance, no visible quality at all — and ask what is left to conceive. Berkeley's answer: nothing; extension *in abstract* is a fiction of the abstracting intellect, and every actually conceived figure arrives clothed in some sensible quality. Therefore the primaries cannot be peeled off and lodged in a mind-independent material substratum while the secondaries stay in the mind: both stand or fall together, and since the secondaries are admittedly ideas, so are the primaries. What exactly remains after the operation, on Locke's behalf? Only a mathematical skeleton — coordinates without content — and Berkeley's challenge is to show that such a skeleton is a description of ideas' order, not a world behind them. Locke's side retains the causal point (something must ground the order), which Berkeley answers with God — the subject of the next question.

**3. Substratum versus God: does God do the rejected work?** Locke's material substratum — the "something, I know not what" supposed to support qualities and cause our ideas — is Berkeley's paradigm of an idle posit: unknowable in itself, invoked only to do jobs (support, causation) it is never shown to do. The skeptical comparison: is not Berkeley's God the same shape of posit — an unseen spirit invoked to perceive the world when we don't, cause our ideas in orderly series, and guarantee continuity? Berkeley's reply, as the entry gives it, rests on an asymmetry of epistemic access. Spirits are not unknown to us: I know my own mind directly — not by idea but by *notion*, through my own activity of perceiving and willing — and I reason to other spirits from effects like my own case. Matter, by contrast, could not in principle be known: an idea can resemble only an idea, an inactive substratum cannot cause, and its existence explains nothing (the order of ideas is *observed* to follow rules — the laws of nature — which a spirit's regular volition accounts for as well as an occult support). And God is not merely a gap-filler: the passivity and independence of my ideas — I cannot choose what I see on opening my eyes — require an active cause other than me. Whether "known by notion" really licenses this much metaphysics is the standing dispute; but the reply shows the two posits are not symmetrical *in Berkeley's own epistemology*, which is the question asked.

**4. The unvisited valley and Johnson's stone.** On Berkeley's account the valley exists, fully and continuously: its trees, riverbed and light are ideas — but not *yours*. Sensible things are perceived by minds; when no finite mind perceives them, they are perceived by the infinite mind, God, whose perception is not intermittent like ours — hence the valley does not flicker in and out of existence as travelers come and go, and its lawlike behavior (the river erodes whether watched or not) is the steadiness of God's ordering of ideas according to the laws of nature. Continuity is guaranteed by the same thing that guarantees order: one permanent perceiver and author of the whole series. Samuel Johnson's famous refutation — striking his foot against a stone and announcing "I refute it *thus*" — misses because Berkeley never denies the stone's solidity, resistance or kick-back: all of those are ideas and sequences of ideas exactly as Johnson experiences them; what is denied is only the philosopher's add-on, a mind-independent material substance *behind* the experience, which the kick does nothing to detect. Berkeley could accept every datum of the kick. The misunderstanding is so complete that Boswell's report of it has become the standard classroom warning: Berkeley denies not the world, but one theory of the world.

## [Hume](../eras/03-early-modern/david-hume.md)

**1. The problem of induction and the "change of subject" charge.** The reconstruction (Enquiry IV, and the Enquiry study guide): (i) all reasoning about matters of fact beyond present sense and memory rests on cause and effect; (ii) causal inference moves from observed to unobserved on the presupposition that the future will resemble the past — that nature is uniform; (iii) that presupposition cannot be demonstrated, since the contrary (nature changing course) is distinctly conceivable without contradiction; (iv) it cannot be supported from experience either, since arguing "nature has always been uniform, so it will be" itself infers the unobserved from the observed — the very move in question; (v) therefore inductive inference has no rational foundation in argument; it is produced in us by custom or habit. Assess a reply. The *pragmatic vindication* (Reichenbach's shape): induction is justified not as truth-conducive by proof but as the best available policy — if any method will succeed, induction will, since its success-condition is nature's uniformity; choosing it is rational the way choosing the only possible rescue plan is rational. The *reliabilist* reply: a belief-forming process that is in fact reliable confers justification whether or not the believer can prove its reliability — induction needs no credentials issued by argument. Do they justify, or change the subject? Hume's question was whether *reason* — demonstrative or probable argument — warrants the inference; both replies concede that it does not, and relocate warrant to policy or to external fact. That is a genuine change of subject — but defenders reply that Hume's subject was defined to be unwinnable, and changing it is the philosophy. Award the point to Hume on his own terms, and note the terms are the dispute.

**2. Causation as constant conjunction.** At the billiard table, what is actually perceived? One ball in motion, contact, the second ball in motion: contiguity in space, priority in time, and — across repetitions — constant conjunction of such sequences. What is never perceived is a *tie*: no impression of power, force or necessary connection passes between the balls; try as we might, the senses report succession only. Yet we have the idea of necessary connection — from where? Hume's answer (Enquiry VII): from an *internal* impression. After repeated conjunction, the mind is carried by custom from the impression of one ball to the enlivened idea of the other, and this felt determination of the mind — the unavoidable expectation — is the impression from which the idea of necessity is copied; we then spread the feeling onto the objects and speak of power in them. Hence the two definitions of cause: an object followed by another, *and* where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second; or, in the second register, an object whose appearance conveys the mind to the other. This is more than "one thing after another": mere sequence (*post hoc*) lacks the constant conjunction and the mind's resulting inference; causation, for Hume, is regular succession *plus* the customary transition it produces in us — which is why a single accidental pairing is not causation and a lawlike one is.

**3. The miracles maxim applied.** The case: multiple apparently credible witnesses report an event that violates a firmly established law of nature — say, a verified corpse returning to life, attested by medics with no detectable motive to lie. Hume's maxim (Enquiry X): no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle unless the testimony's falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it attests; and even then, belief is proportioned to the balance — "a wise man proportions his belief to the evidence." Weigh as directed. On one side: the uniform experience of mankind that the dead do not return, which is as strong as any law of nature we have — the evidence *against* the event is, in effect, the whole of observed biology. On the other side: the improbability of coordinated falsehood or error by these witnesses — real, but of a familiar kind: people deceive, misperceive, misremember and are deceived, and the history of attested marvels that collapsed is long, whereas verified resurrections number zero. The falsehood of the testimony is therefore *less* improbable than the fact, and the wise person withholds belief in the miracle — while believing, note, that something odd occurred worth investigating (fraud, catalepsy, error), since the testimony is still evidence of *something*. Hume's application is fallibilist, not a priori: testimony of sufficient weight could in principle win — he gives the hypothetical of universal testimony to eight days' darkness, which he says philosophers ought to accept as a natural phenomenon to be explained. The maxim is a weighing rule, not a veto.

**4. Is–ought and the missing step.** The report establishes only an *is*: the program reduces suffering. The conclusion — the program *ought* to be adopted — introduces a relation (ought) that appears in none of the premises; in the Treatise passage (3.1.1), Hume says he has observed authors slide from is-copulae to ought-copulae without explaining the new relation, and demands the step be made explicit and justified, since it would "subvert all the vulgar systems of morality" if it cannot be. What further step does the author need? A premise connecting fact to value: for example, that suffering is bad and its relief is to be pursued — a moral premise, which must itself be owned and defended, not smuggled in by the data. How does Hume's own moral philosophy supply the step? Morality, for Hume, is founded in sentiment: we approve what is useful or agreeable from a common point of view, and sympathy makes others' suffering move us — so the step from "reduces suffering" to "ought to be adopted" is taken not by deduction but by a sentiment that is (he argues) natural, shared and correctable by general rules. Does that supply or complicate it? Both: it explains how the step is *actually* and legitimately taken — moral conclusions are expressions of a disciplined sentiment, not illicit deductions — but it complicates any claim that the ought is *compelled* by the facts: someone who lacks the sentiment is not contradicted, only found morally deaf. Hume's own position is thus subtler than the "guillotine" later named after him, and the entry keeps the passage's exact force — a demand for the step to be shown — at the center.

## [Rousseau](../eras/03-early-modern/jean-jacques-rousseau.md)

**1. The *Discourse on Inequality* as diagnostic model.** Rousseau's natural human is a solitary, sturdy animal moved by *amour de soi* — self-preservation, self-love without comparison — and by *pitié*, a pre-reflective repugnance at others' suffering that checks self-love before reason exists. Such a being has no morality, no property, no language worth the name, and crucially no *amour-propre*: the comparative self-love that needs others' recognition and ranks itself above or below. The fall is social and staged: association, then agriculture and metallurgy, then property — "the first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying *This is mine*" — creates dependence; comparison creates amour-propre; inequality of possessions hardens into inequality of power, ratified by a political order the rich propose and the poor accept. Why does the "diagnostic, not historical" status matter? Because the argument's force is not archaeological but analytical: the state of nature is a controlled subtraction — remove society and see which miseries are ours by convention — and Rousseau says explicitly that the facts are to be set aside ("commençons par écarter tous les faits") since his conjectures identify mechanisms, not events. Objecting "but early humans lived in groups" therefore misses the genre: the model isolates the *generator* (comparison plus dependence) wherever it operates, including now. Its vulnerability lies elsewhere — in whether the subtraction is coherent (can pity and self-love even be specified outside society?) — which is where the entry's debates concentrate.

**2. General will versus will of all.** The *will of all* is an aggregate: the sum of private wills, each answering "what do I want?" — it takes private interest for its object and can err by addition. The *general will* is not a sum but a different act: each citizen, voting, asks not "what do I want?" but "what does the common good require?" — and the general will is the will directed at the common interest, which for Rousseau cannot err about its object (it always tends to the public good) though judgment about means can be deceived. How can a majority then be wrong? Because a majority vote only *declares* the general will under conditions: voters must be adequately informed, must deliberate as citizens rather than as factions ("partial associations" distort the aggregation of judgments into a faction's will masquerading as the whole), and the question must be general. When the majority is wrong, what its error shows the minority is not that their interest was voted down but that they themselves misjudged the common good — Rousseau's austere consolation: "when the opinion contrary to mine prevails, this proves merely that I was mistaken." The consolation depends entirely on the conditions holding; corrupted conditions convert majority verdicts back into the will of all, or of the strongest faction — a distinction the next question weaponises.

**3. "Forced to be free."** The objection, sharpened by Talmon (totalitarian democracy) and Berlin (positive liby's dangers): if dissent from the majority means I have mistaken my own real will, then a power claiming to know the general will can coerce me *for my own freedom* — and the doctrine licenses exactly the terror it fears, since any opposition can be redescribed as error or faction. The defenders' reply builds from Rousseau's constraints, as the entry presents it. First, generality of law: legitimate law must be general in object and source — it cannot name persons or privilege classes — so the sovereign *structurally cannot* target a dissenter; coercion applies to all citizens equally under laws they have given themselves. Second, sovereignty cannot be represented: no party, leader or vanguard can embody the general will on others' behalf — the moment a body claims to be its custodian, sovereignty is usurped, on Rousseau's own criteria. Third, the citizen coerced under a self-given general law is in the position of Ulysses bound by his own prior rational will against his momentary appetite. Assess: the reply genuinely blocks the *institutional* road from Rousseau to a party-state — his texts condemn the instruments such a state needs — but Berlin's deeper point survives at the level of ideas: the doctrine that my true will can diverge from my avowed will, and that others may know it better, is portable beyond its safeguards, and history shows it being ported. Award the reply the texts, and the objection the reception; the entry keeps both.

**4. The referendum under his criteria.** The case: a modern referendum, its campaign dominated by wealthy factions, delivering a law that names and burdens one group. Apply Rousseau's conditions one by one. Generality of law fails outright: a law naming one group is a decree, not a law, on his definition — the sovereign act must consider citizens in the abstract. The deliberative condition fails: wealthy factions are precisely his "partial associations," whose organized wills, amplified by money, ensure that the outcome expresses the strongest partial will, not citizens judging the common good in relative independence; Rousseau's remedies would include strict equality of political weight and the suppression or multiplication of factions. Information fails insofar as purchased messaging substitutes for citizens' judgment. The verdict: the outcome cannot express a general will by his criteria — at best it is the will of all corrupted by a faction, and the targeted group owes it no more moral allegiance than to any act of force (civil obligation, for Rousseau, extends only to legitimate acts). Compare Locke: government by consent sets a lower but different bar — majority decision under a trust, bounded by natural rights to life, liberty and estate; the targeted law likely breaches the trust on Locke's terms too (partial legislation violating natural rights dissolves obedience's ground), but Locke's test is the law's *content* against rights, Rousseau's is its *source and form* against generality. The two verdicts converge here by different roads — rights versus procedure — which is why both remain live templates for judging democratic legitimacy.

## [Kant](../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)

**1. The lying promise under both formulations.** The maxim: "When I believe myself in need of money, I will borrow money and promise to repay it, although I know I never can." Universal-law test: conceive this maxim as a universal law — everyone in need makes false promises. In that world the institution of promising destroys itself: no one would believe promises, so the promise could not secure the loan, and the maxim, universalized, contradicts its own purpose — a contradiction in conception. There is also a contradiction in the will: I cannot rationally will a world in which the practice my maxim parasitises does not exist. Humanity formulation: the deceived promisee is used merely as a means — his rational agency is bypassed by a lie; he cannot consent to the maxim because he is kept ignorant of it, and his own ends (the loan's security) are treated as nothing. The two formulations converge: universalization exposes the maxim's parasitism structurally; the humanity formula names the same wrong interpersonally — the promisee's rational nature, an end in itself, is instrumentalised. Kant's point in the *Groundwork* (worked in full in the Groundwork study guide) is that the formulations are one law seen from different sides.

**2. Mill on the same promise.** A utilitarian justification proceeds by consequences: lying generally erodes the trust on which promising, credit and communication depend — goods so weighty that Mill (following the rule-utilitarian logic of *Utilitarianism* ch. II) treats veracity as a rule of near-exceptionless utility, since the social practice collapses if exceptions become common knowledge. What it weighs that Kant excludes: outcomes, probabilities, the empirical strength of the trust-effects, and the agent's situation — e.g., the stakes of this loan, the likelihood of detection, the harm the truth would do. Where verdicts diverge: in a case where a false promise would (conjecturally) produce clear net good with no erosion of trust — a secret lie to a tyrant's agent that funds resistance, undetectable and unrepeatable — Mill's calculus can license it once the rule's utility is genuinely outweighed in the exceptional case (Mill allows that even sacred rules may yield where the breach is public-spirited and acknowledged), whereas Kant's test still condemns it, since universalization does not ask how *this* lie turns out. Conversely, where consequences are murky, Kant gives a determinate verdict and Mill an estimate. The contrast is structural: Kant prices the maxim's form, Mill its forecast — and each thinks the other's currency is counterfeit.

**3. "Every event has a cause" as synthetic a priori.** A judgment is *analytic* if its predicate is contained in its subject (true by definition), *synthetic* if the predicate adds something; it is *a priori* if known independently of experience. "Every event has a cause" is not analytic: the concept *event* does not contain *caused* — nothing contradicts a sheerly uncaused occurrence, as Hume showed by conceiving effects without causes distinctly. Nor is it learned a posteriori from any particular experience: experience shows successions, never the *necessity* that the judgment asserts of every event whatever. Yet we do make it, universally and necessarily — it is synthetic a priori, and Hume's challenge is that no such judgment should be possible. Kant's Copernican turn reverses the direction of fit: instead of our cognition conforming to objects, objects (as appearances) conform to our cognition. The mind does not receive a ready-made world; it constitutes experience by imposing forms — space and time as forms of intuition, and the categories, causality among them, as forms of understanding. A judgment is synthetic a priori when it states a condition the mind itself supplies for there being experience of objects at all: causality is valid of every event because nothing counts as an *event in one objective time-order* except through rule-governed succession. The price, which the entry keeps visible: such judgments hold only of appearances, not of things in themselves.

**4. Empty thoughts, blind intuitions.** "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (*Critique* A51/B75) distributes the failures of Kant's two predecessors between two faculties. *Sensibility* gives intuitions — the immediate, passive reception of a manifold; *understanding* gives concepts — the spontaneous unification of that manifold under rules. Descartes overplays the understanding's side: pure intellect claiming to know substance, soul and God from concepts alone — thought spinning content out of itself, which for Kant produces empty thought, concepts with no intuition to fill them and hence no knowledge (the rationalists' metaphysics is the case study). Hume overplays sensibility's side: impressions as self-sufficient atoms and concepts as faded copies — but a stream of intuitions without concepts is *blind*, not yet experience of objects at all: Hume's own bundle cannot explain why the manifold is unified into one experience of a world, and his skepticism about necessary connection is the symptom of having only one faculty. Kant's claim for the division of labor: concepts without intuitions have form but nothing to be concepts *of*; intuitions without concepts are given but are nothing *to* anyone — cognition requires both stems, neither reducible to the other ("the understanding can intuit nothing, the senses can think nothing"). Each predecessor is thus half of Kant's architecture, and their characteristic errors are the two ways to have one faculty impersonate the other.
