# Study Guide: John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689/90) — the learning-path selections

**Work:** *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* · **Author:** John Locke (1632–1704) · **First published:** December 1689 (title page dated 1690) · **What this guide covers:** the selections the [learning path](../learning-path.md) assigns in Stage 3 — Book I as a summary pass, Book II in depth (above all chapters i–ii, viii, xii, xxi, and xxvii) — with argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings. Book III and Book IV are mapped in outline only, since the path does not assign them; §1.9 explains what they contain and when to go back for them.

## About the work

Locke worked on the *Essay* for nearly twenty years — a first draft dates from 1671 — and published it at the end of 1689, in the same extraordinary season as the *Two Treatises of Government* and the *Letter Concerning Toleration*. He issued four editions in his lifetime, revising as he went; two revisions matter for this guide. Chapter II.xxi, "Of Power," was substantially rewritten for the second edition (1694), which introduced the doctrine that *uneasiness*, not the judged greater good, determines the will. And II.xxvii, "Of Identity and Diversity" — the chapter that founded the modern debate on personal identity — did not exist in the first edition at all: Locke added it in 1694 at the urging of his friend William Molyneux.

The book's stated aim is modest and radical at once: "to enquire into the original, certainty, and extent of human knowledge; together with the grounds and degrees of belief, opinion, and assent." In the Epistle to the Reader Locke casts himself as an "under-labourer" clearing ground for master-builders such as Boyle and Newton — but the clearing is a demolition project. If we know exactly where our ideas come from and how far our understanding reaches, whole departments of scholastic dispute will turn out to be noise: words used without ideas, questions our faculties cannot settle. Book I removes the rival account of the mind's furniture (innate ideas). Book II gives the positive account: every idea derives from sensation or reflection, and complex ideas are built from simple ones. Book III examines the words we think with and the abuses they invite. Book IV draws the map of knowledge and probability that the first three books have made possible.

The learning path reads the *Essay* alongside the *Second Treatise* (chapters 2, 5, and 7–9, in Laslett's Cambridge Texts edition), and the pairing is deliberate: the *Essay*'s audit of ideas is Locke's epistemological ground-clearing, the *Treatise*'s account of nature, property, and consent is his political one. This guide covers the *Essay* only.

The *Essay*'s afterlife is part of its content for a reader on the path. It defined the terms of British philosophy for a century: Berkeley radicalized its way of ideas from within, Hume announced he was completing its program, and Leibniz thought it worth a book-length reply (the *New Essays*, written 1704, published 1765), answering Locke chapter by chapter from the rationalist side. Where the walkthrough below flags a debate — the veined marble against the white paper, Berkeley against the primary/secondary line, Butler and Reid against II.xxvii — those debates are the book still working, and the path's Stage 3 sequence (Locke, then Berkeley, then Hume) is arranged so that you read the *Essay* as its two greatest critics read it: as the position to beat.

## How to use this guide

Read each assigned chapter of the *Essay* first, then the corresponding entry below. The argument reconstructions in §2 — the primary/secondary quality distinction and the consciousness theory of personal identity — are the two arguments to master thoroughly; the anti-innatism argument is reconstructed briefly at §2(a) because Book I is only a summary pass in the path. Work the checkpoint questions at the end of each session, covering the answers (collected in §5) until you have attempted them.

Locke's prose is plain by seventeenth-century standards but his sentences are long and his terminology exact. When he capitalizes a distinction — idea, quality, power, person — he means something technical by it. Where scholars disagree about what Locke meant (and on personal identity and the resemblance thesis they disagree vigorously), this guide says so rather than settling the question for you.

One practical note on references: the *Essay* is cited by book, chapter, and section — II.viii.9 means Book II, chapter viii, §9 — and the sectioning is identical in every modern edition, so citations in this guide, in the scholarship, and in Berkeley's and Hume's replies all land in the same place regardless of the copy on your desk. Locke's sections are usually short, sometimes a single sentence; read whole chapters rather than isolated sections, because his qualifications routinely arrive three sections after the claim they qualify.

## 1. Chapter-by-chapter guide

### 1.1 Book I: against innate ideas (summary pass)

**Summary.** Book I argues that no ideas or principles are innate — neither speculative maxims (the principle of non-contradiction) nor practical ones (moral rules). The master argument is from universal assent: if a principle were stamped on every mind, everyone would assent to it once they understood the terms; but children and people with severe cognitive impairments assent to no maxims at all, and whole peoples violate supposedly innate moral rules without remorse. Principles arrived at only after teaching and reasoning are acquired, not found ready-made. Chapter iv adds that the idea of God is not innate either: travelers report peoples with no idea of God, and conceptions of God vary wildly where they exist.

**Key passages.** I.ii.1–5 (the universal-assent argument); I.ii.15–25 (maxims known only when reason matures — and the emptiness of calling that "innate"); I.iii.1–9 (no innate practical principles; moral diversity); I.iv.8–15 (the idea of God not innate).

**Main argument.** Book I is ground-clearing, and the path treats it as such. What matters for everything after is the dilemma Locke builds for the innatist, reconstructed at §2(a): either "innate" means consciously assented to (false) or it means "assented to when reason matures" (in which case everything reason can discover is innate, and the doctrine says nothing). Leibniz's *New Essays* — the point-by-point reply, published posthumously in 1765 — is the standing objection, and Locke's entry in this library gives it in full.

### 1.2 II.i: the two fountains of ideas

**Summary.** Book II opens with the *Essay*'s most famous sentence: "Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas; how comes it to be furnished?" The answer: "in one word, from EXPERIENCE." All ideas come from two sources. **Sensation** supplies ideas of external objects — yellow, heat, solidity, motion — through the senses. **Reflection** supplies ideas of the mind's own operations — perceiving, thinking, doubting, believing, willing — as the mind notices what it is doing. External objects furnish the mind with sensible qualities; the mind furnishes the understanding with ideas of its own operations. Every other chapter of the *Essay* is an audit conducted against this inventory.

**Key passages.** II.i.2 (white paper and the answer "from EXPERIENCE"); II.i.3–5 (sensation and reflection defined); II.i.23–25 (reflection comes later than sensation — children notice their own operations only after the senses have stocked the mind).

**Main argument.** Note two things a quick read misses. First, "white paper" denies innate *ideas*, not innate *faculties*: perceiving, retaining, comparing, and abstracting are native equipment, a point the entry in this library stresses and §4.1 returns to. Second, reflection is itself experience — experience turned inward — so Locke's empiricism is not the claim that all knowledge concerns the external world. The mind's own operations are observed, not intuited from an innate stock.

### 1.3 II.ii: simple and complex ideas

**Summary.** Locke divides ideas by their construction. **Simple ideas** — of one sense (color, sound), of more than one sense (space, figure, motion), of reflection (thinking, willing), or of both (pleasure, pain, power, existence, unity) — are received passively: "the mind is wholly passive" in receiving them, and cannot invent a new one. (No one can imagine a color they have never seen, any more than they can will themselves a new sense.) **Complex ideas** are made by the mind itself, voluntarily combining simple ones — the subject of II.xii. The simple/complex distinction is Locke's analytical engine: to test any grand concept (substance, cause, infinity, God), trace it back to its simple ingredients and see whether the inventory covers it.

**Key passages.** II.ii.1–2 (the four classes of simple idea; the mind's passivity); II.iii–vii (the simple ideas catalogued: space, duration, number, infinity, pleasure and pain).

**Main argument.** The passivity of simple reception is what makes the audit binding. If the mind could generate simple ideas from itself, experience would not be the measure of our concepts; because it cannot, a concept with no pedigree in sensation or reflection is counterfeit. Hume's copy principle is Locke's audit sharpened, and Hume will run it against Locke himself — on substance, on the self, on necessary connexion.

### 1.4 II.viii: primary and secondary qualities

**Summary.** This is the *Essay*'s most consequential chapter, and the one Berkeley will attack. Locke distinguishes **qualities** (powers in objects to produce ideas in us) from the **ideas** they produce, then divides qualities into two kinds. **Primary qualities** — solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, and number — are "utterly inseparable from the body," really in objects whether perceived or not, and our ideas of them *resemble* them: the idea of a figure is like the figure. **Secondary qualities** — colors, sounds, odors, tastes — are "nothing in the objects themselves but powers to produce various sensations in us" by the bulk, figure, texture, and motion of the objects' insensible parts; our ideas of them resemble nothing in the object. The exhibits: porphyry has no color in the dark, though nothing in the stone has changed; pound an almond and its color and taste change while only the texture of its parts has altered; the same water feels warm to one hand and cold to the other, so the warmth and cold as felt cannot both be in the water as they appear.

**Key passages.** II.viii.8 (the primary list and inseparability); II.viii.9–10 (secondary qualities as powers grounded in the insensible parts); II.viii.15 (the resemblance thesis stated); II.viii.16–21 (porphyry, the almond, the two hands in water).

**Main argument.** The distinction is Locke's peace treaty between common sense and the new mechanical science of Boyle and Newton: the world science describes (particles, textures, motions) is the real one, and the colors and tastes of experience are its effects on us — real effects, lawfully produced, but not resemblances. Note carefully what Locke does **not** say: he does not say secondary qualities are unreal or illusions. The fire's power to produce the idea of warmth is as real as its shape; what is denied is that the felt warmth pictures anything in the fire. Berkeley's attack (in his entry in this library) presses exactly here: figure and extension vary with the perceiver's position just as color does, so the resemblance line cannot be drawn where Locke draws it. The full reconstruction is at §2(b).

### 1.5 II.xii (and II.xxiii): complex ideas and substance

**Summary.** The mind builds complex ideas in three acts: **combining** simple ideas into one (a unicorn, a statue), **relating** two ideas by comparison (cause and effect, identity), and **abstracting** — separating an idea from the circumstances of time, place, and other ideas, which is how general ideas arise. Complex ideas sort into **modes** (combinations that do not subsist by themselves: gratitude, murder, a triangle), **substances** (collections of qualities observed to go together, plus a supposed substratum), and **relations**. II.xxiii takes up the hard case: why do we collect the qualities of gold under one substance? Because we suppose "something" in which they inhere — a substratum whose nature we do not know. Of material substance in general, Locke confesses, our idea is of "something, I know not what," supposed as the support of qualities.

**Key passages.** II.xii.1–6 (combining, relating, abstracting); II.xxiii.1–3 (the idea of substance as supposed support); II.xxiii.15 (we have no clear idea of substance in general apart from its qualities).

**Main argument.** II.xxiii is the loose thread Berkeley and Hume will pull. Locke is honest that the substratum is a *supposition* — we cannot conceive qualities floating free, so we postulate a bearer — but his own audit asks what idea corresponds to the word, and the answer (an obscure relative idea of "what supports these") is uncomfortably thin. Berkeley keeps spiritual substance and discards material substance as licensing nothing; Hume discards both. Watch Locke concede the obscurity rather than pretend otherwise: it is his empiricism disciplining his own system.

### 1.6 II.xxi: of power — liberty, uneasiness, and suspension

**Summary.** The longest chapter in the *Essay* (73 sections) and the one Locke rewrote most heavily. It begins with the idea of **power** itself (II.xxi.1–7): we get the idea of active and passive power from observing changes — fire melting wax, the sun bleaching it — and from the will's command over our own thoughts and limbs. Locke then relocates the free-will question. Freedom belongs not to the *will* but to the *agent*: "liberty" is a power of a person to do or forbear an action according to the mind's determination, and asking whether the will itself is free is, he argues, as improper as asking whether a man's sleep is swift or his virtue square (§§8–21). A man is free where he can act on his preference; he is not free where the action is not in his power (the man locked in a room stays voluntarily but not freely, §10).

The second half rides to the chapter's second-edition revolution. What determines the will to one action rather than another? Not, as the first edition had said, the greater good as judged by the understanding — for we routinely fail to pursue goods we judge greatest (the drunkard knows his ruin and drinks). What determines the will is **uneasiness**: a present disquiet — at the absence of some good, at pain, at desire — which the will acts to relieve (§§29–47). But we are not slaves to the strongest uneasiness, because the mind has a power the chapter makes central in its later sections: to **suspend** the prosecution of a desire, examine it, and judge before willing (§47, §52). In this suspension "lies the source of all liberty": we can hold our desires up to the light of our considered judgment of lasting happiness, and the last judgment of the understanding, after due examination, is what a free agent follows.

**Key passages.** II.xxi.4–7 (the idea of power from change and volition); II.xxi.8–10 (liberty belongs to the agent; the locked room); II.xxi.21 (freedom defined as acting or forbearing according to the mind's determination); II.xxi.29–40 (the greater good does not determine the will; the drunkard); II.xxi.47 (uneasiness determines the will — the second edition's new doctrine); II.xxi.52 (suspension: "the source of all liberty").

**Main argument.** Read the chapter as an empiricist's compatibilism avant la lettre. Locke does not deny that volitions have causes; he denies that causation by motives is what unfreedom means. Freedom is the agent's power to act on his own determinations and — the second edition's deepening — to suspend determination itself until judgment has examined the desire. Hume's Section VIII of the *Enquiry* (in this library's guide) takes up the same question with the same verdict by a different road, and the two chapters reward being read against each other.

### 1.7 II.xxvii: of identity and diversity — personal identity

**Summary.** The chapter Locke added in 1694 distinguishes three questions usually run together: sameness of **substance**, of **man** (the living organism), and of **person**. A person is "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places; which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable from thinking" (§9). Personal identity consists in sameness of **consciousness**: I am the same person as the doer of a past action insofar as my consciousness reaches back to it — I remember it from the inside, as mine. The consequences are drawn fearlessly. If the consciousness of a prince, carrying all his memories, entered the body of a cobbler, the cobbler-bodied being would *be* the prince (§15). If Socrates waking and Socrates sleeping share no consciousness, they are not the same person (§19). And if I am conscious of a past action, I am accountable for it; if not, I am no more its author than if it had been done by another — for "person" is "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit" (§26). On the day of judgment, Locke adds, no one shall be made to answer for what he knows nothing of (§22) — a theological answer to the obvious worry about forgotten sins.

**Key passages.** II.xxvii.9 (person defined; identity by consciousness); II.xxvii.13–16 (same man, same substance, same person distinguished; the prince and the cobbler); II.xxvii.19–20 (Socrates waking and sleeping; the sober and drunk man); II.xxvii.22 (the great day: no one accountable for what he is not conscious of); II.xxvii.26 ("person" as a forensic term).

**Main argument.** The chapter's power is its separations. Because identity of person is constituted by consciousness rather than by body or soul-substance, the questions "same organism?", "same soul?", and "same person?" can receive different answers — and it is the third, Locke argues, that praise, blame, punishment, and reward actually track. The objections were immediate and have never gone away: Butler's charge of circularity (consciousness of identity presupposes the identity it is meant to constitute) and Reid's brave-officer case (memory chains violate transitivity). A full reconstruction, with both objections, is at §2(c).

### 1.8 The rest of Book II, and Book III, in outline

The path does not assign the remaining chapters of Book II, but know what is in them, because later figures raid them: simple ideas of space, duration, and number (II.xiii–xvii); the origin of our idea of infinity by endless addition (II.xvii); ideas of pleasure and pain, the springs of the passions (II.xx–xxi); the long catalogue of modes (II.xviii–xxii, xxiv–xxxi) whose notable resident is II.xxviii, on moral good and evil and the three laws — divine, civil, and the law of opinion or reputation — by which actions are judged. Book III, "Of Words," argues that words are signs of ideas in the mind of the speaker, not of things; that general words get their generality by abstraction; and that language's imperfections and abuses (III.ix–xi) — words without ideas, unsteady signification, affected obscurity — are the proximate cause of most philosophical dispute. It is the *Essay*'s built-in warning label, and Wittgenstein's readers will find its spirit familiar.

### 1.9 Book IV in outline (beyond the path's assignment)

Book IV is where the *Essay* cashes out. **Knowledge** is defined (IV.i) as "the perception of the connexion and agreement, or disagreement and repugnancy of any of our ideas," under four heads: identity or diversity, relation, coexistence or necessary connexion, and real existence. It comes in degrees (IV.ii): **intuitive** (the mind perceives agreement immediately, as that white is not black), **demonstrative** (agreement perceived through a chain of proofs, as in mathematics), and **sensitive** (the existence of external things present to the senses — real, Locke insists in IV.xi, but the least certain of the three). Where knowledge runs out, **judgment** and **probability** govern assent (IV.xiv–xvi), and Locke's rule is the proportionality rule Hume will inherit: assent should be proportioned to the grounds of probability. The book closes by policing boundaries: reason and faith are distinct provinces (IV.xviii), faith cannot override clear knowledge, and "enthusiasm" — ungrounded persuasion mistaking itself for revelation — is diagnosed as a failure of the proportionality rule (IV.xix). The path leaves Book IV unassigned, but its definitions are the ones Hume's fork refines and Kant's *Critique* sets out to answer; read IV.i–ii and IV.xiv–xvi first if you return.

## 2. Argument reconstructions

### (a) The argument against innate ideas (Book I)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. A truth counts as innate only if it is in the mind before experience furnishes it — and the natural mark of such a truth is universal assent: everyone assents to it, at least once the terms are understood.
2. No speculative principle commands universal assent: children and people with severe cognitive impairments assent to no maxims at all, not even "whatsoever is, is" or the principle of non-contradiction.
3. No practical (moral) principle commands universal assent either: whole peoples break supposed innate rules — justice, keeping faith — without remorse or surprise.
4. If the innatist retreats to "assented to when reason matures," then every truth reason can ever discover counts as innate, and the doctrine no longer distinguishes a special inborn stock from ordinary learning.
5. Therefore, no ideas or principles are innate: the mind is furnished by experience, through sensation and reflection.

**Conclusion.** Innatism is either false (on the assent criterion) or empty (on the maturity criterion). Leibniz's dispositional innatism — innate tendencies, like veins in marble predisposing it to one figure — is the sophisticated reply; whether it escapes Locke's dilemma or concedes premise 4 is the standing debate (see §4.6).

### (b) The primary/secondary quality distinction (II.viii)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. Qualities are powers in bodies to produce ideas in us; the idea produced must be distinguished from the quality that produces it.
2. Some qualities are inseparable from body as such: divide a grain of wheat as fine as you like, each part still has solidity, extension, figure, and mobility. These are the **primary qualities**.
3. Our ideas of primary qualities resemble the qualities themselves: the idea of a shape is like the shape.
4. Other qualities — colors, sounds, tastes, smells — vary with conditions in ways their ideas cannot track as resemblances: porphyry shows no color in the dark though the stone is unchanged; pounding an almond changes only the texture of its parts yet changes its color and taste; the same water feels warm to one hand and cold to the other, and cannot be both warm and cold in the way it is felt.
5. What such variation shows is that these **secondary qualities** are powers, grounded in the primary qualities of the object's insensible parts, to produce ideas in perceivers; the ideas resemble nothing in the object.
6. Therefore, primary qualities are real features of bodies as they are in themselves, of which our ideas are resemblances; secondary qualities are real powers in bodies whose produced ideas are not resemblances.

**Conclusion.** The mechanical world of particles and textures is the world as it is; the colored, sounding, tasting world is how that world affects us — real as effect, unreliable as portrait.

**One standard objection.** Berkeley's: the line cannot be held. Extension, figure, and motion vary with the perceiver's position, distance, and condition exactly as color does — a coin looks round from above, elliptical from the side; motion looks swift or slow relative to the observer. If variability disqualifies color from resembling anything in the object, it disqualifies figure and motion too; and if all our ideas are mind-dependent, the postulated material substratum behind them is an idea Locke's own principles cannot supply. The distinction collapses, and with it material substance.

**One reply.** Locke's defender can separate two claims Berkeley runs together: that our ideas of primary qualities are *exactly* like the qualities (which Locke need not hold in every detail) and that primary qualities belong to bodies whether perceived or not while secondary qualities, as felt, do not. Variability of appearance is compatible with a stable underlying magnitude — the elliptical look is itself lawfully produced by a round coin — whereas warmth felt by two hands at once cannot locate a single felt temperature in the water at all. Whether this saves the resemblance thesis, or only a weaker causal-realist version of it, is debated; the distinction's influence (it frames the science/appearance contrast for two centuries) is not.

### (c) Personal identity as sameness of consciousness (II.xxvii)

**The argument, in numbered premises:**

1. Identity is always relative to a kind: to ask whether A and B are "the same" we must ask the same *what* — substance, man (organism), or person.
2. A person is a thinking being that can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, at different times and places.
3. What makes a present person the same person as a past one is consciousness: the present self's capacity to extend itself backward to a past experience or action, remembering it from the inside as its own.
4. Consciousness can, in principle, diverge from both body and substance: the prince's consciousness in the cobbler's body would make the cobbler's body house the prince; one body and one soul with disjoint consciousnesses (Socrates waking and sleeping) would house two persons.
5. "Person" is a forensic term: it is the person, not the organism or the soul considered apart from consciousness, that praise, blame, punishment, and reward properly attach to.
6. Therefore, personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, and can diverge from sameness of body and sameness of soul-substance alike.

**Conclusion.** I am accountable for exactly those past actions my consciousness owns — no more (forgotten deeds are not mine in the forensic sense) and no less (a different body or an undetected soul-swap would not excuse me).

**One standard objection.** Butler and Reid, in different registers. Butler: consciousness of personal identity *presupposes* personal identity — genuine memory already distinguishes my actually experiencing the deed from merely seeming to; the criterion is circular. Reid: memory is not transitive in the way identity must be. The boy was flogged at school; the young officer remembers the flogging; the old general remembers the officer's deeds but not the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is the officer, the officer is the boy, yet the general is not the boy — a contradiction, since identity is transitive.

**One reply.** The standard Lockean repair — developed by later writers rather than by Locke himself — is to distinguish *direct* memory connection from psychological *continuity*: the general is the boy because a chain of overlapping memory connections links them, even though no single memory spans the distance; transitivity is restored by the chain. Against Butler, defenders argue that quasi-memory — apparent memory that does not presuppose the rememberer's identity with the agent — can be defined without circularity. Both repairs move some distance from the letter of II.xxvii, and whether they save Locke's theory or replace it is itself debated — appropriately, since II.xxvii's achievement was to set the problem every successor must answer.

## 3. Checkpoint questions

Attempt these before reading the answers in §5.

1. What mark does Locke use in Book I to test whether a principle is innate, and why does the case of children matter to the test?
2. Locke says that if "innate" means "assented to when reason matures," the doctrine becomes empty. Why?
3. State the two sources of ideas in II.i, and explain why reflection counts as experience rather than as an innate stock.
4. What is a simple idea, and why does the mind's passivity in receiving simple ideas matter to Locke's audit of concepts?
5. List the primary qualities, and state the resemblance thesis Locke asserts for them.
6. Give two of Locke's exhibits for treating colors as powers rather than resemblances (name the porphyry, the almond, or the water), and say what each is meant to show.
7. What does Locke mean by calling the fire's warmth-producing character a "power," and why does denying resemblance not make secondary qualities unreal?
8. What is the substratum in II.xxiii, and why is Locke's account of it ("something, I know not what") a problem for his own system?
9. In II.xxi, why does Locke say the question "is the will free?" is improperly posed? What does liberty properly belong to?
10. What determines the will, on the second edition's doctrine, and what evidence does Locke give against the view that the judged greater good determines it?
11. What is the power of suspension in II.xxi, and why does Locke locate liberty in it?
12. In II.xxvii, distinguish sameness of substance, of man, and of person. Which does personal identity consist in?
13. Work the prince-and-cobbler case: who is the person in the cobbler's body, and what does the case show about body and soul criteria?
14. Why does Locke call "person" a forensic term, and what follows for a defendant who genuinely cannot remember his crime?

## 4. Common misreadings

1. **"Tabula rasa is Locke's term and his doctrine."** The phrase is later shorthand. Locke's image is "white paper" (II.i.2), and his claim is that there are no innate *ideas or principles* — not that the mind has no innate faculties, structure, or temperament. Perception, memory, abstraction, and natural inclinations are native equipment; what is denied is ready-made knowledge.
2. **"Book I refutes innatism once and for all."** Locke refutes the crude versions — innate conscious maxims — with the assent argument. Leibniz's dispositional innatism (innate tendencies and inclinations, the veined marble) is a genuine development designed to survive the dilemma, and the Locke–Leibniz dispute is a live exchange, not a knockout.
3. **"Secondary qualities are illusions, unreal, 'in the mind' in Berkeley's sense."** For Locke, secondary qualities are *powers in the object*, grounded in its real microstructure — as real as its shape. Only the *resemblance* claim fails: the felt color does not picture anything in the object. Berkeley's further step — treating primary qualities the same way and concluding both are ideas — is Berkeley's argument against Locke, not Locke's position.
4. **"Locke is a naive realist who thinks ideas copy the world."** Only primary-quality ideas are said to resemble their causes; the whole point of II.viii is that most of experience does not resemble the world that produces it. And Locke's talk of "ideas" as the immediate objects of thought is itself the feature Berkeley and later critics target as a veil between mind and world — a debated feature, not a naive transparency.
5. **"Personal identity for Locke is identity of soul."** It is the reverse: II.xxvii's founding move is that sameness of person can diverge from sameness of immaterial substance. A soul whose consciousness did not reach my deeds would not make those deeds mine; consciousness, not substance, is the criterion.
6. **"Locke's memory criterion means you are whoever you seem to remember being."** False and implanted memories are the standard modern pressure on any memory theory, and Locke's own text requires consciousness of having *done* the action, not mere seeming; his defenders distinguish genuine from apparent memory — which is exactly where Butler's circularity charge bites, and why the debate he started is unfinished rather than silly.
7. **"Book IV is where the real philosophy is, so Books I–III are throat-clearing."** The path inverts the priority for good reason: Book IV's definitions of knowledge and probability presuppose the audit of ideas, and the assigned chapters are where Locke's originality (qualities, power, identity) actually lives. Book IV matters — §1.9 maps it — but it is the capstone, not the foundation.
8. **"Locke's *Essay* and *Second Treatise* are separate projects."** The *Essay*'s standards — trace concepts to their experiential source, distrust words without ideas — are the same standards the *Treatise* applies to "paternal power," "conquest," and "divine right." Reading the path's two Locke assignments as one project (audit, then build) is the intended experience.

## 5. Answers to checkpoint questions

1. Universal assent: an innate principle would be assented to by everyone, at least once understood. Children assent to no principles at all — not even the law of non-contradiction — so no principle meets the test; assent that arrives only after teaching and reasoning is acquired in the ordinary way.
2. If every truth becomes "innate" merely because reason can eventually assent to it, then all discoverable truths are innate and the term marks nothing special. The innatist wanted a distinctive stock of God-given principles needing no experience; the maturity criterion makes innateness indistinguishable from the general capacity to learn, which Locke never denies.
3. Sensation (ideas of external objects — color, solidity, motion) and reflection (ideas of the mind's own operations — perceiving, doubting, willing). Reflection is experience because it is the mind's *notice*, acquired over time, of operations it performs; children have sensations long before they reflect. It is observation turned inward, not a pre-installed inventory.
4. A simple idea is an uncompounded perception the mind receives passively and cannot invent (a color never seen cannot be imagined). The passivity matters because it makes experience the binding source of conceptual materials: a purported concept that cannot be decomposed into received simple ideas is counterfeit — the audit Hume will sharpen into the copy principle.
5. Solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, and number (II.viii.8–9). The resemblance thesis: our ideas of them resemble the qualities as they exist in bodies; the idea of a figure is like the figure (II.viii.15).
6. Porphyry in the dark shows no color, though the stone is unchanged — so the color as perceived is not constantly in the stone as it appears. The pounded almond changes color and taste while only the texture of its insensible parts has changed — so the color and taste track the perceiver-affecting arrangement, not a resembling quality. The water that feels warm to one hand and cold to the other cannot be both as felt — so felt warmth and cold are ideas produced in us, not resemblances in the water.
7. A power is a capacity in a body, grounded in the primary qualities of its insensible parts, to produce an idea in a perceiver. Denying resemblance denies only that the produced idea *pictures* its cause; the power itself is a real, law-governed feature of the fire, as real as its shape. Secondary qualities are unreal as portraits, real as powers.
8. The substratum is the supposed support in which a substance's qualities inhere — the "something, I know not what" beneath the observed collection (II.xxiii). The problem: Locke's own audit demands an idea for every legitimate concept, and the idea of substratum turns out to be an obscure supposition rather than a traced experiential content. Berkeley (discarding material substance) and Hume (discarding spiritual substance too) both exploit the concession.
9. Because "liberty" names a power, and powers belong to agents, not to faculties of agents. Asking whether the *will* is free treats the will as if it were itself an agent who wills — Locke compares it to asking whether sleep is swift or virtue square. Liberty properly belongs to the person: a man is free insofar as he can do or forbear an action according to his will's determination.
10. Uneasiness — a present disquiet at pain or at the absence of an enjoyed good (§47). The evidence against the greater-good view is ordinary weakness of will: the drunkard judges sobriety the greater good, knows drink is ruining his health and prospects, and drinks anyway; what moves him is the uneasiness of craving and company, not his judgment of the greatest good.
11. The mind can suspend the execution and satisfaction of a desire — hold it back from determining the will — while reason examines its true weight against lasting happiness (§52). Locke locates liberty here because suspension is what makes our volitions answerable to considered judgment rather than to whichever uneasiness happens to be strongest.
12. Sameness of substance: the same immaterial soul-stuff (unknowable by us, and irrelevant, Locke argues). Sameness of man: the same living organism, continuous life. Sameness of person: sameness of consciousness — the capacity to own past experiences and actions by remembering them from the inside. Personal identity consists in the third alone.
13. The prince: the being in the cobbler's body, with the prince's consciousness, *is* the prince — he would be accountable for the prince's deeds, not the cobbler's. The case shows that neither bodily continuity nor (since we could suppose the same soul uninvolved) soul-substance fixes the person; consciousness does.
14. Because "person" is the term by which actions and their merit are appropriated — the unit of accountability in law and morals (II.xxvii.26). It follows that a defendant with genuine, total amnesia for the crime is not, by Locke's criterion, the same person as the criminal, and punishing him for the deed is as unjust as punishing another man — the conclusion Locke embraces, while noting (II.xxvii.22) that human courts, unable to distinguish genuine from feigned forgetfulness, must proceed on evidence, and the final judgment will not.
