# Study Guide: Sartre's *Existentialism Is a Humanism* and *Being and Nothingness* (selections)

**Work:** *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (lecture delivered 1945, published 1946) and *Being and Nothingness* (1943), in selections · **Author:** Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) · **Covers:** The lecture in full; and from *Being and Nothingness*, the Introduction, Part I, Chapter 1 (the origin of negation) and Chapter 2 (bad faith), Part II, Chapter 1 in brief and Chapter 3 (transcendence), Part III, Chapter 1 (the Look), and Part IV, Chapters 1–2 (freedom and situation) — with a reading plan, section-by-section guide, reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Jean-Paul Sartre](../eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Start with the lecture. *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (*L'existentialisme est un humanisme*) was delivered on 29 October 1945 to a packed Club Maintenant audience in Paris and published in 1946. Read it in Carol Macomber's translation (Yale University Press, 2007), the current standard English edition and the one the learning path names; Philip Mairet's older translation (1948) circulates as *Existentialism and Humanism*, but its wording differs at famous points, so do not mix the two mid-argument. The lecture takes one sitting — its virtue and its danger.

Its contested status in Sartre's own eyes must be flagged honestly: it was a popularization, a public defense against critics already using "existentialism" as a term of abuse. Sartre later distanced himself from it as oversimplified, with partial regret that a compressed lecture had become the public statement of his philosophy. He did not retract its central claims; but read it as he came to read it — a map drawn quickly for strangers, accurate in outline, flattening in detail. Where lecture and treatise diverge in emphasis — above all on freedom — the treatise governs.

For *Being and Nothingness* (*L'être et le néant*, 1943), this guide uses Hazel Barnes's translation (Philosophical Library, 1956, and its reissues), in selections only; navigate by part and chapter, not page. One qualification, following the learning path: Barnes is flawed in places, and Sarah Richmond's translation (Routledge, 2018) is generally preferred for new work; every selection here is findable by the same references in Richmond.

A note on quotations: the short phrases quoted are Sartre's famous formulations in the standard translations. Everything else is paraphrase, so your reconstruction work is in your own words.

## How to use this guide

The two works fail readers in opposite ways. The lecture is too easy: readers finish it believing Sartre said anyone can become anything by deciding to. *Being and Nothingness* is too hard: seven hundred pages in which vivid examples — a waiter, a keyhole, an absent friend — sit inside an apparatus that can swallow them. Use each to correct the other: the lecture supplies the theses, the treatise the qualifications.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching this library's reading rule:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a section, read its part of this guide. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each section, write the main argument in numbered premises, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

## Reading plan

The lecture runs to roughly fifty pages; the *Being and Nothingness* selections to roughly two hundred. At two hours of primary reading per week, the program takes about seven weeks.

## Section-by-section guide

### The lecture — Existence precedes essence

The central thesis is stated through a manufactured object. For a paper knife, essence precedes existence: the artisan conceives what the thing is — its purpose and form — before making it. Traditional pictures of the human being copy that order: if God creates humanity, he creates it according to a conception in the divine mind; secular versions keep the structure while dropping God, positing a human nature each individual instantiates. Atheistic existentialism reverses the order: existence precedes essence. The human being first exists, thrown into the world without a prior blueprint, and only then defines himself through what he does. Two qualifications, both in the lecture and both routinely lost: Sartre's summary — that man is nothing else but what he makes of himself — concerns what a life *amounts to*, not whether its materials are chosen (the given materials are facticity, below); and the thesis is existentialism's *first principle*, not its whole content.

**Main argument:** Because there is no creator's blueprint and no given human nature, a person is not an instance of a prior essence but the author of what he becomes — and authorship is responsibility.

### The lecture — The starting point of atheistic existentialism

Sartre presents existentialism as a family, not a sect. He distinguishes Christian existentialists — he names Jaspers and Gabriel Marcel — from atheistic existentialists, among whom he places Heidegger and himself, sharing, he says, the conviction that existence comes before essence, or that one must begin from subjectivity. The atheistic branch takes one further step consistently: if God does not exist, no mind conceives human nature, or the good, in advance; there is at least one being whose existence precedes its essence, and who is therefore what he makes of himself. He invokes the idea he associates with Dostoevsky — that if God does not exist, everything is permitted — and draws from it the opposite of a licence: with no given values and no excuses lodged in a determining nature, nothing outside a person's choices supplies or removes his reasons. Note that Heidegger later resisted the "existentialist" and "humanist" labels (in his *Letter on Humanism*, 1947): treat this taxonomy as Sartre's polemical map of 1945, not a settled classification.

**Main argument:** Atheistic existentialism begins from subjectivity and draws the consequence of God's non-existence consistently: no given nature, no a priori good, and therefore no excuses.

### The lecture — Anguish, abandonment, and despair

These three terms are technical, each narrower than its everyday sense. **Anguish** is the experience of one's own total responsibility in choosing: Sartre's illustration is a commander interpreting ambiguous orders for an attack whose meaning his interpretation will in part create — no precedent decides for him, and he knows it. Anguish is not fear of a danger but dizziness before the fact that the choice, and the values it affirms, are his. **Abandonment** names the consequence of God's non-existence for guidance. Sartre's famous illustration is a student torn between staying in occupied France to care for his mother and leaving to join the Free French abroad: Christian and Kantian principles can each be invoked on both sides; He is abandoned not as helpless, but as having no authority to relieve him of deciding what counts as a reason. **Despair**, the most misread term, is a discipline of action: rely only on what depends on your will and on the probabilities that make action possible, and do not stake yourself on outcomes beyond them

**Main argument:** Anguish is responsibility felt, abandonment is the absence of any authority that could decide in one's place, and despair is the discipline of acting on what depends on one's will without leaning on hope.

### The lecture — "Condemned to be free"

The lecture's most quoted phrase condenses its doctrine of freedom. The human being is condemned to be free: condemned, because he did not create himself and did not choose to be thrown into the world; free, because once thrown into it, he is responsible for everything he does. Freedom here is not a power to obtain what one wants. Even not choosing is a choice — abstention has consequences the abstainer owns — and even obedience is a stance the obedient person maintains. Passion, character, and orders are real, but none functions as an alibi: a passion is what a person sustains, not a force that acts instead of him. *Being and Nothingness* complicates the picture with facticity and situation without withdrawing the claim that the stance taken toward a situation is the agent's own — and whether that claim survives extreme cases (coercion, oppression, the prisoner) is the oldest objection to Sartre, treated as an open debate below.

**Main argument:** Freedom is condemned, not granted: we did not choose to be free, we cannot resign from choosing, and therefore no passion, order, or character can serve as an alibi.

### The lecture — In choosing myself, I choose man

The lecture's boldest and most contested move is its universalizing step. Choosing, Sartre argues, is never merely private: in choosing myself I affirm the value of what I choose — to choose it is to say it is good, and good not merely for me. In fashioning myself I fashion an image of man as he ought to be; in choosing myself, I choose man. Responsibility is therefore total in two directions: for myself, and for a conception of humanity my act legislates by example. It echoes Kant, but without Kant's foundation in a shared rational nature, which Sartre has just denied exists. Critics ask what licenses the inference from "I choose this" to "I choose this for all": is universalization built into the logic of valuing, or an extra moral premise imported into a philosophy that claims none is given in advance? Defenders reply that to value something just is to commend it, not merely to prefer it.

**Main argument:** Every choice affirms the value of what is chosen and thereby legislates an image of man — so responsibility extends beyond the chooser to humanity as he conceives it, on an inference critics continue to contest.

### The lecture — Subjectivity and inter-subjectivity

Against the charge that beginning from subjectivity strands each person in a private world, Sartre gives "subjectivity" two senses and argues the second is contained in the first. In the first sense it is the individual's self-making. In the second it is inter-subjectivity: the world in which a person decides what he and others are, among others doing the same. The bridge is the Cartesian cogito, adopted as existentialism's starting truth: in the *I think* I discover myself — and, Sartre claims, the other too, as a condition of my self-experience rather than an inference from a resembling body; This is the lecture's compressed version of the analysis *Being and Nothingness* carries out in the Look, below — a promissory note rather than a completed proof.

**Main argument:** Subjectivity, properly understood, is already inter-subjectivity: the self that chooses itself discovers the other within its own experience, not as an inference added afterwards.

### The lecture — The criticisms Sartre answers

The lecture's final movement answers named reproaches. **The quietism charge** (pressed by Marxist and Communist critics): existentialism is a philosophy of despair leaving its adherents contemplating their impotence — contemplative and ultimately bourgeois. Sartre's reply turns on despair's technical meaning: a doctrine defining a person by the ensemble of his acts, with man's destiny within himself, is the opposite of quietism; his critics' determinism, which teaches people their situation makes them, is the genuinely quietist position. **The Catholic objections**: that existentialism dwells on the sordid; that removing God removes any ground for values, leaving everything permitted; and that it denies human solidarity by isolating the choosing individual. Sartre replies that beginning from subjectivity is not isolating the subject (see inter-subjectivity, above), and that values are relocated — invented in action rather than inscribed in an intelligible heaven. **The judging-others objection**: if each person invents his values, no one can judge anyone, and existentialism collapses into relativism. Sartre replies that judgment survives in two registers: a choice can be judged to rest on error — on bad faith — and choices that will freedom itself can be preferred. Whether these registers suffice against the sincere oppressor, who may be neither self-deceived nor mistaken about his aims, is a standing difficulty, developed by Beauvoir in *The Ethics of Ambiguity*; treat it as an exposed seam, not a solved problem. **The wider Marxist objection** — that existentialism is subjective idealism, treating material conditions as scenery and dissolving history into individual projects — the lecture largely postpones: Sartre's fuller answer, the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), attempts to house existential freedom inside Marxist accounts of scarcity and structure.

**Main argument:** Against quietism, Sartre pleads action; against the Catholic charges, relocated values and inter-subjectivity; against relativism, judgment by error and by freedom — replies of uneven strength, the weakest of which he spent later work trying to repair.

### *Being and Nothingness* — Being-in-itself and being-for-itself (brief)

The treatise divides being into two regions, and everything in the selections that follow presupposes the distinction. The *in-itself* (*en-soi*) is the being of things — a stone, a table, an inkwell: full, solid, and self-identical, it is what it is, with no gap between itself and itself, no lack, no possibility held in reserve; it simply is, without justification — Sartre stresses its contingency, which *Nausea* had dramatized earlier. The *for-itself* (*pour-soi*) is consciousness, defined by the opposite structure: it is aware of its object and, in the same moment, aware — non-positionally, without taking itself as an object — of itself as aware. It exists at a distance from itself; it is what it is not and is not what it is, in Sartre's deliberately paradoxical formula — its past only as surpassed, its future only as not yet. The for-itself, by contrast, is lack and possibility.

**Main argument:** Being divides into the in-itself, which is fully what it is with no distance from itself, and the for-itself — consciousness — which exists precisely by not coinciding with itself.

### *Being and Nothingness* — Consciousness and nothingness

If the in-itself contains no gaps, where do absence, negation, and possibility come from? Part I, Chapter 1 argues they are brought into the world by the for-itself. Negation is not found among things: a table does not contain the not-being of anything. But a man who enters a café expecting Pierre experiences Pierre's absence as a real, structuring feature of the scene — the café organizes itself as a ground from which Pierre's figure fails to emerge, and every face glanced at is apprehended as not-Pierre. The absence is not a judgment added to a neutral perception; it is lived in the perception, because expectation has nihilated the scene, carved a nothingness into it. To question anything is already to admit the possibility of a negative answer, hence of non-being; and only a being that is a gap in itself could hold what is at a distance against what is not, as imagining and deliberating require. Nothingness is thus introduced into being by the for-itself — the machinery under the lecture's slogans, since a consciousness that can stand back from what it is given as is the root of freedom and anguish alike.

**Main argument:** Nothingness enters the world through consciousness: the for-itself's power to nihilate — visible in Pierre's lived absence — is what makes questioning, imagination, negation, and freedom possible.

### *Being and Nothingness* — Bad faith

Bad faith (*mauvaise foi*), the subject of Part I, Chapter 2, is the characteristic evasion of the structure just described: a person flees anguish by denying one pole of what he is — collapsing the duality of facticity and transcendence (next unit) in whichever direction is convenient. The two famous illustrations are Sartre's own, and should be read as his illustrations — crafted, literary, and, in the second case, carrying the gender assumptions of their moment — not as neutral case reports. Sartre's waiter plays at being a waiter, in his telling, a little too completely: movements a little too precise, identity poured into the role as though a waiter were a waiter in the way an inkwell is an inkwell — a thing whose being is exhausted by its function; the evasion treats himself as in-itself, so that nothing in him exceeds or answers beyond the role. Sartre's woman on a first date runs the evasion the other way: she leaves her hand resting in her companion's hand without noticing it — neither consenting nor resisting — treating her body as a thing that happens to be there while her self, all disembodied esteem, converses above it; she postpones decision, in Sartre's account, by splitting herself into a transcendence that does not descend and a body that does not count. Bad faith is not ordinary lying, and the difference generates the chapter's puzzle, taken up in reconstruction (b): in a lie, deceiver and deceived are two people; in bad faith they are one. It is a project consciousness sustains — metastable, poised between awareness and disavowal.

**Main argument:** Bad faith is the flight from anguish by denying one pole of human reality — playing at being a thing with a fixed nature, like Sartre's waiter, or a pure freedom untouched by one's body and situation, like his woman on a date.

### *Being and Nothingness* — Facticity and transcendence

Part II, Chapter 3 gives the two poles their systematic names. *Facticity* is the given side of the for-itself: my body, my past, my birthplace and class, my position among others, the brute fact that I exist and will die — not chosen, and not undoable; my past is what it is, and no project rewrites it. *Transcendence* — in Sartre's technical sense, not a religious one — is the for-itself's perpetual surpassing of the given toward its possibilities: the given never appears except in the light of a project that gives it meaning and weight. A crag is an obstacle to the climber, an irrelevance to the walker, a livelihood to the guide: its resistance is real, but what it *is for* a life is settled by the project encountering it. Neither pole exists alone. This is the treatise's standing correction to the lecture's slogans: whenever Sartre sounds as though the given does not matter, check whether he means instead that the given does not *decide*.

**Main argument:** A human life is facticity taken up by transcendence: the given is unchosen and unalterable, but its meaning and weight in a life are conferred by the project that surpasses it — and neither pole can be honestly denied.

### *Being and Nothingness* — The Look and being-for-others

Part III, Chapter 1 argues that the Other is not an inference but an encounter, and its instrument is the Look. Sartre's scene: at a keyhole, absorbed in what I am seeing, I am not an object even to myself; I *am* my looking. Then I hear footsteps in the corridor, or a rustle, and suddenly I experience myself as *seen*: I am ashamed. Nothing in my visual field need have changed, and no eyes need actually be present. What has arrived is a new dimension of my being — being-for-others: I am an object in a world not mine, possessed of an outside I live but can never occupy. Shame is his probative case: shame of oneself *before* the Other — an immediate, non-inferential recognition that I am as another sees me, not a conclusion from behavior. The Other is thus encountered as a subject, not one more object in my world: an object could not steal my world, reorient it around a center I cannot reach, and give me an outside. This is the structure *No Exit* (1944) dramatizes — perpetual fixation by others' judgments is hellish as a claim about objectification, not about company — and the same structure also conditions pride, love, and language. Whether the argument proves what it claims is reconstruction (c)'s question.

**Main argument:** In the Look — dramatized by the keyhole and known immediately in shame — I discover myself as an object for a subject I cannot control: being-for-others is an original dimension of my being, and the Other is encountered, not inferred.

### *Being and Nothingness* — Freedom and situation

Part IV, Chapters 1–2 state the doctrine of freedom with the qualifications the lecture omits: freedom is the autonomy of choice, the for-itself's determination of itself by its projects. It is explicitly not the power to obtain what one chooses — Sartre distinguishes choosing from getting, and counts a prisoner, whose stance and meaning remain his own under total constraint of outcome, as free in the first sense and radically unfree in the second. Critics press the example: does freedom of stance, severed from any power to alter one's condition, honor or trivialise oppression? Sartre's later work is in part an attempt to answer them, so record it as his most contested illustration, not a settled intuition. The corrective concept is *situation*: freedom exists only in a situation — place, past, environment, fellow men, and death are Sartre's heads — the joint product of brute givenness and the project that lights it up. An obstacle is an obstacle only for a freedom that wants to pass: the given has a coefficient of adversity, which is real — the rock does resist — yet is revealed as resistance only in the light of an end. Hence Sartre's most demanding conclusions: no limits to freedom except those freedom itself encounters as limits, and responsibility even for the unchosen, insofar as its meaning in one's life is one's own — his example is the conscript who, he controversially argues, has in this sense his war. These are the high-water mark of early Sartre, and the claims his later philosophy and Beauvoir's situated freedom most visibly qualify; do not soften them into a claim that attitude matters — Sartre claims far more, and the interest lies in the excess.

**Main argument:** Freedom is autonomy of choice within a situation it did not make: obstacles resist really, but count as obstacles only in the light of a project — so responsibility extends even to the unchosen, a doctrine Sartre's critics, and later Sartre himself, found hardest to sustain.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction follows the learning path's discipline: premises, conclusion, one objection, one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) Existence precedes essence (the lecture)

**Premises:**

1. For a manufactured object, essence precedes existence: the artisan conceives what the thing is before making it.
2. A created humanity would copy that order: if God existed as creator, human nature would be a conception in his mind, prior to any individual's existence.
3. On the atheistic premise, God does not exist, and there is no other source for a prior human nature — no human nature, because no mind to conceive it.
4. What a person is therefore cannot be read off a prior blueprint; it is settled only by what he does — the ensemble of his acts.

**Conclusion:** Existence precedes essence: a person first exists, without a given nature, and makes himself what he is — and is responsible for what he makes.

**Objection:** The argument seems to deny the obvious: human beings arrive with givens — bodies, temperaments, a species biology — and *Being and Nothingness* itself describes universal structures of the for-itself, which look like an essence re-entering under another name. Beauvoir's related objection, from within existentialism, is that situation precedes choice too asymmetrically for unqualified talk of self-making.

**Reply:** Sartre can distinguish a blueprint from a condition. Facticity supplies materials and resistances, and the for-itself's structures are formal conditions of any self-making; neither supplies the *content* of a life, and it is a content-supplying essence — one that could excuse or pre-script a life — that the thesis denies. Whether the distinction between formal structure and essence can bear the load placed on it remains a genuine interpretive debate, not a settled defense.

### (b) Bad faith as self-deception without a deceiver (Part I, Chapter 2)

**Premises:**

1. In an ordinary lie, the roles are distinct: the deceiver knows the truth, the deceived does not — which is why lying is intelligible.
2. In self-deception, the roles coincide in one consciousness: I must know the truth in order to hide it from myself, and not know it in order to be hidden from — an apparent contradiction.
3. Splitting the mind does not dissolve the paradox. Sartre argues that the Freudian censor, meant to keep repressed material from consciousness, would itself have to discern what to repress — reintroducing exactly the knowing deceiver the split was meant to explain.
4. Consciousness, on Sartre's account, is translucent and self-present in a pre-reflective mode: it is non-positionally aware of its own projects without taking them as objects of explicit knowledge.
5. A person can therefore exploit the duality of facticity and transcendence — attending to one pole, sustaining a project of not-facing what he pre-reflectively lives — in a metastable attitude neither explicit knowledge nor innocent ignorance.

**Conclusion:** Bad faith is possible without a hidden deceiver: a project consciousness sustains against its own pre-reflective awareness, collapsing itself now into its facticity, now into its transcendence.

**Objection:** The paradox may be renamed rather than dissolved: if consciousness is genuinely translucent and aware of the truth it flees, then it knows, and the coexistence of knowing and being deceived still needs explaining; if it is not aware, bad faith collapses into ordinary error. Psychoanalytic critics add that dreams, slips, and symptoms seem to require a real opacity of mind to itself that translucency cannot accommodate.

**Reply:** Sartre's reply rests on the mode distinction: pre-reflective awareness is not propositional knowledge I can state, and bad faith works in the gap between living a truth and thematising it — the gap confirmed whenever someone is the last to know what everyone can see about his own motives. His alternative is existential psychoanalysis, sketched at the end of *Being and Nothingness*: recovering a person's fundamental project from his acts, without positing an unconscious mechanism. Whether it handles the clinical evidence as well as the theory it rejects is still disputed.

### (c) The Look as proof of the Other (Part III, Chapter 1)

**Premises:**

1. Absorbed in a project — Sartre's man at the keyhole — I am not an object to myself: I am my looking, with no outside.
2. Hearing footsteps, or merely taking myself to be seen, transforms the experience at once: I am ashamed — I experience myself as an object in a world organized around another center.
3. Shame of this kind is not an inference. I do not observe a body, reason by analogy that it houses a mind like mine, and then conclude I am seen; the being-seen is given in the shame itself.
4. A mere object could not produce this transformation: objects do not reorient my world around themselves or give me an outside I can live but never occupy. Only a subject — a freedom for which I am an object — does that.

**Conclusion:** The Other's existence is not a probable hypothesis built on analogy but an encounter as certain, in its order, as the cogito: being-for-others is disclosed immediately in shame before the Look.

**Objection:** The experience is fallible: the footsteps may be no one's, the watcher a mannequin or my own mistake, and shame can arise alone, before an internalized audience. If experiences of being seen can be wholly mistaken, what the Look delivers looks less like the certain existence of another mind and more like my standing *possibility* of being an object — a structure of my own consciousness.

**Reply:** Sartre can grant fallibility at one level and deny it at the other: that *this* Look comes from a particular person is probable and corrigible, but the dimension the mistake presupposes — that I am a being who *can* be seen, who has an outside for others — is not. Even shame before an imagined watcher testifies to being-for-others as a structure of my being. Whether that amounts to a proof of other minds, rather than a proof that I am constituted as if for others, remains contested.

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check against the answers at the end.

1. Explain the paper-knife contrast, and state what reversing its order for human beings claims — and does not claim.
2. What distinguishes atheistic from Christian existentialism in Sartre's 1945 taxonomy, and why must the atheistic version deny a given human nature?
3. Define anguish, abandonment, and despair in Sartre's technical senses, and say why none means what it ordinarily means.
4. What does the student's dilemma show, and why can neither a doctrine nor an adviser settle it for him?
5. Explain "condemned to be free": why condemned, why free, and why is not choosing still a choice?
6. State the universalizing move — in choosing myself, I choose man — and identify the step critics most often question.
7. What are the two senses of subjectivity, and how is the cogito meant to connect them?
8. Give the quietism charge and Sartre's reply; then the judging-others objection and his reply. Which reply is weaker, and why?
9. Contrast the in-itself and the for-itself in three respects.
10. How does Pierre's absence in the café show that nothingness is brought into the world by consciousness, not found in things?
11. Describe Sartre's waiter and his woman on a date as illustrations of the two directions of bad faith, and note one caution about using them.
12. What is the paradox of self-deception, why does Sartre think the Freudian censor fails to solve it, and what does his own solution rest on?
13. Define facticity and transcendence, with one example showing why neither can be denied without bad faith.
14. Walk through the keyhole scene: what changes when the footsteps are heard, and why does shame show the Other is encountered rather than inferred?
15. What qualification did Sartre himself later place on the lecture, and how should it affect the weight you give it against *Being and Nothingness*?

## Common misreadings

**1. "Existentialism is gloomy nihilism — nothing matters, so nothing is worth doing."**
Sartre's claim is that values are not given, not that they are absent: they are created in action, and a person is defined by the ensemble of his acts. Anguish, abandonment, and despair are technical terms for responsibility, not a mood.

**2. "'Condemned to be free' is a celebration of liberation."**
It is a sentence before it is a slogan: we did not choose to exist, to be free, or to bear responsibility, and cannot resign from any of them. Freedom is also not success — not getting what one wants — but inescapable authorship of one's stance within a situation.

**3. "Bad faith is ordinary lying, hypocrisy, or just doing a job."**
Bad faith is self-deception about the duality of facticity and transcendence — deceiver and deceived in one consciousness — which is what distinguishes it from lying to others. The waiter is not in bad faith for waiting tables, nor for doing it well; the evasion is *playing at being* a waiter as a thing is its function, so as to disown the freedom exceeding the role.

**4. "Existence precedes essence means I can become anything whatsoever by willing it."**
Sartre denies this in both works: essence is made, but from materials and within a world the agent did not make — body, past, class, others' judgments are facticity, and no project abolishes them. What the thesis denies is that those materials carry their verdict with them.

**5. "Sartre simply denies the unconscious, and the facts of the mind refute him."**
As a settled verdict, this misstates a real debate. Sartre rejects the Freudian unconscious as a mechanism, on the argued ground that the censor reintroduces the paradox of self-deception inside the mind; he replaces it with translucency, pre-reflective awareness, and an existential psychoanalysis reading a life as a fundamental project. Psychoanalytic critics reply that repression, symptoms, and dreams require a mental opacity his model cannot supply, and many contemporary accounts owe something to both sides.

**6. "The Look is about literal eyes, and 'hell is other people' means company is hell."**
The Look requires no eyes: footsteps or the mere sense of exposure can enact it — what matters is being-an-object for a subject, not an optical event. And the *No Exit* line is not misanthropy: the play's dead torture one another by fixing each other in judgment, but the same being-for-others is the condition of pride, love, language, and self-knowledge.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **The paper knife:** for an artefact, the artisan's conception precedes production, so essence precedes existence. **Reversed for human beings,** no prior conception fixes what a person is: he exists first and makes himself by his acts. It **does not claim** his materials are chosen — only that they arrive without a blueprint settling what they mean.
2. **Christian existentialists** (Sartre names Jaspers and Marcel) and **atheistic existentialists** (he names Heidegger and himself) share the starting point that existence precedes essence. The atheistic version must deny a given human nature because such a nature would have to be conceived by a God; with no God, there is no blueprint.
3. **Anguish:** total responsibility in choosing, felt — no order decides for the commander. **Abandonment:** no authority — values, doctrine, adviser — can decide in one's place. **Despair:** a discipline of action, counting only on what depends on your will and on workable probabilities. None is a mood.
4. **The student's dilemma** — stay with his dependent mother or join the Free French — shows abandonment: principles can be marshalled on both sides, any adviser was chosen by him (so consulting one is itself an unadvised choice), and even a sign would need his interpretation.
5. **Condemned,** because we did not create ourselves or choose to be free, and cannot resign; **free,** because we are responsible for everything we do, with no passion, order, or character as an alibi. **Not choosing is a choice** because abstention is a stance with consequences the abstainer owns.
6. **The universalizing move:** choosing myself affirms the value of what I choose and fashions an image of man as he ought to be, so in choosing myself I choose man. **The questioned step** is the inference from valuing-for-myself to legislating-for-all — whether commendation is built into every choice, or a Kantian premise is being imported.
7. **Subjectivity** is first the individual's self-making, second **inter-subjectivity** — the world of others among whom he decides what he and they are. **The cogito connects them:** in discovering myself in the *I think*, Sartre claims, I also discover the other as a condition of my self-experience, not by inference.
8. **Quietism charge:** a philosophy of despair leaves people contemplating impotence. **Reply:** despair is technical, and a doctrine defining man by his acts, with no excuses, is the opposite of quietism. **Judging-others objection:** invented values cannot be judged. **Reply:** choices resting on error or bad faith can be judged, and choices willing freedom preferred. **The second reply is weaker:** it struggles with the sincere, undeceived oppressor — the gap Beauvoir's *Ethics of Ambiguity* tries to fill.
9. **In-itself:** fully what it is, self-identical, no distance from itself; fullness, no possibility; simply is, without self-awareness. **For-itself:** exists by not coinciding with itself, aware of objects and non-positionally of itself; lack and possibility; its past as surpassed, its future as not yet.
10. **Pierre's absence:** I do not perceive a neutral café and then judge him missing; the café organizes itself around his non-presence, each face lived as not-Pierre. Since the in-itself contains no absences, the nothingness structuring the scene is contributed by the expecting consciousness — negation enters the world through the for-itself.
11. **The waiter** plays at being a waiter as a thing is its function, denying his **transcendence**. **The woman on a date**, in Sartre's telling, leaves her hand unnoticed in her companion's and converses as disembodied intellect, denying her **facticity**. **Caution:** both are Sartre's crafted illustrations — the second carrying its period's gender assumptions — not clinical evidence.
12. **The paradox:** self-deception requires one consciousness to know the truth (to hide it) and not know it (to be deceived). **The censor fails** because deciding what to repress requires discerning it — the knowing deceiver reappears inside the mind. **Sartre's solution** rests on pre-reflective, non-positional awareness, which bad faith exploits as a metastable project.
13. **Facticity:** the unchosen given — body, past, birthplace, class, mortality. **Transcendence:** the surpassing of the given toward possibilities, conferring its meaning. **Example:** the rock face is facticity; it is an obstacle only for the climber's project, a livelihood for the guide. Denying its resistance denies transcendence; treating "I am not a climber" as a fixed nature denies facticity.
14. **The keyhole:** absorbed in looking, I am my project, not an object even to myself. **At the footsteps,** I experience myself as seen — ashamed, possessed of an outside fixed by a freedom I cannot control. **Shame shows encounter, not inference:** the being-seen is given in the shame itself.
15. **Sartre's later qualification:** he distanced himself from the lecture as an oversimplification, regretting in part that the compressed public version had become its popular statement. **Effect:** treat the lecture as a map, and let *Being and Nothingness* govern where emphasis diverges, above all on freedom.
