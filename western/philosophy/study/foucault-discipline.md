# Study Guide: Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (selections)

**Work:** *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison*, 1975; Alan Sheridan translation, 1977 · **Author:** Michel Foucault (1926–1984) · **Covers:** Part Three, "Discipline," in full, plus the opening of Part One, Part Two on the reformers, Part Four on the prison and the carceral, and a bridge to *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* — with a reading plan, section-by-section guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Michel Foucault](../eras/05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Read the Alan Sheridan translation, *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison* (1977; first published in French as *Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison*, Gallimard, 1975). Sheridan's is the standard English text, and the vocabulary of the secondary literature — "docile bodies," "normalising judgement," "Panopticism," "the carceral" — follows his renderings. *Surveiller* is to watch over, not merely to watch.

The book has four parts: Part One, "Torture" (public execution and judicial torture); Part Two, "Punishment" (the reformers and their alternative); Part Three, "Discipline" — the theoretical center: docile bodies, the means of correct training, Panopticism; and Part Four, "Prison" (why the prison survived its critics, ending with the "carceral continuum"). This guide covers Part Three in depth, plus the opening of the book and its close.

A note on quotations: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows Sheridan's translation. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires. Historians have checked his claims against the archives; the resulting debates are flagged below.

## How to use this guide

Foucault rarely states a thesis and then defends it in the manner of analytic philosophy. Instead he juxtaposes documents, follows a practice across institutions, and lets a pattern emerge. Hold both together: concrete mechanisms first, general claims about power second, and constant attention to what would count against those claims.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a section, read its part of this guide. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each section, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

## Reading plan

The selections covered here run to roughly 200 pages in the Sheridan translation. At about two hours of primary reading per week, the program below takes about six weeks.

Do not skip week 2 either — without the reformers, discipline looks like the only alternative to torture, when a third option was proposed and lost.

## Section-by-section guide

### 1 — The opening contrast: Damiens and the timetable

The book opens with two documents placed side by side, some eighty years apart, and no commentary at first. The first records the public execution in Paris in 1757 of Robert-François Damiens, who had attempted to assassinate Louis XV: a prolonged, ritualised killing, carried out before a crowd, in which the condemned man's body was made to display the restored power of the offended sovereign. The second document, from Léon Faucher's rules for a house of young prisoners in Paris, dated by Foucault to 1838, contains no violence at all. It is a timetable: rising, work, meals, school, prayer, and sleep allotted exact hours, day after identical day.

Foucault's question is not "which is crueller?" It is: what changed between a punishment that spends itself in spectacular excess on the body and a punishment that economizes, schedules, and trains? His answer is that punishment's *object* and *technology* both changed. The object shifted from the body, on which sovereign vengeance was inscribed, to the "soul" — the offender's habits, character, and future risk, which institutions claim to know and correct. The technology shifted from intermittent, spectacular displays of force to continuous, minute techniques for observing and shaping conduct.

Crucially, Foucault refuses to let humanitarian progress be the whole explanation. He does not deny that reformers were often sincerely horrified by the scaffold, or that the new punishments are in obvious respects less physically terrible. His claim is that "less cruelty" does not explain the *form* the replacement took. A society that merely wished to punish more humanely could have punished less, or more proportionately. What it built instead was a machinery for knowing individuals in detail and training them continuously, reaching far more of life than the executioner ever did. The puzzle is therefore double: why did punishment become gentler in its means, and why did it become more thorough in its grip at the same time?

**Main argument:** Between 1757 and 1838 punishment changed its object from the body to the "soul" and its technology from spectacle to schedule — a transformation humanitarian sentiment alone cannot explain, because the new power punishes less spectacularly while penetrating conduct more continuously and in more detail.

### 2 — Torture, sovereign vengeance, and the risks of the scaffold

To see why the timetable is a departure, you first need the logic of what it replaced, and Foucault reconstructs that logic from within rather than treating torture as mere sadism or backwardness. Under the classical penal order, a crime was not only a harm to a victim; it was an offense against the sovereign, in whose person the law resided. Punishment was therefore a political ritual as much as a legal one: the sovereign, injured by the crime, restored his power publicly on the body of the condemned. Judicial torture also had an epistemic function: confession was the strongest proof, the "queen of proofs," and public execution displayed both the crime's truth and the prince's vengeance in one ceremony.

That ceremony carried structural political risks, and "The spectacle of the scaffold" is largely a catalogue of them. The crowd was not a passive audience; it was a necessary participant, summoned to witness and ratify. But a crowd could refuse its role. It could pity or admire the condemned, turn a smuggler or rioter into a popular hero, resent the executioner, riot to rescue the prisoner, or treat execution day as carnival rather than deterrent. The condemned's last words could invert the whole ceremony, presenting the criminal as the just man and authority as the villain. Foucault's point is analytical, not romantic: a power that must stage itself spectacularly, and depends on the crowd's cooperation to complete the staging, has built instability into its own central ritual. It was also irregular and uneconomical: crimes went unpunished or were punished with wild disproportion, and terror did nothing to sort, train, or reclaim offenders.

One caution: Foucault is describing the *logic* of a penal regime, not claiming every crowd rebelled; historians debate how typical scaffold disorder was (see reconstruction (c)). Nor is he mourning this world — its costs and risks made a different technology attractive.

**Main argument:** Public torture and execution were a political ritual in which the sovereign restored his injured power on the condemned body — but because the ritual required the crowd's participation, it was inherently unstable, irregular, and uneconomical, and those defects helped make a new technology of punishment desirable.

### 3 — The reformers: punishing the soul through representation

Before the prison won, Foucault insists, there was another candidate. The late eighteenth-century reformers — Beccaria is the best-known name, alongside French reforming jurists and legislators — attacked the old system in the name of legality, proportionality, and humanity: fixed laws, public trials, punishments fitted to crimes, and an end to torture and arbitrary royal pardons. But their positive program was more than a softening. The reformers wanted punishment to work on *representation* — on citizens' minds and on the offender's soul rather than the flesh. Punishment was to be a legible sign, a moral semiotics, in which the penalty represented the crime and taught that crime does not pay. Hence the formulation Foucault takes from Mably: punishment should strike the soul rather than the body.

This "gentle way in punishment" had features the prison later displaced. Punishment should be precisely calibrated and publicly legible, so the association between crime and penalty forms in every citizen's imagination; aimed at prevention, not vengeance, and only as severe as deterrence requires; and offenders could be put to useful public work, their labor a visible lesson and reparation. The reformers' model punishments — fines, public works, banishment, calibrated shaming — were mostly *not* long imprisonment. Detention was one tool among many.

Why, then, did the prison become within a few decades the nearly universal form of punishment? Part of Foucault's answer is that the prison borrowed the reformers' language — humanity, correction, prevention — while implementing a different technology, the disciplinary technology perfected in barracks, schools, workshops, and hospitals. The reformers also shared a deeper aim with the administrators who displaced them: not to punish less powerfully, but to punish *better* — more deeply, evenly, and efficiently, with fewer gaps and riots. That shared aim makes the story a history of power, not a morality tale.

**Main argument:** The reformers proposed a punishment of signs and representations aimed at the soul and at prevention — a genuine alternative to both torture and the prison — but their project converged with a disciplinary technology developed elsewhere, which took over their language of correction while implementing continuous training instead.

### 4 — Docile bodies

Part Three opens with the book's most portable concept. A body is *docile*, Foucault writes, when it "may be subjected, used, transformed and improved" — when it can be made both more capable and more obedient by the same operations, so that aptitude and domination increase together rather than trading off. The soldier is his opening example: by the late eighteenth century the soldier is not recognized by innate marks but *made* — posture corrected, movements drilled. Discipline is the set of techniques that performs this making, under four heads.

**The art of distributions** concerns space. Discipline encloses (the boarding school, the barracks, the workshop), partitions space so each individual has a place and each place an individual, creates functional sites where bodies are placed by use and can be supervised, and assigns *rank* — a position in a series or classification — so distribution itself becomes a way of knowing and comparing persons.

**The control of activity** concerns time and movement. Its instruments are the timetable, inherited from monastic communities and spread to schools and factories; the temporal elaboration of the act, in which a gesture is broken down, timed, and prescribed in sequence; the correlation of body and gesture, so posture and efficiency are trained together; the articulation of body and object, as the soldier's movements are fitted to his rifle or the pupil's hand to handwriting; and exhaustive use, extracting ever more time and useful force from the day. The Faucher timetable is this concept, institutionalized.

**The organization of geneses** concerns development over time. Disciplinary time is divided into segments and stages, each with graded exercises and tests at the thresholds; individuals are placed in series by progress, so training becomes a managed evolution — the curriculum, the apprenticeship ladder, the graded drill.

**The composition of forces** concerns combination. Discipline also arranges trained bodies into an efficient machine — a class, a platoon, a production line — in which each body's force is located, signalled, and combined with others under a system of command.

None of these techniques is inherently sinister — a timetable can teach music as well as obedience. Together they constitute a transferable technology for producing individuals at once more productive and more governable.

**Main argument:** Discipline produces "docile bodies" — bodies rendered more capable and more obedient at once — through four families of techniques: distributing bodies in space, controlling their activity in time, organizing their development in graded series, and composing their forces into efficient collective machines.

### 5 — The means of correct training

If docile bodies are the product, this chapter names the instruments. Foucault calls them the "means of correct training," and there are three.

**Hierarchical observation** is surveillance organized as an apparatus: architecture, placement, and relays of observers arranged so conduct is visible and observation is part of how the institution works. The military camp is his model; schools, hospitals, and workshops develop versions in which supervisors are themselves supervised and observation is built into the building.

**Normalizing judgment** is the micro-penalty of discipline. Alongside law and its punishments, institutions run a small penal system of their own, punishing lateness, inattention, incorrect posture, insolence, and failure to reach a standard — offenses against *norms* rather than laws. Its sanctions are corrective rather than expiatory: exercises, repetitions, demotions, small humiliations and privileges. Its master operation is comparison against a norm: measuring gaps, ranking individuals, defining the abnormal as departure from the rule. Foucault's compressed point is that the norm works by homogenising while individualizing — pressing everyone toward the same standard precisely by measuring each person's specific deviation from it.

**The examination** combines the first two, and for that reason it is the most important. In the examination — the school test, the medical inspection, the psychiatric interview, the military review — observation and normalizing judgment meet in a ritual that turns each person into a *case*: observed, measured, compared, described, and filed. The examination reverses the old economy of visibility. Under sovereign power it was the ruler who was displayed and the subjects who remained in shadow; under disciplinary power it is the subjects who must be ceaselessly visible, documented, and knowable, while power itself operates discreetly. Writing is central: registers, dossiers, and case histories accumulate around the individual, and the human sciences, on Foucault's account, are born in these rituals as much as in universities. The individual-as-case is thus also discipline's product, not only its raw material.

**Main argument:** Correct training works through hierarchical observation, which makes conduct visible; normalizing judgment, which compares every act and person with a norm and corrects deviations; and the examination, which combines both to turn each person into a documented case — reversing the old visibility of power and producing the individual as an object of knowledge.

### 6 — Panopticism

The chapter "Panopticism" is the book's center of gravity. Foucault begins not with Bentham but with two models of power over danger. The leper, in the classical age, was handled by *exclusion*: cast out, in a binary division between pure and impure. The plague-stricken town, by contrast, was handled by *disciplinary segmentation*: streets sealed, every inhabitant registered, assigned a place, observed daily, and reported on, in a grid from which detailed knowledge of the whole town emerged. Leprosy gives power as banishment; the plague gives power as analysis. The nineteenth century increasingly combines the two — the abnormal individual both marked off and minutely known.

Into this frame he sets Jeremy Bentham's *Panopticon* (published 1791, from letters written in 1787): an annular building of individual cells around a central inspection tower. Each cell is backlit, so the occupant is perfectly visible from the center; the tower's blinds and partitions mean the occupant can never see whether anyone is in it. The arrangement's principle, in Foucault's analysis, is to make surveillance *visible and unverifiable*: the inmate must know he may be observed at any moment, and must never know whether he is being observed at this moment. He therefore behaves as if observed always. Power becomes, in Foucault's term, *automatic*: it functions independently of whoever occupies the tower — guard, visitor, nobody — because its real operator is the inmate's own self-restraint. It is also economical — few watchers, no chains, yet more constant pressure than sporadic force — and disindividualised: anyone can operate the machine, whatever their motives.

From the building, Foucault extracts a *diagram* — the "panoptic schema" — and this step is the chapter's argumentative hinge. The schema generalizes wherever individuals must perform tasks, learn skills, or recover health under observation: hospitals (patients separated, observed, charted), schools (pupils distributed, ranked, examined), factories (workers and output supervised together). It can also serve as a laboratory: a place to experiment on conduct and produce knowledge about the people inside. Hence the wider claim that panopticism is a political technology detachable from any single institution, spreading through the social body and helping to form what Foucault calls a *disciplinary society*. Two limits are in the text: Bentham's building was realized rarely and imperfectly — the claim concerns the diagram's diffusion — and the schema is a mechanism, not a total description of society.

**Main argument:** Bentham's Panopticon makes power automatic by making surveillance visible and unverifiable, so the watched internalize the watcher; abstracted from the building, this panoptic schema generalizes across hospitals, schools, and factories as the diagram of a disciplinary society.

### 7 — The carceral continuum and the prison's productive "failure"

Part Four asks an embarrassing question. The prison was denounced — as producing recidivists, corrupting inmates, costing too much, and failing to deter — from the 1820s onward, by critics whose complaints were already fully formed within decades of its triumph. A form so judged should have been replaced; instead it was reformed in detail and preserved in essence. Foucault's answer: ask not why it fails but what it *does*, reliably, and for whom that is useful.

What it does, he argues, is produce *delinquency*. Delinquency is not simply crime, and the delinquent is not simply someone who broke the law. Through confinement among other offenders, stigma, records, post-release supervision, and exclusion from work, the prison manufactures from offenders — many involved in the diffuse popular illegalities of the poor — a distinct, closed milieu of habitual offenders: delinquents, each with a biography, a file, a predicted future. This delinquency is useful in connected ways: a known milieu can be infiltrated, watched, and used by police as diffuse popular illegality cannot; it separates "criminality" from other classes' illegalities, handled more leniently; it justifies extending supervision beyond the prison walls; and it supplies the figures — the dangerous individual, the recidivist — through which fear of crime is organized. None of this requires a conspirator designing the system; Foucault's claim is functional and historical, about effects that stabilise a practice, not about a hidden committee's intentions — a distinction reconstruction (c) examines.

The prison also does not stand alone. The closing chapter describes the *carceral continuum*, or "carceral archipelago": a graduated network from the prison through reformatories, supervised release, welfare institutions, and schools and hospitals for the difficult, to the mildest corrective tutelage — institutions resembling the prison in miniature, extending its normalizing judgment far beyond its walls. Foucault's famous inversion follows: the prison "resembles factories, schools, barracks, hospitals, which all resemble prisons." The carceral is thus not a building but a gradient of coercion and correction across the social body, and the line between the punished and the merely assisted, educated, or treated becomes a continuum rather than a wall.

**Main argument:** The prison persists not despite but through its apparent failure: it reliably produces delinquency — a closed, knowable, politically useful category of offenders — and it anchors a carceral continuum of corrective institutions that extends normalizing judgment across society.

### 8 — A short pointer: from discipline to biopower

This guide ends where Foucault's next book begins, because the bridge is short and the learning path takes it immediately. In Part Five of *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* (1976), "Right of Death and Power over Life," Foucault sets discipline inside a larger history of *biopower* — power over life itself. Sovereign power, he argues, was essentially a right of seizure whose highest expression was the right to kill: in his compressed formula, it made die and let live. From the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a different power grows alongside it, devoted to fostering, optimizing, and administering life: it makes live and lets die.

That power develops at two poles: the *anatomo-politics of the human body* — the disciplines of *Discipline and Punish*, training individual bodies and increasing their forces — and the *biopolitics of the population*: regulatory controls over the species-body (birth and mortality rates, life expectancy, epidemics, hygiene), supported by statistics and demography, aimed at equilibria in populations rather than obedience in individuals. The poles are linked, not alternative epochs: medicine, the school, and sexuality operate at both levels at once — which is why sexuality becomes such a charged political object in the later book.

Use this as a pointer, not a substitute: the full argument, including the critique of the "repressive hypothesis" and state racism inside biopower, belongs to the *History of Sexuality* guide. Fix the map: sovereign power over death, discipline over individual bodies, biopolitics over populations — three technologies, historically layered, all still in play.

**Main argument:** Discipline is one pole of a wider biopower: anatomo-politics trains individual bodies while biopolitics regulates populations, and the two together mark the shift from a sovereign power that made die and let live to a modern power that makes live and lets die.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) Power produces: the argument for productive power

**Premises:**

1. The inherited model treats power as essentially repressive: a sovereign or a class holds it, and it operates by forbidding, censoring, and punishing conduct that would otherwise occur naturally.
2. The practices examined in *Discipline and Punish* do not fit that model. Timetables, examinations, dossiers, and normalizing judgments do not merely forbid; they create capacities (the drilled soldier, the literate pupil), bodies of knowledge (criminology, pedagogy, clinical description), and kinds of person (the delinquent, the abnormal, the case).
3. These products are not incidental by-products. The institutions could not operate without them: the examination must produce its file, normalization must produce its categories of deviation, and the prison must produce a known class of offenders to supervise.
4. A model of power that cannot register these productions will systematically misdescribe the practices, treating as secondary effects what is in fact their regular output.

**Conclusion:** Power, at least in its modern disciplinary form, is productive before it is repressive: it produces knowledge, capacities, institutions, and subjects, and its prohibitions are one instrument among others within that production.

**Objection:** The normative-confusion charge, pressed most sharply by Nancy Fraser and by Jürgen Habermas. If power is everywhere, productive of everything — including criticism's own categories — Foucault has no apparent standpoint from which discipline is objectionable. Yet his prose plainly condemns: prisons are "intolerable," subjugation is bad. On what grounds? Fraser's charge: he relies on liberal values his framework declares effects of power and cannot avow. If resistance is just more power, why prefer it?

**Reply:** Defenders reply, first, that Foucault offers a minimal internal criterion: he distinguishes mobile *power relations*, where the other's actions remain open, from *states of domination*, where relations are frozen so reversal becomes practically impossible — and criticizes the freezing, not power as such. Second, genealogy criticizes without a universal foundation by showing that arrangements presenting themselves as necessary are contingent and were made, which reopens them to change. Whether this answers Fraser and Habermas, or concedes their point while declining their remedy, is a live debate.

### (b) The Panopticon as a diagram of power

**Premises:**

1. Bentham's Panopticon arranges cells around a central tower so that each occupant is visible from the tower but cannot see into it, and therefore cannot know at any moment whether he is being watched.
2. A subject who cannot verify the presence of the watcher, but knows observation is always possible, has reason to behave continuously as if watched; external coercion is thereby transferred into self-surveillance.
3. This makes power economical and automatic: it requires few personnel, little force, and no continuous intervention, and it functions regardless of the identity or intentions of whoever occupies the tower.
4. The arrangement is not tied to the prison. The same schema — distribute individuals, render them visible, individualize them by records and comparison — is adapted in hospitals, schools, and factories to induce conduct, train skills, and generate knowledge.

**Conclusion:** The Panopticon is best understood not as a building but as a diagram of disciplinary power: a transferable mechanism in which the internalization of possible surveillance makes coercion continuous, cheap, and self-applying.

**Objection:** The diagram is easily overextended — including, critics note, by Foucault's own more sweeping readers. Actual surveillance is patchy, contested, and often resisted or gamed; institutions mix discipline with negotiation and evasion; and Bentham's building was almost never constructed. A model drawn from an unbuilt proposal, the objection runs, cannot bear a general theory of modern society, and calling every camera or appraisal panoptic dissolves useful distinctions.

**Reply:** Foucault's texts support a more careful claim: the Panopticon is a *diagram*, an ideal mechanism, and its diffusion is claimed for its elements (distribution, visibility, examination, records), not circular architecture. He also insists that where there is power there is resistance: power presupposes subjects who can act otherwise, so a disciplinary society is never a closed total system. Whether the careful version still explains enough — or surrenders the bold thesis — remains debated.

### (c) The prison's failure is its function

**Premises:**

1. From within decades of its general adoption, the prison was criticized on the same grounds repeated ever since: it does not reduce crime, it produces high rates of reoffending, and it corrupts and stigmatises those it confines.
2. Despite this continuous, well-documented criticism, the prison was not abandoned; it was preserved as the central punishment, with reforms altering its details rather than its form, for well over a century.
3. The prison does, however, reliably produce one thing: delinquency — a distinct, documented, relatively closed milieu of habitual offenders, separated from the wider field of popular illegalities, and available for police knowledge, infiltration, and supervision.
4. That product is useful to the wider system of policing and social management: it concentrates attention on a known category, legitimises extended surveillance and corrective intervention, and distinguishes the "delinquent" poor from other forms of illegality treated more indulgently.
5. A practice that persists for generations despite failing at its declared aim, while succeeding reliably at an undeclared one that stabilises it, is better explained by the undeclared success than by collective irrationality.

**Conclusion:** The prison's "failure" to eliminate crime is compatible with — and partly explains — its survival, because its actual function is the production and management of delinquency, not the abolition of offending.

**Objection:** The historian's objection. Historians of punishment — Patricia O'Brien, Pieter Spierenburg, and others — dispute parts of the empirical picture: the chronology is compressed (physical punishments persisted long after discipline supposedly displaced them, and disciplinary techniques long predate the modern prison); the spectacle's decline had other causes, including changing sensibilities and state formation; and premise 5's functional inference is contested, since an effect's usefulness does not by itself explain a practice's persistence. "Failure is function" risks becoming an unfalsifiable slogan.

**Reply:** Defenders concede corrections to dates and emphases while holding the central facts: early critics did make the recidivism charge, the prison was retained, and nineteenth-century policing did organize itself around a known delinquent milieu; genealogy traces a technology's emergence and spread, not an overnight replacement. As method, the functional claim is explanatory, not conspiratorial: effects benefiting powerful institutions help explain why a practice is reproduced, without anyone intending the design. Critics reply that this defense weakens the thesis each time it is used. The dispute is unresolved — and is the right place to press the book hardest.

## Common misreadings

**1. "Foucault says surveillance is simply evil, and discipline is total."**
He analyses mechanisms rather than issuing one verdict, and his framework excludes totality in principle: power relations exist only where subjects can act otherwise, so resistance is internal to every disciplinary arrangement. A hospital's observation chart and a prison's are the same *kind* of instrument to different ends — an analytical claim, not a claim of moral identity.

**2. "'Power is everywhere' means everything is a conspiracy, or that everything is equally power."**
No conspirator is posited and no center directs the system: techniques spread because institutions copy what works. Nor is everything equally power — Foucault distinguishes fluid power relations from frozen states of domination, and specific relations (a timetable, an examination) from the vague slogan.

**3. "Bentham's Panopticons were built everywhere in the nineteenth century."**
The building was a proposal, constructed rarely and in modified forms. The claim is about the *diagram*: distribution, unverifiable visibility, individualizing records, and examination diffusing across institutions that look nothing like a circular prison. Refuting the architectural claim leaves the diagram claim untouched.

**4. "Foucault denies that humanitarian motives or real progress existed at all."**
He denies that humanitarian sentiment is a *sufficient explanation* of the form punishment took. Reformers' horror at torture was real, and he nowhere recommends restoring the scaffold. His question is why humanitarianism, when it won, produced continuous training and surveillance rather than simply milder or rarer punishment.

**5. "Disciplinary power is just state power by another name."**
Discipline operates where the state is not the obvious actor: schools, hospitals, factories, charities, families, professions — often before or independently of state direction. The state is one relay and beneficiary among others; the prison chapters show administrators as heirs of techniques developed elsewhere, not their inventors.

**6. "The prison's failure thesis means prisons do nothing, or that reform is pointless."**
The thesis is the opposite of "does nothing": the prison produces delinquency, authorizes supervision, and anchors the carceral continuum — which is why it survives criticism. The analysis alone does not settle what should be done; readers draw abolitionist, reformist, and diagnostic conclusions from the same text.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **The two documents** are Damiens's public execution in Paris in **1757** and Léon Faucher's timetable for a Paris house of young prisoners, dated **1838**. Foucault's question: what changed — in object, technology, and reach — between a spectacular killing on the body and a silent schedule ordering a whole day?
2. **Humanitarianism is insufficient** because it explains at most why punishment became less terrible; it does not explain the replacement's form — detailed knowledge of offenders, continuous training, timetables, surveillance across many institutions. The new power is milder in means and more thorough in grip; an explanation must cover both.
3. A crime offended the sovereign because **the law resided in the sovereign's person**, so every offense injured his power as well as its victim. Public execution performed **an epistemic function** — displaying the crime's truth, crowned by confession — and **a political function**: restoring the sovereign's wounded power on the condemned body.
4. The spectacle was risky because **it required the crowd's participation**, and the crowd could refuse: pitying or heroising the condemned, turning the last speech against authority, resenting the executioner, or rioting. A power staging itself through a crowd builds inversion into its central ritual.
5. The reformers proposed **legality, proportionality, and a semiotics of punishment**: fixed laws, penalties representing the crime and advertising its disadvantage, prevention over vengeance, useful public work. It aimed at the **soul** because its target was representations in citizens' and offenders' minds — the imagined calculus of crime and penalty — not pain on the body.
6. A **docile body** can be subjected, used, transformed, and improved — made more capable and more obedient by the same operations. The four techniques: **art of distributions** (space), **control of activity** (time and movement), **organization of geneses** (graded development), **composition of forces** (combining bodies into an efficient whole).
7. **Distributions:** each pupil assigned a fixed, ranked classroom place. **Control of activity:** a timetable prescribing the timed sequence and posture of an act, as in drill or handwriting. **Geneses:** a curriculum divided into graded stages, each closed by a test. **Composition of forces:** trained bodies arranged into a platoon, class, or line whose combined force exceeds the sum of its parts.
8. The three means are **hierarchical observation**, **normalizing judgment**, and **the examination**. The examination matters most because it **combines the other two**: it observes, compares against a norm, and turns the person into a documented case — where power produces both individuality and knowledge of individuals.
9. Normalizing judgment **homogenises** by pressing everyone toward one standard; it **individualizes** by measuring and recording each person's particular gap from that standard — the specific lateness, weakness, or deviation that makes this individual the object of correction.
10. The Panopticon makes power automatic because the inmate, in a backlit cell facing a tower he cannot see into, must assume he may be watched at any moment and so watches himself; it works whoever — or no one — is in the tower. **Visible**: the tower is constantly before his eyes. **Unverifiable**: he can never confirm whether the gaze is on him now.
11. As a **building**, the Panopticon is Bentham's circular design, rarely constructed. As a **diagram**, the panoptic schema is the abstract mechanism — distribution, visibility, individualizing records — adaptable to schools, hospitals, and factories. Foucault's claim is the diagram's diffusion, so the scarcity of circular prisons does not touch it.
12. The **leper** models power by exclusion: casting out the impure. The **plague town** models power by disciplinary inclusion: quarantine, registration, daily inspection, minute recording of everyone in place. The nineteenth century **combined** them: categories marked off as abnormal are simultaneously subjected to detailed disciplinary management.
13. **Delinquency** is not crime in general but the penal system's product: a documented milieu of habitual offenders — the delinquent as a type, with biography and predicted future — manufactured by imprisonment, stigma, records, and post-release supervision. It is **useful** because a known milieu can be watched and infiltrated, it separates criminalised poverty from better-tolerated illegalities, and it legitimises supervision beyond the prison.
14. The **carceral continuum** is the graduated network of corrective institutions — prison, reformatory, supervised release, welfare and educational tutelage — across which normalizing judgment extends, so punishment shades into assistance without a sharp boundary. The resemblance claim means these institutions **share techniques** — enclosure, timetables, observation, examination — not that a school is morally a jail.
15. Biopower's poles are the **anatomo-politics of the human body** (disciplines training individual bodies) and the **biopolitics of the population** (regulation of births, mortality, health, life expectancy). **"Make die and let live"** is sovereign power, whose essence is the right to kill; **"make live and let die"** is biopower, which fosters and administers life and lets die by neglect or exclusion.
