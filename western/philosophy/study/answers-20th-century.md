# Worked Answers: 20th-Century Study Questions

Model answers to the Study questions at the end of the 20th-century core entries. These are study aids, not substitutes for your own attempt: work each question book-closed first, as the [learning path](../learning-path.md) directs, then compare. Where a question asks you to judge a debate, the answer sets out the main positions rather than a verdict — a differently argued judgment can be equally valid. Where a question applies a framework to a fresh case, the worked application shows the method; other applications may be equally sound.

## [Frege](../eras/05-20th-century/gottlob-frege.md)

**1. Sense does the work in the identity puzzle.**
Both sentences are true, and in both the two flanking expressions have the same *reference* — the planet Venus. If meaning were exhausted by reference, the two sentences would say the same thing, and the first could not have been an astronomical discovery. Frege's solution: an expression also has a *sense*, its mode of presentation of the reference. "The Morning Star" presents Venus as the body visible in the morning sky; "the Evening Star" presents the same body under a different mode. The identity statement is informative because it links two distinct senses and asserts they converge on one reference; the trivial statement links a sense to itself. So: *sense* does the explanatory work — the difference in cognitive value is a difference of sense — while sameness of *reference* is what makes both statements true, and indeed makes the first one an identity at all rather than a falsehood. The thought (the sentence's sense) differs; the truth-value does not.

**2. Russell's paradox and the neo-Fregean rescue.**
Basic Law V equates two concepts' extensions exactly when the concepts apply to the same objects, so every concept determines a set-like extension. Form the concept "extension that does not fall under its own concept" — its extension either falls under that concept or not; each supposition yields the other, contradiction. Frege's system collapses. The neo-Fregean reply: the derivation of arithmetic never really needed Law V. Hume's Principle — the number of Fs equals the number of Gs iff the Fs and Gs pair off one-to-one — added to second-order logic suffices to derive the Peano axioms (Frege's Theorem, which Frege's own work in effect contains). Rescued or relocated? The derivation is a genuine mathematical result, so the program's core claim — arithmetic from a single abstraction principle plus logic — is revived. But critics press two points, both noted in the entry: Hume's Principle may not itself be *logic* (it commits to infinitely many objects), and "bad company" principles formally like it are inconsistent, so some further criterion must sort acceptable principles from Law V — and supplying it looks like the original problem in new dress. The debate continues; call it a partial rescue with the foundational assumption moved, and arguably clarified.

**3. Against Kant, against Mill.**
Against Kant: arithmetic is not synthetic a priori. Its truths are *analytic* — derivable from logic and definitions — so no appeal to pure intuition is needed: large-number identities no one can intuit follow by proof, and proof, not intuition, is what actually justifies them. Against Mill: arithmetic is not empirical generalization. Mill confuses the *applications* of arithmetic with its content and the psychology of learning with justification; arithmetical truths are not revisable by counter-instances the way empirical laws are, and they hold of everything thinkable, not only of observed physical aggregates. The context principle — ask for the meaning of a word only in the context of a proposition — hits Mill more directly. Mill's picture treats numerals as naming properties of heaps, i.e. asks what numbers *are* by pointing at collections; the context principle blocks exactly that move, insisting number-words get their meaning from whole numerical statements ("the number of moons of Jupiter is four" says something of a concept). Kant's error is about arithmetic's *source*, which logicism answers globally; Mill's is about what number-statements *mean*, which the context principle targets point-blank.

**4. The bestselling novelist.**
"The author of the next bestselling novel" is a definite description not yet attached to any existing book. On the Fregean analysis, the expression has a perfectly determinate *sense*: it presents, under a mode of presentation, whoever will satisfy the condition. Its *reference* is unsettled: strictly, on Frege's handling of empty or not-yet-satisfied descriptions, the phrase currently fails to refer to any actual person — no one yet stands as *the* satisfier. The sentence nevertheless expresses a complete *thought*, because thoughts are senses, and sense does not require reference: we grasp exactly what would have to be the case. Whether the sentence, as opposed to the thought, has a truth-value is the sharp edge: Frege's official view treats sentences with reference-failing parts as lacking a truth-value (fiction being his paradigm of sense without reference), though the thought remains assessable as a prediction once the future settles the reference. Note the contrast with Russell (next entry): he would dissolve the description quantificationally and return a plain truth-value now. Either way, the headline's meaningfulness *before* any such novel exists is precisely what the sense/reference distinction was built to explain.

## [Russell](../eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md)

**1. The King of France analysis.**
Russell unpacks "The present King of France is bald" into three clauses: (i) *existence* — there is at least one present King of France; (ii) *uniqueness* — there is at most one; (iii) *predication* — whatever is a present King of France is bald. The description is an "incomplete symbol": it contributes this quantificational structure and then disappears; it does not itself refer. Because clause (i) is false, the conjunction is *false* — not meaningless, because the unpacked sentence is a complete, assessable claim about the world; and no non-existent king is needed, because nothing in the analysis requires an object for the description to stand for. The Meinongian alternative — stock reality with a non-existent king for the sentence to be about — is dissolved rather than refuted piecemeal: show the logical form, and the pressure to posit the entity evaporates. That method, displaying logical form beneath misleading surface grammar, is the theory's larger legacy.

**2. The first person on Mars, twice.**
*Frege*: "the first person to set foot on Mars" has a sense — a determinate mode of presentation — but at present no reference; the sentence expresses a thought whose truth-value awaits the world, much as the Frege answer above treats the novelist. *Russell*: the description unpacks quantificationally — there will be exactly one first-footer, and whoever it is will be famous — a claim that already has a truth-value on the evidence of how exploration and celebrity work, no future object required. Which better explains why the sentence is *already meaningful, and plausibly true*? Russell's analysis explains both conjuncts directly: meaningfulness is structure, not reference, and "plausibly true" is a judgment we actually make and can defend now. Frege explains meaningfulness elegantly through sense but must park the truth-value gap on a case where ordinary judgment confidently says "almost certainly true." That said, the Fregean wins a different contest: substitution in belief reports ("she hopes she will be the first person…"), where sense does work Russell's analysis must reconstruct with scope machinery. For the question as posed — present meaningfulness plus present plausibility — Russell's is the better fit; the broader rivalry remains open.

**3. Strawson's presupposition objection.**
Strawson: Russell confuses sentences with their uses. To *utter* "the King of France" is not to assert that a king exists but to *presuppose* it — existence is a precondition of the referring act, not part of what is asserted. Where the presupposition fails, the utterance is neither true nor false: the question of truth simply does not arise, as with a move in a game whose starting position never obtained. Russell's analysis therefore falsifies ordinary language by forcing a verdict of "false." The Russellian reply: the point of the analysis is *logical form*, and inside a proof — mathematics, inference — descriptions must combine, negate, and take scope in ways presupposition-talk does not by itself regiment. If "the King of France is not bald" is to do inferential work, we need its truth conditions, and Russell supplies them; presupposition can then be layered on top as a fact about use without displacing the analysis. Which side better handles the sentence *inside a proof*? Russell's, by design — proofs need bivalence and scope. Strawson's better describes the pragmatics of conversation. The dispute, as the entry says, remains a live disagreement about what an analysis of language is *for*.

**4. The two readings of the negation.**
"The present King of France is not bald" is ambiguous in scope. Reading A — the negation takes *wide* scope over the whole analysis: it is not the case that (there is exactly one present King of France and he is bald). Since there is no king, the bracketed claim is false, so Reading A is *true*. Reading B — the negation takes *narrow* scope, inside the predication: there is exactly one present King of France, and he is not bald. This asserts existence, so it is *false*. Ordinary speech does not mark the difference: the surface grammar keeps one negative word in one place while the logical form has two distinct places for it. That is Russell's Exhibit A for his method: a contradiction-threatening puzzle ("Is he bald or not? Neither seems right") dissolves once scope is made explicit — the two readings have different truth-values and there is no single question to be stuck on. Surface grammar is not a reliable guide to logical form.

**5. The paradox, and the "grammatical fiat" charge.**
Unrestricted comprehension says every condition defines a set. Take the condition "is not a member of itself" and form R, the set of all such sets. If R ∈ R, then by its defining condition R ∉ R; if R ∉ R, then it satisfies the condition, so R ∈ R. Contradiction — so comprehension is false. Russell's type theory blocks the construction by stratifying entities into types so self-membership is ill-formed. The charge: this merely *bans* the paradox by grammatical fiat, and also bans harmless self-reference. The iterative conception is the defenders' answer, and it is stronger than a patch: on it, sets are *formed in stages*, each stage collecting only objects already available earlier. Then there is no stage at which a set could contain itself, and "the set of all sets" is not forbidden by rule but fails to correspond to any possible stage of formation — the restriction follows from a positive picture of what sets *are*. Does it fully answer the charge? It motivates *a* hierarchy (the one ZF embodies), arguably better than it motivates Russell's own ramified types with their reducibility axiom; and whether the iterative picture is the concept of set or one good regimentation remains discussable. The paradox's lesson, though — comprehension must go — is permanent.

## [Wittgenstein (early)](../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-early.md)

**1. The picture theory on "The cat is on the mat."**
In the fully analyzed proposition, the names correspond one-to-one to *objects*: a name for the cat, a name for the mat, and the spatial relation expressed by the elements' arrangement. What the proposition shares with the pictured fact is not appearance but *logical form* — the possible way of being combined: the names are combined in the proposition exactly as the objects could be combined in a state of affairs, as a score, a record, and a performance share a structure that lets one be projected onto another. The proposition pictures the *possible* state of affairs that the cat is on the mat. If that state of affairs obtains, the proposition is true; if not, false — but meaningful either way, because it still pictures a possibility. That is bipolarity: a genuine factual proposition must be capable of truth *and* capable of falsity, its sense being precisely the range between the two. A string that could not be false (a tautology) or could not be true pictures nothing and so says nothing, however much it may *show*.

**2. Ramsey's color-exclusion objection.**
Elementary propositions were supposed to be logically independent: no elementary proposition can contradict another, since each pictures an independent atomic fact and truth-values combine freely. But "this is red all over" and "this is green all over," said of the same surface at the same time, exclude each other with something like necessity — yet both look elementary, neither analyzing into simpler propositions whose conflict would explain the exclusion. So either color-ascriptions are not elementary (and the promised analysis that dissolves the exclusion is nowhere supplied), or elementary propositions are not independent, and the truth-functional machinery loses its foundation. This is internal, not an external quibble, because independence is not a decorative feature: bipolarity plus independence is what makes every non-elementary proposition a truth-function of elementary ones and gives the system its account of sense. A contradiction *within* the atoms cannot be repaired by machinery built on the atoms. Ramsey pressed the point early, Wittgenstein acknowledged the pressure, and it was among the problems that helped bring him back to philosophy in 1929.

**3. Did the Vienna Circle capture or betray the book?**
The Circle read the *Tractatus* as a licence: ethics, aesthetics, and religion fail the test of factual sense and so are nonsense to be eliminated. The textual evidence cuts against the spirit of that reading. At 6.421 Wittgenstein writes that ethics cannot be put into words and is *transcendental* — it belongs with what matters most, not with what is worthless — and in the 1919 Ficker letter he said the book's point was ethical, consisting of the part written *and* the part not written, "and it is precisely this second part that is the important one." Drawing a limit to factual speech was meant to *protect* the ethical from being debased into pseudo-fact, not to dismiss it. So the Circle captured the letter — the demarcation of sense from nonsense — and betrayed the point: what they filed as refuse, Wittgenstein filed as the unsayable that gives the sayable its significance. Fairness requires one concession: the book's own ladder invites the misreading, since its propositions do not mark their protected zone except by gesture — which is why "captures or betrays" is still argued rather than settled.

**4. Applying the ladder to the picture theory itself.**
The sentence "A proposition shares logical form with the fact it pictures" purports to state the very relation that, by the book's doctrine, can only *show* itself: any proposition stating it would have to stand outside the shared form, and nothing can. By the *Tractatus*'s own standard, therefore, it cannot be *said* — it is one of the ladder's rungs, nonsense to be thrown away once climbed (6.54). Two defenses of that verdict are on offer, and the choice between them is the book's central interpretive dispute. The *traditional* (ineffability) reading: the sentence gestures at a genuine truth about logical form that shows itself in every meaningful proposition; it is nonsense by the letter but illuminating nonsense, conveying what cannot be stated. The *resolute* reading (Diamond, Conant): there are no ineffable truths to convey; the rung is plain nonsense like any other, and the therapy works only when the reader gives up the idea that something deep was communicated. Either defense must be *chosen* — the question asks for one, argued: resolute readers stress that ineffable truths reintroduce metaphysics by the back door; traditional readers reply that the resolute reading cannot explain why *this* nonsense instructs. No verdict is given here; the entry presents the dispute as live.

## [Vienna Circle](../eras/05-20th-century/vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md)

**1. Classifying three sentences.**
(a) "All metals expand when heated" — empirically verifiable in principle: observations of metals under heat count for and against it. Cognitively meaningful, synthetic and a posteriori. (Caveat for Q5: no finite run of tests *conclusively* verifies a universal — the seed of the weak-verification retreat.) (b) "The Absolute unfolds itself in history" — neither analytic nor verifiable: no possible experience counts for or against it, and its terms resist logical clarification; Carnap's treatment of Heidegger's "the Nothing nothings" is the model verdict — a pseudo-statement, cognitively meaningless, whatever poetic or emotive force it carries. (c) "Cruelty is wrong" — neither analytic (denying it is no contradiction) nor verifiable (no observation settles it). On Ayer's emotivism it is not therefore useless noise: it *expresses* disapproval of cruelty and aims to arouse the same attitude in hearers — an ejaculation and an incitement, not a factual assertion. The standing objection, noted in the entry, is that this cannot capture moral *disagreement*: we seem to contradict each other, not merely cheer against each other.

**2. The argument against the synthetic a priori.**
Kant's scheme has three boxes: analytic a priori (true by meaning, known without experience), synthetic a posteriori (factual, known from experience), and synthetic a priori (informative about the world yet knowable independently of it) — the box housing geometry and the principles of science. The Circle's charge is that Kant overfilled the third box, and geometry after relativity is the exhibit: what Kant took as the a priori form of spatial intuition turned out, once non-Euclidean geometries proved consistent and Einstein's theory made physical geometry an empirical question, to split in two — pure geometry is analytic (true relative to stipulated axioms, saying nothing factual), applied geometry is synthetic a posteriori (answerable to measurement). Generalizing: every alleged synthetic a priori truth likewise decomposes into a stipulative part and an empirical part. The two-box scheme — analytic/a priori versus synthetic/a posteriori — leaves the third box empty, and with it goes Kant's account of how necessary truth about the world is possible: there is none; necessity is linguistic, factuality is empirical, and never the twain.

**3. Self-refutation, proposal, rejoinder.**
The objection: the verification principle is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, so by its own standard it is cognitively meaningless — the attack on metaphysics saws off its own branch. The positivist reply: the principle is not an assertion at all but a *proposal* — a recommended definition of "cognitively meaningful" for the language of science, to be judged by fruitfulness rather than verified; on Carnap's principle of tolerance, frameworks are chosen, not proven. The critic's rejoinder: a mere proposal can simply be *declined*. The metaphysician who refuses the definition loses nothing, and the principle's polemical force — the claim to have *shown* metaphysics meaningless rather than merely unfashionable — evaporates. Where does this leave the attack? As the entry puts it, not as a knockout but as the opening of an argument about criteria: the positivists can still motivate the proposal (its fruits: clarity about science, dissolution of pseudo-problems), and the metaphysician must show comparable fruit. Most historians judge that no version achieved exactly the intended cut — but "self-refuting, end of story" is too quick in both directions.

**4. Quine's threat from inside.**
The verification principle leans on the analytic/synthetic distinction twice over: a statement passes if it is analytic *or* verifiable, and the verification half is administered statement by statement. "Two Dogmas" attacks both supports from within empiricism itself. First, every attempt to define "analytic" — via synonymy, definition, interchangeability, semantic rules — circles through equally unclear notions, so the first passing-route is an unexamined label. Second, reductionism is false: statements face experience not individually but as a corporate body, so there is no per-statement empirical meaning for verification to certify. The threat is internal because Quine accepts the empiricist's starting point — no synthetic a priori, meaning tied to experience — and derives *more* empiricism than the Circle could house: if confirmation is holistic, the unit of meaning is the whole web, and the principle's case-by-case border patrol has nothing to patrol. Kant is not the casualty; the casualty is the idea that empiricism can keep a sharp analytic border and an atomistic test. (That Neurath's boat anticipates the holism is the Circle's own partial defense — Quine as heir, not only assassin.)

**5. Universal laws and the weak version's concession.**
Strong verification demands that experience be able to *conclusively* establish a statement. A universal law — "all metals expand when heated" — ranges over indefinitely many cases, including future and unobserved ones; no finite set of observations ever conclusively verifies it, since the next metal might not expand. Strong verification therefore classifies the laws of science themselves as cognitively meaningless — a reductio for a philosophy built to honor science. Ayer's weak version retreats: a statement is verifiable if *some* experience is *relevant* to determining its truth or falsity — observations need only count for or against, not settle. What it concedes is the sharpness of the cut: relevance is a matter of degree, and critics charged that on the weak criterion almost anything can find some experience "relevant" to it, letting metaphysical statements creep back across the border the principle existed to police. The dilemma — strong version excludes science, weak version readmits metaphysics — is the technical heart of the principle's troubles, and no agreed middle setting was found.

## [Quine](../eras/05-20th-century/w-v-o-quine.md)

**1. The circularity charge, traced.**
Start with the target: "analytic" means true by meaning alone. Define it by *synonymy*: analytic statements are those reducible to logical truths by swapping synonyms. But "synonymy" itself is undefined without appeal to analyticity — ask what makes "bachelor" and "unmarried man" synonymous and the answer is that they mean the same, which is the notion under construction. Try *definition*: dictionaries report pre-existing synonymies rather than creating them, and stipulative definition covers only the coiner's own cases. Try *interchangeability salva veritate*: "bachelor" and "unmarried man" can be swapped without changing truth — but in a purely extensional language this tests only co-reference ("creature with a heart" / "creature with kidneys"), and restoring the test requires modal words like "necessarily," which presuppose analyticity again. Semantic rules for artificial languages define "analytic-in-L" only relative to a stipulated list, which does not explain the general notion. Each route presupposes the family — meaning, synonymy, necessity — it was meant to found. A non-circular definition would have to supply a criterion stated in terms that do not belong to that family: Quine's standing demand was behavioral — facts about speakers' dispositions to assent and dissent — and his claim is that nothing in behavior marks the analytic off from the deeply believed synthetic.

**2. The web of belief and the underdetermined revision.**
A failed prediction tells the web *somewhere* a revision is due, never where. Concretely: an eclipse expedition's photographs contradict the predicted position. Revision could fall (i) on an *auxiliary hypothesis* — the telescope's optics were mis-calibrated, the photographic plates flawed; (ii) on an *observation report* — the recorded measurement was misread or the conditions misdescribed, so the "recalcitrant experience" itself is withdrawn; or (iii) on a *law near the center* — the gravitational theory used to compute the prediction is itself amended (as, historically, Mercury's perihelion helped amend Newton to Einstein). Experience alone cannot dictate the choice because experience only confronts the *corporate body* of statements: the failed prediction follows from the whole conjunction, and logic says only that the conjunction is false somewhere. Choice among repairs is guided by pragmatic virtues Quine acknowledges — conservatism, minimal mutilation, simplicity — which are maxims of theory management, not deliverances of the tribunal of experience. That is confirmation holism: any statement can be held true if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere, and no statement is immune in principle.

**3. Grice and Strawson's reply.**
Their objection: Quine demands a behaviorally respectable definition and, finding none, denies the distinction — but the inference is too fast. Within actual practice, synonymy-talk is orderly: speakers agree extensively on which sentences are true by meaning, explain words to each other, translate, and correct misuse; a distinction can be real and well-understood in use without yet having a reductive analysis in some approved vocabulary. Plenty of respectable notions (their implied analogy runs) long predate their scientific definitions. Does this answer Quine or change the subject? It answers the *negative* claim that the notion is unintelligible — it manifestly is not, in practice. It does not supply what Quine asked for: an explanation of analyticity in terms an empiricist may use without circularity, and Grice–Strawson do not really attempt one. So the exchange splits: against "the distinction is bogus," they score; against "the distinction is unexplained in acceptable terms," Quine's challenge stands and later work (conceptual-role semantics, two-dimensionalism) is still trying to meet it. Judging which half matters more is judging what philosophy owes — a usable distinction or a reductive foundation.

**4. "Pelu": two manuals, no decider.**
An informant says "pelu" whenever rain begins. Manual A translates "pelu" as "Rain is starting" — a report of a weather event. Manual B translates it as "A rain-stage is manifesting itself" — a claim about a temporal slice of the world's rain-process, in the way "gavagai" might mean *undetached rabbit part* or *rabbit-stage* rather than *rabbit*. Both manuals predict assent to "pelu" in exactly the same circumstances: whenever rain begins, both a rain-start event and a rain-stage are present — the two posited referents are *co-instantiated* in every observable situation, so no disposition to speech behavior can separate them. Probing further fails systematically: ask about yesterday's rain, parts of rain, the same rain again — each probe's translation itself depends on the manual's apparatus for tenses, identity, and individuation, which is precisely what is underdetermined; the linguist can always adjust the background translation to compensate. Hence Quine's conclusion: there is no further *behavioral* fact of the matter that decides; translation is indeterminate not for lack of evidence but because meaning, on a behavioral basis, does not contain the deciding facts. (Critics dispute the behaviorist premise — see the entry on Chomsky — but within it the argument is tight.)

**5. Kim's normativity objection.**
Kim: epistemology's founding question is normative — how *should* we form beliefs, what *justifies* a belief — and a descriptive psychology of how we *do* form them, however good, cannot answer it. Replace epistemology with psychology and you have not naturalized the normative question but discarded it, changing the subject and losing what made the enterprise epistemology at all. Quine's best available reply has two layers. Doctrinal: the normative/descriptive border is itself one of the old dualisms; once there is no first philosophy outside science, "justification" can only mean, from inside our going theory of the world, what actually conduces to true belief — and *that* is an empirical, technological question (his later phrase: normative epistemology survives as a kind of engineering of truth-seeking, the technology of anticipating nature). The ought is not discarded but relocated: it becomes the hypothetical imperative of methods that work. Whether that satisfies the objector depends on whether one thinks epistemic norms need more authority than instrumental success — Kim's side says a method could "work" by luck or manipulation and we would still ask if belief was *reasonable*. The entry lists this among the live debates, and it is one.

## [Wittgenstein (late)](../eras/05-20th-century/wittgenstein-late.md)

*The study guide [Philosophical Investigations](wittgenstein-investigations.md) gives the full treatment of §§2, 185–315; these answers compress it.*

**1. The builders, analyzed twice.**
*Tractatus* analysis: "block," "pillar," "slab," "beam" are names standing for objects; the spoken call, fully analyzed, pictures an arrangement — it is true or false according as the assistant's bringing matches a pictured state of affairs, and its sense is bipolar. What the assistant must learn, on this account, is the correlation: which object each name names, and the configuration each utterance pictures. *Investigations* redescription: the calls are moves in an activity — a language-game woven into building. "Slab!" functions like an order within a trained practice; its meaning is its *role* in the game, not an object it stands for. What the assistant must learn now is a technique: to fetch the right piece on the call, as one learns by training, correction, and going-on — mastery of a practice, not possession of a correlation table. The contrast exposes the shift: on the early account understanding is picturing, uniform across language; on the later, the builder's language works perfectly without naming inner objects at all, and real language is this game grown vastly richer, not a different kind of thing.

**2. §258: the diarist and the collapse of seeming/is.**
The diarist wants "S" to mean a particular sensation. He fixes it by private ostension: concentrating on the sensation and resolving "this shall be S." Next day a sensation occurs and he writes "S." Is he right? Rightness requires a criterion independent of the present impression — something against which this application could be checked and found wrong. But the only candidate check is his memory of the original sensation, and memory here is itself just another seeming: if it *seems* the same, that seeming is the very thing in question. Where whatever seems right is right, "right" has lost its contrast and so its meaning — a rule needs the possibility of *mis*application, of seeming to follow it while not following it. The failure is therefore not that the diarist might make mistakes; it is that the set-up gives "correct" no work to do. Private ostension cannot fix meaning because fixing meaning is establishing a technique of use with an independent standard, and a standard constituted by the impression it is meant to standardize is no standard.

**3. The newspaper reply and the Crusoe case.**
The objector: memory *can* check the diarist — he compares today's sensation with his memory of the defining one; and a lifelong solitary, a Crusoe, could surely keep a private diary. Wittgenstein's reply (§265): justifying a memory by appealing to another memory-impression is like buying several copies of the same morning newspaper to confirm its report — the copies are not independent sources, so agreement among them adds nothing. Justification needs something outside the circle of impressions. Does it meet Crusoe? Partly, and the boundary is genuinely debated. If Crusoe learned language among others before his isolation, Wittgenstein need not object: his diary uses a public technique carried into solitude — the criteria were established in practice with others. If Crusoe is imagined language-less from birth, the Wittgensteinian bites the bullet: whatever regularities he falls into lack the normative structure of rule-following, because nothing in solitary behavior distinguishes being guided by a rule from merely behaving regularly *and believing oneself guided*. Defenders of Crusoe reply that this smuggles in the contested premise that correctness requires a community — the same fault line as the Kripke dispute. Judgment left open: the newspaper reply defeats *independent-check-by-memory*; the born-solitary case turns on the deeper premise.

**4. The beetle in the box.**
Suppose everyone has a box containing something called a "beetle," which no one else can see, and which might differ from person to person, change constantly, or be absent altogether. Wittgenstein's point: if the word's use in the language-game is unaffected by all that variation, then the thing in the box is *not part of the language-game at all* — it drops out as irrelevant, like a cog that turns nothing. Sensation-words work in our shared practices — avowals, expressions, circumstances, responses — and their meaning is that use, not an inner object each of us privately inspects and names. The picture being rejected is the Cartesian inner-theater model: experience as a private gallery of objects, known by inner ostension, to which sensation-words refer — the picture that generates the private-language temptation and the "problem of other minds" in their classic forms. Is pain itself being denied? No — explicitly not. Wittgenstein is not a behaviorist denying that people hurt; he denies a *theory* of how "pain" means. The pain is not a something, he says in the surrounding remarks, but not a nothing either: what is rejected is the grammatical model, not the sensation.

**5. Application: is speedrunning a game?**
Do not look for an essence; survey the resemblances. Speedrunning shares with paradigm games: rule-governed activity (categories, timing rules, verification), skill that can be trained and ranked, competition — against a leaderboard, against other runners' times, against one's own personal best — winning and losing relocated into times rather than opponents, and play undertaken for its own sake within conventions a community maintains. It differs: no opponent *within* the run, no win-state supplied by the underlying video game for this purpose (the runner imposes the goal on a game designed for another), and the "contest" is asynchronous. Family resemblance handles this directly: board games and ball games and ring-a-ring-a-roses share no single common feature either, only "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing." Speedrunning sits inside the network — skill, rules, competition, autotelic play are each shared with central cases, and the missing features (a live opponent, an internal win condition) are missing from some accepted games too (patience, a child throwing a ball at a wall). Verdict on this method: yes, a game — not by definition satisfied but by resemblance dense enough that withholding the word would need a purpose. A community that needed to exclude it (say, for a tournament charter) could draw a boundary for that purpose; boundaries are drawn, not found.

## [Heidegger](../eras/05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md)

**1. The broken hammer.**
Mid-task, absorbed in hammering, the carpenter does not contemplate the hammer at all: it shows up as *ready-to-hand* — transparent equipment within a web of assignments (hammer-for-nails-for-joining-for-the-cabinet), its being exhausted by its involvement. When it breaks, involvement is interrupted and the hammer becomes conspicuous: it now shows up as *present-at-hand*, a mere object with weight, a cracked handle, occupying space — stared at rather than used. Heidegger's claim is that the second stance is *derivative*: the detached, theoretical regarding of things as objects with properties is what remains when the primary relation — skilled, concerned involvement — breaks down or is deliberately suspended. Science's object-world is a privative modification of the workshop world, not the other way round. Hence philosophy's traditional starting point (a subject inspecting objects) studies a secondary phenomenon and mistakes it for the foundation. The breakdown is thus methodologically precious to him: it is in disturbance that the ordinarily invisible structure of the ready-to-hand world lights up.

**2. Ownmost, non-relational, unsurpassable.**
Death is *ownmost*: it is in each case mine — no one can take my dying from me or die it in my place; it is the possibility in which my being is wholly at stake for me alone. *Non-relational*: in facing it, all relations to others fall away as unable to share or relieve it — others can accompany, none can substitute. *Unsurpassable* (not to be outstripped): every other possibility lies on this side of it; it is the limit that bounds the whole of Dasein's possibilities and can be neither postponed indefinitely nor passed beyond. *Anticipating* death differs from *expecting* it as one expects an event: expectation treats death as a future actuality that will arrive someday, locatable in the world's calendar, and so keeps it at arm's length — precisely the They's tranquillization: "one dies, eventually, but for the moment not yet, and not me in particular." Anticipation (running ahead into the possibility) holds death as *possibility now*: it individualizes Dasein, strips the alibis of das Man, and discloses its other possibilities as finite, its own, and to be taken over — the core of authenticity, together with resoluteness and the call of conscience.

**3. The empty-authenticity objection.**
The objection: authenticity, as analyzed, is purely formal — own your finitude, take over your thrownness, resolutely choose. Nothing in the structure discriminates among *contents*: a person could resolutely own a cruel or criminal project, and the analysis would have to call it authentic. A philosophy of existence that cannot condemn the resolute villain seems morally bankrupt, or at best morally silent where it pretends to depth. The defender's reply: *Being and Time* offers an existential *analytic*, not a moral code — it describes the structure within which any choosing, good or bad, occurs, as grammar describes speech without dictating sentences; expecting it to deliver moral content confuses categories, and later Heidegger (and sympathetic readers) point to being-with, conscience, and the later ethics-adjacent writings as supplements. Is the reply enough? As a defense of the book's *project*, largely yes — formal analyses are not refuted by lacking what they never claimed. As a defense of Heidegger's *philosophy as a whole*, critics judge it insufficient: the silence about content is not neutral in a thinker who filled the silence politically as he did, and Levinas's charge — that the ethical relation to the Other is subordinated to ontology — names the structural reason. The entry presents this among the most discussed gaps; the debate is not settled.

**4. Being-in-the-world against Descartes.**
The Cartesian picture: a subject — a thinking thing — exists on one side, an extended world on the other, and knowledge requires a bridge: prove the external world exists, prove the senses connect the two realms. The familiar problem generated is *skepticism about the external world*, with its industry of proofs and refutations. Heidegger claims the problem simply fails to arise on his account, because its terms are artefacts of a false starting point. Dasein is *being-in-the-world* as a single structure: there is no worldless subject first, wondering whether a world exists; we find ourselves always already involved with things and others in a meaningful whole, and the subject-object split is a later abstraction from that prior unity (the present-at-hand stance of Q1 generalized into a metaphysics). Hence Kant's "scandal of philosophy" — that no proof of the external world had been given — is inverted: the scandal is that proofs keep being *demanded* for what was never coherently in doubt. Whether dissolving the problem counts as solving skepticism, or as declining it, is the obvious counter-question, and readers divide on it.

**5. Application: the river as standing-reserve.**
Administered solely as hydroelectric capacity, the river shows up only as *Bestand* — standing-reserve: flow rates to be banked, head of pressure to be ordered on call, its valley's meanings (a place to swim, a sacred site, an ecosystem, a landscape that tells a town who it is) registering purely as obstacles or externalities to the one disclosure. This is *enframing* (*Gestell*): a way of revealing that orders nature in advance as stock to be optimized — and, crucially, orders *us* too, into the managers and consumers of stock. The danger, for Heidegger, is not pollution or ugliness and not machinery as such — a hydroelectric plant is not his villain, and he is explicit that the essence of technology is nothing technological. The danger is *monopoly of disclosure*: that enframing crowds out every other way things can show up (his counter-word is *poiesis*, bringing-forth, the way a craft or a poem lets something emerge rather than challenging it forth), until even human beings appear only as human resources and we lose the capacity to experience ourselves as receivers of a world rather than its orderers. A peasant's bridge and a power station may occupy the same river; what differs is the revealing each belongs to. Critics find the practical upshot — *Gelassenheit*, releasement — thin; the diagnosis is what the question asks for.

## [Sartre](../eras/05-20th-century/jean-paul-sartre.md)

*The study guide [Sartre, Existentialism](sartre-existentialism.md) covers the lecture and the Being and Nothingness selections in full.*

**1. The paper-knife contrast.**
A paper knife is made to an artisan's prior concept: its purpose (cutting paper) and nature are fixed before the object exists — essence precedes existence. Traditional theology made man the same way: an artefact of God, manufactured to a divine blueprint, with a human nature given in advance. Sartre's atheism removes the artisan: there is no God to hold the concept, and no heaven of ideas to supply one, so nothing precedes a human life to define what it must be. The human being first *exists* — is thrown into the world, surges forth — and only afterwards becomes something, by what he does: essence is the accumulated sediment of choices, "man is nothing else but what he makes of himself." What fills the gap left by the absent blueprint is the subject's own project: the future-directed choices, made in anguish and without excuse, by which a person continually drafts the nature he will have had. Nothing guarantees the draft; that groundlessness is not a defect of the view but its first principle.

**2. The waiter and the wired: two bad faiths.**
The café waiter plays at being a waiter *as a thing is a thing*: movements a little too precise, identity poured without remainder into the role, as if a waiter were a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell. He denies his *freedom* — the transcendence by which he is never exhausted by any role — fleeing into facticity-as-essence. The claim "I couldn't help it, that's just how I'm wired" makes the same flight from the other side: it denies freedom by collapsing the self into its given determinants — temperament, upbringing, wiring — treating the for-itself as an in-itself. Conversely, "circumstances don't touch the real me" denies *facticity*, pretending to a freedom unconditioned by body, past, and situation. Bad faith is always the denial of one pole of a being that is irreducibly both. And it is not simply lying: in a lie, deceiver and deceived are two people and the truth is safely known by one of them; in bad faith the same consciousness must both know and not know, which is possible only because consciousness is self-distancing — non-thetically aware of what it thetically denies. Whether that mechanism fully explains self-deception remains, as the entry notes, a standard and productive objection.

**3. Does radical freedom trivialise oppression?**
The objection: if the prisoner is free in the stance he takes, then oppression loses its sting in the theory — chains change nothing essential, resistance and submission become equally "choices," and the doctrine seems to blame victims for their attitude while letting oppressors off. Sartre's best reply begins with *facticity*: freedom was never the power to get what one wants; the prisoner does not choose his chains, his cell, or the menu of his options — he is free only in the meaning those givens take within his projects, and Sartre never denies the givens are crushing. The later work then rebuilds the missing weight structurally: in the *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, *scarcity* makes people rivals from the start, and the *practico-inert* — past praxis hardened into institutions, markets, built environments — constrains the living like an alien force, so that freedom operates inside counter-finalities it did not choose. Assessment: the reply saves the doctrine's coherence, but critics judge that the early rhetoric ("condemned to be free," slavery no bar to freedom) genuinely outran the qualifications, and the later Sartre is best read as partly conceding the point. The entry presents the coherence of radical freedom as the perennial objection; it remains perennial.

**4. Application: "I'm just not a maths person."**
*Facticity* here includes: the student's actual history with mathematics (past teaching, past failures, grades), present ability as it stands today, temperament, and the real costs of study — all genuinely given, none chosen this morning. *Bad faith* enters in the grammatical move from history to essence: "I'm just not a maths person" converts a contingent, revisable record into a fixed nature — an inkwell-self — whose function is to decide the future in advance and excuse the student from the choice. It is the waiter inverted: identity adopted as thinghood precisely where a decision is due. *Anguish*, on Sartre's analysis, would be the lucid moment underneath the remark: the dizziness of seeing that nothing — not the past record, not the label — actually decides whether she tries; that even declining to try is a choice for which she is responsible, and that the label is something she is *doing*, held in place daily, not something she simply has. Sartre's point is not that trying guarantees success (ability and teaching are real facticity) but that the meaning of the facticity — handicap, challenge, or verdict — is hers. Other self-descriptions ("I'm not a languages person," "I can't draw") submit to the same analysis.

## [Beauvoir](../eras/05-20th-century/simone-de-beauvoir.md)

*The study guide [The Second Sex](beauvoir-second-sex.md) treats the Introduction and the opening of Volume II in detail.*

**1. Becoming a woman, beyond Sartre.**
The application of "existence precedes essence" is direct: there is no feminine essence, no eternal womanly nature that female existence must express; what a woman is, is made — "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Femininity is the sediment of a formation, not a blueprint. Beauvoir's departure from Sartre is in the weight she gives the *situation* doing the forming. Sartre's freedom, at least in the early work, can sound as if it meets facticity on equal terms; Beauvoir insists the situation is not neutral material: a girl's world is organized to train her *toward immanence* — dolls and domestic play rehearsing maintenance rather than projects, praise for pleasing rather than achieving, myths and schooling that present transcendence as male — so that by the time she "chooses," the choosing self has been shaped to experience herself as a relative being, defined by relation to men. The becoming is therefore not a free self-invention performed on a given body; it is a lifelong negotiation with a situation that has interests in its outcome. That asymmetry — freedom real, but unequally conditioned — is her clearest advance on the more individualist early Sartre, and the seed of later feminist accounts of socialization.

**2. Why the Other-position is so durable.**
Her answer has several interlocking parts. First, the structure: man sets himself up as both the positive and the neutral — the Subject, the Absolute, the default human — and woman is defined *relatively*, as the Other, the inessential opposite; alterity here is not reciprocal, because she does not return the compliment by constituting him as Other in an organized counter-world. Second, the historical sociology: unlike other oppressed groups, women have been *dispersed* among men — divided by class, race, and household, living with the oppressor, lacking the shared workplaces, territory, and institutions through which a group forges a "we" and revolts; there is no women's ghetto or proletariat to become conscious as a group. Third, *complicity*: freedom is risky and immanence is safe — economic dependence and the rewards of pleasing offer real temptations to surrender transcendence, and the situation profits from the surrender. Durability is thus overdetermined: ideological (myths of the Eternal Feminine), material (dispersion and dependence), and existential (the temptation, open to everyone, to flee freedom). None of this is destiny in her telling — it is historical analysis of why a position that should be unstable has been stabilised — but it explains why formal freedom "for everyone" coexists with a caste-like outcome.

**3. From my freedom to others': the derivation in *The Ethics of Ambiguity*.**
The steps, compressed: (1) My freedom is not a possession but a movement — it exists only in projecting toward ends, disclosing a world. (2) Those projects need a future and a field: they get their meaning from a world that outlasts my moment and from others who can take them up, contest them, carry them further; a freedom alone in a world of things would have nothing to will *toward* that could matter. (3) Therefore, to will my own freedom *concretely* — not as an empty slogan — is to will the conditions under which freedom exists at all, which include other free people: willing myself free commits me to willing the freedom of others, and oppression, which treats freedoms as things, is the central moral evil. The step critics most question is the bridge in (2)→(3): that my projects' *meaningfulness* depends on others' freedom may show I need others to be free the way I need an audience, without yet showing I am *obligated* to will the freedom of those whose unfreedom costs my projects nothing. Beauvoir's defenders reply that the dependence is constitutive, not instrumental — a world of unfree others cannot return my acts to me as meaningful at all. The entry records that whether the derivation succeeds remains the book's central scholarly debate; it is presented here without a verdict.

**4. The care-ethics objection and the reply.**
The objection: Beauvoir's axis — transcendence (projects, creation, risk) honored, immanence (repetition, maintenance) pitied — is a *masculine* standard. Care work, domestic labor, and the sustaining of life are devalued just as patriarchy devalues them, and women are implicitly told liberation means becoming like men: the activities women actually perform are treated as the problem rather than the conditions under which they perform them. Her strongest reply, anticipated in the text: the target is *confinement and compulsion*, not the activities. Housework figures in her analysis as Sisyphean because it is imposed, unpaid, privatized, and permitted to swallow a whole life — not because feeding people is inherently beneath a free being. Care chosen as a project, shared, socially supported, and combined with other forms of transcendence is a different phenomenon from care as a caste assignment with no exit. Whether the reply fully acquits the book is genuinely contested — critics reply that her rhetoric repeatedly slides from condemning confinement to condescension toward the confined life, and her treatment of motherhood is a perennial flashpoint — but the distinction between an activity and its compulsory imposition is hers, and any fair statement of the objection must meet it.

**5. Application: parental leave that ends promotion.**
On paper the policy is neutral; in structure, the case maps cleanly. *Structures of immanence*: the workplace's ideal-worker norm (unbroken availability as the real promotion criterion) confines the leave-taker — statistically still mostly mothers — to the maintenance sphere while reserving the project sphere (visibility, advancement, the work that counts as transcendence) to those who never leave; the leave-taker returns to find her projects reassigned and her trajectory quietly closed, a confinement produced by incentives rather than by any rule anyone will defend aloud. *Complicity*: it appears on several sides — the employee who declines leave she wants, fearing the penalty, surrenders freedom under real pressure (Beauvoir's analysis treats this as temptation under duress, not blame); colleagues and managers who "just follow the numbers" let the structure decide while experiencing themselves as neutral. *Willing others' freedom concretely* would require changing the conditions, not exhorting the individuals: promotion clocks that pause without prejudice, leave actively taken by senior men until the signal dies, evaluation criteria audited against leave-takers' outcomes, and cover arrangements that keep projects alive rather than redistributing them permanently. The Beauvoirian test is whether a parent can take the leave *and remain a subject with a future in the institution* — whether freedom's conditions, not merely its formal permission, have been equalised. Other institutional cases ( tenure clocks, shift bidding) map the same way.

## [Thomson](../eras/05-20th-century/judith-jarvis-thomson.md)

**1. The violinist and the limits of the right to life.**
Grant the premise: the foetus is a person with a full right to life. Thomson's violinist: you wake kidnapped, plugged into a famous violinist whose life only your kidneys can sustain for nine months. He has a right to life — but it does not follow that he has a right to *use your body*, nor that you act unjustly in unplugging yourself. The general point: a right to life is a right not to be killed (unjustly), not a right to everything needed to live; if it were the latter, anyone whose survival required your body's continued service would own that service. Hence a foetus's right to life, even granted, does not by itself settle abortion — the pregnant woman's right to decide what happens in and to her body is a separate claim the personhood premise never touches. The *decency* distinction then limits the conclusion's tone, not its logic: Thomson distinguishes what morality *requires* (what others may demand of you as of right) from what a decent person would do. Unplugging after one hour for a trivial inconvenience might be callous, even indecent — she allows such judgments — without being *unjust*, because the violinist had no claim to the ninth month, let alone the first. Abortion law and abortion ethics, on her framing, mostly enforce the claim side, and the argument's target is the inference from "person" to "may not be unplugged."

**2. The disanalogy objection and the reply.**
Strongest version: the violinist was imposed by kidnappers on a stranger. In most pregnancies the foetus is the woman's own child, not a stranger; kinship generates special obligations (parents owe their children sustenance they owe no one else); and where conception was voluntary, she is *responsible for the foetus's dependence* — she did what foreseeably brought a dependent person into existence needing her body, as a driver who injures a pedestrian owes aid she owes no bystander. Thomson's likely reply, tracking her own restrictions: responsibility and special obligation are real, and she herself narrowed the argument's scope — the violinist strictly models cases like rape, and other cases need other arguments (she supplies some, e.g. against the claim that voluntarily opening a window to burglars grants them occupancy — people-seeds and burglar analogies test exactly the responsibility premise). But note what the objection concedes: it no longer rests on the bare right to life; it must defend an additional premise about what responsibility for dependence *creates* — and that premise, applied generally, would make anyone who foreseeably causes dependence liable to bodily service, a principle its proponents rarely accept elsewhere. Judgment: the differences *narrow* the violinist's direct scope rather than defeat its logical point — the distinction between a right to life and a right to another's body survives; which pregnancies fall under which analysis is then case-by-case work, and scholars divide over how much of the field the narrowed argument still covers.

**3. What Thomson's variants isolated.**
Foot's 1967 tram case put a *driver* to the choice: kill five ahead, or steer onto a spur and kill one. It was built to explore the doctrine of double effect — intended versus merely foreseen harm — inside a single forced choice where the agent must do *something* and both options are drivings of his own vehicle. Thomson's variants changed the agent and the mechanism. The *bystander* at a switch is under no compulsion of role: she may simply walk away and let five die; yet most judge turning permissible for her too — isolating that the permission does not rest on the driver's special plight. The *footbridge* variant keeps numbers, outcome, and "one dies that five live" identical while changing only the means — pushing one man as a brake — and the judgment flips to impermissible. So the contrast isolates the variable Foot's case could not: not harm intended versus foreseen alone (the bystander foresees the one's death in both switch and footbridge), but something about *how* the one is used — redirected threat versus new harm imposed on a person as a means. Why no agreed principle yet explains both judgments: candidate principles (double effect, doing/allowing, rights against being used, Kamm's Principle of Permissible Harm) each capture one intuition at the cost of strained verdicts on nearby variants, and Thomson's own 2008 defection (Q4) suggests even the switch judgment is less secure than the consensus assumed. The problem proper is that explanatory gap.

**4. Application: the surgeon, and the 2008 reversal.**
The surgeon case is the footbridge with scrubs: five dying patients, one healthy patient whose organs would save all five. On Thomson's view what blocks the numbers is the healthy patient's *rights*: he has a claim not to be killed and used as a resource, and that claim is not a quantity to be outweighed by five similar claims summed — each of the five has a right to life, but none has a right to *his* body, any more than the violinist had a right to yours. Aggregation settles what may be done only among options that infringe no one's claims; killing the one is not such an option, so the count never gets started. (Compare Singer's opposite handling in his Q4.) Her 2008 reversal in "Turning the Trolley" bears on the bystander cousin of this case: she there argued *against her earlier self* that the bystander may not turn the trolley either — because turning imposes a death on the one to save the five, whereas the driver, already trapped in a choice between harms his vehicle will inflict, stands differently. On the 2008 view the surgeon and the bystander align (both impermissible), and the switch intuition is the one to be explained away; on her earlier view switch and surgeon divide. Either way the surgeon verdict is constant — the reversal moves the trolley's border, not the operating theater's.

## [Singer](../eras/05-20th-century/peter-singer.md)

**1. The drowning child, exactly.**
The principle: **if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it.** At the pond the application is uncontroversial — muddy clothes are not of comparable moral importance against a child's life. The global extension proceeds by denying the moral relevance of the two apparent differences. *Distance*: proximity cannot matter morally in itself — we accept duties to distant strangers in rescue cases generally, and modern aid is effective at distance; the drowning child's need does not weaken with mileage. *Other bystanders*: that millions of others could also give does not reduce my share, just as a crowd around the pond would not excuse each watcher; if everyone who could help gave a modest amount, the bad outcome would be prevented at a cost to each far below the comparable-importance threshold. Where does the principle stop demanding? At the threshold written into it: when further giving would require sacrificing something of *comparable* moral importance — one's own or one's dependents' genuine needs and central goods. Singer reads that threshold high (well beyond conventional charity, toward giving until the margin), but it is a real limit in the principle, not an afterthought: the demand is severe, not literally unlimited.

**2. Demandingness and the integrity objection.**
The objection, sharpened by Williams: a morality that claims every resource above the comparable-importance threshold leaves no room for the personal projects, attachments, and ground projects that constitute a recognisable life and give a person integrity; an agent who must always calculate impartially is alienated from the commitments that make him *someone*. Therefore, the objection concludes, such a morality cannot be the truth about how anyone ought to live. Singer's reply is a refusal of the inference: showing that a moral demand is *demanding* does not show it is *false*. "Morality is too hard" is a fact about us, not about the principle; the pond case fixes the intuition (we do not say the passer-by's projects excuse him), and distance/bystanders were shown irrelevant in Q1 — so the honest options are to accept the demand or find a morally relevant difference, not to plead cost. Assessment: the reply is logically secure — truth and livability are different properties — but it leaves the objector's deeper point standing as a question about moral theory's office: whether a standard no one can fully live by still functions as morality rather than as a standing reproach, and whether partial compliance (Singer's own practical program: give a serious, growing percentage) is a stable resting place or a compromise the principle itself condemns. The dispute, as the entry says, remains genuinely open.

**3. Application: painful tests on pigs for a cosmetic.**
*Which interests count*: the pigs' interest in not suffering — sentience is the criterion, so their pain counts, and counts *equally* with comparable human pain: equal consideration weighs interests, not species. *How they are weighed*: impartially — an hour of severe pig suffering is not discounted because it is a pig's, and it must be set against the human interests actually served, here the marginal benefit of a new cosmetic: a novel shade or formulation where adequate products exist. *What would have to be true for the tests to pass*: the benefits would have to be of comparable moral weight to the suffering imposed — if the "cosmetic" were in fact a treatment preventing serious human pain or disfigurement with no alternative test route, the calculus changes character; for a merely preferential product, trivial human gains against severe animal suffering fails his test decisively, which is why cosmetics testing is his paradigm of speciesism in practice: the same suffering inflicted for a medical necessity would at least enter the scales with weight on both sides. Note what is not claimed: not that pig and human lives have equal value, and not that all testing fails — only that comparable interests get comparable weight, and trivial interests lose to vital ones whoever holds them.

**4. The surgeon, pulled apart.**
Same theater as Thomson's Q4; opposite machinery. *Thomson's framework*: the healthy patient has rights — claims against being killed and used — that are not quantities in a sum; since none of the five has a right to his organs, the option is excluded before counting begins, and the five's deaths, though worse in aggregate, are not the surgeon's to prevent by this means. *Singer's principle*: ask only what prevents the most bad without a comparable sacrifice; five deaths against one, and the one killed is, in outcome terms, the lesser bad — so the bare principle, honestly applied, points toward operating, and Singer's tradition bites that bullet at the level of the principle while noting that a *rule* permitting surgeons to harvest patients would, in the real world, produce terror, collapsed trust in hospitals, and far more death — consequences the principle itself counts, since it evaluates outcomes, and which in any realistic application reverse the verdict for actual institutions. The premise on which the disagreement turns: whether moral reasoning is constrained by *claims individuals hold against being used* that function independently of aggregate outcomes (Thomson), or whether outcomes — impartially weighed, including systemic ones — are the whole of the matter (Singer). Every other difference (intuitions, hypotheticals, bullet-biting) is downstream of that one.

## [Rawls](../eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md)

*The study guide [A Theory of Justice](rawls-theory-of-justice.md) reconstructs §§1–4 and the difference principle in full.*

**1. From the veil to the two principles.**
The parties in the original position choose principles for the basic structure behind a veil that excludes knowledge of their class, talents, health, sex, race, and conception of the good, while permitting general knowledge of society, economics, and psychology, plus the assurance that they must live under their choice, whoever they turn out to be. The exclusion does the impartiality work: no principle can be tailored to a known advantage. The permission matters as much: because the parties know they have *some* conception of the good and will need liberty to pursue it, equal basic liberties come first and lexically prior — no one would trade freedom of conscience for income they might not need. For the distribution of the rest, Rawls argues the parties reason by *maximin*: with no basis for probabilities, with outcomes that decide a whole life, and with unacceptable losses possible at the bottom, rational caution protects the worst position rather than maximizing expected value — yielding fair equality of opportunity, and then the difference principle: inequalities only where they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. Expected-utility reasoning (Harsanyi's route, Q2) would need probabilities the veil deliberately makes unavailable and stakes it makes irresponsible to invent.

**2. Harsanyi versus the strains of commitment.**
Harsanyi's objection: equiprobability is the rational default behind a veil — you are as likely to be anyone — so rational parties maximize *expected* utility, which means average utilitarianism; Rawls's maximin simply builds in extreme risk-aversion, a psychological quirk dressed as rationality. Rawls's reply runs through the *strains of commitment*: the principles are not a bet but a public charter for a whole life, chosen once, irrevocably, which each citizen must be able to honor *even if she turns out worst off*. A party who gambled on the average and drew the bottom could not comply with principles that sacrificed her basic good to others' surplus — the agreement would unravel in practice, and the parties know it in advance. Which side better fits a one-shot, irrevocable choice? Rawls's considerations are precisely calibrated to those features: where a choice is repeatable, probability-talk and averaging earn their keep (portfolios, policies); where it is singular, uninsurable, and defines the chooser's whole life-prospect, guaranteeing an acceptable floor before optimizing above it tracks how rational agents actually treat ruin-risk. Harsanyi's model fits the mathematics of lotteries; the question is whether constitutional choice *is* a lottery. That — not the arithmetic — is the real disagreement, and it remains live.

**3. Wilt Chamberlain against the basic structure.**
Nozick's example: start from any distribution the patterned theorist calls just (D1). Fans then voluntarily pay small sums to watch Wilt Chamberlain, who ends up rich: D2 is unequal, yet arose from D1 by steps no one forced and no one can call unjust. If D2 is unjust, the state must continually interfere — taxing, forbidding, redistributing — to restore the pattern; hence patterned principles are incompatible with liberty, and justice must be *historical* (entitlement by just acquisition and transfer), not end-state. Rawls's reply relocates the subject: his principles do not govern each transaction and were never meant to freeze a distribution moment by moment; they govern the *basic structure* — the background institutions of property, contract, taxation, and opportunity within which transactions occur. Voluntary exchanges inside a just background are fine; the structure is assessed, and adjusted over time, by whether the whole system keeps inequalities working to the least advantaged's benefit and opportunities genuinely fair. Chamberlain keeps his gate receipts; the tax-and-transfer background that makes the league, the contracts, and the fans' wages possible is what answers to the two principles. Whether this fully meets Nozick — who denies the background can be prised apart from the transactions it enables — is the standing libertarian–contractualist dispute; the entry lists the self-ownership objection among the live ones.

**4. Why Rawls alone doesn't set your charity budget.**
The division is deliberate, not a gap. Rawls's principles are principles for *institutions* — the basic structure that distributes rights, opportunities, and the social bases of welfare — chosen because institutions are the pervasive, coercive background no one can exit. Singer's pond principle is a principle of *individual conduct*: what this agent, facing this preventable bad, ought to do now. Rawls's theory therefore underdetermines personal giving twice over: it does not rank charities, and it does not even fix what an individual owes when institutions fall short of justice (Rawls assigns individuals a natural duty to support just institutions and further just arrangements, but deliberately no distributive formula for private income). Is that a defect? From Singer's side it looks like an evasion — children drown while the theory waits for institutions. From Rawls's side it is a *division of moral labor*: expecting private charity to do the basic structure's work lets unjust institutions offload their obligations onto the conscientious, arbitrarily taxing the generous. A complete practical ethics plausibly needs both levels; the two entries' capstone contrast (and Singer's Q4) shows the levels can also conflict. Which level has priority when they do is a genuine question the learning path's capstone essay asks you to argue, not one either text settles for you.

**5. Application: elite sport funded from the poorest districts' libraries.**
Work in lexical order. *First principle* — equal basic liberties: library cuts do not obviously infringe a basic liberty (Rawls's list runs to conscience, speech, political liberty, rule of law), so the first principle is likely satisfied, though if the cuts foreseeably hollow out the *fair value* of political liberty or basic education needed for citizenship, they begin to bite — note it and proceed. *Second principle, first part* — fair equality of opportunity: libraries in poor districts are opportunity infrastructure; cutting them where opportunity is already thinnest, to fund a facility whose benefits skew to the already advantaged, presumptively *worsens* fair equality of opportunity, and this part is lexically prior to the difference principle, so "rising total welfare" cannot be reached as a justification until it is cleared. *Difference principle*: even if opportunity were untouched, the arrangement must work to the *greatest benefit of the least advantaged*; a policy whose costs fall on the worst-off districts and whose gains are aggregate welfare fails outright — rising totals are exactly the utilitarian currency the difference principle refuses. What would make it permissible: fund the facilities from revenues drawn progressively, or from districts that can bear it, *or* show the package leaves the least advantaged better off than any alternative (e.g. the facilities genuinely anchor regeneration whose first beneficiaries are those districts) *and* restore the opportunity loss — say, expanded library provision funded within the same scheme. As stated, in lexical order, it fails at fair equality of opportunity before the difference principle is even reached.
