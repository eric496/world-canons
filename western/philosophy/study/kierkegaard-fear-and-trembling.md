# Study Guide: Søren Kierkegaard, *Fear and Trembling* (1843)

**Work:** *Fear and Trembling* (*Frygt og Bæven*) · **Author:** Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855), writing as **Johannes de silentio** · **First published:** 16 October 1843 · **What this guide covers:** the whole work — Preface, Tuning Up, Eulogy on Abraham, the three Problemata, and Epilogue — with argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings. The [learning path](../learning-path.md) assigns Problemata I–II as the core; the rest is short, and this guide's reading plan marks what is core and what completes it.

## About the work

*Fear and Trembling* was published on 16 October 1843, the same day as *Repetition*, in the first blaze of Kierkegaard's pseudonymous authorship — the year of *Either/Or*. Its full title is *Fear and Trembling: Dialectical Lyric, by Johannes de Silentio*. Every part of that title works. It is a *lyric*: a meditation in poetic prose, not a treatise. It is *dialectical*: it thinks by opposition — ethical against religious, tragic hero against knight of faith, resignation against faith. And it is by **Johannes de silentio**, "John of Silence," a pseudonym who is himself a character: a man who can describe faith with unmatched precision and who tells us, repeatedly and without false modesty, that he cannot perform it. "I cannot make the movement of faith," Johannes confesses; he can understand Abraham, he says, only by being amazed by him.

The pseudonymity is not decoration, and this guide will keep saying so: Johannes's conclusions are Johannes's. Kierkegaard published signed upbuilding discourses alongside the pseudonymous works, and in his later accounting of the authorship (*The Point of View*) presented the pseudonyms as voices a reader must pass through, not doctrines he was delivering. What *Fear and Trembling* asks — whether faith, as Abraham had it, can be thought at all inside the ethical philosophy of Kierkegaard's Copenhagen — is a question Kierkegaard spent his career inside; what Johannes *answers* must be attributed to Johannes.

The occasion is Genesis 22: God commands Abraham to sacrifice Isaac, the son of the promise, on Mount Moriah. Abraham saddles the ass, travels three days, binds Isaac, raises the knife — and receives Isaac back. Behind the book stands Kierkegaard's broken engagement to Regine Olsen (1841): he had renounced the person he loved most, under a kind of necessity he could not explain to her or to Copenhagen, and his journals return to Abraham for years — one later entry says, in effect, that had he had faith he would have remained with Regine. Scholars debate how directly the book should be read through the engagement; that it resonates is not in doubt, and Johannes's own young man, who renounces his princess (Problema I), is the resonance on the page.

The book's targets are named in its structure. Against Hegelian philosophy — the "System" of his teachers, for whom faith is a primitive stage to be mediated and surpassed by knowledge — Johannes insists faith is not a first immediacy to be gone beyond but the highest passion, arrived at, if at all, after reflection, and kept only in fear and trembling. Against comfortable Danish "Christendom," for whom Abraham is a Sunday-school exemplar, he insists Abraham is either a murderer or a paradox — and that the age has made him cheap by refusing to feel the difference.

## How to use this guide

Read each part of *Fear and Trembling* first, then the corresponding entry below. The book is short — about 130 pages — and unlike most works in this library it should be read twice: once for the shock, once for the structure. The argument reconstructions in §2 — the teleological suspension of the ethical, the absolute duty toward God, and Abraham's silence — are Johannes's three questions taken in order; master them as a set, since each presupposes the last. Work the checkpoint questions after your second reading, covering the answers (in §5) until attempted.

Two reading disciplines matter more here than accuracy of detail. First: track *who is speaking* — every claim below is Johannes's unless flagged, and Johannes stands deliberately outside the faith he describes. Second: resist converting the book into a doctrine. Johannes offers Abraham as a *paradox* to be understood or confessed unintelligible, not as a rule to be applied; §4 lists the disasters that follow from treating him as either.

## 1. Part-by-part guide

### Preface

**Summary.** Johannes opens by declining the credentials of his age: the present author, he says, is no philosopher, only a kind of poet and amateur who writes introductions to books he cannot write. The target is the intellectual fashion of Hegelian Copenhagen, in which doubt and faith alike have become trifles everyone claims to have dispatched in order to "go further": everyone wants to go beyond doubting everything and beyond faith, and no one wants to stop with either. Johannes professes the older reverence — the ancients treated faith as a task for a whole lifetime, attained late if at all. The Preface sets the book's irony: a self-confessed outsider will test whether the age that claims to have surpassed faith can even *describe* it.

**Key passages.** The opening paragraphs (the age's bargain-basement intellectuality); the closing profession (faith as the task of a lifetime, not a starting point to surpass).

**Main argument.** Bookkeeping for the whole work: if faith were a primitive immediacy, surpassing it would be progress; if it is instead the highest passion of inwardness, "going further" is the age's way of never arriving. The Epilogue returns to exactly this.

### Tuning Up (*Stemning*)

**Summary.** Before the eulogy, an attunement: Johannes retells the Abraham story four times, and in each retelling Abraham fails differently. In the first, Abraham resolves to appear to Isaac as a monster — an idolater acting on his own lust — so that Isaac, hating his father, will not lose faith in God; Abraham sacrifices his own standing to preserve the son's faith. In the second, Abraham obeys, but when the ram appears his eye is darkened forever: he cannot forget the demand, and never again knows joy. In the third, Abraham cannot forgive *himself* for having been willing, and begs God's pardon for the readiness that God commanded. In the fourth, on the mountain Abraham's hand trembles and his face shows despair; Isaac sees it, and loses his faith. The sequence ends with the image of a mother weaning her child: she blackens her breast so the child will not grieve for what it loses — blessed the one who needed no such shift, and blessed the one who kept the child close enough not to need it.

**Key passages.** The four variations in sequence; the weaning image that closes the section.

**Main argument.** The variations are diagnostic. Each failed Abraham breaks at a different joint — he protects Isaac's faith at the cost of his own honesty (I), obeys without receiving Isaac back in joy (II), treats obedience as sin (III), or lets the terror show and so destroys the very faith he meant to serve (IV). The biblical Abraham, by contrast, believed *throughout*: he did not doubt, did not explain, did not despair visibly, and received Isaac back with more joy than the first time. Johannes's negative space defines faith before he praises it: faith is not obedience plus anguish management; it is a single, unbroken relation sustained where every human support fails.

### Eulogy on Abraham (*Lovtale over Abraham*)

**Summary.** The eulogy asks what Abraham's greatness consists in. Not in results — world history judges by outcomes, and by outcomes Abraham either failed (a would-be murderer) or was lucky (a ram happened by). Not in wisdom or power. Abraham is great because he *believed*: expecting the impossible against every calculation — that God who demanded Isaac would not finally take him, or would give him back — and believing it, Johannes stresses, "for this life": Abraham believed he would grow old in the land, blessed, remembered, with Isaac beside him, not that he would be compensated in eternity for a loss accepted here. He is the father of faith because he held God's promise and God's demand together, at the point where they annihilate each other, without letting either go.

**Key passages.** The survey of greatness (by power, by wisdom, by suffering — and Abraham's distinct kind); the insistence that Abraham believed for this life; the closing sentence's image of Abraham as the one who, having expected the impossible, became the father of all who believe.

**Main argument.** Two doctrines are planted here that the Problemata harvest. First, the absurd: Abraham's belief is not probability-maximizing or hope-against-hope; its object (Isaac restored) is humanly impossible, and he believes it "by virtue of the absurd." Second, temporality: faith is directed at the finite, at *this* life — which distinguishes it at once from resignation, the knightly renunciation (Problema I) that gives the finite world up in exchange for eternity. Abraham did not renounce Isaac; that is the whole scandal and the whole point.

### Problema I: Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?

**Summary.** The book's core. **The ethical** (*det Ethiske*) is the universal: the moral law as it binds everyone, the sphere in which the single individual's task is to annul his particularity and express the universal — for Kierkegaard's Hegelian contemporaries, the ethical order of family, civil society, and state (*Sittlichkeit*). The ethical is itself teleological: it has its *telos* (end) in itself, and the individual has his telos *in* the universal. A **teleological suspension of the ethical** would be a case in which the single individual, as the particular, stands *higher* than the universal — justified not by the universal's own demand but by an absolute relation to something outside it.

Johannes's test case divides humanity. The **tragic hero** — Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia so the fleet may sail, Jephthah his daughter under a vow, Brutus condemning his sons — surrenders the dearest thing *within* the ethical, for the ethical's own higher expression (the nation, the covenant community, the republic). We weep for him; we understand him; he remains inside the universal, and his act can be explained and justified in speech. Abraham is different in kind. No universal end is served by Isaac's death: not the state, not the people's welfare, not even (Johannes insists) God's public glory in any form the community can comprehend. Ethically expressed, Abraham's act is murder; religiously expressed, it is a sacrifice — and between the two expressions there is no mediation. Either Abraham is lost — a murderer whom piety has mislabeled — or there is a paradox: the single individual, in absolute relation to the absolute (God), is higher than the universal.

The chapter then builds the anthropology of the paradox. The **knight of infinite resignation** makes the first movement: he renounces the finite entirely — Johannes's example is a young man who loves a princess hopelessly, and transposes the love into an eternal renunciation, keeping it in pain forever. This movement any strong person can make by his own strength; Johannes says he can make it himself. **Faith** is the second movement, the double movement: having renounced everything, the knight of faith nevertheless believes, by virtue of the absurd, that he will receive the finite back — the young man believes he *will* marry the princess, in this life, against all impossibility. And the knight of faith is outwardly indistinguishable from the most ordinary bourgeois: he looks like a tax collector, takes delight in his dinner, has no crack through which infinity peeps. Abraham made both movements: he raised the knife without renouncing Isaac in his heart — at every moment believing he would not lose him — and received him back. Johannes closes the problema by confessing his limit: he can describe the movements; the second he cannot make.

**Key passages.** The definition of the ethical as the universal and of the suspension; the tragic heroes (Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus) set against Abraham; the young man and the princess (infinite resignation); the knight of faith's bourgeois ordinariness ("he looks exactly like a tax collector"); the confession that the author can make the first movement but not the second.

**Main argument.** Fully reconstructed at §2(a). Note the burden the problema sets itself: Johannes is not arguing that the ethical is low or dispensable — it is "the universal," divine in its own sphere. The paradox is that faith, if Abraham is its father, requires a relation in which the ethical is suspended *teleologically* — suspended toward a higher telos, not abolished — and that this cannot be said in the ethical's own language without becoming a justification of murder. That unsayability drives Problema III.

### Problema II: Is there an absolute duty toward God?

**Summary.** If the ethical is the highest, then duty toward God is only duty *as mediated by* the universal: I honor God by honoring my neighbor, my child, my city — and a putative command from God that violated the universal would thereby prove itself not God's. This is the position Johannes attributes to the philosophy of his age, and it is Kant's position in substance (see below). Problema II denies the mediation. If there is an absolute duty toward God, it cannot be routed through the universal, because then the universal — not God — would be absolute. Abraham's duty to God therefore stands in absolute relation: he must love God in such a way that, relative to that love, his love for Isaac — itself his ethical duty and his deepest inclination — counts, in Johannes's deliberately shocking expression, as hatred by comparison. Yet Abraham is not thereby discharged from the ethical: he does not cease to love Isaac (if he did not love him, there would be no sacrifice at all, only disposal). The paradox tightens: the single individual determines his relation to the universal by his relation to the absolute, reversing the order the ethical demands — and this, Johannes insists, cannot be mediated, explained, or made into a doctrine, because a doctrine would be universal.

The Kantian contrast is the problema's silent center (Johannes's contemporary readers knew it; Kierkegaard's journals make it explicit): for Kant, Abraham ought to have answered the voice — that I must not kill my innocent son is certain; that you, who appear to me, are God, is not certain and can never become so — and ought to have refused. On Kant's principle no suspension is possible, and Abraham is either deceived or a murderer. Johannes's Abraham stands precisely where Kant says no one may stand. §2(b) reconstructs the argument and the objection together.

**Key passages.** The statement of absolute duty and the reversal (the individual determines his relation to the universal through the absolute, not the reverse); the "hatred" of the dearest by comparison with love of God; the insistence that Abraham's love for Isaac is presupposed, not cancelled.

**Main argument.** Reconstructed at §2(b). The problema's contribution beyond Problema I is the positive category: Problema I shows the ethical *suspended*; Problema II names what it is suspended *toward* — an absolute duty that is no part of the ethical system, and whose very existence the ethical philosophy of the age (Kantian and Hegelian alike) denies.

### Problema III: Abraham's silence

**Summary.** The final problema asks the question from the side of speech: was it ethically defensible for Abraham to conceal his undertaking from Sarah, from Eliezer, from Isaac? The ethical demands disclosure — hiddenness is its reproach; what is done in the universal must bear the light. Aesthetics, by contrast, can justify concealment (the intrigue that saves), and Johannes works through cases: Sarah's story in the book of Tobit, where concealment and disclosure are weighed; the legend of Agnete and the merman, in which the merman — a seducer-spirit holding a girl's fate — can be saved if he confesses himself to her in trust, while concealment is the demonic: the hiddenness in which a person encloses himself with his guilt and will not come out. Faust, who doubts and keeps silent, and Shakespeare's Richard III stand as the demonic's portraits: silence as self-imprisonment.

Abraham fits none of the categories. He is not a tragic hero who can weep and be understood (Agamemnon's household comprehends his grief). He is not demonic: he hides nothing for gain, and would speak if speaking could help. He cannot speak, Johannes argues, because speech is translation into the universal, and what he would have to say — that this is a trial, an ordeal, in which the ethical itself is the temptation — cannot be understood by Sarah, Eliezer, or Isaac without making him, in their ears, a madman or a monster. His one utterance, to Isaac's question about the lamb — "God himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering, my son" — is therefore spoken in irony: a sentence true in a sense its hearer cannot receive, speech that reveals by not revealing. Abraham's silence is the silence of the paradox itself, neither ethical disclosure nor demonic concealment — and it is, Johannes notes, the same silence that makes the book's author a man "of silence."

**Key passages.** The ethical demand for disclosure against the aesthetic justification of hiddenness; the merman and Agnete (confession as release from the demonic); Abraham's inability to speak and the temptation being the ethical itself; the word to Isaac as irony.

**Main argument.** Reconstructed at §2(c). The problema completes the book's logic: the suspension (I) toward an absolute duty (II) necessarily produces a silence (III), because every available language — ethical, aesthetic, demonic — mistranslates the paradox. It also produces the book's most portable distinction: concealment is demonic when it encloses the self in guilt; it is something else — unnamed, unclassifiable — when it is borne as suffering for a relation that cannot be said.

### Epilogue

**Summary.** Two pages, and the Preface's polemic completed. Faith, Johannes concludes, is not an aesthetic emotion, not a first immediacy that a higher reflection surpasses: it is the highest passion in a human being, and no generation can inherit it from a previous one — every generation begins primitively, with the same task, and none gets further than the faith of the fathers except by doing the work itself. The age that wants faith cheap, as a preliminary to "going further," resembles a merchant who would sell his goods below cost. The book ends where it began: faith is a task for a lifetime, and the one who works at it in fear and trembling does not need to be told to go further.

**Key passages.** Faith as the highest passion; no generation learns love or faith from another; the closing refusal of "further."

**Main argument.** The Epilogue is the anti-Hegelian signature on the whole: against mediation and historical supersession — categories by which the System filed faith under "surpassed" — Johannes sets a task that is identical in every generation and completable in none. Read it as the frame that keeps the Problemata from becoming a doctrine: a book about the man who could not be systematized ends by refusing to systematize him.

## 2. Argument reconstructions

### (a) The teleological suspension of the ethical (Problema I)

**Johannes's argument, in numbered premises:**

1. The ethical is the universal: it applies to everyone at every moment, and the single individual's ethical task is to express the universal — in family, society, and state — annulling his mere particularity.
2. Within the ethical, the ethical is itself the telos: there is nothing higher for the individual than the universal, and every justification of an act must be statable in the universal's terms (as with the tragic hero, whose sacrifice of the dearest serves the universal itself and can be publicly justified).
3. Abraham's commanded act serves no universal end: no state, people, or public good is advanced by Isaac's death, and ethically expressed the act is simply murder.
4. So either (i) Abraham is a murderer, and Genesis 22 is an edifying lie; or (ii) there exists a standpoint from which the single individual, as the particular, is higher than the universal.
5. That standpoint cannot be another universal (it would fall under premise 1) — it can only be the individual's *absolute relation to the absolute*: his relation to God, which is not mediated by the ethical order.
6. Therefore, if Abraham is the father of faith and not a murderer, there is a **teleological suspension of the ethical**: the ethical, itself a telos, is suspended in relation to a higher telos — not abolished, since Abraham continues to love Isaac and the ethical remains divine in its sphere — and the individual stands justified by a paradox that the universal cannot mediate or comprehend.

**Conclusion.** Faith, on Johannes's analysis, is this paradox: the single individual, after having been in the universal, isolates himself as higher than the universal through an absolute relation to God — and this, not obedience or resignation, is what made Abraham great, and what the age that treats him as a Sunday-school exemplar has ceased to understand.

**One standard objection.** The argument proves too much, and dangerously. If a private relation to God can suspend the universal moral law, then any fanatic who feels commanded can claim Abraham's exemption; the suspension gives no public criterion distinguishing Abraham from a murderer with a voice in his head — Problema II's own Kantian shadow. A test that Abraham passes and the criminal fails is precisely what Johannes refuses to supply, since any test would be universal, and he calls the refusal profundity. Critics from Kant to contemporary moral philosophers answer: a category that cannot in principle be checked is not a category of faith but an abdication of judgment, and its historical yield is persecution and holy crime, not paradoxical saints.

**One reply.** Defenders of Johannes reply that the objection mistakes the book's genre and claim. *Fear and Trembling* is not a permission structure: Johannes nowhere licenses imitation (Abraham is presented as unrepeatable, inimitable, a source of terror — "either Abraham is a murderer, or we are confronted by a paradox"), and he builds in marks the fanatic characteristically lacks: Abraham does not cease to love Isaac (the sacrifice presupposes maximal love, where the fanatic sacrifices what he hates or values cheaply); Abraham gains nothing, explains nothing, recruits no one; and the suspension is teleological — the ethical is restored in the reception of Isaac, not trampled. Whether such marks suffice is genuinely debated: some readers treat them as a real, if non-algorithmic, discrimination; others hold that any honorific reading of Genesis 22 smuggles sanctity into a story of averted child-killing. The library presents the debate; this guide does likewise.

### (b) Absolute duty toward God (Problema II)

**Johannes's argument, in numbered premises:**

1. If the ethical is the highest sphere, then duties toward God are mediated by it: my duty to God just is my universal duty (to child, city, neighbor) considered religiously, and any purported divine command conflicting with the universal refutes itself.
2. Kant's Abraham-judgment follows from premise 1: that I must not kill my innocent son is certain; that the voice commanding it is God's can never be certain; therefore Abraham should have refused the voice.
3. But the mediation of premise 1 makes the universal, not God, the absolute: God's claim on the individual would be admissible only when ratified by the ethical order, which is to say God would be a function of society's morality.
4. Genesis 22, read as faith's founding story, exhibits the contrary structure: Abraham's duty toward God is absolute and unmediated, while his duty toward Isaac remains fully in force as love — the sacrifice is a sacrifice *because* he loves Isaac with his whole soul.
5. Therefore there is an absolute duty toward God: the single individual determines his relation to the universal through his relation to the absolute, reversing the mediate order; and in that relation the ethical duties, though unabolished, are relativized — Abraham, by the standard of comparison Johannes borrows from the Gospels, must "hate" father, son, and his own life *in comparison with* his love of God.

**Conclusion.** Problema II supplies the positive pole that Problema I lacked: the ethical is suspended not into arbitrariness but toward an absolute duty that outranks mediation itself — at the price, fully acknowledged, that such a duty can never be converted into a universal rule without ceasing to exist.

**One standard objection.** Premise 3's fork is a false dilemma for Kant and for the Hegelian alike: mediation is not demotion. Kant's claim is epistemic, not chauvinist — a finite knower *cannot certify* a supernatural command, so moral certainty must govern religious uncertainty; treating that as "society ratifying God" redescribes humility as usurpation. And the comparative "hatred" of premise 5, however hedged, teaches readers to relabel their strongest loves as expendable whenever an unmediated certainty arrives — the pedagogy of every Abrahamic militancy since. On this objection, Johannes has not refuted mediation; he has simply declined the burden of proof that mediation carries.

**One reply.** Johannes's defenders answer that the reply concedes Johannes's point while thinking it refutes him: of course the paradox cannot carry mediation's burden of proof — that is what makes it a paradox rather than a doctrine. The argument of premise 3 is about *faith's concept*, not about public policy: if Abraham's act is justifiable at all, it is justifiable only on unmediated terms, and the philosophical systems of the age, which praise Abraham while granting only mediated terms, are convicted of praising what their categories condemn. The reply's limit is the same as §2(a)'s: it secures the coherence of Johannes's category, not the credentials of any claimant to it — and Johannes, who confesses he cannot himself make the movement, would be the first to enforce the distinction.

### (c) Abraham's silence (Problema III)

**Johannes's argument, in numbered premises:**

1. The ethical requires disclosure: the individual must reveal himself in the universal; concealment is ethically a reproach, and a deed that cannot be openly justified to those it most concerns (Sarah, Isaac) is ethically indefensible.
2. Concealment is justified in exactly two other registers: aesthetically, as the intrigue that brings about a good the universal cannot see; and demonically, as the self's enclosure in guilt — the merman saved only if he confesses to Agnete, damned if he hides.
3. Abraham's concealment fits neither register: he seeks no aesthetic outcome, gains nothing, and encloses himself in no guilt — he would speak if speech could convey what he does.
4. Speech is translation into the universal; but the content of Abraham's position — that he acts under an absolute duty in which the ethical itself is the temptation — cannot be translated into the universal without being falsified into madness or murder in the hearers' ears.
5. Therefore Abraham cannot speak, and his silence is neither ethical disclosure nor demonic hiddenness but a third thing: the silence of the paradox, in which the individual bears an unutterable relation alone. His single sentence to Isaac — "God himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering" — is speech sustained only as irony: literally responsive, spiritually unrevealing.

**Conclusion.** The silence is a consequence, not a strategy: given the suspension (Problema I) and the absolute duty (Problema II), unintelligibility follows necessarily, and Abraham's greatness includes carrying the misunderstanding of everyone he loves without appeal.

**One standard objection.** The aesthetic and demonic categories do a great deal of quiet work. Sarah and Isaac are treated as audiences for Abraham's inwardness rather than as persons with claims — Isaac is the sacrifice, after all, and Sarah the deceived wife; an ethics centered on them judges the silence as part of the wrong, not as a noble corollary of paradox. Feminist and Levinasian critics press this hardest: the face of the other (Isaac's, on the mountain) is precisely what the "paradox" trains us to look past. Johannes's taxonomy has no room for the victim's understanding — only for the knight's.

**One reply.** Defenders note that Johannes is not blind to the cost: the Tuning Up's variations are precisely studies in what Abraham's silence did to his household and his joy, and variation I — Abraham taking monstrosity on himself to shield Isaac's faith — shows the silence weighed as suffering, not privilege. The reply's honest form is concessive: Problema III describes what faith's paradox *costs its bearer and his family* with unmatched clarity; whether anything could justify the cost is the question the problema leaves standing, and Kierkegaard's own later writings (which move the religious decisively toward confession, disclosure, and neighbor-love) can be read as his own continuing quarrel with Johannes's Abraham. That quarrel is a reason to read the book, not a resolution of it.

## 3. Checkpoint questions

Attempt these before reading the answers in §5.

1. Who is Johannes de silentio, and why does the pseudonym matter for how the book's claims may be stated?
2. What do the Preface and Epilogue jointly claim about "going further" than faith?
3. Summarize the four variations of the Tuning Up: at what joint does each Abraham fail?
4. Why does Johannes insist Abraham believed "for this life"? What error does the insistence block?
5. Define the ethical as the universal, and state what a *teleological suspension* of it would be.
6. Distinguish the tragic hero from the knight of faith, using Agamemnon and Abraham.
7. What is infinite resignation, and what further movement makes faith faith? Use the young man and the princess.
8. Why does the knight of faith look like a tax collector? What point is the ordinariness making?
9. State the Kantian objection to Abraham as Problema II sets it up, and Johannes's counter-claim about mediation.
10. What is the demonic in Problema III, and how does the merman escape it?
11. Why can Abraham not speak to Sarah or Isaac? What is the status of his one sentence to Isaac?
12. In what sense is Abraham's silence neither ethical nor demonic? Name the third thing Johannes says it is.

## 4. Common misreadings

1. **"Kierkegaard teaches the teleological suspension of the ethical."** The suspension is Johannes de silentio's category, argued by a pseudonym who stands outside faith and confesses he cannot make its movement. Kierkegaard's signed writings, and even *The Point of View*'s retrospective, present the pseudonymous works as stages a reader must appropriate critically, not as doctrine delivered. State the claim as Johannes's; the flattening into "Kierkegaard says" is the error this guide exists to prevent.
2. **"'Leap of faith' is Kierkegaard's slogan for believing things without evidence."** Johannes never uses the phrase as a slogan (the "leap" in Kierkegaard's corpus is a qualitative transition, a category of decision, elaborated mainly in the *Postscript* by another pseudonym, Johannes Climacus). Faith here is not generic credulity: it is a specific double movement — resignation plus receiving the finite back — directed at a specific paradox, sustained as lifelong passion. The bumper-sticker version inverts the book: Johannes's faith is the hardest thing he can conceive, not the easiest.
3. **"Abraham is a model for believers to imitate when they feel commanded."** Johannes blocks this explicitly: Abraham is inimitable, a paradox who produces terror and amazement, and the book offers no criterion by which a claimant could certify himself. Readings that turn *Fear and Trembling* into a decision procedure for divine commands keep neither Johannes's warnings nor his structure; see the debate at §2(a), which is presented here as open precisely because the stakes are real.
4. **"Faith means believing absurdities in general."** The absurd is specific: it is the contradiction Abraham held — that God, having promised Isaac as the seed of the covenant, demanded Isaac, and that Abraham would nevertheless receive him back in this life. It names the collision between the promise and the command, not a general commendation of irrational belief.
5. **"Infinite resignation is faith."** It is faith's precondition and opposite. The knight of infinite resignation renounces the finite for eternity and keeps the renunciation in pain — a movement Johannes says he can make himself, by human strength. Faith adds the second movement: believing, by virtue of the absurd, that the finite will be restored. Confusing the two makes Abraham a Stoic and the book a manual of renunciation.
6. **"The book is a coded autobiography about Regine Olsen."** The broken engagement stands behind the authorship, and Kierkegaard's journals connect them — "had I had faith, I would have remained with Regine" is his own later line, in effect. But *Fear and Trembling* is a dialectical lyric about Genesis 22 and the philosophy of its age, not a memoir in costume; reducing it to the engagement explains neither its arguments nor its endurance, and the scholarship treats the biographical reading as a layer, not the key.
7. **"De silentio is a believer describing his own faith."** He is the opposite: a poet-dialectician who can describe the movements of faith precisely because he contemplates them from outside, and who says he cannot make the second movement at all. His standing outside is the book's instrument — faith is being measured by someone who has everything except it.
8. **"Faith is a first immediacy that maturity surpasses."** This is the age's error, and the Preface and Epilogue exist to refute it: faith is not instinct awaiting mediation but the highest passion, reached — if reached — after the movements of reflection and resignation, and maintained as a task for a whole lifetime. Whatever surpasses it in the System, Johannes argues, has simply never arrived at it.

## 5. Answers to checkpoint questions

1. Johannes de silentio ("John of Silence") is the book's pseudonymous author-persona: a self-described non-philosopher and non-believer who meditates on Abraham with dialectical precision while confessing he cannot make the movement of faith. The pseudonym matters because Kierkegaard's pseudonyms are voices, not masks: their conclusions belong to them, and attributing Johannes's claims (the suspension, the paradox) flatly to Kierkegaard ignores the authorship's own architecture, which Kierkegaard later explained in *The Point of View*.
2. Both claim that "going further" than faith is the age's evasion: faith is not a primitive stage to be surpassed by reflection or system but the highest passion and a lifelong task; the generation that claims to have gone beyond it has, on Johannes's diagnosis, simply declined to begin it. The Epilogue adds that no generation inherits faith from another — each begins primitively with the same task.
3. (I) Abraham makes himself appear a monster to Isaac so Isaac's faith in God survives — he fails by sacrificing his integrity to manage another's faith. (II) Abraham obeys but is permanently darkened; he cannot receive Isaac back in joy — he fails by never completing the second movement. (III) Abraham begs God's forgiveness for having been willing — he fails by treating the commanded obedience as sin. (IV) Abraham's hand trembles and Isaac sees his despair and loses faith — he fails by letting the terror become visible, destroying the faith he meant to protect. The biblical Abraham, by contrast, believes throughout and receives Isaac back in joy.
4. Because Abraham's expectation is directed at the finite world: he believed he would keep Isaac, grow old in the land, be blessed in this life — not that he would be consoled in eternity for a loss serenely accepted. The insistence blocks the collapse of faith into resignation (and into otherworldly compensation schemes): resignation gives this life up; Abraham's faith, scandalously, demands it back.
5. The ethical is the universal — the moral order binding all, in which the individual's task is to express the universal and be justified in its terms. A teleological suspension of the ethical is a case where the single individual, as the particular, stands higher than the universal — not by violating it for a higher clause within itself, but by relating absolutely to something outside it (God), so that the ethical, a telos in itself, is suspended in relation to a higher telos.
6. The tragic hero (Agamemnon at Aulis; also Jephthah, Brutus) sacrifices what he loves most *for* the universal — the fleet, the vow, the republic — remains inside the ethical, and can be understood, wept over, and justified in speech. The knight of faith (Abraham) acts against the universal's requirement with no universal end served: ethically he is a murderer, religiously a sacrificer, and no mediation joins the two expressions. Comprehension separates them: the hero is intelligible, Abraham is a paradox.
7. Infinite resignation is the first movement: renouncing the finite wholly — the young man gives up the princess, transposing his love into an eternal, painful renunciation; it is achievable by human strength, and Johannes claims it for himself. Faith is the double movement: after the resignation, believing by virtue of the absurd that the finite will be given back — the young man believes he will marry the princess, in this life, against every impossibility. Abraham does not renounce Isaac in his heart while raising the knife; he believes throughout that he will not lose him.
8. Because faith's second movement restores the whole of finitude: the knight of faith delights in the ordinary — his dinner, his walk, the world — with nothing visibly infinite about him, "exactly like a tax collector." The ordinariness marks the difference from resignation (which is visibly marked by its renunciation, its pain, its otherworldliness) and from aesthetic religiosity: having made both movements, the knight takes absolute interest in the finite precisely because he has let it go and received it back.
9. Kant's objection (as the problema presupposes it): that I must not kill my innocent son is morally certain; that the voice commanding the killing is God's can never be certain; therefore Abraham should have refused — any "command" violating universal duty refutes itself as God's. Johannes's counter-claim: this mediation of duty through the universal makes the universal, not God, the absolute — God is admitted only on the ethical order's ratification. If there is an absolute duty toward God, it must be unmediated: the individual determines his relation to the universal through his relation to the absolute, not the reverse.
10. The demonic is self-enclosure in hiddenness: the self shut up with its guilt or secret, refusing disclosure — the inclosing reserve. The merman, who has Agnete's life and love in his power, escapes the demonic only by confessing himself to her in absolute trust — disclosure that may destroy him — whereas concealment, even to spare her, keeps him demonic. Abraham's silence is tested against this category and found not to fit it (§2(c)).
11. Because speech is translation into the universal, and what Abraham would have to communicate — that he acts under an absolute duty in which the ethical itself is the temptation — cannot be received by Sarah, Eliezer, or Isaac as anything but madness or murder. His one sentence, "God himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering," is spoken in irony: grammatically an answer, actually a non-revelation — true in a sense the hearer cannot access.
12. It is the silence of the paradox: neither the ethical's disclosure (impossible, since the content cannot be universalized) nor the demonic's self-enclosure (Abraham hides nothing from guilt or gain and suffers the silence rather than choosing it). It is the necessary consequence of the suspension and the absolute duty — the isolation of the single individual in an absolute relation that has no public language.
