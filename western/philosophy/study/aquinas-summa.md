# Study Guide: Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* — Selections

**Work:** *Summa Theologiae*, Part I Question 2 and Part I–II Questions 90–97 · **Author:** Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) · **Composed:** 1265–1274 (unfinished)
**Covers:** The Five Ways (Ia q.2) and the treatise on law (Ia–IIae qq. 90–97), with a reading plan, a question-by-question guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Thomas Aquinas](../eras/02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

The *Summa* is not a book you read straight through. It is a work of scholastic disputation, built from articles, and each article follows the same four-step rhythm. Learn the rhythm before you read a word of argument, because it is the whole method:

1. **Objections** (*videtur quod non* — "it seems that not"): the strongest reasons against the position Aquinas will defend, stated fairly and often drawn from Scripture, Augustine, Aristotle, or a predecessor. Aquinas regularly gives the other side its best case.
2. ***Sed contra*** ("but on the contrary"): a brief counter-authority, usually a sentence of Scripture or Augustine, that states the position to be defended.
3. ***Respondeo*** ("I answer that"): the body of the article, where Aquinas gives his own reasoned answer. This is where the argument lives.
4. **Replies** (*ad primum*, *ad secundum*…): point-by-point answers to each opening objection, showing how each is met or where its reasoning goes wrong.

If you skip the objections, you will mistake Aquinas's conclusions for undefended assertions; if you skip the replies, you will miss how he handles the strongest counter-evidence. Read every article whole, objections included. The *sed contra* is not itself the argument — it is a marker of orthodoxy before the reasoning begins — and the *respondeo* is where Aquinas earns his conclusion.

For these selections, read the Davies and Leftow volume, *Thomas Aquinas: Summa Theologiae, Questions on God* (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy), which covers Part I, questions 2–11 in a modern translation with an introduction explaining the article form. For the treatise on law, use Timothy McDermott's *Summa Theologiae: A Concise Translation* (1989) for orientation: it abridges the whole *Summa* into readable continuous prose. Read Edward Feser's *Aquinas: A Beginner's Guide* alongside both, as the learning path directs; it is particularly good on the metaphysics behind the Five Ways (act and potency, essentially ordered series) and on the structure of natural law.

A warning, which the learning path states plainly: do not start with the full *Summa* in the old Dominican translation (the Fathers of the English Dominican Province, in five volumes). It is a complete, serviceable translation and a fine reference set — but as a first read it is a wall of unabridged articles with little guidance. Use it to check a passage or to follow a cross-reference, not as your reading text.

A note on quotations in this guide: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows the standard English renderings. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so that your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

## How to use this guide

Aquinas writes the way a university lecturer disputed: he states a question, hears the opposition, and then answers. Your job as a reader is to reconstruct the answer as an argument, not to collect conclusions. Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a question, read its section below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each question, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed. Do this before reading the reconstruction sections in this guide — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

The learning path places this reading in Stage 2 (Medieval and Renaissance Bridge), after Augustine and Anselm and alongside the Islamic and Jewish bridge figures — Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides — whose arguments Aquinas answers, borrows from, and qualifies. If you have not met Avicenna's distinction between essence and existence, or Maimonides' limits on demonstrating God's attributes, the Five Ways will look thinner than they are. Read the companion entry on Aquinas first, and Feser alongside, not afterwards.

One further discipline: keep the two selections distinct in your mind. Part I, question 2 is natural theology — what reason can show about God from creatures. Part I–II, questions 90–97 is moral and legal philosophy — what law is, and how human law relates to a higher order. Both rest on the same metaphysics of act and potency and on the same conviction, stated in the companion entry, that grace perfects nature rather than destroying it.

## Reading plan

At the learning path's pace, the selections below take about five weeks. The Five Ways reward slow reading: question 2, article 3 is only a few pages, but each Way is a compressed argument whose premises come from metaphysics developed elsewhere.

| Week | Questions | Focus |

| 1 | Ia q.2, aa. 1–3 | The article form; whether God's existence is self-evident, demonstrable, and the Five Ways |

Do not skip questions 92–93 either — natural law (q.94) only makes sense once eternal law (qq. 91, 93) is in place.

## Question-by-question guide

### Ia q.2, a.1: Is the existence of God self-evident?

Aquinas asks whether God's existence is *per se notum* — known through itself, needing no proof. The objections make the strongest case for yes: to say "God exists" is to predicate existence of God, and God's essence *is* his existence, so the proposition should be self-evident, like "a whole is greater than its part." Aquinas's answer draws a distinction that does much work throughout the *Summa*: a proposition can be self-evident **in itself** yet not self-evident **to us**. "God exists" is self-evident in itself, because in God essence and existence are identical. But we do not know what God is; we know him only through creatures, by his effects. So his existence is not self-evident to us, and it must be demonstrated.

**Key point:** This article sets the terms for the whole of natural theology. Aquinas is not claiming that God's existence is obvious, nor that it is a matter of blind faith. It is demonstrable, but only *a posteriori*, from effects to cause — the route article 2 defends and article 3 takes.

### Ia q.2, a.2: Can the existence of God be demonstrated?

The question is whether demonstration is possible at all, given that we cannot know God's essence. An objection argues that demonstration proceeds from a cause to its effect, and God's cause is unknown, so no demonstration is possible. Aquinas replies that there are two kinds of demonstration: from cause to effect, and from effect to cause. The second is available here: from God's effects, which are better known to us, we can demonstrate that God exists, even though we do not thereby comprehend what God is. This is why the Five Ways all begin from observed features of the world — motion, causation, contingency, degrees, directedness — rather than from a definition of God.

**Key point:** Demonstration *quia* (from effect) is the only kind natural theology can offer. Anyone who demands a demonstration *propter quid* (from God's essence) is asking for something Aquinas says no human mind in this life possesses.

### Ia q.2, a.3: Does God exist? The Five Ways

The most famous article in the *Summa* is also one of its shortest. The objections are two: that evil exists, which an infinitely good God would not permit; and that everything in nature can be explained by other principles, so God is an unnecessary hypothesis. Aquinas answers the first in his replies — evil is permitted as a condition of greater goods and of the order of the universe — and the second is answered by the body of the article itself: the Five Ways are meant to show that natural explanation, pursued to its end, terminates in something beyond nature.

Each Way follows the same pattern: begin from an observed feature of the world, argue that it cannot be explained by an infinite regress in an essentially ordered series, and conclude to a first term — "and this everyone understands to be God." The crucial qualifier is *essentially ordered*: in Ways 1 and 2 the series is simultaneous and sustaining, like a hand moving a stick moving a stone, where each member moves only insofar as it is being moved here and now. This is not a temporal chain stretching back to a first event. Aquinas held, following Maimonides, that philosophy cannot prove the world had a temporal beginning; that is known by revelation. The Ways concern present, sustaining causation, not a first moment.

The five, in brief:

1. **From motion (change).** Things are moved from potency to act only by something already in act; nothing moves itself in the same respect. An essentially ordered series of movers cannot regress infinitely, so there is a first mover, itself unmoved.
2. **From efficient causation.** In an essentially ordered series of causes, each intermediate cause has only borrowed causal power. Remove the first, and the intermediates cause nothing. So there is a first efficient cause.
3. **From contingency and necessity.** Things we observe can be and not be — they come into being and pass away. If everything were contingent, then at some time nothing would have existed, and nothing could have begun to exist. So something necessary exists; and necessary beings that receive their necessity from another trace back to a being necessary through itself.
4. **From degrees of perfection.** Things are more or less good, true, noble. Such gradations are measured against a maximum, and the maximum in any genus is the cause of all the rest in that genus — as fire, the maximum of heat, is the cause of heat in other things. So there is a being that is maximally and essentially good, true, and noble, and the cause of those perfections in all else.
5. **From the governance of things.** Things lacking intelligence — natural bodies — act regularly for ends, as the acorn grows toward the oak. What lacks intelligence does not tend toward an end unless directed by something intelligent, as an arrow is directed by an archer. So there is an ordering intelligence.

The step at the end of each Way — "and this everyone understands to be God" — is the most contested sentence in the article, and the misreadings section below takes it up directly. In short: Aquinas does not claim that the first mover *is* the God of Christian faith in all his attributes. Questions 3–11 argue, one attribute at a time, that the first cause must be simple, perfect, good, infinite, and so on. The Five Ways are the doorway, not the whole theology.

**Main argument:** The observed world, pursued to its explanatory ground, terminates in an uncaused cause — and that terminus is what the name "God" picks out.

### Ia–IIae q.90: The essence of law

The treatise on law opens with a definition, built article by article. Law, Aquinas concludes, is **an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community, and promulgated**. Each element is argued separately, and each matters:

- **a.1 — Law belongs to reason.** Law is a rule and measure of acts, and reason is what rules and measures human acts. Law is therefore an ordinance of reason, not of will alone — not mere command backed by force.
- **a.2 — Law is ordained to the common good.** Since reason's first practical principle directs us to the good, and law is reason's ordinance, law aims at the good of the community as a whole, not at private advantage.
- **a.3 — Law is made by one who has care of the community.** Only the ruler, or the whole people acting as ruler, can make law, because only they can direct the community to its common good. A private person's ordinance is counsel, not law.
- **a.4 — Promulgation is necessary.** A rule cannot guide those it governs unless it is made known to them. An unpublished ordinance fails as law for the same reason an unheard command fails as a command.

### Ia–IIae q.91: The kinds of law

If law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, whose reason, and for which community? Aquinas distinguishes four kinds, in a descending order of universality:

- **a.1 — Eternal law:** the reason of divine wisdom directing all actions and movements — God's rational governance of the universe. It is the standard against which every other law is measured.
- **a.2 — Natural law:** the participation of the eternal law in the rational creature. Rational beings are not merely governed by eternal law, as stones are; they participate in it by understanding it, through reason's grasp of first practical principles.
- **a.3 — Human law:** the particular determinations of natural law for a particular community — the traffic regulations, tax codes, and constitutions that apply general principles to concrete circumstances.
- **a.4 — Divine law:** law given by revelation, necessary because human reason is limited and fallible — it errs about the good, and it cannot direct us to our supernatural end. Divine law is then divided into the **Old Law** (a.5), given to prepare for Christ, and the **New Law** (a.6), the law of grace, principally written on the heart.

### Ia–IIae q.92: The effects of law

**a.1** asks whether the effect of law is to make men good: Aquinas answers yes, in the sense that law's proper effect is to lead its subjects to their proper virtue — to make them good simply, not merely compliant. **a.2** lists the acts of law: to **command** (what is virtuous), to **forbid** (what is vicious), to **permit** (what is neither required nor forbidden), and to **punish** (violations). The inclusion of permission matters: law is not only prohibition.

### Ia–IIae q.93: Eternal law

Question 93 returns to eternal law in depth. Its main claims: eternal law is the supreme reason in God, not something external to him; it is known to all, though not equally — the wise grasp it more fully than the foolish; and every law, insofar as it has the character of law, derives from eternal law. The question also extends eternal law's reach: all of nature is subject to it, since the orderly movements of non-rational things are themselves governed by divine reason. The difference is in the mode of subjection: irrational things are governed by being moved, rational creatures by understanding the law and choosing to follow it — which is why rational creatures can disobey, and why their disobedience is a moral failure rather than a mere breakdown.

**Key point:** Eternal law is the metaphysical background of the whole treatise. When Aquinas later says an unjust human law "is not law," he means it fails to participate in eternal law — the standard is already in place here.

### Ia–IIae q.94: Natural law

The heart of the treatise. **a.1** defines natural law as the participation of the eternal law in the rational creature — reason's possession of the first principles of practical reason. **a.2** derives its precepts, and this is the article to read most slowly. Aquinas's argument runs through the order of human inclinations: first, the inclination shared with all substances, to preserve one's own being — hence precepts about preserving life. Second, inclinations shared with other animals, toward sexual union and the education of offspring — hence precepts about marriage and child-rearing. Third, inclinations proper to reason: to know the truth about God and to live in society — hence precepts about avoiding ignorance, not offending those we live with, and pursuing truth. Over all of them stands the first, self-evident principle of practical reason: **good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided**. All other precepts are specifications of it, as the first principles of speculative reason (such as non-contradiction) ground all other knowledge.

The remaining articles qualify the theory. **a.3:** all acts of the virtues belong to natural law, though some more directly than others. **a.4:** natural law is the same for all in its first principles, but in its secondary conclusions it may vary — what follows from the principles is not equally obvious to everyone, just as conclusions in geometry are not equally obvious. **a.5:** natural law can change only by addition, not by subtraction of first principles. **a.6:** natural law cannot be blotted out from the heart in its first principles, though it can be obscured in secondary precepts by vicious custom and corrupt habits — the *synderesis*, the habitual grasp of first principles, is ineradicable, even when a person's reasoned conclusions go badly wrong.

**Key point:** Natural law is a theory of practical reason, not a list of instincts. The inclinations matter because reason grasps them as goods to be pursued in an ordered way — and the ordering is reason's work, which is why the theory has room for both universality and variation.

### Ia–IIae q.95: Human law

Human law is valid, Aquinas argues, only insofar as it derives from natural law, and it derives in two ways. **a.2** is the key article: by **conclusion**, as a deduction from natural law's principles (as "do not kill" follows from the precept to preserve life), and by **determination**, as a specification where natural law leaves the matter open (as a particular speed limit or tax rate settles what natural law does not fix). Both kinds bind, but they bind differently: conclusions bind with natural law's own force, determinations bind because a legitimate authority has settled an otherwise open question. **a.1** defends the usefulness of human law against the objection that natural law suffices: human law is needed because some people need coercion, and because general principles need local application. **a.3** sets the qualities of good law — just, possible, in accordance with nature and custom, suitable to place and time, necessary, useful, clear, and aimed at the common good rather than private benefit. **a.4** divides human law into the *ius gentium* (law of nations, conclusions common across peoples) and *ius civile* (the civil law proper to each people).

### Ia–IIae q.96: The power of human law

Question 96 asks how far human law's authority extends, and Aquinas's answers are consistently restrained. **a.1:** law should be framed for the community, not for individuals. **a.2:** law should not repress all vices — only the graver ones that harm others and threaten social order; it should leave lesser vices to be corrected otherwise, since a law that tried to forbid everything would drive people to worse evils. **a.3:** by the same logic, law should not prescribe every act of every virtue, but only those acts that bear on the common good. **a.4:** human law binds in conscience — but only insofar as it is just, that is, insofar as it derives from natural law; an unjust law does not bind in conscience, though it may sometimes be obeyed to avoid scandal or greater disorder. **a.5:** all are subject to human law, including in principle the ruler, who is subject to its directive force even when exempt from its coercive force. **a.6:** in cases of necessity, where observance of the letter would contradict the law's own purpose, one may act beside the letter — the seed of what later tradition calls *epikeia*, equity.

### Ia–IIae q.97: Change in laws

The treatise closes with change. **a.1:** human law should be changed when reason or circumstances show the change serves the common good — law is an ordinance of reason, and reason can improve. **a.2:** but not whenever a marginally better law appears, because change itself has a cost: custom breeds stability, and frequent change weakens law's hold. **a.3:** custom can obtain the force of law, because custom expresses the reason and will of the community. **a.4:** rulers can dispense from human law in particular cases, since they made it — but not from natural or divine law, which no human authority can dispense from.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The First Way: from motion (Ia q.2, a.3)

**Premises:**

1. Some things in the world are in motion — that is, they change, being reduced from potency to act (a cold thing becoming hot, a place changing position).
2. Nothing is reduced from potency to act except by something already in act: what is merely potentially hot cannot make itself actually hot.
3. Nothing moves itself in the same respect at the same time: the same thing cannot be both in potency and in act in the same respect.
4. An essentially ordered series of movers — where each member moves only insofar as it is being moved here and now, like a hand moving a stick moving a stone — cannot regress infinitely, because if there were no first mover, there would be no motion at all in the series.
5. Therefore there is a first mover, itself unmoved by another.

**Conclusion:** There exists an unmoved first mover, and this everyone understands to be God.

**Objection:** Premise 4 assumes what it needs to prove. Why can an essentially ordered series not be infinite? An infinite chain of movers, each moved by the one before, seems conceivable; and even if a first mover is required, nothing in the argument shows it is one, simple, or intelligent — it might be a plurality of movers, or a physical principle rather than God.

**Reply:** Aquinas's point about essentially ordered series is not about conceivability in the abstract but about borrowed power: an intermediate mover has no power of its own in the moment of moving — the stick moves the stone only while the hand moves the stick. An infinite series of borrowers, with no lender, explains nothing. As for the identity of the first mover, Aquinas agrees the First Way alone does not settle it: that is the work of questions 3–11, which argue the first cause must be one, simple, and perfect. The Way concludes to a terminus; the divine attributes are argued afterwards, not smuggled into the conclusion.

### (b) The Third Way: from contingency and necessity (Ia q.2, a.3)

**Premises:**

1. Some things we observe can be and not be — they come into existence and pass away (plants, animals, artefacts).
2. What can fail to exist, at some time does not exist: if a thing's nature does not guarantee its existence, there is a possible time at which it does not exist.
3. If everything were contingent in this way, then at some time nothing would have existed.
4. But if at some time nothing existed, nothing could begin to exist, since nothing comes from nothing.
5. Therefore something exists necessarily — not everything is contingent.
6. Necessary beings that receive their necessity from another trace back to a being that is necessary through itself, not through another — otherwise the regress continues without ground.

**Conclusion:** There exists a being necessary through itself, the cause of necessity in all other things, and this everyone understands to be God.

**Objection:** Premise 3 is the weak step, and it has been pressed since the Middle Ages: even if each contingent thing at some time does not exist, it does not follow that there is a time at which *all* contingent things simultaneously do not exist. Contingent things could succeed one another without end, so that at every time something exists, even though nothing exists necessarily. The argument trades on moving from "each thing, at some time, is not" to "at some time, everything is not" — a quantifier shift.

**Reply:** Defenders of the Way reply that Aquinas's argument is better read through his metaphysics of participation than through the temporal reading of premise 3: contingent beings have existence received from another, and a series of receivers, however long, still requires a giver whose existence is not received. On that reading the argument does not depend on a first moment or a simultaneous gap, but on the present dependence of every contingent thing's existence. Whether that reply rescues the argument or rewrites it is itself debated — and the debate is a good test of whether you have understood the difference between temporal and essentially ordered series.

### (c) The Fifth Way: from the governance of things (Ia q.2, a.3)

**Premises:**

1. Things that lack intelligence — natural bodies — act regularly for ends: they behave in consistent, predictable ways that achieve results (the acorn grows toward the oak, heavy bodies fall).
2. What lacks intelligence does not tend toward an end by itself; it does so only as directed by something intelligent, as an arrow flies toward its target only as aimed by an archer.
3. Regular, end-directed behavior in non-intelligent things therefore requires an ordering intelligence that directs them, as the archer directs the arrow.
4. This intelligence cannot itself be one more non-intelligent thing directed by another, on pain of regress; it must be an intelligence that directs without being directed.

**Conclusion:** There exists an intelligent being by whom all natural things are directed to their ends, and this everyone understands to be God.

**Objection:** The argument anthropomorphises nature. Regular behavior in non-intelligent things is explained by natural laws and physical dispositions, not by purposes: an acorn grows into an oak because of its genetic and chemical constitution, not because it aims at oakhood. To call this "acting for an end" is to project human intention onto processes that need none. Modern science, on this objection, explains the regularities without an ordering mind.

**Reply:** Aquinas's notion of an end is broader than conscious purpose: a thing acts for an end when its activity is ordered toward a determinate outcome rather than happening at random, and the question is what explains that orderedness. The defender replies that laws of nature describe regularities but do not explain why matter behaves regularly at all. Critics respond that this moves the argument from observed directedness to a demand for ultimate explanation that the Way itself does not supply. The dispute turns on whether "acting for an end" is a real feature of nature or a metaphor — which is why the Fifth Way is best read alongside Aristotle's *Physics*, where final causation is defended on its own terms.

### (d) The derivation of natural law (Ia–IIae q.94, a.2)

**Premises:**

1. The first principle of practical reason is self-evident: good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided.
2. Whatever practical reason naturally apprehends as a good for human beings belongs to the precepts of natural law, since natural law is reason's participation in eternal law.
3. Human beings have natural inclinations, which reason apprehends as goods, in an ordered sequence: first, the inclination to preserve one's own being, shared with all substances; second, inclinations shared with other animals, toward sexual union and the education of offspring; third, inclinations proper to reason — to know the truth about God and to live in society.
4. The precepts of natural law therefore follow the order of these inclinations: preserve life, unite and educate offspring, shun ignorance, avoid offending those with whom one must live, and pursue truth about God.

**Conclusion:** Natural law consists of a hierarchy of precepts, all specifications of the first principle that good is to be done and evil avoided, ordered according to the inclinations of human nature rightly understood by reason.

**Objection:** The argument slides from "is" to "ought": that human beings have inclinations does not show those inclinations ought to be followed — we have inclinations toward aggression and domination as well, and reason's job might be to restrain inclinations rather than to ratify them. If the derivation merely baptizes whatever humans tend to do, it cannot criticize corrupt inclinations, and it collapses into the naturalistic fallacy.

**Reply:** Aquinas's reply, as developed in question 94 as a whole, is that the inclinations count only as apprehended by reason — reason grasps them as goods, ordered and judged, not as brute urges to be obeyed. Aggression is an inclination of the sensitive appetite, but it is not an inclination reason apprehends as a human good in the relevant sense, and the order among inclinations is reason's ordering, not nature's raw list. Whether this distinction is principled or question-begging — whether reason can identify "inclinations of human nature rightly understood" without already knowing the moral conclusions — is the central modern criticism of natural law.

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these book closed, then check yourself against the answers at the end of this guide.

1. In Ia q.2, a.1, what distinction does Aquinas draw between a proposition being self-evident in itself and self-evident to us, and why does it matter?
2. Why does Aquinas say God's existence can be demonstrated only from his effects, not from his essence?
3. State the difference between an essentially ordered series and an accidentally ordered (temporal) series, and explain why it matters for the First and Second Ways.
4. In the Third Way, what work does the step "if everything were contingent, at some time nothing would have existed" do, and what is the standard objection to it?
5. Why does the Fourth Way appeal to a maximum, and what example does Aquinas use?
6. What does the Fifth Way mean by "acting for an end" in things that lack intelligence, and why is the archer analogy central?
7. What is the force of the closing phrase of each Way, "and this everyone understands to be God"? Does it by itself establish the Christian God?
8. Give Aquinas's definition of law from q.90, and explain why each of its four elements is necessary.
9. Name the four kinds of law in q.91 and state the relation between them.
10. What are the two ways human law derives from natural law in q.95, a.2, and why does the distinction matter?
11. According to q.94, a.2, what is the first principle of practical reason, and what are the three levels of inclination from which the precepts of natural law are derived?
12. How does q.94, a.4–a.6 qualify the universality of natural law?
13. According to q.96, a.2–a.4, what are the limits on human law: which vices should it repress, and when does it bind in conscience?
14. Why does q.97, a.2 say human law should not be changed whenever a better law appears?
15. What is *epikeia*, and where does its seed appear in q.96?

## Common misreadings

**1. "The Five Ways are five scientific proofs of God's existence."**
They are compressed metaphysical demonstrations inside an Aristotelian framework of act and potency, essentially ordered causation, and participation — not empirical hypotheses, and not arguments from ignorance. They do not work by pointing to a gap in scientific explanation and inserting God; Aquinas thought natural causes explain natural effects fully at their own level. The Ways ask a different question: what grounds the whole order of causes, including the regularities science describes. Read as science, they fail; read as metaphysics, they must be judged on metaphysical terms.

**2. "The Five Ways by themselves establish the Christian God."**
Each Way ends with "and this everyone understands to be God," but Aquinas does not treat that phrase as proving the Trinity, the Incarnation, or any revealed doctrine — those, he insists, exceed reason's reach. What the Ways conclude to is a first cause; questions 3–11 then argue that this cause must be simple, perfect, good, and one. Scholars debate how much weight the closing phrase can bear on its own: some read it as a mere identification of the conclusion's referent, others as a substantive step that needs the later questions to be earned. The guide takes the second view as the safer one — the phrase points forward, and the attributes must be argued, not assumed.

**3. "'First mover' means God started the universe a long time ago."**
The essentially ordered series in Ways 1–2 is about present, sustaining causation — the hand moving the stick moving the stone here and now — not about a first event in time. Aquinas explicitly held that reason cannot prove the world had a temporal beginning; that is known by faith. A first mover who only wound the clock and walked away would not be what the argument concludes to, and a defender of the Ways must keep the sustaining reading in view.

**4. "Natural law means 'do what is natural' — follow your instincts."**
Natural law is a theory of practical reason, not of biology. The inclinations in q.94, a.2 count only as apprehended and ordered by reason, and reason can judge an inclination corrupt — which is why the theory has room for the claim, in a.6, that secondary precepts can be obscured by vicious custom. Treating every urge as a precept inverts the theory: for Aquinas, reason rules the inclinations, it does not rubber-stamp them.

**5. "'An unjust law is not a law' means you may disobey any law you judge unjust."**
Aquinas does say that a law conflicting with natural law is a corruption of law and does not bind in conscience (q.96, a.4). But he immediately qualifies the practical conclusion: such an enactment may still be obeyed to avoid scandal or greater disorder, and the judgment that a law is unjust is itself a judgment reason can get wrong. The slogan without the qualification licenses private nullification; Aquinas's position is narrower and more cautious, and modern debates about civil disobedience that cite him usually have to reckon with that caution.

**6. "Natural law is a fixed, identical code for everyone."**
Question 94, a.4–a.6 denies exactly this in its simple form: natural law is the same in its first principles, but secondary conclusions vary in how obvious they are, and they can be obscured by custom and habit. Add question 95's distinction between conclusion and determination, and the picture is a universal core with legitimate local variation — not a code that settles every concrete question in advance. Readers who want natural law to decide every case without prudence are asking it to do work Aquinas assigns to the virtue of prudence.

**7. "Aquinas thinks law should enforce all of morality."**
Question 96, a.2–a.3 says the opposite: human law should repress only the graver vices that harm others and threaten social order, and prescribe only those virtuous acts that bear on the common good. Law, for Aquinas, is a blunt instrument aimed at the conditions of common life, not a tutor of every soul. A legal moralism that would punish every vice fails by Aquinas's own standard.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **Self-evident in itself vs. to us (Ia q.2, a.1):** "God exists" is self-evident in itself because God's essence is his existence — to grasp what God is would be to see that he exists. It is not self-evident to us because we do not know God's essence; we know him only through creatures. So his existence must be demonstrated from effects.
2. **Demonstration from effects (a.2):** Demonstration from God's essence (*propter quid*) is unavailable in this life, since we do not comprehend that essence. Demonstration from effects (*quia*) is available: from what God causes, which is better known to us, we can prove that he exists without grasping what he is.
3. **Essentially vs. accidentally ordered series:** An essentially ordered series is simultaneous and sustaining — each member acts only insofar as it is being acted on here and now (hand–stick–stone). An accidentally ordered series is temporal — a father begetting a son who begets a grandson, where earlier members need not still exist. The First and Second Ways concern essentially ordered series; an infinite regress there would leave the present motion or causation without a source.
4. **The Third Way's temporal step:** If everything were contingent, there would be a time when nothing existed, and from nothing nothing comes — so something necessary must exist. The standard objection is a quantifier shift: from "each contingent thing at some time does not exist" it does not follow that "at some time all contingent things simultaneously do not exist"; they could succeed one another without end.
5. **The Fourth Way's maximum:** Things are more or less good, true, and noble, and gradations are measured against a maximum in the genus, which is the cause of the perfection in the rest — as fire, the maximum of heat, is the cause of heat in other things. So there is a maximally good, true, and noble being, the cause of those perfections in all else.
6. **"Acting for an end" (Fifth Way):** Non-intelligent things behave regularly toward determinate outcomes, as the acorn grows toward the oak. The archer analogy matters because an arrow hits its target only as aimed: directedness without intelligence in the thing directed implies intelligence in the director.
7. **"And this everyone understands to be God":** The phrase identifies the conclusion's referent with what "God" picks out, but does not by itself establish the Christian God in his revealed attributes. Those are argued in questions 3–11, and the Trinity and Incarnation remain, for Aquinas, matters of revelation, not demonstration.
8. **Definition of law (q.90):** An ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community, and promulgated. Reason, because law rules and measures acts; the common good, because law aims at the community's good; the ruler, because only the one charged with the community can direct it; promulgation, because a rule cannot guide unless known.
9. **Four kinds of law (q.91):** Eternal law (God's rational governance of the universe); natural law (rational creatures' participation in eternal law); human law (particular determinations of natural law); divine law (revealed law: Old Law and New Law). They are one order at four levels, not four competing systems.
10. **Conclusion and determination (q.95, a.2):** By conclusion, as a deduction from natural law (e.g. "do not kill" from preserving life); by determination, as a specification where natural law leaves the matter open (e.g. a particular penalty or speed limit). Conclusions bind with natural law's force; determinations bind by legitimate authority's settlement of an open question.
11. **First principle and inclinations (q.94, a.2):** "Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided." The three levels of inclination: self-preservation (shared with all substances); sexual union and education of offspring (shared with animals); knowing the truth about God and living in society (proper to reason).
12. **Qualifications (q.94, a.4–a.6):** Natural law is the same for all in its first principles, but secondary conclusions vary in obviousness; it can change only by addition, not by subtraction of first principles; and while the first principles cannot be blotted out, secondary precepts can be obscured by vicious custom and corrupt habits.
13. **Limits on human law (q.96, a.2–a.4):** Law should repress only the graver vices that harm others and threaten order, not all vices; it should prescribe only virtuous acts bearing on the common good; and it binds in conscience only insofar as it is just — an unjust law does not bind in conscience, though it may be obeyed to avoid scandal or greater disorder.
14. **Change and its cost (q.97, a.2):** Change itself harms law's stability, since custom and familiarity give law its hold. A new law must therefore be sufficiently better that its benefit outweighs the damage done by changing.
15. ***Epikeia* (q.96, a.6):** Equity — acting beside the letter of the law in a case of necessity where observing the letter would contradict the law's own purpose and the common good. The ruler's intention, not the letter alone, governs such cases.
