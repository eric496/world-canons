# Study Guide: Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (Selections)

**Work:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (*Kritik der reinen Vernunft*) · **Author:** Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) · **Composed:** 1781 (A edition); substantially revised 1787 (B edition)
**Covers:** The selections prescribed by the learning path — both Prefaces, the Introduction, the Transcendental Aesthetic, the Analytic of Concepts with the Transcendental Deduction in outline, the Schematism in brief, the Paralogisms and Antinomies in selection, and the Canon of Pure Reason — with a reading plan, an explicit skip list, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [Immanuel Kant](../eras/03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)
**Focus study:** This guide is the session companion for Weeks 2–4 of the [Kant focus-study dossier](../focus-studies/immanuel-kant/README.md), whose eight-week plan works the *Groundwork* whole, the *Prolegomena*, the second *Critique* and *Perpetual Peace* around these *Critique* selections.

## Edition note

Read the *Critique* in the translation by Paul Guyer and Allen W. Wood (Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy). This is the text the learning path names, and the standard teaching edition in English: modern, terminologically consistent, with a useful introduction and glossary, and — the point that matters most in daily use — the pagination of both original editions printed in the margins throughout.

Learn to navigate by those numbers from the first day: they are how the *Critique* is cited everywhere. **A** followed by a number is a page of the first edition of 1781; **B** followed by a number is a page of the second, revised edition of 1787. A citation such as A51/B75 — "thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" — points to a passage appearing in both editions. Where a passage exists in only one, you get a single letter: the Refutation of Idealism is B274–279, because Kant added it in 1787.

Do not use Norman Kemp Smith's translation (1929) or J. M. D. Meiklejohn's (1855) as a first read, and do not use an unlabeled [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/) text. Both are older translations whose terminology can mislead a beginner, and cheap reprints of them often lack the A/B pagination altogether.

The A/B problem needs stating plainly. There are not two books here: there is one book, published in 1781, which Kant revised — in places heavily — for 1787, partly in response to early reviewers who had mistaken him for a Berkeley-style idealist. Large stretches are identical in both, but three parts were substantially rewritten. The **Preface** is new in B — it contains the Copernican analogy and the remark about denying knowledge to make room for faith. The **Transcendental Deduction** was rewritten wholesale: the A and B Deductions are different arguments in structure and emphasis, not two printings of one. And the **Paralogisms** chapter was recast in B, in shorter and quite different form. Smaller additions matter too, notably the Refutation of Idealism, new in B.

The practical rule, here and in almost all teaching, is: **read B where both versions exist, with the A Deduction as the famous alternative**. B is Kant's considered second attempt and what the secondary literature presupposes. The A Deduction — more psychological, built on a threefold synthesis — strikes many readers as more approachable, and remains a live alternative rather than a discarded draft.

Quotations follow the Guyer and Wood wording; the guide otherwise paraphrases.

## How to use this guide

Begin with the honest fact: in the Guyer and Wood edition the *Critique* runs to roughly 800 pages of Kant's text — some 900 pages in all. A cover-to-cover first read, at self-study pace alongside a job, is not a plan; it is the most reliable known method of stopping Kant altogether. The learning path therefore prescribes **selections only** — a design feature, not a compromise: the Prefaces, the Introduction, the Aesthetic, the core of the Analytic, the Paralogisms and Antinomies in selection, and the Canon.

The selections trace the book's own spine. The Prefaces and Introduction state the problem: how is synthetic a priori knowledge possible, and what are reason's limits? The Aesthetic and Analytic give the positive answer; the Dialectic selections show the negative payoff — reason trying to know the soul, the world as a whole, or God — and the Canon hands the questions theory cannot answer to practical reason. State that arc in your own words and you have the *Critique*.

Work in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a part of the *Critique*, read its section below. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each part, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed — before reading the reconstructions in this guide, which are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction. Critique always comes second, never first.

The learning path places these selections in Stage 3 (Early Modern to Kant), at the end of the Kant sequence — and the sequence is the whole game: first "What is Enlightenment?", then the *Groundwork* whole, then the *Prolegomena*, and only then the *Critique*. Do not invert that order. The *Prolegomena* (1783) matters especially: Kant's own shorter, clearer restatement of the *Critique*'s argument, written after the first edition was misunderstood. A reader who has worked through it meets the *Critique* knowing where the road goes; if you have not read it yet, stop and do so first. Use Scruton's *Kant: A Very Short Introduction* and the [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)'s opening section for orientation only; full secondary treatments come after your own reconstruction.

One warning, stated plainly: **the Transcendental Deduction is the hardest stretch of the book, and the section most readers are tempted either to skip or to grind on until they quit. Do neither.** This guide asks for it in outline — conclusion and shape of argument. An outline grasp you can reconstruct book closed is a successful first pass; a month lost inside §§16–26 is the classic Stage 3 stall.

## Reading plan

At the learning path's pace of 4–5 hours a week, a first pass takes about **four to five weeks — roughly 18 to 22 hours** — inside the Kant block at the end of Stage 3, after the *Groundwork*'s three weeks and the *Prolegomena* week. ## Section-by-section guide

### The two Prefaces (A: 1781; B: 1787): What kind of book is this?

Read both Prefaces in full — together under fifty pages, and the book's most accessible programmatic writing. The **A Preface** presents the *Critique* as a tribunal: reason itself is to sit in judgment on reason's own claims, deciding which metaphysical questions it may legitimately answer and which it must permanently abandon. Human reason, Kant says, is burdened by questions its own nature sets but which exceed its powers to answer. Pre-Kantian metaphysics is the battlefield on which those questions are fought without decision; the *Critique* is the court meant to end the fighting by fixing the jurisdiction.

The **B Preface** restates the project for readers who had misunderstood the first edition, and gives it its famous image. The sciences, Kant argues, became secure only when they stopped assuming that cognition must conform to objects and recognized that we know a priori of things only what we ourselves put into them. His analogy is Copernicus: when astronomy failed on the assumption that the heavens revolve around the observer, Copernicus tried making the observer revolve instead. Kant proposes the same reversal — objects of experience must conform to our cognition — and the whole *Critique* works out that hypothesis. The B Preface also contains the book's most quoted sentence of intent: Kant had to *deny knowledge in order to make room for faith* (Bxxx) — a demarcation, not a surrender: theory cannot reach God, freedom, and immortality, so critique relocates them to practical reason.

**What to watch for:** the Copernican analogy concerns the *form* of experience, not a claim that the mind invents the world: Kant is an empirical realist, and objects of experience are public, law-governed, and independent of any particular mind.

**Difficulty warning:** low — the easiest reading in the plan.

**Main argument:** Reason must critique itself before it can do metaphysics, testing the hypothesis that objects of experience conform to the a priori structure of our cognition.

### Introduction (A1–14 / B1–30): The driving question

The Introduction poses the question the entire book answers: **how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?** Unpack the two distinctions slowly; everything later presupposes them. A judgment is **analytic** if its predicate is already contained in the concept of its subject — "all bachelors are unmarried" adds nothing — and **synthetic** if it genuinely extends knowledge. Independently, knowledge is **a posteriori** if it rests on experience, and **a priori** if it does not depend on any particular experience — shown in necessity and strict universality, which experience alone can never supply.

Mathematics and natural science appear to contain judgments that are synthetic yet a priori: that 7 + 5 = 12, that every event has a cause. Hume's skepticism is the pressure behind the question: Hume had shown, to Kant's satisfaction, that experience supplies no necessity — we observe one billiard ball follow another, never the *must* connecting them. If such judgments come neither from experience nor from unpacking concepts, where does their necessity come from? Kant's answer, in one sentence, is the book: they articulate the structure our faculties contribute to experience — hence necessary for any experience we can have, and valid only within it. The Introduction also maps the work ahead: the **Aesthetic** studies sensibility; the **Transcendental Logic** the understanding, subdivided into the **Analytic** (conditions of possible experience) and the **Dialectic** (reason's overreach beyond it).

**Difficulty warning:** low to moderate. Keep the two-by-two grid (analytic/synthetic × a priori/a posteriori), with Kant's examples, on a card.

### Transcendental Aesthetic (A19/B33 – A49/B73): Space and time

The Aesthetic is the most accessible stretch of the *Critique* proper. Its subject is **sensibility**, the faculty through which objects are *given* to us in intuition. Kant's claim: sensibility contributes two a priori **forms of intuition**. **Space** is the form of outer sense — nothing can appear to us as outside us except in space. **Time** is the form of inner sense — we represent our own states only as successive in time — and so, mediately, of all appearances whatsoever.

Take the argument for space as the model; time runs in parallel. Kant's *metaphysical exposition* argues, first, that space is not an empirical concept abstracted from experience: to represent things as outside me, or side by side, I must already represent them in space — space is presupposed by outer experience, not derived from it. Second, space is necessary: I can represent it empty, but nothing outer except in it. Third, space is not a general concept but a single, infinite *intuition* of which particular spaces are limitations. The *transcendental exposition* draws the payoff: only if space is an a priori form of sensibility can geometry be synthetic a priori knowledge, necessary yet informative about everything that can appear to us.

The conclusion is **transcendental idealism**, held in both halves at once. Space and time are **transcendentally ideal**: they belong to the conditions of our cognition, not to things as they are in themselves. And they are **empirically real**: within experience, the spatial and temporal world is public, measurable, and law-governed, not a private dream. Hence the central distinction: we know **phenomena**, things as they appear under our forms and categories; **things in themselves** are not objects of possible knowledge for us.

Here you meet the *Critique*'s most durable scholarly dispute, on which this guide takes no side. On the **two-world (two-object) reading**, phenomena and things in themselves are two distinct realms: appearances in space and time, and a separate unknowable reality behind them. On the **two-aspect reading**, there is one world of things, considered two ways: as they appear to beings with our forms of cognition, and as they are in abstraction from those forms. Each has serious difficulties: the two-world reading must explain how things in themselves can affect us when causality holds only within experience; the two-aspect reading must explain passages where Kant speaks of them as distinct from appearances. The dispute is live; a first pass needs only to state both readings and notice which a commentator assumes.

**Difficulty warning:** moderate, mostly terminological. Read the space exposition twice — but do not skip time.

**Main argument:** Space and time are not perceived among other things, nor abstracted from experience, but are the a priori forms of our sensibility — which explains geometry's necessity and establishes transcendental idealism.

### Analytic of Concepts (A64/B89 – A147/B187): The categories and the Deduction

The Analytic is the *Critique*'s core and its hardest stretch. Its question mirrors the Aesthetic's, one faculty up: sensibility contributes forms of intuition — what does the **understanding** contribute? Kant's answer is the **categories**: twelve pure concepts — among them unity, substance, causality, and necessity — the a priori rules by which a manifold of intuition is unified into experience of *objects*: to experience an event, rather than a mere sequence of impressions, is already to apply them.

The chapter's parts are unequally hard. First, the **Metaphysical Deduction** derives the twelve categories from the twelve forms of judgment in the logic of Kant's day: the same function that unifies representations in a judgment, he argues, also unifies a manifold in intuition, so the table of judgments is the clue to the table of categories. Read this for its conclusion — the list, and its claimed completeness — and do not stall on whether the derivation succeeds. Second, and centrally, the **Transcendental Deduction**: the argument that the categories *legitimately* apply to everything that can be an object of experience. Third, the **Schematism**, treated below.

"Deduction" here is legal before it is logical: it means vindicating an entitlement — *quid juris*, by what right do we use these concepts of objects, as against *quid facti*, how we came by them. Hume, on Kant's reading, answered the second question empirically — custom, association — and thereby destroyed the concepts' claim to necessity. The Deduction must show the categories are conditions of possible experience itself.

Read the **B Deduction** (§§15–27) as primary, in outline, along this line. Kant begins from **synthesis**: a manifold becomes one experience only by being combined, and combination is an act of the mind, not something given. The supreme condition of all combination is the **original unity of apperception**: "The *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations" (B131). A representation that could not in principle be taken up into one self-consciousness would be, for me, nothing. Kant then argues this unity is not merely subjective: combination in one consciousness according to rules *is* what we mean by an object's unity — the object is that in the concept of which a manifold is united — and the forms of that combination are precisely the categories. The second step (§§24–26) closes the gap: the manifold given in space and time is itself unified under the same synthesis, so the categories govern everything given in intuition at all. The conclusion is bounded: objective validity for **objects of possible experience**, and nothing beyond it.

You are not expected to master this machinery on a first pass. What you owe the checkpoint is its shape: self-conscious experience requires the combinability of all my representations in one "I think"; such combination is rule-governed; the categories are those rules; therefore anything that can be an object for me falls under them — within experience only. The A Deduction reaches a similar destination via the threefold synthesis of apprehension, reproduction, and recognition, and many first readers find that road easier; if B defeats you twice, read A as your outline instead. What you may not do is read neither.

**What to watch for:** the distinction between the *subjective* unity of consciousness (how my thoughts happen to hang together) and the *objective* unity of apperception (the necessary unity that makes representations representations *of an object*) — the argument turns on it.

**Difficulty warning:** high — the highest in this guide, concentrated in §§16–26. Use the edition's notes, read the SEP treatment only *after* your own first attempt, and expect to understand it properly only on the buffer-week second pass.

**Main argument:** The categories are the understanding's a priori rules for unifying intuition into objective experience; because any manifold belonging to one self-consciousness must be combinable under those rules, they apply necessarily to all objects of possible experience — and to nothing beyond it.

### The Schematism (A137/B176 – A147/B187), in brief

A short chapter with a real problem and a forbidding presentation; read it once, for the problem and the conclusion, and move on. The problem is application: the categories are pure concepts, wholly non-sensible, while intuitions are sensible through and through. How can an appearance fall *under* a pure concept — how does causality get a grip on what is seen and felt? A mediator homogeneous with both sides is needed, and Kant's candidate is **time**: a **transcendental schema** is a determination of time according to a category's rule. The schema of substance is permanence of the real in time; the schema of causality is succession of the manifold *insofar as it is subject to a rule*; the schema of necessity is existence at all times. Schemata are not images but procedures of the imagination for giving a concept its sensible significance.

**Difficulty warning:** short, but the highest obscurity-per-page in the Analytic. Its conclusion matters more than its mechanism at this stage.


### Analytic of Principles (A148/B187 – A309/B366): A note — mostly skippable on a first pass

The honest instruction here is: **take it in outline, and in full only two things**. The chapter systematizes the principles resulting when the categories are applied under the conditions of sensibility — the Axioms of Intuition, the Anticipations of Perception, the Analogies of Experience, and the Postulates of Empirical Thought — and much of it works out machinery you already have, at a level of detail a first pass does not need (see "What to skip" below).

The two exceptions matter. First, the **Analogies of Experience**, in outline. Objective temporal relations cannot be read off perception, since time itself cannot be perceived, so they must be determined by rules: permanence of substance (First Analogy), succession according to the law of cause and effect (Second Analogy), and thoroughgoing interaction (Third Analogy). The Second Analogy is the one to reconstruct: Kant's direct answer to Hume, arguing that we can distinguish an objective succession — an *event* — from the merely subjective order of our perceptions only if the succession is conceived as following a rule. Causality is thus a condition of experiencing events at all, not a habit. Second, the **Refutation of Idealism** (B274–279), new in B: consciousness of my own existence as determined in time presupposes the perception of something permanent *outside* me — so inner experience, Descartes' starting point, is possible only through outer experience. It is reconstructed as argument (d) below.

**Difficulty warning:** the Analogies reward slow reading in outline; the surrounding system is where readers drown in completeness.


### Paralogisms (A341/B399 onwards; B version prioritized): The soul

The Dialectic begins here, and the reading gets easier even as the stakes rise. Transcendental Dialectic studies reason's characteristic illusion: reason demands the *unconditioned* — the complete, ultimate ground — and mistakes that demand for knowledge of objects beyond experience. Its first target is **rational psychology**, which argued from the "I think" to the soul as a simple, persisting substance, naturally immortal.

Kant's diagnosis is uniform: a *paralogism* is a formally fallacious syllogism, and each trades on the same slide. From the fact that the "I" is the logical subject of thought — that which thinks, and can be thought only as subject, never as predicate — rational psychology infers that the soul is a **substance**. From the unity of consciousness it infers the soul's **simplicity**; from the identity of the "I think" over time, its **numerical identity** as a person. In each case a formal feature of self-consciousness — the very unity the Deduction established — has been mistaken for an intuition of a thing. We have no intuition of the soul as an object: the "I think" accompanies representations but is not itself given among them.

Read a selection: the opening and the first paralogism in full, the rest in summary. The **B Paralogisms** were substantially rewritten, so read B as primary; the A version is a reasonable second-pass comparison. Note the residue: Kant denies only that *theory* can settle the soul's nature — leaving the practical questions where the *Groundwork* put them.

**Difficulty warning:** moderate — the effort lies in holding "logical subject of thought" and "substance" apart.


### Antinomies (A426/B454 onwards; selection: First and Third required): The world as a whole

The Antinomies are the Dialectic's centerpiece and, for many readers, its most enjoyable stretch. When reason treats the **world** — the totality of appearances — as a completed object that could be known, it generates four pairs of apparently rigorous proofs for contradictory conclusions, **thesis** against **antithesis**; the collision itself is the datum to be explained.

The **First Antinomy** concerns the world's limits: thesis, the world has a beginning in time and is enclosed in spatial limits; antithesis, it is infinite in both. The **Second** concerns divisibility: whether everything composite consists of simple parts, or nothing simple exists. Kant calls these the *mathematical* antinomies, and resolves them by holding **both sides false**: both assume the world as a whole is an object given in itself, whereas it exists only in the regress of our experience, never as a completed totality — so it is neither finite nor infinite. The **Third Antinomy**, required in full alongside the First, concerns causality: thesis, a causality through **freedom** must be assumed alongside causality according to laws of nature; antithesis, there is no freedom, and everything takes place solely according to laws of nature. The **Fourth** concerns a necessary being. These two are the *dynamical* antinomies, resolved differently: **both sides can be true**, from different standpoints — the antithesis true of appearances, the thesis possibly true of things in themselves.

The Third Antinomy's resolution is the hinge between this book and the *Groundwork*, so be precise about what it delivers. It does **not** prove that we are free. It shows that freedom and universal natural determinism do not formally contradict each other once the phenomena / things-in-themselves distinction is in place: the same action can be viewed, as appearance, as fully determined by prior causes, and, as the act of an intelligible agent, as free. Theory can neither establish nor exclude freedom; it clears the ground and hands the question to practical reason, exactly where the *Groundwork*'s Section 3 took it up.

Read the First and Third Antinomies with their resolutions; take the Second and Fourth in summary, reading for the diagnosis — *the world as a completed totality is not a possible object of experience*.

**Difficulty warning:** low to moderate — the easiest Dialectic reading in the plan. The risk is overconfidence: if "both false" and "both possibly true" seem like wordplay, the gap is upstream, in the Aesthetic and Analytic.

**Main argument:** Reason, treating the world as a knowable completed totality, proves contradictories — which shows the assumption is at fault. Under transcendental idealism the mathematical antinomies have both sides false, and the dynamical ones both sides possibly true from different standpoints; freedom in particular is shown thinkable alongside natural necessity, though never theoretically proved.

### The Canon of Pure Reason (A795/B823 – A831/B859): What may I hope?

Close the selection where Kant closes the book's argument: the Canon, first chapter of the Doctrine of Method. Kant asks what pure reason is *for*, organizing philosophy around three questions (A805/B833): **What can I know? What ought I to do? What may I hope?** The first is speculative, and the *Critique* has answered it. The second is practical, and belongs to the *Groundwork*. The third is both at once, and the Canon is Kant's compressed answer to it.

The reasoning runs through the **highest good**. If I do what I ought — if I make myself worthy of happiness — what may I then hope? Kant's answer: happiness in proportion to worthiness, but only on the supposition of a moral author of the world who can guarantee that proportion, since nature distributes happiness without regard to virtue. God and a future life appear here not as objects of knowledge — the Dialectic's critique of rational theology has closed that road — but as presuppositions of practical reason: articles of rational **faith**, in Kant's taxonomy of opining, believing, and knowing. The Canon thus states, inside the first *Critique* itself, the doctrine the *Critique of Practical Reason* will develop as the postulates: freedom, God, and immortality rationally believed, never known.

**Difficulty warning:** low. The companion entry's postulates section covers the highest good in summary.

**Main argument:** Theory answers what I can know (appearances only); practice, what I ought to do; and hope — happiness proportioned to virtue — is rational only as faith in God and a future life, never as knowledge.

## What to skip on a first pass, and why

Skipping, in this book, is a strategy, not a confession. Everything below is skipped because its conclusion is covered elsewhere in the selection or matters only once the outline is secure.

- **Most of the Analytic of Principles beyond the Analogies in outline** — the Axioms, Anticipations, and Postulates systematize results you already have; the detail pays only on a return visit.
- **The Amphiboly of the Concepts of Reflection** — a technical polemic against Leibniz on identity and difference; fascinating, and entirely detachable on a first pass.
- **The A Deduction, as required reading** — a famous alternative route to the Deduction's conclusion, not a second required argument (unless the B Deduction defeats you, per Week 3).
- **The Second and Fourth Antinomies in full** — their resolutions repeat the First and Third's patterns (both false; both possibly true); summary gives you the pattern.
- **The Ideal of Pure Reason** — Kant's critique of the proofs of God's existence matters, but its upshot (no theoretical proof *or* disproof of God) is stated in the Canon, which you are reading.
- **The Doctrine of Method except the Canon** — the Discipline, Architectonic, and History of Pure Reason are housekeeping about philosophy itself; the Canon alone carries doctrine you need.
- **The Paralogisms beyond the first, in full** — one paralogism, fully understood, teaches the uniform diagnosis; the rest are variations.

A second pass adds exactly what the first deferred: the Principles in full, the Amphiboly, the Ideal, and the A Deduction read against the B.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction follows the learning path's discipline: premises, conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The Aesthetic's argument for space as an a priori form (A23–26/B38–41)

**Premises:**

1. To represent anything as outside me, or things as side by side, I must already represent them in space; space is presupposed by outer experience, not abstracted from it.
2. I can represent space empty of objects, but no outer object except in space; space is a necessary representation underlying all outer intuition.
3. Space is represented as one and infinite: particular spaces are limitations of it, not instances under a general concept. Space is therefore an intuition, not a concept.
4. Geometry consists of synthetic a priori propositions about space, holding necessarily of everything that can appear in space.

**Conclusion:** Space is neither a thing in itself nor a property of things in themselves, but the a priori form of outer sense — empirically real, transcendentally ideal — and geometry is possible as synthetic a priori knowledge because it articulates this form.

**Objection:** Premises 3 and 4 lean on Euclidean geometry as unique and necessary. Non-Euclidean geometries, unknown to Kant, show alternative spatial structures are consistently thinkable, and modern physics treats physical space empirically — so Kantian space looks like one geometry among others, not a necessary form of all intuition.

**Reply:** Kantians distinguish the *form of intuition* — spatiality as such — from the determinate metric geometry Kant took to articulate it. His Euclidean claims may fall while the core claim survives: outer experience still requires *some* a priori spatial form, contributed by sensibility rather than read off the world. Whether that retreat saves the argument or concedes its center is a live dispute.

### (b) The Deduction's unity-of-apperception argument, in outline (B, §§16–26)

**Premises:**

1. All my representations must be capable of being accompanied by one "I think"; a representation that could not in principle belong to one self-consciousness would be nothing for me.
2. Representations belong to one consciousness only through synthesis — combination of a manifold according to rules; combination is an act of the understanding, never given through the senses.
3. The rules of combination that yield unity in a manifold are also what make the manifold a representation *of an object*: an object is that in the concept of which a manifold is united.
4. The concepts expressing those rules of unity are the categories; and the manifold of our intuition is given in space and time, themselves unified under the same synthesis.

**Conclusion:** The categories apply necessarily and legitimately to everything that can be an object of experience for us — but only to objects of possible experience, never beyond it.

**Objection:** The argument risks proving too little: at most it shows we must *experience* the world as categorially structured. It does not follow, without a bridge principle, that objects themselves conform. This is Barry Stroud's challenge to transcendental arguments generally: necessity in our thought is not yet necessity in its objects.

**Reply:** Within Kant's system the gap is meant to collapse: objects of experience just *are* appearances structured by our forms and categories, so a condition necessary for experiencing objects is thereby a condition those objects, as experienced, satisfy. The reply works only given transcendental idealism — which is why later philosophers who borrow the strategy without the idealism owe the bridge Stroud demands.

### (c) The Third Antinomy: freedom and natural necessity (A532/B560 onwards)

**Premises:**

1. Taken as a thing in itself, the world generates contradiction: reason can prove both that a causality through freedom must exist and that no freedom exists (a free beginning would break the unity of experience).
2. The contradiction arises from assuming that appearances are things in themselves, so that the standpoint from which an action is judged is the only standpoint there is.
3. Distinguish standpoints: as appearance, every action stands under natural causality without exception; as the act of an intelligible agent — the agent considered as a thing in itself — the same action can be thought as freely begun, without formal contradiction.

**Conclusion:** Freedom and universal natural determinism are both thinkable together; theoretical reason can neither prove freedom nor rule it out, and the question of freedom's reality passes to practical reason.

**Objection:** Compatibility bought by unknowability looks like a cheat: the "intelligible" standpoint is one about which, by Kant's own account, nothing can be known. Locating freedom there protects it from refutation only by placing it where no claim can be checked — and critics have asked whether such a freedom is worth wanting.

**Reply:** Kant's aim is deliberately minimal: not to establish freedom, but to remove a supposed *theoretical veto* on it. The *Groundwork* and second *Critique* supply the positive practical ground — we must act under the idea of freedom. Whether a merely thinkable freedom can bear the weight Kant's ethics puts on it remains a central debate.

### (d) The Refutation of Idealism (B274–279)

**Premises:**

1. I am conscious of my own existence as determined in time: my inner states stand in a determinate temporal order.
2. All time-determination presupposes something permanent in perception, against which succession and duration can be determined.
3. This permanent thing cannot be found in me — in my representations, which are in constant flux — nor be a mere representation of an outer thing; it must be a thing outside me, actually perceived.

**Conclusion:** Consciousness of my own existence in time is possible only through the existence and perception of things outside me; inner experience presupposes outer experience, and the skeptical idealism that doubts the outer world while trusting the inner is refuted.

**Objection:** Premises 2 and 3 may carry more weight than they can bear: a determinate temporal order might require only a stable *framework* of representations, not actually existing outer things. Kant's slide from "something permanent" to "a permanent *thing outside me*" has struck critics since his contemporaries as the vulnerable joint.

**Reply:** A merely represented permanence would itself need determination in time, restarting the regress: something must be *perceived*, not merely imagined, to anchor temporal order, and it cannot be the flux of inner sense itself. The Refutation's brevity — barely two pages — has made it one of the book's most debated passages; its conclusion is in any case what Kant's empirical realism requires.

## Common misreadings

**1. "Kant says we can't know anything."**
Kant says we cannot know things *in themselves*, beyond possible experience. Within experience, knowledge is genuine and extensive: mathematics and natural science are his paradigms of secure cognition, whose possibility the *Critique* exists to explain.

**2. "Phenomena are illusions, and Kant's world is a dream."**
Phenomena are things as they appear under the conditions of human cognition — public, law-governed, and empirically real. Kant added the Refutation of Idealism to the B edition precisely to answer the dream-idealism charge. Appearance is not illusion (*Schein*), which is a separate failing of judgment.

**3. "The thing in itself is a hidden second world behind ours."**
That is the two-world reading stated as settled fact; it is one contested interpretation among two, as the Aesthetic section explains. On the two-aspect reading, "thing in itself" names the same things considered in abstraction from our cognitive conditions. Either way, it functions primarily as a limit-concept.

**4. "Kant proved that God does not exist."**
Kant argued that the traditional *proofs* of God's existence fail as theoretical knowledge — and equally that no theoretical disproof is possible. God re-enters his system as a postulate of practical reason. "Deny knowledge to make room for faith" is a partition, not an atheist verdict.

**5. "The Deduction is just psychology — a description of how the mind happens to work."**
The Deduction answers a question of right (*quid juris*), not of fact (*quid facti*): the categories *must* structure any experience a self-conscious being can have — not an empirical claim about how brains process inputs. Kant contrasts his deduction with Locke's "physiology of the human understanding." The A Deduction's psychological flavour tempts this misreading; one reason this guide reads B as primary.

**6. "The Copernican turn means the mind invents the world."**
The mind contributes the *form* of experience — space, time, the categories — not its content, which is given through sensibility and not of our making. Empirical objects remain independent of what any particular person thinks or wishes.

**7. "The Antinomies show reason is useless and Kant a sceptic."**
The Antinomies are diagnostic, not despairing: the contradictions arise from one misuse — treating the world as a completed totality given in itself — and dissolve under transcendental idealism. Reason emerges disciplined, not abolished.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **A = the first edition (1781), B = the second, revised edition (1787)**, cited by original page numbers. Read B where both exist; keep the A Deduction as the famous alternative route.
2. **As Copernicus explained the heavens better by moving the observer, objects of experience conform to our cognition** rather than cognition to objects — a reversal about the *form* of experience.
3. **Analytic:** the predicate is contained in the subject. **Synthetic:** it adds something. **A priori:** independent of particular experience, necessary and strictly universal; **a posteriori:** resting on experience. Synthetic a priori judgments extend knowledge yet hold necessarily — neither unpacking nor experience seems able to supply both features.
4. **"How are synthetic a priori judgments possible?"** They articulate the structure our faculties contribute to experience, so they hold necessarily of everything experienceable — and of nothing else.
5. Any two of: **space is presupposed, not derived** (representing things as outside me already requires space); **space is necessary** (empty space is representable, an outer object outside space is not); **space is a single infinite intuition**, not a general concept.
6. **Transcendentally ideal:** space and time belong to our mode of cognition, not to things apart from it. **Empirically real:** within experience they are fully objective — public, measurable, law-governed, not private or illusory.
7. **Two-world:** phenomena and things in themselves are two distinct realms of objects. **Two-aspect:** one world, considered two ways — as it appears under our cognitive conditions, and in abstraction from them. The dispute is unresolved; this guide takes no side.
8. **The categories are the understanding's twelve pure concepts** — a priori rules for unifying a manifold into experience of objects. The Metaphysical Deduction claims to derive them completely and systematically from the forms of judgment.
9. **Every representation of mine must in principle be capable of being taken up into one self-consciousness.** This unity of apperception is the Deduction's supreme premise, from which the categories' validity is derived.
10. **The categories apply necessarily to all objects of possible experience.** The limit: they yield knowledge only where intuition can be given, and are empty forms of thought beyond it.
11. **The problem of application:** pure, non-sensible concepts must apply to sensible appearances. Kant's mediator is the **schema** — a determination of time according to a category's rule.
12. **Rational psychology slides from a formal feature of thought to a property of a thing:** from the "I" as logical subject, and consciousness's unity and identity, it infers a soul that is a simple, persisting substance.
13. **First Antinomy:** thesis, the world has a beginning in time and spatial limits; antithesis, it is infinite in both. Resolution: **both false** — the world as completed totality is not an object given in itself. The Third resolves differently: **both can be true**, from different standpoints.
14. It establishes that **freedom is thinkable without contradiction alongside universal natural causality**. It does **not** establish that we are free; that question passes to practical reason, as in the *Groundwork*'s Section 3.
15. **What can I know? What ought I to do? What may I hope?** The Canon answers the third through the highest good: if I do what I ought, I may hope for happiness proportioned to worthiness — rational only as faith, never as knowledge.
