# Study Guide: René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641)

*Work guide · Companion to the [Descartes entry](../eras/03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md) · [Learning path](../learning-path.md)*

**Work:** *Meditationes de Prima Philosophia* (*Meditations on First Philosophy*)
**Author:** René Descartes (1596–1650)
**First published:** 1641, in Latin, at Paris, with the Objections and Replies; a second edition followed at Amsterdam in 1642, adding a seventh set of Objections and Replies. A French translation by the Duc de Luynes appeared in 1647.
**What this guide covers:** The six Meditations in order, the arguments they build, the classic objections (especially the "Cartesian circle"), and how to test your own understanding. The Objections and Replies are treated as optional further reading, not as a shortcut around the Meditations themselves.

## Suggested edition

Read the *Meditations* in John Cottingham's translation in the Cambridge edition (*Meditations on First Philosophy, with Selections from the Objections and Replies*, Cambridge University Press), or in Donald Cress's Hackett translation — these are the two editions named in this repo's [learning path](../learning-path.md). Both give you the Meditations with a good selection of the Objections and Replies, which is what you want: Descartes published the work *with* its critics, and reading at least Arnauld and Gassendi alongside him is part of understanding it.

A note on translations you may meet online: the older Haldane and Ross translation (1911) is in the public domain and is widely reproduced on the web. The learning path flags it as one to avoid for serious study, because it renders key terms (notably *cogitatio*, "thought") inconsistently and can obscure the argument. If you do consult it, treat it as a crib, not as your reading text. This guide paraphrases throughout; the few short quotations are given in Cottingham's rendering and attributed as such.

## How to use this guide

The *Meditations* is a short book — roughly a hundred pages in most editions — but it is dense in a way that rewards slowness. Descartes wrote it as a first-person exercise, not as a textbook: the narrator "meditates," and you are meant to meditate along with him, testing each step on yourself rather than merely noting it. That matters for how you read. When the First Meditation doubts the senses, try the doubt on your own beliefs before moving on. When the Second Meditation finds the cogito, check whether you find it in yourself. The arguments are cumulative and the order matters: the proof of God in the Third Meditation is what licenses the recovery of the external world in the Sixth, and the account of error in the Fourth is what explains how a perfect God and a fallible mind can coexist. Read out of order and the structure collapses.

### Reading plan

**Session 1 — Meditation I.** Read it slowly, then re-read the three waves of doubt (senses, dreaming, demon) and write down, for each, one belief of your own that it touches. Do not rush to the rescue in Meditation II; sit with the doubt long enough to feel why it needed answering.

**Session 2 — Meditation II.** The cogito and the wax. The wax passage is the hinge of the whole book: it is where Descartes first shows what the mind contributes to perception. Read it twice — once for the story, once for the argument about what a body *is*.

**Session 3 — Meditation III.** The longest and hardest meditation. Read the causal argument in two passes: first the machinery (objective reality, formal reality, the causal principle), then the application to the idea of God. Keep a glossary of the scholastic terms as you go; they recur in Meditation V.

**Session 4 — Meditation IV.** Short, and often skipped — the learning path's Stage 3 sequence lists Meditations 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 — but do not skip it. It answers the obvious objection to Meditation III ("if God is perfect, why do I err?") and introduces the will, which matters for Meditation VI's account of sensation. It is also the meditation most often underrated by first-time readers.

**Session 5 — Meditation V.** The ontological argument and the essence of material things. Read it against Meditation III: the two proofs of God are meant to reinforce each other, and their differences are instructive.

**Session 6 — Meditation VI.** The real distinction, the existence of material things, and the mind–body union. Read this as the payoff: everything since Meditation I has been building toward the claim that the external world exists and that you are not merely a mind.

**Further reading (optional, after the six):** The Objections and Replies. Start with the Fourth Objections (Arnauld — the Cartesian circle and the real distinction) and the Fifth (Gassendi — the cogito and the idea of God). Then the correspondence with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (1643), the sharpest contemporary statement of the interaction problem. These are not extras; in Descartes's own publishing plan they were part of the book.

**Time:** Six sessions of roughly an hour each for a first pass, plus time for the checkpoint questions at the end of this guide. A second pass, with the Objections, is worth at least as long again.

## Meditation I: Of the things that may be brought into doubt

### Summary

Descartes announces his project: to demolish his old opinions wholesale and rebuild from foundations that cannot be shaken. He will reject any belief that admits of the slightest doubt — not because he believes those beliefs false, but because a foundation must be certain. The doubt advances in three waves.

**First wave — the senses.** The senses sometimes deceive us: a distant tower looks round when it is square, a stick looks bent in water. If the senses have deceived us even once, can we fully trust them? Descartes's point is not that the senses are useless but that they cannot supply certainty.

**Second wave — dreaming.** There are no certain marks by which a waking experience can be distinguished, while it is happening, from a vivid dream. You may be dreaming right now. If so, your beliefs about your body, your room, and the objects around you could all be false, even though they feel certain. What survives this wave? Only the most general features of experience — the simple elements (extension, shape, number) out of which dreams, like paintings, are composed.

**Third wave — the evil demon.** Suppose, instead of God, there is a malicious demon "of the utmost power and cunning" who employs all his energies to deceive you. Then even the simplest truths — that two and three make five, that a square has four sides — might be false whenever you think them. This is the most radical wave because it targets not the senses but reason itself. Descartes is careful: he does not claim such a demon exists, only that its existence cannot be ruled out, and that is enough for doubt.

### Key passages

- The architectural image: a builder who suspects the foundations clears the site rather than patching the walls.
- The dream argument: the impossibility, from inside an experience, of certifying that it is waking.
- The demon hypothesis, introduced at the end of the meditation as the reason to suspend even arithmetic.

### The main argument

Methodic doubt is not a conclusion but a procedure. Its logic: certainty requires immunity to every possible doubt; each wave shows a class of beliefs that is not immune; therefore those beliefs must be suspended until a foundation is found. Note what the meditation does *not* do: it does not prove that the external world does not exist, and it does not conclude that nothing can be known. It clears the ground. Whether the clearing succeeds — whether the demon can really threaten arithmetic — is itself debated (see "Common misreadings" below).

## Meditation II: Of the nature of the human mind, and that it is more easily known than the body

### Summary

Even under the demon's assault, one thing survives: "I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind" (Cottingham trans.). To be deceived, there must be someone who is deceived; to doubt, someone who doubts. Doubting is a form of thinking, and thinking requires a thinker — at least while the thinking is going on.

But what *is* this "I"? Descartes proceeds by stripping away everything the demon could falsify: body, senses, imagination, memory of a past life. What remains is a thinking thing — *res cogitans* — a thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, refuses, imagines, and has sensory perceptions. Note the breadth: "thinking" here covers far more than reasoning. It includes willing, feeling, and seeming to see. Even if nothing I seem to see exists, the *seeming* is a thought, and the thought is mine.

The wax passage then shows why the mind is better known than the body. A piece of wax has every sensory quality — taste, smell, color, shape, hardness, sound when tapped — and then, held near a fire, loses all of them while remaining the same wax. What survives is not any sensory quality but something grasped by the intellect alone: the wax as an extended, flexible, changeable thing. Two conclusions follow. First, bodies are known not by the senses but by the mind. Second, the mind itself is known more directly than any body, because every perception of a body is also, and first, an awareness of the mind perceiving.

### Key passages

- The cogito formula: "I am, I exist" as necessarily true whenever conceived.
- The inventory of thought: doubting, understanding, affirming, denying, willing, refusing, imagining, sensing.
- The wax: the same wax through the total change of its sensory qualities.

### The main argument

The cogito (reconstructed formally in §"Argument reconstructions" below) establishes existence, but only momentary existence: I exist *while* I think. It does not yet establish what kind of thing I am beyond a thinker, whether I persist through time, or whether anything else exists. The wax argument then reframes knowledge of bodies: what we know of a body is its extension and flexibility, grasped by intellect, not the sensory qualities that change. This prepares the Sixth Meditation's claim that the essence of body is extension.

## Meditation III: Of God, that he exists

### Summary

Having secured the thinking self, Descartes looks for a way out of the self. He classifies his thoughts: ideas (images of things), volitions and emotions, and judgments (which combine ideas with affirmation or denial, and are where error lives). Then he asks: where do my ideas come from? Some seem invented (a chimera), some adventitious (arriving from outside, like the heat of a fire), some innate (like the idea of God).

The argument turns on a scholastic distinction between two kinds of reality. A thing has *formal* reality by existing in its own right; an idea has *objective* reality by representing something. Descartes's causal principle: there must be at least as much reality in the cause as in the effect, and in particular the objective reality of an idea must have a cause with at least as much formal reality as the idea has objective reality. A finite mind cannot be the cause of the idea of an infinite, perfect being, because the effect would exceed the cause. Therefore the idea of God must have been placed in me by a being that actually possesses the perfections the idea represents: God exists.

Descartes then answers the obvious objection: could I have derived the idea of God by negating my own finitude, or by augmenting my own qualities? No, he argues — I recognize my own imperfections *by comparison* with the idea of perfection, so the idea of perfection must come first; and negation of the finite does not yield the positive content of infinity. He also answers a second objection: could I, as a finite being, be the cause of my own existence, and so the author of the idea? No — self-creation would require perfections I plainly lack, and conservation through time is itself a kind of continuous creation requiring the same power.

### Key passages

- The classification of thoughts into ideas, volitions/affections, and judgments.
- The causal principle: the cause must contain at least as much reality as the effect.
- The reply to the "negation" objection: I know my defects only by comparison with perfection.

### The main argument

The causal argument for God (reconstructed in §"Argument reconstructions" below) is the load-bearing argument of the book. Everything after it — the criterion of clear and distinct perception, the real distinction, the external world — depends on God's existence and non-deceptive nature. That is precisely why it is the most attacked argument in the book, and why the Cartesian circle objection (below) is aimed at it. Its weakest joint, for most modern readers, is the causal principle itself: the claim that degrees of reality behave like quantities that a cause must match or exceed is a piece of scholastic metaphysics that few philosophers now accept. Descartes's defenders reply that within his framework the principle is principled, not ad hoc — but that defense concedes that the argument's force depends on accepting the framework.

## Meditation IV: Of the true and the false

### Summary

If God exists and is perfect, why do I make mistakes? A perfect creator would not build a deceiver, and a non-deceiving God seems incompatible with a mind that errs. Descartes's answer distinguishes two faculties. The intellect is finite — it perceives some things clearly and others obscurely. The will, by contrast, is in a sense infinite: it can affirm or deny anything at all. Error arises when the will outruns the intellect — when I judge about matters I do not clearly and distinctly perceive. The fault is mine, not God's: God gave me faculties that, used correctly, do not err. Error is a misuse, a privation rather than something positively created.

This meditation also states the criterion that the rest of the book will use: whatever I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true. The criterion is earned, in Descartes's view, by the proof of God: a non-deceiving God would not make my clearest perceptions systematically false.

### Key passages

- The asymmetry of intellect (finite) and will (effectively infinite in scope).
- Error as the will judging beyond what the intellect clearly perceives.
- The statement of the clear-and-distinct criterion.

### The main argument

The theodicy of error: God is exonerated because error comes from my free misuse of a good faculty, not from a defect in the faculties themselves. Whether this exonerates God or merely relocates the problem — why give a finite intellect to a will of unlimited scope? — is a live question. But the meditation's philosophical payoff is the criterion: it is what licenses the arguments of Meditations V and VI, and it is what the Cartesian circle objection calls into question.

### The Cartesian circle — a debate, not a verdict

Antoine Arnauld, in the Fourth Objections, raised the classic objection: Descartes appears to prove God's existence using clear and distinct perceptions, and then to validate clear and distinct perceptions by God's non-deceptive nature. If the criterion needs God, how can it prove God? This is the **Cartesian circle**.

Three main responses are on the table, and scholars still disagree about which, if any, succeeds:

1. **The memory reply.** Only *remembered* clear and distinct perceptions need God's guarantee; a perception attended to in the present moment is self-certifying while it lasts. The proof of God uses present intuitions, so it does not presuppose the guarantee. Critics reply that the proof of God itself takes time, and its steps must be remembered as the argument proceeds.
2. **The scope reply.** What God guarantees is not the criterion itself but the reliability of reason against a lingering metaphysical doubt — the demon hypothesis. The criterion was already evident; God removes the hyperbolic doubt that the demon might make even evident things false. Critics reply that this leaves the criterion doing the work God was supposed to do.
3. **The coherence reply.** Descartes's project is not a linear proof from indubitable premises but a mutually supporting system, and circularity is vicious only if the same premise is used to prove itself in the same step. Critics reply that this concedes the foundationalist ambition the Meditations announced.

Present the circle as a debate: it is one of the most discussed problems in early modern philosophy, and no reading of it commands consensus. What is not in doubt is its importance — if the circle is vicious, the bridge from the cogito to the external world fails.

## Meditation V: Of the essence of material things, and again of God, that he exists

### Summary

Descartes returns to the wax argument's lesson and generalizes it: the essence of material things is extension — length, breadth, depth, shape, position, motion — and these are known clearly and distinctly by the intellect, not the senses. Mathematical truths about bodies (that a triangle's angles sum to two right angles) are certain because they concern essences grasped by reason.

He then offers a second proof of God, the **ontological argument**: existence belongs to the idea of a supremely perfect being just as necessarily as having angles summing to two right angles belongs to the idea of a triangle. To think of God as not existing is as contradictory as thinking of a mountain without a valley. Where the Third Meditation argued from the *cause* of an idea, the Fifth argues from the *content* of the idea itself: a being with all perfections who lacked existence would lack a perfection, and so would not be supremely perfect.

Descartes anticipates the obvious objection — that this merely shows that *if* God exists, he exists necessarily, not that he does exist — and replies that the idea of God is not an invented fiction like a winged horse; it is an innate idea whose content forces itself on the mind, and existence is part of that content in a way it is part of no other idea's content.

### Key passages

- The essence of body as extension, grasped by intellect.
- The triangle analogy for necessary existence.
- The mountain-and-valley analogy for the inseparability of existence from God's essence.

### The main argument

The ontological argument's fate is famous. Kant later argued that existence is not a predicate — it adds nothing to the concept of a thing — so "existence belongs to God's essence" confuses the concept with the thing. Defenders of Descartes reply that Kant's point targets a different formulation, and that Descartes's argument turns on the special character of the idea of a necessary being rather than on treating existence as an ordinary property. Again, present this as a debate: the argument convinced few of Descartes's successors, but its exact flaw, if it has one, is still argued over.

## Meditation VI: Of the existence of material things, and of the real distinction between mind and body

### Summary

The final meditation does three things. First, it argues for the **real distinction**: I can clearly and distinctly conceive mind apart from body and body apart from mind; by God's power, what I can clearly and distinctly conceive as separate can be separated; therefore mind and body are really distinct substances. Second, it argues for the **existence of material things**: I have a faculty of imagination and a passive faculty of sensory perception, and these faculties must have a cause. The cause cannot be God (a non-deceiving God would not give me a strong natural inclination to believe bodies exist if they did not), and it cannot be my own mind (sensory ideas arrive unbidden). So material things exist and cause my sensations. Third, it explains the **mind–body union**: mind and body are not related as a pilot to a ship but are intermingled, so that sensations — pain, hunger, thirst — are confused modes of thought arising from the union, teaching me what benefits or harms the composite human being.

Descartes is careful about the limits of this recovery. The senses teach what is useful or harmful to the mind–body composite, not the true nature of things; for that, intellect is needed. And the existence proof establishes material things in general, not the reliability of any particular sensation.

### Key passages

- The real distinction argument: clear and distinct conceivability of separation.
- The argument from the passivity of sensation to an external cause.
- The pilot-and-ship image, rejected: the mind is intermingled with the body, not merely lodged in it.

### The main argument

The real distinction (reconstructed in §"Argument reconstructions" below) is the conclusion the whole book has been building toward, and it generates the interaction problem that Princess Elisabeth pressed in 1643 and that Spinoza, Malebranche, and Leibniz each rebuilt the system to solve: if mind is unextended and body is extension, how can they causally interact? Descartes's own answer — the union as a primitive notion, known by living it — satisfied few of his readers, and that dissatisfaction is itself part of the book's legacy.

## Argument reconstructions

### (a) The cogito (Meditation II)

1. I am doubting (or: I am thinking — doubting is a form of thinking).
2. Doubting/thinking cannot occur without a thinker existing while the thinking occurs.
3. Therefore, I exist — at least while I am thinking.

**Conclusion:** "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever it is conceived by me.

**Standard objection (Gassendi, Fifth Objections):** "I think" already smuggles in the "I." All the evidence warrants is the impersonal claim "thinking is occurring." To move from an occurrence of thought to a substantial thinker begs the question — you cannot infer the subject from the activity without already assuming a subject.

**Reply (Descartes's line, and its modern defenders):** Thought is never experienced impersonally. Doubting, willing, and feeling are first-personal in their very occurrence: there is no experience of doubting that is not *my* doubting. The "I" is not inferred from thought as an extra premise; it is given *in* the thought itself. Whether this reply succeeds depends on whether first-personal givenness is enough to establish a substantial self rather than a stream of experiences — a question Hume and later critics pressed hard, and which remains debated.

### (b) The causal argument for God's existence (Meditation III)

1. I have an idea of God: an infinite, eternal, immutable, independent, supremely intelligent and powerful being, creator of all things.
2. The objective reality of an idea — the reality of what it represents — must have a cause with at least as much formal reality (reality in its own right) as the idea has objective reality. (The causal principle.)
3. The idea of an infinite, perfect being has infinite objective reality.
4. I am finite, so I cannot be the cause of an idea with infinite objective reality; nor can any finite thing or combination of finite things.
5. Therefore, the cause of my idea of God must be a being with infinite formal reality — that is, God. God exists.

**Conclusion:** God exists, as the only adequate cause of the idea of God in me.

**Standard objection (Hobbes, Gassendi, and most modern critics):** The causal principle (premise 2) is undefended and implausible. Ideas do not carry measurable quantities of "reality" that a cause must match; a finite mind can form the idea of infinity by negating finitude or by extrapolating from finite perfections, without any infinite cause. The argument also assumes the scholastic hierarchy of degrees of reality that the new science was abandoning.

**Reply (Descartes's line, and its defenders):** The idea of God is not a negation or an extrapolation. Negation yields only "not-finite," which is not the positive content of infinity; and I recognize my own finitude only by comparison with a prior idea of the infinite, so the infinite idea cannot be derived from the finite one. Within Descartes's framework, the causal principle is not an ad hoc device but a general metaphysical principle (nothing comes from nothing, and an effect cannot exceed its cause) applied to ideas. The reply's force therefore depends on accepting that framework — which is why the argument persuades those who share Descartes's metaphysics and leaves others cold.

### (c) The real distinction between mind and body (Meditation VI)

1. I have a clear and distinct idea of myself as a thinking, unextended thing.
2. I have a clear and distinct idea of body as an extended, unthinking thing.
3. Whatever I clearly and distinctly understand as separate can be separated by God, and what God can separate is really distinct.
4. Therefore, I (mind) am really distinct from my body, and can exist without it.

**Conclusion:** Mind and body are distinct substances; the essence of mind is thought, the essence of body is extension.

**Standard objection (Arnauld, Fourth Objections, and Elisabeth's correspondence):** Clear and distinct conceivability is not a reliable guide to real separability. I can clearly conceive a right triangle without conceiving the Pythagorean theorem, yet the theorem belongs to the triangle's nature; likewise, my incomplete knowledge of myself may conceal a dependence on body that conceivability misses. And even if the distinction holds, it leaves the interaction problem: how does an unextended mind move an extended body?

**Reply (Descartes's line, and its defenders):** The triangle objection trades on incomplete knowledge, but Descartes claims *complete* knowledge of the relevant kind: I understand my essence as thinking through and through, and body's essence as extension through and through, with nothing left over that could hide a connection. The conceivability here is not mere imaginability but clear and distinct intellectual perception of essences. As for interaction, Descartes appeals to the union as a primitive notion — known by living it, not by analyzing it into its parts. Defenders concede this is the weakest joint in the system; critics regard it as naming the mystery rather than solving it. Either way, the distinction argument is what makes the interaction problem unavoidable, and that problem shaped the next century of philosophy.

## Checkpoint questions

Answer these from memory before looking at the answers at the end of this guide. They are ordered by meditation.

1. What is the difference between methodic doubt and skepticism as a conclusion, and which does Descartes practice?
2. The dream argument targets which class of beliefs, and what, if anything, does Descartes think survives it?
3. Why does Descartes introduce the evil demon after the dream argument — what does the demon threaten that dreaming does not?
4. State the cogito in Descartes's Meditation II formulation, and explain why its scope is limited to the moment of thinking.
5. In the wax passage, what changes and what stays the same — and what does Descartes conclude from this about how bodies are known?
6. What does Descartes mean by "objective reality" and "formal reality," and how does the distinction do work in Meditation III?
7. State the causal principle behind the Third Meditation's proof of God, and name its most vulnerable premise.
8. Why, on Descartes's account, can the idea of God not be derived by negating my own finitude?
9. How does Meditation IV reconcile a perfect, non-deceiving God with the fact that I make errors?
10. What is the Cartesian circle, who raised it, and what is the strongest reply to it?
11. How does the Fifth Meditation's ontological argument differ from the Third Meditation's causal argument?
12. State the real distinction argument in one paragraph, and explain why it generates the interaction problem.
13. In Meditation VI, how does Descartes argue from the passivity of sensation to the existence of material things?
14. Why does Descartes reject the image of the mind as a pilot in a ship?

## Common misreadings

1. **"I think, therefore I am" is a syllogism.** Descartes explicitly denied this. The cogito is not an inference from the premise "everything that thinks exists" — that premise would itself need justification and would be a target for the demon. It is an immediate intuition, performed in the act of thinking. (The *Discourse* formulation "I think, therefore I am" is a compressed slogan; the *Meditations* formulation, "I am, I exist," is the careful one.)

2. **Descartes's doubt is skeptical despair.** No — doubt is his method, not his conclusion. He doubts in order to find what cannot be doubted, and the whole book after Meditation I is a reconstruction. A skeptic ends where Descartes begins.

3. **The cogito proves an immaterial soul.** Not by itself. The cogito proves only that a thinker exists while thinking. That the thinker is an immaterial substance, distinct from body, is argued much later, in Meditation VI, with God's guarantee in between. Collapsing the two steps makes the argument look both stronger and weaker than it is.

4. **Descartes denies that the body exists.** The opposite: Meditation VI is devoted to proving that material things exist. What Descartes denies is that the *essence* of body is its sensory qualities. Bodies exist; they are extension, not color, taste, or smell.

5. **The evil demon is a theological claim.** It is a hypothesis, not a doctrine. Descartes does not assert that a demon exists, still less that God is a deceiver. The demon is a device for testing which beliefs survive the strongest possible doubt. Confusing the device with a belief is the same error as confusing a thought experiment with a creed.

6. **"Cartesian dualism" is Descartes's own label, and it means mind and body never interact.** Descartes never used the term, and he insisted that mind and body form a union and interact constantly — sensations, passions, and voluntary motion are the evidence. The problem he left is explaining *how* interaction works, not whether it happens. The label "Cartesian" as a general term of abuse — for cold, disembodied rationalism — is largely a later invention, and it sits awkwardly with *The Passions of the Soul* (1649), a richly embodied account of emotion.

7. **Clear and distinct perception means "vivid feeling of certainty."** Descartes's "clear" means present to an attentive mind, and "distinct" means sharply separated from everything else, containing nothing confused. A vivid hallucination can feel certain without being clear and distinct in this technical sense. The criterion is intellectual, not psychological.

8. **The Meditations prove that the senses are worthless.** Meditation VI recovers the senses as reliable guides to what benefits or harms the mind–body composite. What the senses cannot do is reveal the true nature of bodies; that is the intellect's work. Descartes demotes the senses from metaphysicians to physicians, and that is a subtler verdict than dismissal.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **Methodic doubt is a procedure; skepticism is a conclusion.** Descartes suspends beliefs that admit of doubt in order to find a foundation, not because he holds that nothing can be known. The book's trajectory — from doubt to God to the external world — is an anti-skeptical project.

2. **The dream argument targets beliefs about the present environment** — that you are sitting by a fire, holding paper, and so on. What survives, Descartes suggests, are the simplest general elements out of which both waking and dreaming experience are composed: extension, shape, quantity, number. These are like the colors on a painter's palette.

3. **The demon threatens reason itself.** Dreaming casts doubt on the senses and on particular present experiences, but arithmetic and geometry seem to hold even in dreams. The demon hypothesis extends doubt to those: a supremely powerful deceiver could make you go wrong even when you judge that two and three make five.

4. **"I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind"** (Cottingham trans.). Its scope is momentary: it secures existence only while the thinking occurs. It does not prove persistence through time, a body, a past, or anything else.

5. **Everything sensory changes** — taste, smell, color, shape, hardness, sound — **while the wax remains the same wax.** Descartes concludes that what we know of the wax is not delivered by the senses but grasped by the intellect: the wax as an extended, flexible, changeable thing. Bodies are known by the mind, not the senses.

6. **Objective reality** is the reality a thing has as represented in an idea; **formal reality** is the reality a thing has by existing in its own right. The distinction lets Descartes argue that an idea with infinite objective reality (the idea of God) needs a cause with infinite formal reality — and only God qualifies.

7. **The causal principle:** there must be at least as much reality in the cause as in the effect; in particular, the objective reality of an idea must be caused by something with at least as much formal reality. Its most vulnerable premise is the principle itself — the claim that "degrees of reality" behave like quantities a cause must match is a piece of scholastic metaphysics most modern readers reject.

8. **Negation yields only "not-finite,"** not the positive content of infinity. And Descartes argues the order runs the other way: I recognize my own defects and finitude only by comparison with a prior idea of perfection, so the idea of perfection cannot be derived from the finite mind it is used to judge.

9. **Error is my misuse of good faculties, not a defect in them.** The intellect is finite; the will is effectively unlimited in scope. Error occurs when the will judges about matters the intellect does not clearly and distinctly perceive. God gave faculties that, used within their limits, do not err; the fault lies in my free assent beyond those limits.

10. **The circle:** Descartes seems to prove God using clear and distinct perceptions, then to validate clear and distinct perceptions by God's non-deceptive nature. It was raised by Antoine Arnauld in the Fourth Objections. The strongest reply, on most readings, is the memory reply: only remembered clear and distinct perceptions need God's guarantee, while present intuitions are self-certifying — though critics object that the proof of God itself takes time and relies on memory.

11. **The Third Meditation argues from the cause of an idea** (only God could cause the idea of an infinite being in a finite mind). **The Fifth argues from the content of the idea** (existence belongs to the idea of a supremely perfect being as necessarily as three-sidedness belongs to a triangle). The Third is causal and a posteriori in form; the Fifth is conceptual and a priori.

12. **The real distinction argument:** I clearly and distinctly conceive mind as thinking and unextended, and body as extended and unthinking; what I can clearly and distinctly conceive as separate, God can separate; therefore mind and body are really distinct substances. It generates the interaction problem because two substances with nothing in common — one unextended, one extended — seem to lack any mechanism of causal contact, yet experience shows them interacting constantly.

13. **Sensory ideas arrive unbidden and involuntarily** — I cannot choose what I seem to see when I open my eyes. The cause cannot be my own mind (the ideas are not produced at will) and cannot be God directly (a non-deceiving God would not give me a powerful natural inclination to believe bodies cause them if they did not). So material things exist as their cause.

14. **Because the pilot is merely lodged in the ship, not united with it.** Descartes insists the mind is intermingled with the body, so that sensations — pain, hunger, thirst — are not mere observations of damage but confused thoughts arising from the union, teaching the composite being what helps or harms it. A pilot would merely *see* the ship's damage; a mind feels its body's pain.
