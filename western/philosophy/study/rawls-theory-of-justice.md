# Study Guide: Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*

**Work:** *A Theory of Justice* (revised edition, Harvard University Press, 1999) · **Author:** John Rawls (1921–2002) · **Covers:** §§1–4, §§11–13 and §17, and §24 in full, with the reasoning of the parties (§§25–29) and the lexical priority of the principles — with a reading plan, section-by-section guide, argument reconstructions, self-test questions, and common misreadings.
**Companion entry:** [John Rawls](../eras/05-20th-century/john-rawls.md) · **Study plan:** [Learning Path](../learning-path.md)

## Edition note

Read the revised edition of 1999, not the 1971 original. Rawls revised the text substantially — restating the two principles and reworking the argument for them — and the revised edition is the one current scholarship cites. The section numbers in this guide are the revised edition's, so navigate by section rather than by page.

The sections this guide covers, in the revised edition's numbering, are:

- §§1–4: the role of justice, the subject of justice, the main idea of the theory, and the original position and justification.
- §11: the two principles of justice stated.
- §12: interpretations of the second principle — the systems of natural liberty, liberal equality, and democratic equality.
- §13: democratic equality and the difference principle.
- §17: the tendency to equality — what the difference principle does to the distribution over time.
- §24: the veil of ignorance, in full.
- §§25–29: the rationality of the parties and the reasoning that leads to the two principles rather than to average utility.

A note on quotations: where a short phrase is quoted, it follows Rawls's own wording. The guide otherwise paraphrases, so your reconstruction work is in your own words — as the learning path requires.

On dates: *A Theory of Justice* first appeared in 1971; the revised edition followed in 1999, after Rawls had spent nearly thirty years answering critics. *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001) is Rawls's own shorter, clearer version of the whole theory. The companion entry's advice stands: do not start with *A Theory of Justice* cover to cover. Read the Restatement first for the map, then use this guide for the classic text's argument where it matters most.

## How to use this guide

Rawls's prose is deliberately careful, and the difficulty is structural — the argument for the two principles is spread across the original position (§4), the statement of the principles (§11), the interpretation of the second principle (§§12–13), and the reasoning of the parties (§§24–29), with the defense against utilitarianism running through all of it. Reading any one section in isolation gives you a slogan; reading them together gives you the theory.

Work through the guide in three passes, matching the reading rule used throughout this library:

1. **Skim for structure.** Before reading a section, read its part of this guide. Know what question it answers and where its argument turns.
2. **Read slowly, reconstructing.** After each section, write the main argument in numbered premises and a conclusion, book closed, before reading the reconstructions below — they are a check, not a substitute.
3. **Evaluate.** Only then read the objection and reply for each reconstruction, and form your own judgment. Critique always comes second, never first.

## Reading plan

The sections covered here run to roughly 120 pages in the revised edition. At about two hours of primary reading per week, the program below takes about six weeks.

| Week | Reading | Focus |

| 2 | §24, §§25–26 | The veil of ignorance and the reasoning of the parties |
| 3 | §§27–29 | Why the parties reject average utility and choose the two principles |
| 4 | §11, §12 | The two principles stated; the three interpretations of the second principle |

## Section-by-section guide

### §1 — The role of justice

Rawls opens with a sentence worth memorizing: "Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought." A theory may be elegant and still be rejected if untrue; an institution, however efficient and well arranged, must be reformed or abolished if unjust. Justice is not one value among others to be traded off against welfare or stability. It sets the constraint within which other goods are pursued.

Two ideas do the work here. First, society is a cooperative venture for mutual advantage, but one marked by both a conflict and an identity of interests: everyone benefits from cooperation, yet everyone prefers a larger share of its fruits. Principles of justice are needed precisely because of that combination — pure identity of interests would need no principles, and pure conflict would admit none. Second, Rawls assumes the circumstances of justice: moderate scarcity and the fact that people have different life plans and conceptions of the good. Under those circumstances, a shared conception of justice is what lets free and equal persons cooperate without coercion being arbitrary.

**Main argument:** Because society is both cooperative and conflictual, it needs principles of justice that all can accept — and those principles constrain, rather than compete with, other social goods.

### §2 — The subject of justice

The subject of justice, for Rawls, is the basic structure of society: the way its major institutions — the political constitution, the legally recognized forms of property, the organization of the economy, the family in its institutional aspect — distribute fundamental rights and duties and determine the division of advantages from social cooperation. This is the primary subject because these institutions shape people's life prospects from the start, before any choice they make, and citizens cannot simply opt out of them.

Two consequences follow. First, Rawls is not offering a complete moral theory. His principles judge institutions, not individual actions; a society can satisfy them while its citizens still face ordinary moral questions the theory does not answer. Second, the focus on the basic structure explains why Rawls can treat the distribution of natural talents as morally arbitrary without denying that talents are real. The question is never whether the talented deserve their gifts in some cosmic sense; it is whether institutions may let those gifts determine people's prospects without limit. Keep this distinction in mind through §13: the difference principle is a rule for institutions, not a judgment on persons.

**Main argument:** Justice is primarily about the basic structure, because that is what distributes life prospects before choice begins.

### §3 — The main idea of the theory

Here Rawls states the theory in one paragraph, and the rest of the book unpacks it. The guiding idea is that the principles of justice are those that free and rational persons, concerned to further their own interests, would accept in an initial position of equality as defining the fundamental terms of their association. The initial position is not a historical gathering; it is a hypothetical situation with certain features, designed so that the principles chosen in it are fair. The name Rawls gives the theory is "justice as fairness": fairness is built into the choice situation itself, so that whatever principles emerge from a fair choice situation count as just.

The section also introduces the idea of a well-ordered society: one in which everyone accepts the same principles of justice, everyone knows the others do too, and the basic structure actually satisfies those principles. The original position is a device for finding the principles of such a society — not a description of how any real society began.

Note what Rawls is and is not claiming. He is not claiming that real people have consented to anything. He is claiming that principles chosen under fair conditions are principles we have reason to accept — the fairness of the procedure is what justifies the outcome. Whether a hypothetical choice can carry that justificatory weight is the first serious question to put to the theory, and it recurs in the objection to reconstruction (a) below.

**Main argument:** Just principles are those that would be chosen in a fair initial situation of equality — and building fairness into the choice situation is what makes the outcome just.

### §4 — The original position and justification

This is the section that introduces the machinery, and it repays the closest reading in the first four sections. The original position is a device of representation: it models the idea of free and equal persons choosing the terms of their association fairly. Its defining feature is the veil of ignorance, treated in full in §24, but introduced here: the parties do not know their place in society, their natural assets, or their conception of the good. They do know general facts about human society — political affairs, economics, psychology, social organization — because without that knowledge they could not choose principles at all.

Rawls is explicit about why the constraints are justified, and the justification matters more than the image. The veil models two moral convictions most readers already hold: that no one deserves the advantages of birth — family, class, native talent — and that those advantages should not by themselves determine a person's political standing or life prospects. The original position is therefore not an arbitrary thought experiment; it is an attempt to give those convictions a precise form, so that their implications can be worked out rigorously.

The section also introduces reflective equilibrium, Rawls's method of justification, though its full treatment comes later. We test the original position and its output against our considered judgments — convictions we hold with confidence, such as that slavery and religious persecution are unjust. If the principles condemn a judgment we cannot give up, we revise the principles or the description of the position; if a principle we trust exposes a judgment as prejudice, we revise the judgment. Justification is this back-and-forth until principles and judgments cohere. There is no foundation outside it. Critics ask whether coherence merely systematizes the prejudices we started with; Rawls's answer is that wide reflective equilibrium — which tests judgments against rival theories and background facts — is the best justification moral philosophy can honestly offer.

**Main argument:** The original position translates the moral arbitrariness of birth into a choice situation, and reflective equilibrium — mutual adjustment of principles and considered judgments — is how we test whether the translation is right.

### §24 — The veil of ignorance (in full)

Section 24 is the hinge of the book. Read it slowly, and note exactly what is hidden and what is left known, because every common misreading of Rawls gets one of these two lists wrong.

**What the parties do not know:** their place in society — class position and social status; their fortune in the distribution of natural assets and abilities; their conception of the good — the particulars of their rational plan of life; the special features of their psychology, such as aversion to risk; and the particular circumstances of their own society. They do not even know which generation they belong to, so they cannot rig principles to favor the present at the expense of the future.

**What the parties do know:** general facts about human society — that the circumstances of justice obtain, the principles of economic theory, the laws of human psychology, the basis of social organization. They know they are choosing principles for a society that will endure, and that the principles must be ones they can live with whoever they turn out to be. They also know they have a conception of the good and a sense of justice, in the thin sense: they want to pursue their own plan of life, whatever it is, and they care about being able to do so.

Three features of the veil deserve emphasis. First, it is thick but not total: the parties are not amnesiacs. They retain exactly the knowledge needed to choose principles rationally, and lose exactly the knowledge that would let them tailor principles to their own advantage. Second, the veil enforces unanimity in a strong sense: since the parties are symmetrically situated and no one can identify an advantage, they reason alike, and the choice is effectively that of one representative person. Third, the veil is what makes the agreement fair rather than a bargain. In an ordinary negotiation, the strong extract concessions; behind the veil there is no strong and no weak, so the outcome cannot reflect bargaining power. That is why Rawls can call the result fair without smuggling in a prior standard of fairness.

**Main argument:** By hiding morally arbitrary facts while preserving general knowledge, the veil forces impartial choice — principles no one could tailor to their own advantage, and therefore principles all could accept.

### §§25–29 — The rationality of the parties and the reasoning leading to the two principles

Sections 25 to 29 contain the argument most readers skip and most critics attack: why would the parties choose Rawls's two principles rather than something else — in particular, rather than average utility, the principle of maximizing average welfare?

Rawls begins (§25) with the parties' rationality. They are rational in a deliberately thin sense: they aim to advance their own interests as defined by their conception of the good, whatever it turns out to be, and they prefer more rather than less of the primary goods — rights, liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect — because those are all-purpose means useful for any life plan. They are not assumed to be risk-averse as a psychological trait; the veil hides their attitudes to risk. What they are assumed to care about is securing their fundamental interests, and in particular the basic liberties and the social bases of self-respect, which cannot be traded for material gain without undermining the very capacity to pursue a life plan.

Sections 27 and 28 then take on average utility directly. The average principle asks each person to gamble: accept institutions that maximize average welfare even if you turn out to be among those sacrificed for the total. Rawls argues the parties would not take that gamble, for three reasons developed in §29. First, they have no basis for assigning probabilities to ending up in any particular position, so expected-value reasoning has nothing to work with. Second, the stakes are not ordinary: the choice determines the fundamental terms of a whole life, and a bad outcome means a life without basic liberties or adequate prospects — not a loss that can be compensated later. Third, the two principles offer a satisfactory alternative: a guaranteed minimum that is itself attractive, so there is no need to gamble for a higher average that might leave you at the bottom. This is the maximin rule: choose the arrangement whose worst outcome is best — a decision rule for radical uncertainty about fundamental interests, not a psychological quirk.

Section 29 gathers the main grounds for the two principles: they protect the basic liberties that make any life plan possible, they express reciprocity (inequalities must be justifiable to those who benefit least), and they fit the publicity condition — principles must be ones citizens can publicly affirm, knowing others do too. Average utility, Rawls argues, fails publicity in a telling way: a society run on utilitarian principles would have to conceal from its worst-off members that their sacrifices are required by the total, since openly telling them would undermine their allegiance. Whether that charge is fair to utilitarianism — and whether Rawls's own principles pass the same test — is debated; hold it as a debate, not a verdict.

**Main argument:** Under radical uncertainty about fundamental interests, with no probabilities and a satisfactory guaranteed alternative, rational parties choose the two principles over average utility — the maximin rule applied to the basic structure.

### §11 — The two principles of justice

Section 11 states the principles the parties choose, and the statement matters word for word. In the revised edition, Rawls gives them in this form:

**First principle:** Each person has an equal right to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties which is compatible with a similar scheme of liberties for all.

**Second principle:** Social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so that they are both: (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged, consistent with the just savings principle, and (b) attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity.

The basic liberties named include political liberty, freedom of speech and assembly, liberty of conscience, freedom of the person, and the rights covered by the rule of law. Economic freedoms such as freedom of contract as such are not basic liberties — a restriction that is itself contested.

The principles are ordered lexically: the first takes absolute priority over the second, and within the second, fair equality of opportunity (b) takes priority over the difference principle (a). Lexical priority is stronger than mere weight: a gain under the second principle cannot justify a loss under the first, however large. Basic liberties may not be traded for economic advantage. The ordering is the structural expression of Rawls's rejection of utilitarian trade-offs, and it is what makes the theory liberal before it is egalitarian.

**Main argument:** Justice requires equal basic liberties first, fair opportunity second, and inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged — in that strict order.

### §12 — Interpretations of the second principle

Section 12 asks what the second principle could mean, and distinguishes three interpretations by how they read its two parts — open positions and benefit to all.

**The system of natural liberty** reads "open to all" as merely formally open: careers open to talents, with no barriers of birth or law. Inequalities are acceptable if they arise from a fair competition. Rawls rejects this because it lets morally arbitrary advantages — family wealth, social connections, native talent — determine outcomes, while calling the result fair because the race was formally open.

**Liberal equality** corrects this by adding fair equality of opportunity in a substantive sense: persons with the same talents and motivation should have the same prospects regardless of class origin. Schools, training, and culture must be arranged to neutralise class disadvantage. Rawls finds this an improvement but still insufficient, because it leaves the distribution of natural talents itself uncorrected: those born gifted still reap the rewards, and those born less gifted still lose, through no choice of their own.

**Democratic equality** combines fair equality of opportunity with the difference principle: inequalities are permitted only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. This is Rawls's own reading, and it is the one §13 defends. Learn the three interpretations as a ladder: each corrects the arbitrariness the previous one left standing.

**Main argument:** Of the three readings of the second principle, only democratic equality corrects for the arbitrariness of both social class and natural talent.

### §13 — Democratic equality and the difference principle

Section 13 is the heart of the egalitarian argument, and it needs to be read with §12, not instead of it. The difference principle says: social and economic inequalities are just only if they are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged.

First, "least advantaged" is defined by an index of primary goods — income and wealth, powers and prerogatives of office, and the social bases of self-respect — not by any single measure, and Rawls is explicit that identifying the least advantaged in practice is a difficult empirical matter. The principle is a standard for judging institutions, not a formula for computing tax rates. Second, the principle permits inequality; it does not forbid it. If allowing higher pay for doctors, engineers, or entrepreneurs produces a larger, better-organized economy in which the worst-paid are better off than under strict equality, the inequality is not merely tolerated but required by the principle. If the same inequality leaves the worst off no better — or worse — it is unjust, however much it increases total output.

The deeper argument is about reciprocity and justification. A society's institutions are a shared scheme of cooperation; the talented did not create their talents, and the less advantaged did not choose their disadvantages. An arrangement can therefore be justified to the least advantaged only if it works to their advantage too. The talented may earn more, but only in a system that also raises the floor — a system the least advantaged could endorse, knowing it benefits them.

What the principle forbids is equally precise: inequalities justified only by gains to the already advantaged, or by total output that leaves the worst off behind; and any trading of basic liberties or fair opportunity for economic gains, since the lexical order means the difference principle governs only the inequalities that remain after the first principle and fair opportunity are satisfied. G. A. Cohen's objection — that the principle as Rawls applies it lets the talented hold society hostage through incentive demands — is the most influential egalitarian criticism of the theory, and is presented as a debate in reconstruction (b) below.

**Main argument:** Inequality is just only when it benefits the least advantaged — a rule of reciprocity for institutions, not a demand for equal outcomes.

### §17 — The tendency to equality

Section 17 asks a dynamic question the static statement of the principles leaves open: what happens to the distribution over time under the difference principle? Rawls's answer is that the principle has a built-in tendency toward equality. Under it, the expectations of the least advantaged are maximized; as the economy grows and institutions improve, those expectations rise, and the gap between the least advantaged and others tends to narrow rather than widen, because any further inequality must again be justified by benefit to the bottom.

Three qualifications keep this claim honest. First, it operates within the just savings principle: each generation must preserve enough capital — material, institutional, and cultural — for the next. Second, it is a tendency, not a guarantee: it depends on institutions actually being arranged as the principles require, including the fair value of political liberty, which prevents wealth from converting into political domination. Third, the difference principle does not aim at equality as such. If the least advantaged are best off under some remaining inequality, the principle keeps it. Equality is the expected direction of travel, not the destination — a distinction that separates Rawls from strict egalitarians.

**Main argument:** Arranged as the principles require, institutions tend to narrow inequality over time — but equality is the tendency, not the target.

## Argument reconstructions

Each reconstruction below follows the learning path's discipline: premises, then conclusion, then one standard objection and one reply. Reconstruct them yourself, book closed, before comparing.

### (a) The argument from the original position to the two principles

**Premises:**

1. Principles of justice should be ones that free and equal persons would accept in a fair initial situation of equality, where no one can tailor principles to their own advantage.
2. The original position models that situation: parties choose principles behind a veil of ignorance, knowing general facts about society but not their own place, talents, or conception of the good.
3. Behind the veil, the parties are rational, mutually disinterested, and concerned to secure primary goods — basic liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect — whatever their eventual position.
4. Under radical uncertainty about their position, with no basis for assigning probabilities and with fundamental interests at stake, the parties will choose by maximin: they will prefer the arrangement whose worst outcome is best.
5. The two principles, in lexical order, offer the best worst outcome: guaranteed equal basic liberties, fair opportunity, and inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged. Average utility, by contrast, permits outcomes in which a person lacks basic liberties or adequate prospects for the sake of a greater total.

**Conclusion:** The parties in the original position would choose the two principles of justice, and those principles are therefore justified as the principles of a just basic structure.

**Objection:** The argument smuggles in its conclusion. The veil is designed by Rawls to exclude exactly the information that would lead to other principles, and the parties are modelled as caring about exactly the goods Rawls's principles protect. A utilitarian can reply that behind a genuinely impartial veil, parties who knew they might be anyone would maximize expected welfare — average utility — rather than maximin, unless they were assumed to be extremely risk-averse. And communitarian critics add that the parties are already liberal individualists, stripped of the attachments and communities that real persons reason from, so the choice situation presupposes the theory it is meant to justify.

**Reply:** Rawls can answer that the veil's design is itself justified by reflective equilibrium, not by fiat: it models the conviction that birth advantages are morally arbitrary, and the test is whether the whole system coheres with considered judgments. On maximin versus expected utility, the choice is not a gamble with known odds but a decision about a whole life under radical uncertainty, with a satisfactory guaranteed alternative available — conditions under which maximin is rational, not timid. Whether that reply succeeds is the central dispute about the book's argument.

### (b) The argument for the difference principle over utilitarianism

**Premises:**

1. Utilitarianism judges institutions by their total or average welfare, and therefore permits sacrificing some persons' basic interests when the gains to others are large enough.
2. Such aggregation does not take seriously the separateness of persons: what is rational for one person — accepting a present pain for a greater future gain within a single life — cannot be transferred to society, because there is no single social subject who experiences the total.
3. Behind the veil, no one would accept principles that might license sacrificing their basic liberties or prospects for a larger sum of welfare enjoyed by strangers, since each person lives only their own life.
4. The difference principle replaces aggregation with reciprocity: inequalities are permitted only when they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged, so that every arrangement can be justified to those who benefit least from it.

**Conclusion:** The difference principle, not average utility, should govern social and economic inequalities, because it respects the separateness of persons and the requirement that institutions be justifiable to each citizen.

**Objection:** From the egalitarian left, G. A. Cohen argues the difference principle is too permissive: it lets the talented demand incentive payments as the price of their cooperation, so that inequalities persist not because they are necessary but because the talented refuse to work without them. If the talented internalized the principle, they would not need bribing, and the distribution would be more equal. From the libertarian right, Nozick argues the principle is too demanding: it treats talents and their fruits as a common asset, violating self-ownership and requiring continual interference with free exchange. Both objections agree the principle sits in an unstable middle; they disagree about which direction it should move.

**Reply:** Rawls's reply to Cohen is that the principles govern the basic structure, not individual choices within it: once institutions are just, individuals are free to pursue their own plans, and requiring the talented to refuse incentives would turn a theory of institutions into a comprehensive moral doctrine. Against Nozick, the reply is that no one deserves their genetic and social starting points, so institutions that let those starting points determine prospects without limit cannot be justified to the least advantaged. Both replies are contested; the Cohen–Rawls–Nozick debate is the best single route into the theory's fault lines.

### (c) The argument for the priority of liberty

**Premises:**

1. The basic liberties — political liberty, freedom of speech and assembly, liberty of conscience, freedom of the person, and the rule of law — are the conditions under which persons can form, revise, and pursue a conception of the good, and exercise their sense of justice.
2. A person deprived of basic liberties cannot be compensated by material gains: wealth without liberty of conscience or political voice is not a life a free person could endorse, because the capacities that make a life one's own have been removed.
3. Behind the veil, the parties know they will have a conception of the good and a sense of justice, whatever their content, and they know basic liberties are necessary for exercising both.
4. Therefore no rational party would trade basic liberties for economic advantage, however large, because the trade sacrifices the very conditions of pursuing any good at all.

**Conclusion:** The first principle takes lexical priority over the second: basic liberties may not be sacrificed for economic gains, and economic inequality is permitted only within a scheme that first secures equal liberty for all.

**Objection:** The priority is too rigid to be credible. There are cases — extreme poverty, famine, societies below the level where basic liberties can be fully exercised — where trading some liberty for subsistence seems not only rational but required, and Rawls himself allows that the priority holds fully only once a society reaches a level of material development where basic liberties can be effectively enjoyed. Critics ask whether a priority with that exception is really lexical at all, and whether the distinction between basic and non-basic liberties — which excludes economic freedoms such as freedom of contract — can be drawn without begging the question in favor of Rawls's own conclusions.

**Reply:** Rawls can concede the developmental qualification while holding the priority: lexical priority governs the principles for a society that has reached the material conditions for their full exercise, and below that threshold the theory's application is admittedly incomplete rather than refuted. On the basic/non-basic line, the defense is that basic liberties are those necessary for the two moral powers — a conception of the good and a sense of justice — while economic freedoms, though important, are protected under the second principle instead. Whether that distinction holds is a live question, and the right place to press the theory.

## Common misreadings

**1. "The veil of ignorance means the parties know nothing at all."**
The veil is thick but not total. The parties retain general knowledge — economics, psychology, the circumstances of justice — and lose only particular knowledge about themselves: their class, talents, conception of the good, and generation. A party who knew nothing could not choose principles at all; a party who knew everything about themselves would tailor principles to their own advantage.

**2. "The difference principle means maximise the position of the worst off at any cost."**
The principle governs only the inequalities that remain after the first principle and fair equality of opportunity are satisfied — the lexical order is a hard constraint, not a tiebreaker. It cannot justify sacrificing basic liberties or fair opportunity for the sake of the worst off, and it permits inequality whenever inequality benefits the least advantaged.

**3. "Rawls requires strict equality of outcome."**
He requires the opposite of a slogan and something subtler than equality: equal basic liberties, fair opportunity, and inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged. A society in which everyone has the same income but the least advantaged are worse off than they would be under an unequal arrangement violates the difference principle, not satisfies it. Rawls's target is arbitrary inequality, not inequality as such.

**4. "The original position is a historical event, or a contract real people signed."**
No such event happened and no signature is claimed. The original position is a device of representation — a way of modeling fair choice — and its justificatory force comes from reflective equilibrium, not from consent. Treating it as history misses the argument entirely; the question is whether principles chosen under fair conditions are ones we have reason to accept.

**5. "Maximin is just risk aversion — Rawls assumes people are timid."**
The veil hides the parties' attitudes to risk, so no psychological assumption is made. Maximin is defended as rational under three specific conditions: radical uncertainty with no basis for probabilities, fundamental interests at stake across a whole life, and a satisfactory guaranteed alternative. Remove any one condition and Rawls's argument weakens — which is why critics attack the conditions, not the psychology.

**6. "The difference principle is just redistribution — take from the rich, give to the poor."**
Redistribution after the fact is only one instrument, and not Rawls's preferred one. The principle governs the basic structure — property rules, tax regimes, education, the organization of the economy — so that the distribution it produces is just in the first place, rather than unjust and then corrected.

**7. "Rawls is a utilitarian with extra safeguards."**
Priority of liberty over welfare gains is a structural rejection of maximizing trade-offs, not a safeguard added to them. The separateness-of-persons objection holds that aggregation across persons is illegitimate in a way no safeguard can fix, because there is no single subject who enjoys the total.

## Answers to checkpoint questions

1. **Justice as the first virtue** means an institution, however efficient, must be reformed or abolished if unjust — just as a theory, however elegant, must be rejected if untrue. Justice constrains other social goods rather than competing with them.
2. **The basic structure** is the way society's major institutions — constitution, property system, economic organization — distribute fundamental rights, duties, and the advantages of cooperation. It is primary because it shapes life prospects from the start, before choice, and citizens cannot opt out of it.
3. **The guiding idea:** the principles of justice are those free and rational persons would accept in an initial position of equality as defining the fundamental terms of their association. The position is **hypothetical** because it models fair choice; it describes no historical event and claims no actual consent.
4. **Reflective equilibrium** is the mutual adjustment of principles and considered judgments until they cohere. **Considered judgments** — convictions held with confidence, such as that slavery and religious persecution are unjust — are the fixed points: if principles condemn one, revise the principles; if trusted principles expose a judgment as prejudice, revise the judgment.
5. **Do not know:** class position; natural assets and abilities; their conception of the good (and their generation). **Do know:** general facts about society — economics, psychology, the circumstances of justice. The second list matters because without it the parties could not choose principles at all; the veil hides only the knowledge that would let them favor themselves.
6. **Unanimity** follows because the parties are symmetrically situated: no one can identify an advantage, so all reason alike. The outcome is **fair rather than a bargain** because bargaining power has been removed — there is no strong and no weak behind the veil.
7. **Maximin** is the rule: choose the arrangement whose worst outcome is best. The parties use it because they face radical uncertainty with no probabilities, fundamental interests across a whole life, and a satisfactory guaranteed alternative. It is **not risk aversion**: the veil hides their attitudes to risk.
8. **Three reasons:** (i) no basis for assigning probabilities to their eventual position, so expected-value reasoning has nothing to work with; (ii) the stakes are fundamental — a whole life's basic liberties and prospects, not a compensable loss; (iii) the two principles offer a satisfactory guaranteed alternative, so there is no need to gamble for a higher average.
9. **First principle:** each person has an equal right to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar scheme for all. **Second principle:** social and economic inequalities must be (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged, and (b) attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity.
10. **Lexical priority** means the first principle must be fully satisfied before the second counts — a gain under the second cannot justify a loss under the first. **Within the second principle, fair equality of opportunity (b) takes priority over the difference principle (a).**
11. **Natural liberty:** careers formally open to talents; leaves class and native talent uncorrected. **Liberal equality:** adds substantive fair opportunity, neutralising class disadvantage; leaves natural talents uncorrected. **Democratic equality:** adds the difference principle; this is Rawls's own reading.
12. **Permitted:** higher pay for doctors that produces a better health system in which the worst-paid are better off than under strict equality. **Forbidden:** higher pay that increases total output while leaving the worst off no better. The difference is whether the inequality benefits the least advantaged, not whether it increases the total.
13. **The least advantaged** are those worst off by an index of primary goods — income and wealth, powers of office, and the social bases of self-respect. Identifying them is **empirical** because it requires judging real institutions against that index, not merely applying a definition.
14. **The tendency to equality** is §17's claim that under the difference principle, the expectations of the least advantaged are maximized, so the gap between them and others tends to narrow as institutions improve. It is a **tendency, not a target**: if the least advantaged are best off under some remaining inequality, the principle keeps it.
15. **The publicity condition** requires that principles be ones citizens can publicly affirm, knowing others do too. Rawls uses it against average utility by arguing that a utilitarian society would have to conceal from its worst-off members that their sacrifices are required by the total — a failure of publicity.
