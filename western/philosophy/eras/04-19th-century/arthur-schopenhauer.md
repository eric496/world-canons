# Arthur Schopenhauer

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1788–1860
- **Place:** Danzig (Gdańsk), Hamburg, Weimar, Berlin, and finally Frankfurt am Main, Germany
- **School/tradition:** Post-Kantian idealism; philosophical pessimism
- **Known for:** The doctrine that the world's inner nature is a blind, aimless Will, and that life is therefore structured by suffering — with release possible through art, compassion, and renunciation.

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md), [Fichte](johann-gottlieb-fichte.md) · **Answered by:** [Nietzsche](friedrich-nietzsche.md)

## Biography

Arthur Schopenhauer was born in Danzig in 1788 into a wealthy merchant family. His father Heinrich, a successful trader, intended him for business and even named him Arthur because the name reads the same in several European languages — practical branding for a future merchant. A long tour of Europe as a teenager, which exposed the boy to the poverty of industrial England and France, seems to have fixed his bleak view of life early. His father died in 1805, possibly by suicide, falling from a warehouse window in Hamburg.

Freed from the counting-house, Schopenhauer studied at Göttingen and Berlin, hearing Fichte (whom he came to loathe) and writing his doctoral dissertation, *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason* (1813). In Weimar, his mother Johanna — by then a successful novelist hosting a famous salon that included Goethe — introduced him to Goethe, with whom he discussed color theory. Mother and son quarrelled bitterly and never reconciled; she died without seeing him again. Through an orientalist friend in Weimar he encountered the Upanishads in Latin translation, a lifelong influence he ranked beside Plato and Kant.

His masterwork, *The World as Will and Representation*, appeared at the end of 1818 and sank almost without trace. Convinced of his genius and furious at neglect, he obtained a lectureship in Berlin and, in a legendary act of hubris, scheduled his lectures at the same hour as Hegel's. Students flocked to Hegel; Schopenhauer lectured to a nearly empty room and never taught again. He lived thereafter as a private scholar on inherited capital in Frankfurt, keeping a rigid daily routine — flute practice, long walks, a poodle always named Atman — and revising his system in supplements. Only with *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851), a two-volume collection of essays and aphorisms written in a brilliantly accessible style, did fame arrive, in his sixties. He died in Frankfurt in 1860, celebrated at last, and reportedly not much softened by it.

## Major theories and concepts

### The world as representation

Following Kant, Schopenhauer holds that the world we experience is *Vorstellung* — representation — structured by the a priori forms of space, time, and causality. Everything we encounter as an object stands in relations of ground and consequent governed by the principle of sufficient reason: nothing is without a reason why it is so and not otherwise. But where Kant declared the thing-in-itself unknowable, Schopenhauer claimed one privileged access point: our own bodies.

### The world as Will

Each person knows their body in two ways: from outside, as one object among others, and from inside, as *willing* — desire, striving, aversion. Schopenhauer generalizes this insight to all nature: the inner essence of everything, the thing-in-itself, is Will — a single, blind, aimless striving without purpose or rest. Gravity, magnetism, animal appetite, and human ambition are all objectifications of the same Will at different grades. Crucially, this Will is not personal willpower, not a cosmic person or god, and not something your ego controls; individual wills are its splintered appearances, set against one another because the Will, having nothing outside itself to feed on, feeds on itself.

### Pessimism: suffering and boredom

If existence is endless willing, its arithmetic is bleak. Desire is lack, and lack is pain; satisfaction is brief and merely negative, the momentary silencing of a want, which promptly spawns new wants — or, if no want arises, the torment of boredom. Life, in his famous image, swings like a pendulum between pain and boredom. Since willing can never be finally satisfied, suffering is not an accident of badly arranged lives but the structure of existence itself. This is a metaphysical argument, not a mood: change the metaphysics and the pessimism would fall with it.

### Release: art, compassion, and denial of the Will

Three exits are offered, ascending in completeness. First, *aesthetic contemplation*: in disinterested perception of beauty we stop wanting and become "pure subjects of knowing," temporarily freed from the Will's service. Music holds the highest place among the arts because, Schopenhauer argues, it does not copy the world's appearances but is a direct copy of the Will itself — which is why it feels so immediately profound. Second, *compassion* (Mitleid): seeing through the illusion of separateness, the compassionate person experiences another's suffering as their own; for Schopenhauer this, not Kantian duty or divine command, is the sole genuine basis of morality. Third and rarest, *ascetic denial of the Will*: the saint's renunciation of desire, which stills willing altogether. Notably, Schopenhauer argues *against* suicide: the suicide wills life, just not its sufferings, and so affirms the Will rather than denying it — death destroys only one of its appearances.

## Key works

- *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason* (1813) — His dissertation: everything explicable falls under four forms of the principle of sufficient reason. The foundation stone of the system.
- *The World as Will and Representation* (completed 1818; published December 1818, title page dated 1819; second edition 1844, with a new supplementary second volume) — The masterwork: Books 1–2 on representation and Will, Book 3 on art, Book 4 on ethics and salvation.
- *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851) — "By-works and things left out": the essays and aphorisms, including the wise, quotable pieces on reading, noise, fame, and women (the latter notoriously hostile), that finally made him famous.

## Contributions

Schopenhauer was among the first major nineteenth-century philosophers to argue that the universe is at bottom *not* rational — a direct inversion of Hegel, mounted at exactly the same time and place. He carried Kant's framework somewhere Kant never went, put desire and the body at the center of philosophy, made pessimism a rigorous metaphysical position rather than a temperament, and was the first major Western philosopher to integrate Indian thought (Upanishadic and Buddhist ideas) into a systematic European philosophy.

## Impact and legacy

Richard Wagner, entranced by Book 3, shaped *Tristan und Isolde* under his influence. The young Nietzsche took him as an "educator" before revolting against the pessimism. Tolstoy, Turgenev, and later Thomas Mann, Beckett, and Borges absorbed him; Freud acknowledged that Schopenhauer's Will anticipated the unconscious drives of psychoanalysis. His aesthetics of music and his ethics of compassion continue to be mined independently of the metaphysics.

## Criticisms and debates

The obvious objection: how can Schopenhauer *know* the thing-in-itself, when his own Kantian premises forbid such knowledge? Defenders reply that the body's double knowledge is an exception built into the system; critics judge it a contradiction. Others question the leap from "my inner nature is willing" to "all nature is one Will." His system also sits uneasily with itself: if individuation is illusion, why is one person's ascetic denial more than another appearance? Feminist critics rightly note the misogyny of essays like "On Women." Nietzsche's counter — that pessimism is itself a symptom of declining life — remains the most consequential philosophical objection.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The Will is not willpower, ambition, or a personal god; it is impersonal, aimless striving.
- The pessimism is a metaphysical argument, not personal gloom or practical advice.
- Schopenhauer opposed suicide on his own premises; he was not a philosopher of self-destruction.
- He loathed Hegel; do not merge them into a generic "German idealism."

## What to read first

1. The essays and aphorisms in *Parerga* (e.g. the Penguin *Essays and Aphorisms*) for the voice and the worldview in miniature.
2. *The World as Will and Representation*, Book 1 (representation) and the key chapters of Book 2 (the Will).
3. Book 4 for the ethics of compassion and the denial of the Will — his constructive conclusion.
