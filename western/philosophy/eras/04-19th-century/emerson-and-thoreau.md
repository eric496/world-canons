## At a glance

- **Dates:** Ralph Waldo Emerson (1803–1882) and Henry David Thoreau (1817–1862)
- **Place:** Concord, Massachusetts, United States
- **School/tradition:** American Transcendentalism
- **Known for:** Emerson, the prophet of self-reliance — *Nature* (1836) and "Self-Reliance" made intellectual independence a philosophy; Thoreau, his younger friend and sometime lodger, who tested the doctrine in a cabin at Walden Pond and, in "Civil Disobedience," gave principled law-breaking its classic defense

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md) (filtered through Coleridge and Carlyle), German idealism, and the Unitarian establishment both men were raised in · **Answered by:** [James](william-james.md), [Nietzsche](friedrich-nietzsche.md)

## Biography

Ralph Waldo Emerson was born in Boston in 1803, son of a Unitarian minister who died when Waldo was eight, leaving the family poor and genteel. He went to Harvard, taught school, and in 1829 was ordained minister of Boston's Second Church — and in 1832 resigned, unable in conscience to administer the Lord's Supper. His young wife Ellen had died of tuberculosis the year before. A voyage to Europe in 1832–33 gave him his real education — Coleridge, Wordsworth, and above all Carlyle, a lifelong correspondent. Settled in Concord and remarried, he invented the career of the American public lecturer, honing thousands of lyceum talks into essays.

Three productions made him. *Nature* (1836), published anonymously, was Transcendentalism's founding manifesto. "The American Scholar" (1837), delivered at Harvard, was called by Oliver Wendell Holmes "our intellectual Declaration of Independence." The Divinity School Address (1838), attacking historical Christianity and preaching the divinity of the present soul, scandalized Harvard, which did not invite him back for some thirty years. Around him gathered the informal Transcendental Club and the journal *The Dial* (1840–44), edited first by Margaret Fuller, then by Emerson himself. His *Essays* (1841, 1844), *Representative Men* (1850), and *The Conduct of Life* (1860) followed; so did action against slavery — addresses against the Fugitive Slave Law and a defense of John Brown in 1859. He died in Concord in 1882, America's sage.

Henry David Thoreau — born David Henry in Concord in 1817, the name reversed as a young man's gesture — was the son of a pencil-maker, went to Harvard on scholarship, and came home to teach, briefly running a school with his brother John, whose death from tetanus in 1842 marked Thoreau for life. Emerson took the intense, awkward young man into his household (1841–43) as handyman and gardener: patron, landlord, audience — eventually, a burden to them both. Thoreau worked in the family pencil business (his engineering improved American pencils), tutored on Staten Island, surveyed land, and wrote.

From 4 July 1845 to September 1847 he lived in a cabin he built himself on land Emerson owned at Walden Pond — a deliberate experiment in living economically and fronting "the essential facts of life." In July 1846 the Concord constable jailed him for refusing to pay the poll tax, his protest against slavery and the Mexican–American War; a relative paid the tax against his wishes and he was released the next morning. His first book, *A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers* (1849), failed commercially — he got back some 700 unsold copies of the thousand printed, joking that his library now held nearly nine hundred volumes, over seven hundred of them his own. *Walden* (1854), condensed from the two pond years into one, did better in his lifetime and immeasurably better after it. He lectured, traveled to Maine and Cape Cod, wrote natural history of real scientific ambition, and in 1859 astonished even his friends by defending the hanged John Brown when respectable opinion fled. Tuberculosis killed him in 1862, aged forty-four. Emerson's eulogy was loving and, in places, famously ungenerous — the ambition of the younger man, he said, had been wanting, as if "instead of engineering for all America, he was the captain of a huckleberry party." The judgment says as much about the friendship's asymmetries as about Thoreau.

## Major theories and concepts

### Nature and the transparent eye-ball

Emerson's *Nature* divides the world into Nature and the Soul, and asks what nature is *for*. Its answer: nature is the symbol of spirit. Facts of nature correspond to facts of mind — "every natural fact is a symbol of some spiritual fact" — so the world is an emblem-book to be read, and the perceiving self, purged of petty ego, becomes "a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all; the currents of the Universal Being circulate through me; I am part or parcel of God." The "transparent eye-ball" is not pantheist dissolution but a discipline of perception: see truly, and the boundary between inner and outer goes porous. This doctrine of **correspondence** underwrites everything both men wrote about nature.

### Self-reliance

"Self-Reliance" (1841) is Emerson's signature essay and America's most quoted piece of prose. Its doctrine is usually flattened into individualism; the text is stricter and stranger. "Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string." Self-reliance is fidelity to one's own moral intuition — which Emerson takes to be the universal soul speaking in the individual, not private preference. Hence the famous asperities: "Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist"; "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds" — consistency with one's *past*, not coherence itself, is the hobgoblin. Society, charity as fashion, travel as self-escape, prayer as begging: all come under the lash. The essay's wager is that genuine self-trust and genuine universality coincide — a claim Thoreau spent his life testing in practice.

### The Oversoul and the moral sentiment

In "The Over-Soul" (1841) Emerson names the unity his essays everywhere assume: "that great nature in which we rest… that Unity… within which every man's particular being is contained and made one with all other." Religion, on this account, is not mediated by churches, creeds, or even by Jesus treated historically — the offense of the Divinity School Address — but is the soul's immediate perception of moral law, the "moral sentiment" that is at once feeling and reason. His later work darkens without retracting: "Experience" (1844) sees "not directly, but mediately," and "Fate" (1860) holds freedom and necessity in unresolved tension — "if we must accept Fate, we are not less compelled to affirm liberty."

### Thoreau: deliberate living and the economy of Walden

*Walden* is an experiment report disguised as a memoir. Its first chapter, "Economy," is a mock-ledger and a manifesto: the cabin cost $28.12½; the point of the accounting is spiritual arithmetic — "the cost of a thing is the amount of… life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the long run." Thoreau's famous motive sentence governs the whole: "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived." **Deliberate living** means attention as a moral practice: morning as a discipline, reading as athletics, beans hoed as metaphysics. The pond years (two years, two months — compressed into one literary year) are a controlled reduction: strip needs to the bone and see what the self is owed and what it owns.

### Civil disobedience

The 1849 essay "Resistance to Civil Government" — published posthumously (1866) under the now-canonical title "Civil Disobedience" — begins from the maxim "that government is best which governs least" and asks what conscience owes an unjust state. Its arguments are spare and quotable: the state is a machine, and friction in the machine is sometimes a duty; a man is not bound to devote himself to eradicating a wrong, "but it is his duty, at least, to wash his hands of it"; voting for the right is "but a cheap vote," a wish feebly expressed; and the famous conclusion, "any man more right than his neighbors constitutes a majority of one already." The night in jail is the essay's credential, not its argument: a state that imprisons the just has made prison "the only house in a slave State in which a free man can abide with honor." Thoreau did not design a technique of mass protest — he described an individual's refusal — and the distance between his act and the movements that later claimed him is part of his history.

### The naturalist's turn

Thoreau's last decade of journals (some two million words) and essays like "The Succession of Forest Trees" (1860) turn outward, to seed dispersal, forest succession, and phenology — empirical work whose flowering-time records now serve climate research. Whether this is the correspondence-doctrine matured into science, or science quietly replacing it, is a live question in Thoreau scholarship.

## Key works

**Emerson**
- *Nature* (1836) — The founding manifesto: correspondence, the transparent eye-ball, nature as symbol of spirit.
- *Essays: First Series* (1841) — Contains "Self-Reliance," "The Over-Soul," "Compensation," "History."
- *Essays: Second Series* (1844) — "Experience," "The Poet," "Character" — the darker, autumnal Emerson.
- *Representative Men* (1850) and *The Conduct of Life* (1860) — The mature lectures, including "Fate" and "Power."

**Thoreau**
- *A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers* (1849) — The first book; a river elegy for his brother John.
- "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849; as "Civil Disobedience," 1866) — The essay of principled refusal.
- *Walden* (1854) — The pond experiment; "Economy" and "Conclusion" are its philosophical spine.
- "A Plea for Captain John Brown" (1859) — The radical late voice, defending violence against slavery.

## Contributions

Emerson effectively created the role of the American public intellectual and gave American thought its founding themes: self-culture over inherited authority, experience over system, the present moment as revelation. Thoreau contributed the two most durable experiments in that tradition — a worked example of examined living, and a worked theory of conscientious refusal — plus a body of nature writing that helped invent the genre. Between them, Concord became the unlikely capital of a philosophy built from lectures, journals, and one small pond.

## Impact and legacy

Emerson's influence runs through American pragmatism (William James's debt was explicit), through Nietzsche — who read him lifelong, carried his essays on journeys, and praised him above almost all moderns — and through the whole American literature of self-making. Thoreau's afterlife is the more dramatic. Gandhi, in South Africa, read "Civil Disobedience," summarized it in *Indian Opinion* (1907), and borrowed Thoreau's own phrase — "civil disobedience" — to explain his struggle to English readers; finding it too narrow, he later replaced it with his own coinage, "civil resistance." Martin Luther King Jr. recalled in *Stride Toward Freedom* reading the essay at Morehouse College and being convinced "that noncooperation with evil is as much a moral obligation as is cooperation with good." Tolstoy recommended Thoreau, British labor readers reprinted him, and *Walden* became a founding text of modern environmentalism. The line from the Concord tax-refuser to the Salt March and the Montgomery boycott is real, but it runs through creative reinvention: Gandhi and King built mass disciplines out of one man's single night in jail.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Philosophy or literature?** The academic verdict long treated Emerson as a sage to be quoted rather than an argument to be answered, and Thoreau as a nature writer. Twentieth-century scholarship (Stanley Cavell above all) reversed this, reading *Walden* as a philosophical scripture written sentence by sentence and Emerson as a thinker of skepticism and moral perfectionism. The debate is now mostly settled in their favor; its terms are still argued.
- **Self-reliance and society.** Critics charge Emersonian individualism with licensing exactly the commercial selfishness he despised, and note that his politics long ran to moral suasion rather than organization. Defenders answer with the abolitionist record and with the essay's own logic: the self trusted is the universal in the person. How far the defense succeeds is honestly disputed.
- **The Walden myth.** Thoreau dined in the village, had visitors in the cabin, and (the favorite gibe) sent his laundry home. Detractors call the experiment a stunt; defenders note *Walden* hides none of this — the town is a mile away in the text, the visitors crowd its chapters — and that the claim was always about economy and attention, not wilderness survival. The fraud charge survives mainly where the book goes unread.
- **The friendship.** Emerson's condescension in the eulogy, and Thoreau's journal jabs at his mentor, fuel an ongoing reassessment: patronage enabled the experiment and also deformed it, and scholars now read influence in both directions — Emerson's later nature writing owes debts to his protégé.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **Self-reliance is not selfishness, and not "do your own research."** It is obedience to a moral intuition Emerson believed universal; the nonconformity is in service of a law, not of appetite.
- **Thoreau was not a hermit in the deep woods.** The cabin stood on his friend's land about a mile from the village he visited constantly. The experiment was in living, not in hiding.
- **"Civil Disobedience" is not a how-to manual for protest movements, and Thoreau did not coin its title.** The essay the reader knows by that name appeared as "Resistance to Civil Government"; the famous title is posthumous, and the mass movements came later, by reinvention.
- **Emerson and Thoreau are not interchangeable.** Mentor and protégé shared a town and a journal; they differed in temperament, method, and — as the eulogy shows — in what each thought a life was for.

## What to read first

1. "Self-Reliance" (Emerson) — twenty pages that contain the whole Concord program, hobgoblins included.
2. *Walden*, "Economy" and "Conclusion" (Thoreau) — the ledger and the sermon; then "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For."
3. "Civil Disobedience" (Thoreau) — read it after "Economy," and notice how short the famous night in jail actually is.
4. *Nature* (1836) and "Experience" (Emerson) — the young manifesto and its chastened sequel, read as a pair.
