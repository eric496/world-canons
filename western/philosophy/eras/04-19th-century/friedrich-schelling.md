# Friedrich Schelling

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1775–1854
- **Place:** Born in Leonberg, Württemberg; taught at Jena, Würzburg, Munich, and Berlin, Germany
- **School/tradition:** German Idealism; Romantic philosophy of nature
- **Known for:** The philosophy of nature (*Naturphilosophie*), the identity philosophy, the 1809 essay on human freedom, and the late "positive philosophy" — a career that begins beside Fichte and Hegel and ends by challenging Hegel's whole method

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Fichte](johann-gottlieb-fichte.md), [Spinoza](../03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md) · **Answered by:** [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md), [Kierkegaard](soren-kierkegaard.md)

## Biography

Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling was born in 1775 in Leonberg, the son of a Württemberg pastor and orientalist. Admitted at fifteen to the famous Tübinger Stift, the Protestant seminary at Tübingen, he shared a room with two older students who became his lifelong measures and rivals: Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel and the poet Friedrich Hölderlin. The three young men thrilled to the French Revolution — tradition has them planting a liberty tree together, an anecdote historians repeat with a caution — and read Kant and the first rumblings of Fichte together.

Schelling published fast. Works on myth (1793) and on the "I" as principle (1795) made him Fichte's most promising heir; the *Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature* (1797) and *On the World Soul* (1798) struck out in his own direction and won Goethe's admiration. In 1798, aged twenty-three, he was called to a chair at Jena, then the white-hot center of German philosophy, where Fichte still taught and the Romantic circle of the Schlegels, Novalis, and Tieck gathered. There followed in rapid succession the *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800), dialogues and lectures elaborating his "identity philosophy," and marriage in 1803 to Caroline Böhmer-Schlegel, the brilliant center of the Jena Romantics, after her divorce from August Wilhelm Schlegel.

The Jena world dissolved: Fichte had already been dismissed, Hegel arrived and rose, and Schelling moved to Würzburg (1803) and then Munich (1806), where he worked in the Bavarian academies rather than a university chair. Caroline's sudden death in 1809 shattered him. The works of these years are his deepest: the *Philosophical Inquiries into the Nature of Human Freedom* (1809) and the unfinished *Ages of the World*, drafted repeatedly between 1811 and 1815 and never published in his lifetime — a book he could not finish because its subject, God's own coming-to-be in time, kept outrunning his system. He lectured at Erlangen in the 1820s without a formal chair, took a Munich professorship in 1827, and published little; meanwhile Hegel's fame eclipsed his, and Hegel's *Phenomenology* preface (1807) had already mocked an absolute conceived as an undifferentiated night — a gibe Schelling took, not unreasonably, as aimed at him.

In 1841 came the famous summons: the new Prussian king, Friedrich Wilhelm IV, called the sixty-six-year-old Schelling to Berlin, hoping — as the phrase went — that he would stamp out "the dragon-seed of Hegelian pantheism." The opening lectures were among the century's great intellectual events. In the audience sat the young Søren Kierkegaard (at first delighted — Schelling spoke of "actuality" — then contemptuously bored, as his letters and journals record), Friedrich Engels (who rushed out pamphlets defending Hegel), Mikhail Bakunin, and the historian Jacob Burckhardt. Schelling lectured on his "positive philosophy" in Berlin for years, sued — unsuccessfully — to stop Paulus's pirated edition of his lectures (1843), and gradually withdrew. He died in 1854 at Bad Ragaz in Switzerland — having outlived Hegel by twenty-three years and published almost nothing for four decades, while the posthumous edition of his works, edited by his son, kept the late philosophy in circulation for readers like Kierkegaard's generation and beyond.

## Major theories and concepts

### Naturphilosophie: nature as visible spirit

Schelling's first originality was to reverse Fichte's direction. Where Fichte derived nature from the self-positing I, Schelling insisted nature is itself productive spirit — "nature shall be Mind made visible, Mind the invisible nature," in the *Ideas*' famous formula. Nature is not a machine of dead matter but a self-organizing productivity, working through polar forces (attraction and repulsion, positive and negative electricity, chemical opposites) up a ladder of stages — matter, light, organism — towards consciousness, which is nature finally awake to itself. The philosophy of nature was built in close argument with the science of the day (galvanism, chemistry, biology), and its speculative method is the standing question about it: daring unification that inspired some working scientists — Hans Christian Ørsted acknowledged its influence on his search for electromagnetism — or metaphor dressed as physics? Both verdicts have had distinguished holders, then and since.

### The System of 1800 and intellectual intuition

The *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800) runs the film in the other direction: from consciousness down through its own unconscious productivity to nature. Its hinge is **intellectual intuition** — the self's direct, productive awareness of its own activity, which Schelling, going beyond Kant's prohibition and radicalizing Fichte, made the organ of philosophy. Its conclusion is famous: the artwork is philosophy's highest document, because in aesthetic production the conscious and unconscious activities are visibly one. Art, not science, completes the system — a thesis that tells you how close Jena philosophy and Jena Romanticism were.

### Identity philosophy: the absolute as indifference

In the works of 1801–04 (*Presentation of My System*, *Bruno*, *Philosophy and Religion*), subject and object, nature and spirit, are two series of one and the same Absolute, whose essence is **absolute identity** — the "point of indifference" where opposites are neither distinguished nor yet separated. Finite things are quantitative imbalances within it. This is the philosophy Hegel's "night in which all cows are black" targeted; whether the gibe fairly hits Schelling — who had elaborate accounts of differentiation — or a caricature of his followers, remains a genuine scholarly dispute, and the friendship never recovered.

### The freedom essay (1809): ground, existence, and real evil

The *Philosophical Inquiries into the Nature of Human Freedom* is widely judged his masterpiece. Schelling distinguishes in God between **existence** and the **ground of existence** — a dark, self-willing nature *in* God that God eternally overcomes and orders. Evil is therefore no mere absence or illusion (the standard theodicy he rejects): it is a real, positive possibility, rooted in the ground, actualized when the creature's particular will usurps the center and tries to be the whole. Human freedom is accordingly defined not as mere choice between options but as "a capacity for good and evil." Pantheism, rightly understood, does not destroy this freedom, because beings live in God without being God. The essay's psychology of evil — selfhood inverting its proper order — reaches forward to later philosophies of evil and of the unconscious.

### The Ages of the World: time in God

The unfinished *Weltalter* drafts (1811–15) attempt a narrative philosophy of God's life: an eternal oscillation between a contracting, self-centered will and an expanding, self-giving will, whose resolution is creation and time itself. The past, Schelling held, is not dead but borne within the present — "the past is known, the present recognised, the future divined." He rewrote the book for years and abandoned it; its fragments became some of the most studied remains of German idealism.

### Positive philosophy: existence before essence

The late Schelling divided philosophy in two. **Negative philosophy** reasons about essences — what things must be if they exist — and reaches its completion, and its limit, in Hegel's Logic: a system of concepts that can never explain why anything exists at all. **Positive philosophy** begins instead from sheer existence, the **"unprethinkable being"** (*das unvordenkliche Sein*) that precedes all thought and can only be encountered, not deduced; from there it proceeds through mythology and revelation as the historical record of consciousness's encounter with God. Kierkegaard came to Berlin for exactly this — a philosophy of actuality — and left disappointed with the lectures while absorbing the problem; the negative/positive distinction, and the insistence that existence outruns concept, is one seedbed of later existential thought.

## Key works

- *Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature* (1797) — The *Naturphilosophie* manifesto: nature as self-organizing productivity.
- *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800) — Consciousness reconstructed from its unconscious production; art as the system's crown.
- *Bruno* (1802) — Dialogue form of the identity philosophy.
- *Philosophy and Religion* (1804) — The identity system's account of the fall of finite things from the Absolute.
- *Philosophical Inquiries into the Nature of Human Freedom* (1809) — The freedom essay: ground and existence, evil as real, freedom as capacity for good and evil.
- *The Ages of the World* (drafts 1811–15; posthumous) — The great unfinished theogony of time.
- Lectures on the *Philosophy of Mythology* and *Philosophy of Revelation* (1840s; posthumous) — The positive philosophy in full.

## Contributions

Schelling restored nature to German philosophy: after Kant's limits and Fichte's subjectivism, he made the natural world philosophically productive in its own right, and his staged, polar, self-organizing nature influenced Romantic science and every later philosophy that refuses to treat mind as an alien intruder in matter. His freedom essay gave modern thought one of its deepest accounts of evil. His late critique of Hegel — that a logic of concepts cannot manufacture existence — set problems that Kierkegaard, and through many channels existentialism and theology, inherited.

## Impact and legacy

Through Jena Romanticism and Coleridge (who borrowed from him liberally, to the point of plagiarism charges), Schelling's ideas passed into English and American letters; the Transcendentalists drank from this stream. Within Germany his reputation lived a double life: eclipsed as Hegel's forerunner in the standard textbooks, yet continuously rediscovered — by Kierkegaard's generation as Hegel's critic, by twentieth-century scholars (from Walter Schulz to Andrew Bowie and others) as a philosopher of nature, freedom, and finitude whose questions outlasted the Hegelian system that buried him. The *Ages of the World* and the late lectures are now read as major works in their own right, not as appendices to a career that "failed."

## Criticisms and debates

- **How many Schellings?** The standard joke counts a new system every decade. The scholarly debate is real: radical discontinuity (each phase refutes the last) versus a single persistent project — the relation of freedom, nature, and the Absolute — worked through successive failures. Current scholarship leans towards continuity of problem, but the periodization is still argued.
- **Naturphilosophie: fertile or obscurantist?** Its speculative physics drew contempt from the later nineteenth century's scientists, yet it demonstrably motivated real research programs (Ørsted most famously) and its organism-model fed biology. How much credit a heuristic metaphor deserves remains contested.
- **The Hegel feud.** Beyond the "night" gibe lies a substantive charge and counter-charge: Hegel's Absolute is a result emptied of life, says Schelling; Schelling's identity is an empty abstraction that cannot generate difference, says Hegel. Adjudicating it is a small industry, and partisan verdicts should be distrusted.
- **Berlin 1841: fiasco or seedbed?** Contemporary verdicts were mixed and often hostile (Engels's pamphlets were effective polemic), and Kierkegaard's boredom is famous. But the lectures' distinction between negative and positive philosophy had a long subterranean influence — a "failure" whose failure is itself debated.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **He was not merely a bridge from Fichte to Hegel.** That is the old textbook picture. Schelling's career spans sixty years, outlasts Hegel's by two decades, and ends in a philosophy — the positive philosophy — aimed *against* the Hegelian completion.
- **Naturphilosophie was not anti-science.** It engaged the physics, chemistry, and biology of its day closely; its speculative excesses are a real criticism, but ignorance of science is not among its sins.
- **"All cows are black" is Hegel's polemic, not Schelling's doctrine.** Identity as the "point of indifference" is not the claim that everything is simply the same; whether the defense succeeds is debatable, the caricature is not the position.
- **Kierkegaard did not simply learn existentialism in Berlin and leave grateful.** He arrived enthusiastic, soured within months, and mocked the lectures — while the problem Schelling set him (existence versus system) stayed with him for life. Both halves matter.

## What to read first

1. The *Freedom Essay* (1809) — his masterpiece, in the Love & Schmidt translation (*Philosophical Investigations into the Essence of Human Freedom*, SUNY) or the older Gutmann version; read it whole, slowly.
2. The Introduction to the *Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature* — the *Naturphilosophie* program in his own slogans and arguments.
3. The conclusion of the *System of Transcendental Idealism* (Part VI) — the philosophy of art that crowns the early system.
4. *On the History of Modern Philosophy* (the Munich lectures) — his retrospective on Descartes to Hegel, the clearest map of where he thought he stood.
