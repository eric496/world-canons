# Henry Sidgwick

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1838–1900
- **Place:** Skipton, Yorkshire; Trinity College, Cambridge, England
- **School/tradition:** Utilitarianism; Cambridge moral philosophy
- **Known for:** *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), the summit of classical utilitarianism — famous for ending not in triumph but in the unresolved "dualism of practical reason," and for quietly shaping Moore, Parfit, and Singer.

**Responds to:** [Bentham](jeremy-bentham.md), [J. S. Mill](john-stuart-mill.md) · **Answered by:** [G. E. Moore](../05-20th-century/g-e-moore.md), [Parfit](../05-20th-century/derek-parfit.md), [Singer](../05-20th-century/peter-singer.md)

## Biography

Henry Sidgwick was born in Skipton, Yorkshire, in 1838, the son of a clergyman and headmaster. At Rugby and then Trinity College, Cambridge, he was the star of his generation: first-class honors in classics and mathematics, then a fellowship at Trinity in 1859, at twenty-one. The fellowship came with a condition typical of the old universities — subscription to the Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England. Sidgwick's religious doubts deepened through the 1860s as he studied theology, Hebrew, and Arabic in a genuine effort to keep his faith, and in 1869 he resigned his fellowship rather than subscribe dishonestly. It is the defining biographical fact: a man who gave up his career rather than affirm what he could not believe, and who then spent the rest of his life applying the same scrupulosity to ethics.

Cambridge, to its credit, kept him: he continued as a lecturer, the university statutes changed, and in 1883 he became Knightsbridge Professor of Moral Philosophy, a chair he held until his death. With his wife Eleanor Balfour — sister of the future prime minister Arthur Balfour, and later principal of Newnham College — he was central to the founding of Newnham (1871/75), one of the first Cambridge colleges for women, at a time when this was controversial philanthropy. His other famous extra-philosophical venture was the Society for Psychical Research (1882), of which he was first president: he investigated telepathy and séances with experimental rigor and characteristic honesty, hoping — as in religion — that evidence might settle what longing could not. The results were negative, and he said so.

His masterpiece, *The Methods of Ethics*, appeared in 1874 and went through edition after edition of revision (the seventh, posthumous, appeared in 1907). Sidgwick died in 1900, widely regarded as the most careful philosophical mind in England — and privately convinced that his central problem had defeated him.

## Major theories and concepts

### The methods of ethics

Sidgwick's method is to take the moral reasoning people actually use and make it systematic. A **method of ethics** is any rational procedure for deciding what one ought to do. He finds three real candidates in reflective common sense: **egoism** (maximize one's own happiness), **intuitionism** (follow self-evident moral rules and principles — the method of most "common-sense morality"), and **universal hedonism**, i.e. utilitarianism (maximize happiness overall). The book examines each at enormous length, testing common-sense morality for the coherence it claims. The famous result of this examination is double-edged: common-sense rules turn out, on inspection, to be rough approximations to utilitarian reasoning — yet the examination also produces principles (about the equal moral weight of persons and times) that seem to support utilitarianism directly. Derek Parfit called *The Methods* "the best book on ethics ever written," and its strategy — clarify, systematize, and only then judge — became the template for analytic moral philosophy.

### The axioms and the case for utilitarianism

Sidgwick's positive argument rests on a small set of abstract principles he thought genuinely self-evident once clearly stated. The **axiom of justice or equity**: whatever action any of us judges right for himself, he implicitly judges right for all similar persons in similar circumstances. The **axiom of rational benevolence**: from "the point of view of the universe" (his famous phrase), the good of one individual is of no more importance than the good of any other, so a rational agent ought to aim at the greatest good overall. And the axiom of **prudence**: a smaller present good should not be preferred to a greater future good — mere difference in *time* is no more a rational ground for caring less than mere difference in *person*. If you accept the parallel between times within a life and persons within a society, utilitarianism follows with unusual tightness. This is why Sidgwick matters so much to modern consequentialists: he did not merely assert the greatest-happiness principle, he derived it from premises about rational consistency that are hard to reject one by one.

### The dualism of practical reason

And yet the book's engine is a failure. The egoistic axiom — that it is rational to pursue one's own greatest happiness — struck Sidgwick as equally self-evident, and he could find no rational argument that reconciles egoism with universal benevolence when the two genuinely conflict. He called this the **dualism of practical reason**, and called it, in effect, a fundamental contradiction in our practical thought: reason issues two supreme commands that can collide. The only reconciliation he could imagine was theological — a moral order of the universe, guaranteed by God, in which duty and interest ultimately coincide — and this was exactly what his honest agnosticism forbade him to assert. The *Methods* therefore ends in a kind of noble collapse, which Sidgwick reported with characteristic candor. Every serious later utilitarian, from Moore to Parfit (for whom dissolving the dualism was a lifelong project), has had to confront the problem Sidgwick refused to paper over.

### Esoteric morality

In examining utilitarianism Sidgwick noticed a consequence that still generates debate. If the right act is the one that maximizes happiness, then it may sometimes maximize happiness for people *not* to believe utilitarianism: a society whose members follow sturdy common-sense rules, and are never told the theory behind them, might do better than a society of amateur calculators. Utilitarianism may thus recommend its own concealment — an **esoteric morality** for the enlightened few alongside a public morality for everyone else. Sidgwick accepted this implication quietly and without relish. Bernard Williams later made it a centerpiece of his attack on utilitarianism as "Government House utilitarianism" — a doctrine fit for colonial administrators ruling natives who must not be let in on the theory. Defenders reply that Sidgwick is honestly following his premises and that transparency itself has consequences a consequentialist must count; the debate remains a standard test case for whether a moral theory may hide itself.

### Intuitions, graded

Although grouped with the utilitarians, Sidgwick is also the great analyst of **intuitionism**: his conditions for a moral principle to count as genuinely self-evident — clarity, careful reflection, consistency with other such principles, and broad consensus among competent judges — remain the standard criteria in the methodology of ethics, used today even by anti-utilitarians. His grading of certainty explains his verdict on common sense: its deliverances are mostly vague, mutually conflicting approximations, not the precise intuitions philosophy needs.

## Key works

- *The Methods of Ethics* (1874; 7th edition 1907) — the masterpiece: egoism, intuitionism, and utilitarianism examined, and the dualism exposed.
- *Outlines of the History of Ethics* (1886) — a compact, lucid history from the Greeks to the nineteenth century, long used as a textbook.
- *The Elements of Politics* (1891) — applied utilitarian political theory; less read, but the systematic complement to the *Methods*.
- *Practical Ethics* (1898) — essays on applied questions, showing the method at work on real cases.

## Contributions

Sidgwick completed classical utilitarianism. Bentham had proclaimed it and Mill had humanised it; Sidgwick gave it rigorous foundations, precise axioms, and an honest map of its unsolved problem. In doing so he effectively created the modern academic study of normative ethics: the comparative examination of rival methods, the demand that intuitions pass tests of clarity and consistency, and the separation of meta-questions (what "ought" and "good" mean) from normative ones. His work on the history of ethics and his role in building women's education at Cambridge are substantial secondary legacies.

## Impact and legacy

The line from Sidgwick runs straight through twentieth-century ethics. G. E. Moore studied the *Methods* closely, and *Principia Ethica* (1903) begins where Sidgwick's questions about the meaning of "good" leave off. Through Moore and Cambridge he stands behind Keynes and Bloomsbury as well as analytic philosophy. Derek Parfit acknowledged Sidgwick as his deepest influence; *Reasons and Persons* and *On What Matters* can be read as attempts to finish Sidgwick's project, dualism included. Peter Singer and the modern effective-altruism movement inherit his point of view of the universe and his temporal-impartiality arguments (Sidgwick's axioms are quoted at the foundations of the literature on impartial beneficence). The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/)'s assessment reflects the consensus: the *Methods* is the culmination of the classical utilitarian tradition and set the agenda for the century of ethics that followed.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The dualism:** failure or discovery? Some read the unresolved dualism as the book's flaw; others (Parfit among them) treat it as Sidgwick's greatest finding — a genuine problem every theory must face. Whether his axioms really are self-evident, or smuggle in utilitarian conclusions, is the technical heart of the debate.
- **Esoteric morality:** Williams's "Government House" charge versus the reply that Sidgwick merely notes a possibility every consequentialist must weigh. The debate now anchors wider arguments about transparency in public ethics.
- **Hedonism:** Sidgwick defines the good as desirable consciousness — pleasure, broadly understood. Moore's critique of defining "good" at all, and later accounts of well-being (desire-satisfaction, objective lists), are all responses to the target he built.
- **Dryness as a defect:** his exhaustive even-handedness can read as indecision; whether the *Methods* defends utilitarianism or quietly subverts it has been debated since 1874.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Sidgwick was just Mill with footnotes."** Mill's utilitarianism is a reformer's creed; Sidgwick's is a proof attempt with axioms, and it ends somewhere Mill never went — in an admitted contradiction of practical reason.
- **"The dualism means Sidgwick refuted utilitarianism."** He thought utilitarianism was the best-supported method; the dualism is a problem about egoism's rationality, not a retraction.
- **"Esoteric morality was his recommendation for how to run society."** It is a theorem he accepted reluctantly inside a discussion of utilitarianism's possible self-effacingness — presented as a cost to be weighed, not a program.

## What to read first

Read *The Methods of Ethics* in the standard guided excerpts: Book I, chapter 1 (what a method is), Book III, chapter 13 (the axioms), and the concluding chapter of Book IV (the dualism, in Sidgwick's own words). Then the *Outlines of the History of Ethics* gives you his map of the whole field in a weekend. Save the full *Methods* for when you want to watch a great mind refuse to cheat.
