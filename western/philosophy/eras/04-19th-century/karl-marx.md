# Karl Marx

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1818–1883
- **Place:** Trier and Bonn/Cologne, Germany; exile in Paris, Brussels, and London
- **School/tradition:** Historical materialism; co-founder (with Engels) of the theory later called Marxism
- **Known for:** The analysis of capitalism as a historically specific system built on the exploitation of wage labor, and of history as shaped by modes of production and class struggle.

**Responds to:** [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md), [Feuerbach & Stirner](sidebars-feuerbach-and-stirner.md) · **Answered by:** [Frankfurt School](../05-20th-century/frankfurt-school.md), [Popper](../05-20th-century/karl-popper.md)

## Biography

Karl Marx was born in Trier in 1818, the son of a lawyer who had converted from Judaism to Protestantism largely for professional survival in Prussian Germany. He studied law and then philosophy at Bonn and Berlin, falling in with the Young Hegelians — restless intellectuals radicalizing Hegel's inheritance — and completing a doctoral dissertation on Democritus and Epicurus in 1841.

Barred from an academic career by the political climate, Marx turned to journalism, editing the liberal *Rheinische Zeitung* until Prussian censorship closed it in 1843. That year he married Jenny von Westphalen, from a higher social station, in a marriage that endured extreme poverty with notable loyalty. In Paris he met Friedrich Engels, the son of a textile manufacturer who had written a devastating report on English factory conditions. Their partnership lasted forty years: Engels supplied financial support, business-world knowledge, and a lifetime of intellectual collaboration; Marx supplied the theory.

The pattern of Marx's life was exile driven by print. Expelled from Paris, he wrote in Brussels (where the *Communist Manifesto* was commissioned by the small Communist League and published in 1848, weeks before revolutions swept Europe), was expelled again, briefly edited a paper in Cologne, and in 1849 settled permanently in London. There, in the British Museum Reading Room, he spent decades on political economy while the family endured real destitution — several children died young, and the household survived substantially on Engels's subsidies and small inheritances. Marx was also a working political organizer, a leading figure in the International Working Men's Association (the First International, founded 1864), though its fractious collapse in the 1870s embittered him.

He published the first volume of *Capital* in 1867 and never finished the rest. He died in London in 1883, stateless, and was buried in Highgate Cemetery; Engels delivered the graveside speech. Engels then spent his remaining years editing Marx's chaotic drafts into volumes 2 and 3 of *Capital* (1885, 1894) — an editorial achievement that also means parts of the "Marxist" canon are Engels's construction, a fact scholarship has had to reckon with ever since.

## Major theories and concepts

### Historical materialism

Marx's theory of history, stated most crisply in the 1859 Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, holds that the way a society organizes production — its *forces of production* (tools, skills, technology) together with its *relations of production* (who owns the means of production, who must work for whom) — fundamentally shapes its law, politics, religion, and dominant ideas. The economic "base" conditions the legal-political-intellectual "superstructure." Epochs change when developing forces of production come into conflict with existing relations: the relations become fetters, and a period of social revolution begins. Within this frame, "the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles," as the *Manifesto* opens.

This is not the claim that every poem or law is mechanically caused by the economy — Engels himself later qualified such crude readings — but its exact strength is disputed: whether "determines" means *causes*, *limits and conditions*, or *explains in the last instance* is a central scholarly argument (see Criticisms).

### Alienation

In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* (unpublished until 1932), the young Marx analyzed wage labor as *alienation* (Entfremdung) in four dimensions. The worker is alienated from the *product*, which belongs to another and confronts him as an alien power; from the *activity* of labor itself, experienced as forced and external rather than self-expression; from his *species-being* — the distinctively human capacity for free, conscious, creative production — which labor under capitalism degrades into a mere means of survival; and from *other people*, who meet as competitors and owners rather than collaborators. A worker on an assembly line who never sees, owns, or chooses anything about what she makes illustrates all four at once. Alienation is not "feeling unhappy at work"; it is an objective structure that would persist even in a cheerful employee.

### Labor-power, surplus value, and exploitation

Marx's mature economics turns on a distinction: what the worker sells is not labor but *labor-power*, the capacity to work. Like any commodity, its value is set by what it costs to reproduce it — roughly, subsistence. But labor-power has a unique property: it can produce more value than it itself costs. If a worker's labor-power is worth six hours of production per day but she works ten, the extra four hours create *surplus value*, appropriated by the capitalist as profit. Exploitation, for Marx, is therefore structural: it requires no fraud, no unusually cruel employer, and no violation of the rules of fair exchange. It follows from the system working exactly as designed — which is what makes his critique of capitalism a theory rather than a complaint about greed.

### Commodity fetishism

The first chapter of *Capital* argues that under capitalism, social relations between people take on "the fantastic form of a relation between things." The value of a coat or a phone appears to be a natural property of the object, like its weight, when it actually embodies relations among producers, workers, and markets that have become invisible. Unmasking this misappearance — showing the human labor and social organization hidden inside prices — is one of Marx's most portable ideas, later enormously influential in cultural theory.

### Praxis and the Theses on Feuerbach

In the *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845, published by Engels in 1888), Marx breaks with both idealism and contemplative materialism: truth is a practical question, and human beings make themselves by transforming their world. The eleventh thesis became the movement's motto: "The philosophers have only *interpreted* the world, in various ways; the point is to *change* it."

## Signature arguments

**The alienation argument**

1. Labor, for Marx, is the distinctively human activity: in free, conscious production, human beings realize themselves and express their *species-being* — their capacity to create deliberately, in common with others.
2. Under private property and wage labor, the worker is separated from the product of labor, which belongs to another and confronts him as an alien power.
3. He is also separated from the activity itself, since labor is forced and external — a means of survival, not self-expression — and thereby from his species-being, which is degraded into mere subsistence.
4. Finally, he is separated from other people, who meet him as owners, managers, and competitors rather than as fellow producers.
Therefore, wage labor under capitalism is alienated labor: the worker's own activity confronts him as an alien power.

**Objection.** The argument seems to depend on an essentialist picture of human nature — a fixed species-being against which actual labor is measured. On some readings, notably Louis Althusser's thesis of an “epistemological break” around 1845, the later Marx abandoned that humanist essence, and without it “alienation” loses its standard.

**Reply.** Defenders argue that the critique survives in the later work in altered form, above all as the analysis of commodity fetishism and of workers dominated by the products of their own labor in *Capital*. On this continuity reading, the vocabulary changes more than the underlying charge. The break reading itself is contested, and scholars present the young-versus-mature Marx question as a genuine debate, not a settled division.

**The exploitation argument**

1. In *Capital*, Vol. 1, the value of a commodity is determined by the socially necessary labor time embodied in it.
2. Labor-power — the worker's capacity to work, which is what he actually sells — is itself a commodity, whose value is the cost of reproducing the worker: the labor time needed for his subsistence and training.
3. Labor-power has the peculiar property that its use creates more value than it costs: the worker can work longer in a day than the time required to produce the equivalent of his wage.
4. The capitalist, who buys labor-power at its value and owns the product, appropriates the difference.
Therefore, that difference — surplus value — is appropriated by the capitalist, and profit rests on unpaid labor time.

**Objection.** The labor theory of value faces two standard challenges. Marginalist economics explains prices by supply, demand, and marginal utility rather than embodied labor, and the transformation problem notes that, in Marx's own later volumes, prices systematically diverge from values.

**Reply.** Defenders distinguish value from price and read *Capital*'s value theory as an account of the social form of labor under capitalism, not a simple price formula. Others take a different route: analytical Marxists such as John Roemer restate exploitation through unequal property relations, without relying on the labor theory of value. Whether exploitation needs Marx's value theory, or survives its abandonment, remains part of the debate.

## Key works

- *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* (written 1844; published 1932) — The alienation analysis; the key text for the "young Marx."
- *The German Ideology* (with Engels, 1845–46; unpublished in their lifetimes) — The first statement of the materialist conception of history, wrapped around hundreds of pages of polemic against rivals like Stirner.
- *Manifesto of the Communist Party* (with Engels, 1848) — The famous pamphlet: brilliant, rhetorical, and programmatic; the [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/) warns it is "not the best guide to his thought" taken alone.
- *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) — Marx as concrete political analyst: Louis-Napoléon's coup explained through class fractions and their illusions, and the source of the line that history repeats itself, "the first time as tragedy, the second time as farce." Routinely counted among his finest works.
- *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) — Contains the Preface stating historical materialism in canonical form.
- *Capital, Vol. 1* (1867) — The masterwork: commodity, value, surplus value, accumulation. Vols. 2–3 were edited by Engels from drafts (1885, 1894).
- *Grundrisse* (written 1857–58; first published 1939–41) — The vast notebooks behind *Capital*: the method of political economy, the "Fragment on Machines," and pre-capitalist economic forms; central evidence in debates over the mature Marx.

## Contributions

Marx added several things that did not previously exist as unified ideas: a systematic theory of history driven by production and class; a concept of ideology — dominant ideas as serving material interests while presenting themselves as universal; the analysis of exploitation compatible with free and equal exchange; and the method of *immanent critique*, judging capitalism by its own standards of freedom and equality and showing how its normal operation betrays them. He turned Hegel's dialectic from a story about Spirit into a story about labor, ownership, and power. The *Eighteenth Brumaire* (1852) remains the standard exhibit of the method in action — historical materialism applied to a single unfolding political crisis.

## Impact and legacy

No nineteenth-century thinker has had a larger political footprint: socialist and communist movements, labor parties, and twentieth-century states claimed his authority, and much modern sociology (class analysis, conflict theory), economic history, and cultural studies works downstream of him or in argument with him. Within philosophy, Western Marxism (Lukács, Gramsci, the Frankfurt School), analytical Marxism, feminist and postcolonial theory all rework his tools. His legacy is inseparable from the contested history of regimes that ruled in his name — a reception history that is real, and that goes well beyond anything the texts straightforwardly license or forbid.

## Criticisms and debates

**The young vs. mature Marx question.** Are the humanistic manuscripts of 1844 (alienation, species-being) continuous with *Capital*, or did Marx abandon them? Louis Althusser argued for an "epistemological break" around 1845: the mature Marx is a scientist, the young one an ideologue. Others (the continuity readers) find alienation alive inside commodity fetishism. This is a genuine scholarly debate about what "Marx's view" even is, and it should be presented as one.

Other standing objections: the labor theory of value faces the "transformation problem" (deriving prices from values) and marginalist critiques from mainstream economics; Popper charged historical materialism with unfalsifiability — its predictions (immiseration, revolution in the most advanced countries) did not arrive as expected; liberals object that the theory under-rates ideas, nations, and individuals, and that its political descendants produced tyranny; defenders reply that the predictive core is about capitalism's tendencies and crises, and that Marx's own politics (he supported democratic struggles and free speech in his lifetime) cannot be simply identified with later regimes.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Historical materialism is not crude economic determinism; Engels explicitly qualified mechanical readings.
- Marx is not the *Manifesto* alone, and his texts are not a simple endorsement of every twentieth-century regime that invoked him.
- Surplus value is not the slogan "profit is theft"; the theory says exploitation occurs through legitimate exchange.
- Alienation is a structural condition, not a synonym for job dissatisfaction.

## What to read first

1. The *Communist Manifesto* together with the 1859 Preface — the program and the theory, in one sitting.
2. The alienation chapter of the 1844 *Manuscripts* for the human core of the early work.
3. *Capital* Vol. 1, chapter 1 (the commodity and fetishism), with a guide, before attempting more.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the alienation argument: name its four dimensions and show how each follows from private property and wage labor itself, not merely from a cruel employer or a boring job.
2. Reconstruct commodity fetishism from *Capital*, chapter 1: why does value appear as a natural property of things, like weight, and what social relations among producers does that appearance conceal?
3. Give one serious objection to each account: for alienation, that it depends on an essentialist species-being the later Marx may have abandoned; for fetishism, that prices need no hidden labor-essence to explain them. State Marx's best reply in each case.
4. State precisely what Marx takes from Hegel's master–slave dialectic and what he inverts: how does self-formation through labor become, in Marx, production under definite relations of ownership and class?
5. Apply both concepts to a gig driver managed entirely by an app: where is the alienation, where the fetishism, and what would have to change for the work to count as unalienated on Marx's terms?
