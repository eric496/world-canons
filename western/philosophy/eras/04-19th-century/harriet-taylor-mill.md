# Harriet Taylor Mill

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1807–1858
- **Place:** London, England
- **School/tradition:** Liberal feminism and social reform
- **Known for:** Author of "The Enfranchisement of Women" (1851) and intellectual partner — and later wife — of John Stuart Mill; she pushed liberalism toward full equality of the sexes a generation before organized suffragism took it up.

**Responds to:** [J. S. Mill](john-stuart-mill.md) · **Answered by:** the early suffragists who cited her essay and the feminist scholars who later recovered her work

## Biography

Harriet Taylor was born Harriet Hardy in London in 1807, into a prosperous Unitarian family. She married John Taylor, a wholesale druggist, in 1826 at eighteen, and had three children. The marriage was civil but intellectually lonely; through her Unitarian minister W. J. Fox's circle she moved in London's most radical intellectual milieu, publishing poems and discussing the rights of women.

In 1830 she met John Stuart Mill, then twenty-four and already a formidable thinker. Their intense friendship — conducted under intense social scrutiny and, by both accounts, kept within the bounds of propriety while her husband lived — became the central intellectual relationship of both their lives. For twenty years they exchanged drafts, argued positions, and, on Mill's later testimony, developed their mature views together. John Taylor died in 1849; after a decorous interval, Harriet and Mill married in 1851, Mill publicly renouncing the legal powers the marriage law gave him over a wife as "odious" and contrary to his convictions.

The marriage lasted seven years, much of it spent traveling for Harriet's fragile health. She died of a lung congestion in Avignon, France, in November 1858, while the two were traveling; Mill bought a house nearby and lived close to her grave for much of the rest of his life. Within months he published *On Liberty* (1859), whose dedication states, in substance, that its ideas were hers as much as his — the claim that launched a 150-year scholarly argument.

## Major theories and concepts

### The enfranchisement of women

Taylor Mill's one major lifetime publication, "The Enfranchisement of Women" (1851, in the *Westminster Review*), argues for the complete legal and civil equality of women: the vote, eligibility for office, equal education, and access to all occupations. Its reasoning is characteristic of her style. Existing arrangements, she argues, rest on force and custom, not nature: we cannot know what women's "nature" is, because women have never been allowed to develop outside subordination — an argument Mill would later make famous in *The Subjection of Women* (1869). Where Mill's later essay is comparatively cautious, Taylor Mill's is not: she demands admission of women to "all the rights and privileges of citizenship" without qualification.

### Equality within marriage

Her unpublished writings on marriage and divorce (written around 1832, when the two Mills were first exchanging essays) are more radical still. She argued that marriage law made wives legally and economically dependent, that easy divorce was essential to genuine equality, and that women's economic independence through work was the precondition of free choice in private life. On these questions she was consistently ahead of her famous partner — a pattern Mill himself acknowledged when he wrote that on practical matters her judgment was the bolder and more far-sighted of the two.

### Socialism and the scope of reform

In the 1848 first edition of Mill's *Principles of Political Economy*, chapters on property and the future of the laboring classes bear her strong influence; in the 1852 edition the two jointly revised them toward a qualified sympathy with socialist experiments and workers' cooperatives. Scholars treat this strand — a liberalism open to socialist reconstruction — as one of the clearest fingerprints of her hand in his published work.

## Key works

- "The Enfranchisement of Women" (1851) — her signature essay, published anonymously in the *Westminster Review*; the fullest statement of her feminism.
- Essays on marriage and divorce (c. 1832, unpublished in her lifetime) — early, radical statements on marriage law, written in exchange with Mill's matching essays.
- *Principles of Political Economy* (1848; rev. 1852), with J. S. Mill — especially the chapters on property and the laboring classes.
- *On Liberty* (1859) — posthumously dedicated to her; the extent of her contribution is the central debate about her work (see below).

## Contributions

Taylor Mill's distinctive contribution is to have drawn the conclusions of liberal individualism for the half of humanity its male founders largely ignored, and to have done so in the 1830s–1850s, before an organized women's movement existed in Britain. The argument that apparent female "nature" is an artefact of upbringing and law — now a standard move in feminist philosophy — is fully present in her 1851 essay. Within Mill scholarship, she is the key case for recognizing collaborative, cross-gender intellectual production behind canonical single-author texts.

## Impact and legacy

Published anonymously, her work was long absorbed into Mill's reputation; *The Subjection of Women* is still routinely credited to Mill alone. Twentieth-century feminist historians and philosophers — notably in editions of her *Complete Works* (1998) — recovered her as a thinker in her own right, and she now figures in standard histories of feminist philosophy. Her essay was cited by early suffragists, and the recovery debate itself changed how scholars write intellectual history: attribution questions about her work are now a standard cautionary example about women's invisible labor in philosophy.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The authorship debate.** How much of *On Liberty* and Mill's other writings is hers? Mill's own testimony is maximal: the dedication says the book's ideas were jointly produced, and his *Autobiography* calls her the joint author of most of what he published. One camp of scholars takes Mill at his word and treats the writings as genuinely collaborative. Another argues Mill, grief-stricken and prone to idealizing her, exaggerated, and that surviving drafts (largely in his hand) show a conventional author–critic relationship. A third position distinguishes domains: strong direct influence on the writings about women, marriage, and socialism; a role of critic and stimulus elsewhere. The manuscripts that survive are consistent with intensive discussion and some joint drafting, but they do not settle the question, and honest scholarship presents it as open.
- **Radicalism vs. respectability.** Some feminist readers note her arguments are framed for educated middle-class women and say little about working-class women's double burden; others reply that her insistence on women's access to *all* occupations was precisely the point at which her argument reached beyond her own class.
- **Whose voice in the joint works?** Where scholars detect differences in argument (e.g., the stronger egalitarianism of 1851 versus the caution of 1869), the direction of influence is debated rather than assumed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- She was not merely Mill's "muse." Whatever the verdict on particular texts, she published a substantial feminist argument under conditions of anonymity, wrote on marriage and property independently, and is documented as changing his mind on identifiable questions.
- Conversely, the dedication of *On Liberty* is not proof she wrote it. Treating Mill's tribute as a title page repeats, in reverse, the error of treating its absence as proof she contributed nothing.
- *The Subjection of Women* (1869) is Mill's posthumous solo work, written a decade after her death — deeply influenced by her, but not a joint text in the way they both used the term.

## What to read first

Read "The Enfranchisement of Women" itself — short, forceful, and the firmest ground for judging her own voice — then the dedication and Chapter 1 of *On Liberty*, and the relevant chapters of Mill's *Autobiography*. Compare her 1851 essay with Chapter 1 of *The Subjection of Women* to form your own view of the influence debate.
