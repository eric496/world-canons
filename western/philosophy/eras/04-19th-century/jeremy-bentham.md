## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1748–1832
- **Place:** London, England
- **School/tradition:** Utilitarianism (founder); legal and political reform
- **Known for:** Founding modern utilitarianism — the principle that actions and laws are right insofar as they promote "the greatest happiness of the greatest number."

**Responds to:** Blackstone's *Commentaries* and the unreformed English common law · **Answered by:** [J. S. Mill](john-stuart-mill.md), [Foucault](../05-20th-century/michel-foucault.md)

## Biography

Jeremy Bentham was born in London in 1748, a prodigy of forbidding precocity: Latin at three or four, Oxford at twelve, called to the bar at twenty-one. Called, but never content: he attended court and was disgusted by what he saw — a legal system of deliberate obscurity, technical fictions, and vested interests ("Judge & Co.") that enriched lawyers and baffled everyone else. He decided, astonishingly early, that the law needed not practitioners but a science: a standard outside itself by which every law could be judged.

That standard was utility. Backed by an inheritance that freed him from earning a living, Bentham spent more than sixty years writing — thousands upon thousands of manuscript pages, much of it unpublished or unfinished in his lifetime — aimed at a complete rational reconstruction of law, punishment, government, and morals. His first public success, *A Fragment on Government* (1776), was a demolition of Blackstone's reverent *Commentaries* on English law. His great statement of principle, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, was printed in 1780 and published in 1789.

Bentham was more than a desk philosopher. He designed the Panopticon, a circular prison in which a single unseen inspector might be watching any inmate at any time, and spent years and much of his fortune trying to get it built — corresponding with governments, buying land, lobbying Parliament — before the project collapsed, leaving him embittered and convinced of the "sinister interests" that block reform. (He received substantial compensation, and the idea outlived the building; see the legacy section.) He wrote codification proposals for Russia, France, the United States, Spain, and the new Latin American republics, believing rational law codes were exportable goods. Around him gathered the "Philosophic Radicals" — James Mill above all, and through James, his son John Stuart Mill, raised inside the movement — who carried Benthamism into Parliament, the East India Company, and the press.

His private life was eccentric and warm in unexpected ways: he kept pet animals, invented words by the hundred (codify, international, maximize, minimize), and arranged the century's strangest memorial to himself. By his will, his body was dissected for science and his skeleton dressed in his own clothes, seated in a mahogany case with a wax head — the "Auto-Icon," still displayed at University College London, the institution whose founding he inspired (he was its spiritual, not literal, founder). He died in 1832, on the eve of the Great Reform Act his followers had helped bring about.

## Major theories and concepts

### The principle of utility

"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, *pain* and *pleasure*," the *Introduction* opens. The principle of utility judges every action, law, and institution by a single standard: does it augment or diminish the happiness of those affected? An action conforming to the principle may be called right; a law that fails the test deserves reform or repeal, whatever its pedigree. The maxim associated with the doctrine — the greatest happiness of the greatest number — makes the test radically impartial: the ruler's pleasure counts for no more than the laborer's, and (Bentham insisted, against the moral common sense of his age) no one's suffering is discounted because of who they are.

### The felicific calculus

Bentham tried to make the test operational. The value of a pleasure or pain can be assessed by seven dimensions: *intensity*, *duration*, *certainty*, *propinquity* (nearness in time), *fecundity* (tendency to produce more of the same), *purity* (tendency not to produce the opposite), and *extent* (how many people are affected). Run a proposed law through these variables and you can, in principle, compare it with alternatives. Nobody has ever literally computed a law this way, and Bentham knew practice would be approximate; but the calculus states the doctrine's ambition — morality and legislation as matters of public, checkable calculation rather than intuition, scripture, or tradition.

### Against natural rights and fictions

Bentham was a fierce enemy of the language of natural and imprescriptible rights: rights talk unanchored in law was, in his notorious phrase, "nonsense upon stilts." Real rights are creatures of law, and laws are justified only by utility. His theory of *fictions* — legal and metaphysical entities like "corporations" or "duties" that are not real things — demanded that such terms be paraphrased back into sentences about real pleasures, pains, and actions before they could do honest work, a demand that anticipates later analytic philosophy.

### Punishment and the Panopticon

Since punishment is itself an evil (it inflicts pain), it is justified only where it excludes some greater evil: deterrence, incapacitation, and reform, calibrated to the minimum necessary — an early, systematic statement of parsimony in sentencing. The Panopticon applied the same engineering to inspection: architecture that makes power visible-but-unverifiable, so the watched internalize the watcher.

## Key works

- *A Fragment on Government* (1776) — The youthful attack on Blackstone that launched his career.
- *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed 1780; published 1789) — The founding text of utilitarianism: the principle, the calculus, and the theory of punishment.
- *Panopticon; or, The Inspection-House* (1791) — The prison design and its general logic of surveillance.
- *The Rationale of Judicial Evidence*, *Constitutional Code*, and much else exist substantially in editions assembled by disciples (notably Étienne Dumont's French recensions, through which much of Europe actually read Bentham).

## Contributions

Bentham invented the doctrine his century then argued about: a single, public, impartial standard for morals and law, a theory of legislation as a science, and a vocabulary of reform. He founded no successful prison, but he founded a way of evaluating institutions that underlies cost–benefit analysis, welfare economics, and modern public policy. He supported, far ahead of respectable opinion, the decriminalization of homosexuality (in unpublished essays), animal welfare ("The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"), near-universal manhood suffrage in his late radical phase, and the abolition of the death penalty in most cases.

## Impact and legacy

Through James Mill and the Philosophic Radicals, Benthamism shaped Victorian administration, law reform, and the young John Stuart Mill — who inherited the framework and then humanised it. In the twentieth century the Panopticon found a second, darker fame when Foucault made it the emblem of disciplinary power in *Discipline and Punish* (1975) — an appropriation Bentham, who designed it as humane and accountable, would have found perverse. The Bentham Project at UCL continues to edit his manuscripts, and new volumes keep changing the scholarly picture.

## Criticisms and debates

The classic objection targets the calculus: pleasures are not commensurable units, and pretending otherwise smuggles in the very judgments the calculus was meant to replace. Rights theorists reverse his slogan — some things (torturing an innocent, say) seem wrong even if useful, which is exactly what "nonsense upon stilts" was meant to dismiss. Marx dismissed him as a "genius in the way of bourgeois stupidity"; John Stuart Mill's essay "Bentham" (1838) judged his knowledge of human nature fatally narrow — a judgment from inside the family that still frames his reputation. Scholars also debate the "two Benthams": the liberal reformer of the early works versus the increasingly authoritarian administrative engineer of the later *Constitutional Code*.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Bentham was not a crude hedonist for whom all pleasures are pushpin-equal; "quantity of pleasure being equal, pushpin is as good as poetry" is a line about impartial measurement, and later scholars note how often it is quoted against its purpose.
- Utilitarianism is not egoism: the standard is everyone's happiness, impartially.
- The Panopticon was designed as a *humane* alternative to the prisons of his day; its modern use as a symbol of oppression is Foucault's reinterpretation, not Bentham's intention.
- He did not found UCL in the administrative sense; he was its inspiration and prophet.

## What to read first

1. *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, chapters 1, 4, and 13–17 — the principle, the calculus, and the cases for punishment.
2. The *Fragment on Government* Preface — his critical voice at its sharpest.
3. John Stuart Mill's essay "Bentham" (1838) — the best contemporary corrective, by the heir who knew him best.
