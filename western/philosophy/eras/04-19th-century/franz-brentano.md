# Franz Brentano

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1838–1917
- **Place:** Born in Marienberg am Rhein, Germany; worked mainly in Würzburg and Vienna
- **School/tradition:** Act psychology — its founder, and reviver of the scholastic concept of **intentionality**, the "aboutness" of mental phenomena
- **Known for:** Less a finished system than the extraordinary generation of students he trained, above all Edmund Husserl, founder of phenomenology.

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md) · **Answered by:** [Husserl](../05-20th-century/edmund-husserl.md), [Heidegger](../05-20th-century/martin-heidegger.md)

## Biography

Brentano came from an intellectual Catholic family of Italian-German descent — the Romantic writer Clemens Brentano was his uncle, and his brother Lujo Brentano became a leading economist. He studied philosophy and theology at Munich, Würzburg, Berlin, and Münster, steeping himself in Aristotle and the medieval scholastics at a time when German universities largely ignored them. Ordained a Catholic priest in 1864, he qualified as a lecturer (*Privatdozent*) at Würzburg in 1866 — extraordinary professor in 1872, full professor in 1873 — and wrote a respected early study of Aristotle's psychology.

His career then collided with the Church. Brentano rejected the doctrine of papal infallibility proclaimed in 1870 and, in 1873, resigned his chair and left the priesthood (he formally left the Church in 1879). Appointed professor at the University of Vienna in 1874, he became one of the most magnetic lecturers in Europe — but when he married Ida von Lieben in 1880, Austrian law, treating him still as a priest, would not recognize the marriage in his university capacity, and he was forced to resign his chair, continuing for fifteen years as a mere *Privatdozent*. The injustice rankled for the rest of his life; in 1895 he left Vienna, settling in Florence in 1896, and after Italy entered the First World War he moved to Zurich, where he died in 1917, blind and dictating revisions to the end.

His fame rests disproportionately on his teaching. His Viennese students included Edmund Husserl, Alexius Meinong, Christian von Ehrenfels, Kazimierz Twardowski, and (in his lecture audience) the young Sigmund Freud, who had attended Brentano's courses and translated a volume of Mill at his recommendation. Through Twardowski, Brentano's ideas seeded the great Lvov–Warsaw school of Polish logic; through Husserl, phenomenology; through Meinong and Ehrenfels, Gestalt psychology and the theory of objects.

## Major theories and concepts

### Intentionality

Brentano's most famous idea appears in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874). Every mental phenomenon, he wrote, "includes something as object within itself": in presentation something is presented, in judgment something is affirmed or denied, in love something is loved, in hate hated. He called this the **intentional inexistence** of an object — "intentional" revives the scholastic *intentio*, the mind's directedness toward a thing; "inexistence" means the object's existing *in* the act, not that it exists in the world (you can fear a ghost that isn't there). This aboutness, Brentano claimed, marks off the mental from the physical: no merely physical phenomenon is *about* anything. The thesis raises hard questions he and his students wrestled with: what exactly is the status of non-existent objects of thought, and is aboutness really absent from the physical world? Those questions became research programs for a century.

### Descriptive psychology

Brentano distinguished two psychological sciences. **Genetic psychology** explains mental processes by their physiological causes — the business, roughly, of the experimental laboratory. **Descriptive psychology** (or psychognosy) classifies and analyses the elements of consciousness from within, prior to any causal theory, and he regarded it as the more fundamental and the properly philosophical discipline. Its core classification divides mental phenomena into three irreducible classes: **presentations** (merely having something in mind), **judgments** (accepting or rejecting something as true or false), and **phenomena of love and hate** (the whole family of emotions, desires, and acts of will). Every mental act, on his view, is or rests on a presentation, and judgments and emotions are distinct attitudes taken toward what is presented — an architecture Husserl and others inherited even while rejecting its details.

### The four phases of philosophy

Brentano also taught a philosophy of history: each cultural era cycles through a rising phase of rigorous, science-like inquiry, then decline through mysticism and skepticism. He placed himself — and his Aristotelian-scholastic training — as the agent of a new rising phase. This is why his revival of scholastic distinctions was not nostalgia: he believed the medievals at their best had done philosophy with scientific seriousness.

### Ethics and the theory of value

Brentano grounded value in the emotions: we call something good when it is the object of a *correct* love, just as we call something true when it is the object of a correct judgment. In *The Origin of Our Knowledge of Right and Wrong* (1889) he argued that emotions, like judgments, can be experienced as fitting or unfitting to their objects, and that this gives ethics an evidential basis without collapsing it into mere feeling. Meinong, Scheler, and the early phenomenologists all developed versions of this idea.

## Key works

- *On the Several Senses of Being in Aristotle* (1862) — his dissertation-era study of Aristotle's doctrine of the categories; Martin Heidegger later said reading it set him on his philosophical path.
- *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) — the major published work: intentionality and the classification of mental phenomena. Only the first part of a projected larger work appeared; much of his system survives in posthumous editions of lectures.
- *The Origin of Our Knowledge of Right and Wrong* (1889) — a short lecture, the classic statement of his value theory.
- *On the Classification of Mental Phenomena* (1911) — a revised late restatement of the 1874 classification, issued as he rethought parts of the original doctrine.

## Contributions

Brentano contributed a question more than a doctrine: *what makes a state mental?* By reviving intentionality he gave philosophers of mind their central problem in modern form, and he built the institutional and conceptual bridge between scholastic philosophy and the two great successor movements of the 20th century — phenomenology and analytic philosophy of mind. His demand that philosophy proceed with the rigor of a science, and his descriptive method, are the common root of both. He also pioneered the rigorous classroom as a philosophical instrument: the Brentano school is a large part of why Vienna became a philosophical capital.

## Impact and legacy

Husserl transformed the descriptive psychology he learned from Brentano into phenomenology, while dropping the claim that aboutness needs objects existing "in" the mind. Meinong's theory of objects, Ehrenfels's Gestalt qualities, and Twardowski's distinction between the content and object of presentations all begin as Brentano problems. In analytic philosophy, Roderick Chisholm revived "Brentano's thesis" in the 1950s, and intentionality remains a standard topic in philosophy of mind — usually debated through Brentano's formulation. Freud's exposure to Brentano is documented, though how deeply Brentanian concepts entered psychoanalysis remains a specialist debate.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Is intentionality really the mark of the mental?** Sensations like pain or undirected moods seem mental without being clearly *about* anything; conversely, thermostats and sentences are in some sense about things. Defenders refine the thesis; critics take these cases to refute it.
- **"Intentional inexistence."** Later Brentano himself retreated from talk of objects immanent in the mind toward a spare ontology of real things only (*reism*). Scholars debate how to read the 1874 doctrine — literal immanent objects, or a misleading way of stating directedness — and Husserl's and Meinong's divergent readings both claim Brentanian ancestry.
- **A fragmentary legacy.** Because so much of his system remained in lecture manuscripts, edited posthumously by students with agendas of their own, Brentano interpretation is unusually entangled with editorial history — a debate about the texts as much as the ideas.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Brentano was not a phenomenologist; phenomenology is his student's transformation of his method, with crucial changes he did not live to endorse.
- "Intentional" in Brentano has nothing to do with intentions or doing things on purpose in the everyday sense; it means directedness or aboutness.
- He was not a laboratory experimentalist manqué. His "empirical standpoint" meant grounding psychology in careful first-person description and inner perception, and he was pointedly skeptical of reducing psychology to physiology.

## What to read first

Book II, Chapter 1 of *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* is the essential text — short, clear, and endlessly commented on. Pair it with *The Origin of Our Knowledge of Right and Wrong* for his ethics in miniature, then read Husserl's early work (or the Husserl entry in the 20th-century era) to see what his most famous student made of him.
