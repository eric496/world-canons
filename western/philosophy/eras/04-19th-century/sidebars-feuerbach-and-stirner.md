# Sidebars: Feuerbach & Stirner

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Figures:** Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872) and Max Stirner (1806–1856, pseudonym of Johann Kaspar Schmidt)
- **Place:** Germany — Feuerbach in Landshut, Heidelberg, and rural Franconia; Stirner in Bayreuth and Berlin
- **School/tradition:** Young Hegelians; the radical aftermath of Hegel
- **Known for:** Feuerbach: God as humanity's projection of its own essence. Stirner: every abstraction enthroned over the individual is a "spook"; only the self-owning individual is real.

**Responds to:** [Hegel](g-w-f-hegel.md) · **Answered by:** [Marx](karl-marx.md)

## Biography

**Ludwig Feuerbach** was born in Landshut in 1804, son of the famous criminal jurist Anselm Feuerbach. He began in theology at Heidelberg, heard Hegel in Berlin, and defected to philosophy, taking his doctorate in 1828. His first book, *Thoughts on Death and Immortality* (1830), published anonymously, denied personal immortality — and when his authorship became known, his academic career was over; he never held a chair. He married Bertha Löw, whose family porcelain factory at Bruckberg gave him a rural refuge; from that retirement he wrote the books that made him the most famous philosopher in Germany for a decade. *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) was a sensation — read by Marx, Engels, Kierkegaard, and Wagner. When the factory failed, his later years were straitened; German social democrats adopted him as a hero and buried him in Nuremberg in 1872 with a workers' funeral procession.

**Max Stirner** — born Johann Kaspar Schmidt in Bayreuth in 1806 — lived one of philosophy's most obscure lives. He studied in Berlin (attending Hegel's lectures), taught at a Berlin girls' school, and frequented the Young Hegelian circle "Die Freien" (The Free Ones) alongside Bruno Bauer and the young Engels. In 1844 he published *The Ego and Its Own* (*Der Einzige und sein Eigentum*, issued late 1844 with an 1845 title page) — and promptly resigned his teaching post. The book was censored briefly, reviewed widely, and answered at enormous length by Feuerbach, Moses Hess, and above all Marx and Engels, whose refutation of "Saint Max" fills the bulk of *The German Ideology*. Stirner translated Adam Smith and Jean-Baptiste Say for money, failed in a milk-shop venture, was twice imprisoned for debt, and died in Berlin in 1856 in near-total obscurity. Rediscovery came decades later through anarchist and Nietzsche-adjacent readings of the 1890s — Nietzsche never mentions him, and whether he read him remains an unresolved scholarly quarrel.

## Major theories and concepts

### Feuerbach: God as projection

Feuerbach's central claim in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) is disarmingly simple: theology is anthropology. The divine being is nothing but the essence of man objectified and contemplated as another, separate being. Humanity takes its own best collective qualities — reason, will, love — purifies them of limits, projects them outward onto a God, and then relates to its own essence as to a master. The cost is impoverishment by transfer: "to enrich God, man must become poor; that God may be all, man must be nothing." The cure is not sheer unbelief but repossession: the predicates assigned to God are to be recognized as the *species-essence* (Gattungswesen) of humanity itself, and love between human beings — "man is the God of man" (*homo homini Deus*) — takes the place of worship. His later work pushed toward a philosophy of sensuous embodiment, summed up in his pun: *Der Mensch ist, was er isst* — man is what he eats.

### Feuerbach: transformative criticism — and its limits

Feuerbach's method inverted Hegel: wherever speculative philosophy finds Spirit realizing itself, Feuerbach finds human essence alienated from itself, waiting to be reclaimed by inverting subject and predicate (God did not make man; man makes God). Marx absorbed this in 1843–44 and then, in the *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845), turned on his teacher: Feuerbach's humanity remains an abstract essence contemplated passively, not real people changing their world through practice. That critique — contemplative versus practical materialism — is the birth-certificate of Marx's own theory, which is why Feuerbach, though a "sidebar," stands at the crossroads of the era.

### Stirner: spooks and the unique one

Stirner opens *The Ego and Its Own* with "All things are nothing to me," and his target is Feuerbach himself. Feuerbach thought he had freed us by replacing God with Man — but "Man" is the same throne with a new occupant: another fixed idea set above the concrete individual, demanding sacrifice. Stirner calls such ruling abstractions *spooks* (Spuk): God, the State, Morality, Truth, the Nation — anything in whose service the individual is taught to be unselfish. His counter-concept is the *Unique One* (der Einzige) — deliberately an empty name, since any defined content would become another spook — and *ownness* (Eigenheit): taking one's own cause as one's only cause. Egoism, so redefined, is not petty selfishness; it is refusal to serve any cause that is not one's own, while fully permitting love, friendship, and association — as enjoyment, not duty.

### Stirner: the union of egoists

Politically Stirner rejects both the state (which rests on my reverence and will vanish when I withdraw it) and liberalism's abstractions. In their place he sketches the *union of egoists*: a voluntary, revocable association existing as long as it serves its participants — an idea later claimed, with some strain, by individualist anarchism. Property likewise is not a sacred right but what I can hold: "what I have in my power, that is my own."

## Key works

- Ludwig Feuerbach, *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) — The projection theory of religion; Part I is the essential reading.
- Ludwig Feuerbach, *Principles of the Philosophy of the Future* (1843) — His positive program: sensuous, embodied, communal humanity.
- Max Stirner, *The Ego and Its Own* (published late 1844; title page 1845) — His only book; the case against all spooks, Feuerbach's included.

## Contributions

Feuerbach contributed the projection theory of religion — a template for Marx on ideology and Freud on illusion — and the species-being concept that runs straight into the young Marx. Stirner contributed the century's most radical critique of abstraction: the demand to test every ideal by what it asks the individual to sacrifice.

## Impact and legacy

Feuerbach's decade of dominance shaped Marx, Engels, and (by reaction) Kierkegaard; when his star dimmed, his ideas survived inside theirs. Stirner's influence is stranger: Marx thought him dangerous enough to spend hundreds of pages refuting him (the "Saint Max" polemic — indirect evidence of Stirner's sting); he was later adopted by individualist anarchists and argued over by scholars who see in him either a nihilist or a proto-post-structuralist critic of fixed identity.

## Criticisms and debates

Feuerbach was charged — by Stirner first, then by Marx — with leaving his own abstraction, "Man," uncriticised: species-essence replaces God but still stands above real individuals and their history. Stirner attracts the mirror objection: a self stripped of every abstraction may be too empty to act or even to be described (his defenders say the emptiness is the point). Whether Stirner is an anarchist, a nihilist, or a satirist of the Young Hegelians is unresolved — as is the Nietzsche question: remarkable parallels exist, documentary evidence of reading does not, and scholars divide between influence, common sources, and coincidence.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Feuerbach was not simply an atheist debunker; he thought religion encoded real truths about humanity, to be reclaimed rather than merely mocked.
- Marx's debt to Feuerbach is real but so is the rupture of 1845; do not merge them.
- Stirner's "egoism" is not the claim that people ought to be selfish in the ordinary sense; it is a critique of sacred causes and self-sacrifice.
- The "union of egoists" is not a state, party, or doctrine; treating it as one repeats the error he attacked.

## What to read first

1. Feuerbach, *The Essence of Christianity*, Part I, chapter 1 extracts — the projection argument at its source.
2. Stirner, *The Ego and Its Own*, the opening sections ("All Things Are Nothing to Me" and the "spooks" discussion).
3. Marx's *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845) — eleven short paragraphs that show what the next generation did with both men.
