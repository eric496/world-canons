# Friedrich Schleiermacher

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1768–1834
- **Place:** Born in Breslau, Silesia; pastor, professor, and public figure in Berlin; founder-member of its university
- **School/tradition:** Post-Kantian theology and philosophy; founder of modern hermeneutics
- **Known for:** Hermeneutics as the universal art of understanding, religion as the "feeling of absolute dependence," the *Speeches on Religion* (1799), and the theory of translation's two paths

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Spinoza](../03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md), the Berlin Romantic circle · **Answered by:** [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [Gadamer](../05-20th-century/hans-georg-gadamer.md)

## Biography

Schleiermacher was born in 1768 in Breslau, son of a Reformed army chaplain of Moravian sympathies, and educated in the pietist schools of the Herrnhuter Brethren at Niesky and Barby. The formation gave him an inward, experiential piety he never lost — and a system he outgrew: reading Kant and the classics in secret, doubting the doctrines of atonement and eternal punishment he was meant to administer, he wrote to his father in 1787 a painfully famous letter breaking with orthodoxy, and was allowed to transfer to the University of Halle. There he absorbed Kant, Aristotle, and Spinoza at first hand.

Ordained, he became in 1796 preacher at Berlin's Charité hospital and a fixture of the salons — above all Henriette Herz's — where he formed the intense friendship with Friedrich Schlegel that made him a participant in early Romanticism itself: Schlegel's *Lucinde* scandal and Schleiermacher's anonymous *Confidential Letters* defending it (1800) belong to the same season as his first great book, *On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers* (1799, anonymous). A call to the University of Halle (1804) was cut short by Napoleon's closure of it; returning to Berlin he became preacher at Trinity Church (1809), helped Wilhelm von Humboldt plan the new University of Berlin and served as a founding professor of theology (1810) and dean, preached patriotic sermons through the wars of liberation, and worked for Prussian church union and reform. His last decades were consumed by the double labor of the lecture hall — hermeneutics, dialectics, ethics, exegesis — and *The Christian Faith* (1821/22; thoroughly revised 1830/31), the most influential Protestant dogmatics of the century. He died in Berlin in 1834, still preaching and lecturing.

## Major theories and concepts

### Hermeneutics: the art of understanding

Schleiermacher's most durable creation is hermeneutics rebuilt as a *universal* discipline. Interpretation had been a set of special techniques for difficult texts (scripture, law, classics); he made it the general **art of understanding** (*Kunst des Verstehens*), needed wherever discourse occurs — because misunderstanding, he insisted in a deliberate provocation, is not the accident but the default: the stricter practice "starts from the assumption that misunderstanding occurs as a matter of course," and understanding must be willed and worked at, point by point. Interpretation has two inseparable sides:

- **Grammatical interpretation** — the utterance within the shared language: its lexicon, grammar, genre, and the linguistic whole of its age. No text means anything in isolation from the language system it uses.
- **Psychological (later: technical) interpretation** — the utterance as the act of an individual mind: how this author bends the common language to a distinctive style and thought. Genius, for Schleiermacher, is precisely this individuality of productive use.

Two methods cross both sides: the **comparative** (locating the text against similar ones, by type) and the **divinatory** (the conjectural leap into the author's productive center, checked afterwards by comparative evidence). And the two directions form the **hermeneutic circle**: parts are understood through the whole, the whole through its parts, so interpretation is a disciplined spiral, never a one-pass decoding. Its audacious goal formula, adapted from Kant, is to understand an author "first as well as, and then even better than, the author understood himself" — not arrogance, but the claim that method can recover what in the author's own production was unconscious. This program, transmitted through Dilthey, is the ancestor of all modern interpretation theory.

### Religion as feeling of absolute dependence

The *Speeches* (1799) address religion's "cultured despisers" — the enlightened who dismiss it as bad metaphysics and worse morals. Religion, he answers, is neither: neither a way of thinking (doctrine) nor a way of acting (morality), but "sense and taste for the Infinite" — in the second Speech, the immediate consciousness of the universe's action upon us, "intuition and feeling." The mature dogmatics of *The Christian Faith* (§4) fixes the formula: piety is "the consciousness of being absolutely dependent, that is, of being in relation with God." **Feeling** here is a technical term and must be handled with care: *Gefühl* is immediate self-consciousness — the pre-reflective awareness of oneself as not self-originated — not a passing emotion, sensation, or mood. Dependence is "absolute" because, unlike our dependence on nature (which is partial and reciprocal), the whole of our active existence is in question. Whether this relocates religion safely beyond the reach of Kant's critique of metaphysics, or merely retreats from every testable claim, was the charge and defense of his whole reception.

### Translation: the two paths

His 1813 academy lecture *On the Different Methods of Translating* poses the translator's choice with unmatched clarity. "Either the translator leaves the writer in peace as much as possible and moves the reader toward him, or he leaves the reader in peace as much as possible and moves the writer toward him." The first path keeps the work's foreignness — the reader must sense they are meeting an alien mind in its own forms; the second naturalizes, as if the author had written in the target tongue. Schleiermacher argued for the first, foreignising path for serious translation, and practiced it in the great German Plato (1804–28), begun as a joint project with Schlegel and long standard. The lecture is now a founding document of translation studies, routinely cited by theorists like Venuti, who takes up his foreignising banner.

### Dialectics and ethics

The lectures he prized most — the *Dialectic* (from 1811), his answer to Kant on the possibility of knowledge — treat thinking as dialogue directed toward agreement in a shared world, with pure thinking and organic sensation as its poles. His *Ethics* lectures correspondingly refuse Kant's duty-only architecture: ethics has three equal forms, the doctrine of **goods** (the realized achievements of reason in nature — state, church, school, free sociability), of **virtues**, and of **duties**. The ethics of goods, with its theory of institutions and culture, is the part of his system later scholars have found most original.

## Key works

- *On Religion: Speeches to Its Cultured Despisers* (1799) — Romanticism's religious manifesto: religion as intuition and feeling, addressed to the enlightened skeptic.
- *Soliloquies* (1800) — Ethical self-portraits from the same season; individuality as a moral vocation.
- *The Christmas Eve Celebration: A Dialogue* (1806) — Doctrine transposed into salon conversation; piety as shared feeling.
- "On the Different Methods of Translating" (1813) — The two paths of translation; foreignising defended.
- *Hermeneutics* (lectures 1805–33; compendium 1819) — The universal art of understanding, posthumously assembled from his manuscripts — read in the standard English edition by Duke and Forstman, or Kimmerle's edition.
- *The Christian Faith* (1821/22; 2nd ed. 1830/31) — The dogmatics: piety analyzed as the feeling of absolute dependence, every doctrine an account of that consciousness.

## Contributions

Schleiermacher founded two disciplines as we have them: philosophical hermeneutics, generalized from technique into a theory of all understanding, and modern liberal Protestant theology, by regrounding doctrine in religious self-consciousness rather than in metaphysical proof or historical apologetics — a direct answer to the ditch Lessing had dug. His translation theory remains the reference point of its field, and his Plato defined German classical scholarship for generations.

## Impact and legacy

Through Dilthey, who edited his works and wrote his biography, the hermeneutics passed into the human sciences as their methodology; Gadamer's *Truth and Method* is an extended argument with him — absorbing the universality while rejecting the psychological side and the "better than the author" ambition in favor of historically effected consciousness. Twentieth-century theology split over him: Barth's revolt against liberal theology made Schleiermacher the great antagonist ("the father of modern theology" in both praise and blame), while his analysis of religion as such — neither knowledge nor morality — permanently widened the philosophy of religion's map. Hermeneutics after him, down to Ricoeur, works in his frame even when correcting it.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Hegel's attack.** Hegel, his Berlin colleague and rival, mocked the theology of feeling relentlessly — remarking (in the foreword to Hinrichs, 1822) that if religion is the feeling of dependence, "a dog would be the best Christian." Behind the sneer is a serious charge: what cannot rise to conceptual knowledge is not truth but mere subjective certainty. Defenders reply that "feeling" as immediate self-consciousness is precisely not the emotion Hegel targeted — though whether Schleiermacher's exposition always kept the distinction visible is itself debated.
- **Psychology or history?** Gadamer and the hermeneutic tradition after him charge the psychological interpretation with romantic subjectivism — reconstructing an author's interiority instead of meeting the text's claim in one's own horizon. Schleiermacher scholars answer that grammatical interpretation and the comparative method give his program more textual and historical ballast than the charge allows. The debate defines modern hermeneutics.
- **The dog that didn't bark: apologetics.** By grounding faith in self-consciousness, he immunised it against Lessing's ditch — no historical claim can refute a feeling. Critics from Barth onward call that a surrender disguised as a rescue: Christianity becomes the pious consciousness with doctrine as decoration. His defenders point to the dogmatics' rigorous internal architecture as proof he meant the doctrines seriously.
- **Politics.** His sermons and memoranda served Prussian reform and national resistance to Napoleon; his simultaneous work for church union served the Prussian state. Liberal hero of the university's founding or theologian of the establishment — scholarship keeps both portraits in play.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Feeling" does not mean emotion.** The feeling of absolute dependence is immediate self-consciousness of one's total non-self-sufficiency — a structural feature of awareness, present as steadily in dry reflection as in religious ecstasy. Reading it as "religion is a warm feeling" reproduces Hegel's caricature.
- **Hermeneutics is not Bible study.** Scripture is one application; the theory covers all speech and writing, and indeed everyday misunderstanding — that universality is the innovation.
- **He is not simply a Romantic.** He moved in Romantic circles and shared their cult of individuality, but the *Christian Faith* is a work of relentless systematics, and he split with Schlegel personally and intellectually. "Romantic theologian" is a starting label, not the picture.
- **"Better than the author" is not mind-reading.** It names the recovery, by comparative and grammatical method, of what operated unconsciously in the work's production — and it is checked against the text, not free divination.

## What to read first

1. *Speeches on Religion*, the Second Speech — the religion-as-intuition argument at full youthful force; Crouter's Cambridge edition is the standard English text.
2. *Hermeneutics and Criticism* (the 1819 compendium and related manuscripts, trans. Duke and Forstman) — the art of understanding in his own compact formulations, including the strict-practice maxim.
3. "On the Different Methods of Translating" — twelve pages that organize a whole field; findable in translation-studies readers (e.g. in Venuti's *Translation Studies Reader*).
4. *The Christian Faith*, §§3–5 and the Introduction — the dependence formula and the method of dogmatics, before sampling its doctrines.
5. For orientation: the [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/) entry on Schleiermacher, then Gadamer's critique in *Truth and Method* Part II to see the frame he set.
