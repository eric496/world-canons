# William James

*Era: 19th-Century Philosophy (1801 – 1900) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1842–1910
- **Place:** New York City and Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA
- **School/tradition:** Pragmatism (its great popularizer); founder of American psychology
- **Known for:** Making pragmatism a public philosophy — truth as what proves itself in experience, the will to believe, and the stream of consciousness.

**Responds to:** [Peirce](charles-sanders-peirce.md) · **Answered by:** [Dewey](../05-20th-century/john-dewey.md), [Russell](../05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md)

## Biography

William James was born in New York in 1842 into one of America's most remarkable families. His father, Henry James Sr., was a wealthy, restless religious thinker who gave his children a deliberately unconventional education — schools across America and Europe, museums and languages everywhere, no fixed curriculum. His younger brother Henry became the great novelist. The family atmosphere — brilliant, tolerant, spiritually intense — formed William's lifelong conviction that experience, in all its variety, is the final court of appeal.

His path to a vocation was indirect. He studied painting, switched to science at Harvard, then medicine, interrupting everything with an expedition to Brazil with the naturalist Louis Agassiz and with bouts of depression and neurasthenic collapse. His private crisis around 1870 — recorded in a diary entry after reading the French philosopher Charles Renouvier — became his personal proof of free will: "My first act of free will shall be to believe in free will." The episode underlies both his psychology of habit and his later doctrine of the will to believe.

Harvard gave him a lectureship in physiology in 1872; he migrated to psychology — teaching the first American course in the new subject and founding one of its first laboratories, though he disliked lab work — and then to philosophy. His *Principles of Psychology* (1890), twelve years in the writing, was instantly a classic. Thereafter came the works that made him a public sage: *The Will to Believe* (1897), the Gifford Lectures published as *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), and *Pragmatism* (1907). He championed Peirce — crediting him as pragmatism's originator in an 1898 Berkeley address — while popularizing a warmer version Peirce resisted. He opposed American imperialism in the Philippines loudly and publicly, and died of heart trouble in 1910 at Chocorua, New Hampshire. Russell and Wittgenstein both read him closely; Wittgenstein's esteem for the *Varieties* is only the most surprising instance.

## Major theories and concepts

### The pragmatic method and pragmatic truth

James took Peirce's maxim and made it a philosophy. The pragmatic method settles disputes by asking what practical difference each side would make: if none, the dispute is empty (his famous example: whether a man circling a tree "goes round" the squirrel on it — it depends on what you practically mean, and once that's fixed, the quarrel dissolves). His theory of truth goes further, and is the contested step: true ideas are those we can assimilate, validate, and corroborate in experience — "truth *happens* to an idea; it *becomes* true, is *made* true by events." A true idea has "cash-value" in experience: it leads satisfactorily from one part of experience to another and is constrained throughout by reality and the body of prior truths. This is not "comforting = true": a belief that flatters us but fails in practice or collides with recalcitrant sensation is, on James's own criteria, false. Yet critics from Russell onward have charged that the theory licenses exactly that confusion — a debate that has never entirely closed.

### The will to believe

In the 1896 address "The Will to Believe," James defends a narrow permission: where an option between hypotheses is *live* (genuinely appealing to the chooser), *forced* (no neutral way to abstain), and *momentous*, and where evidence cannot decide, our passional nature may lawfully choose — sometimes must, since refusing to believe is itself a choice with consequences. His paradigms are religious faith and interpersonal trust: a friendship may exist only if first believed in, so waiting for proof guarantees failure. The doctrine does *not* license believing against evidence, or where inquiry can settle the question (against W. K. Clifford's "Ethics of Belief," which demanded evidence everywhere); its scope is precisely the residue of cases evidence cannot reach.

### The stream of consciousness and the self

The *Principles* replaced the picture of the mind as a chain of discrete ideas with the *stream of thought*: consciousness is personal, continuous, always changing, selectively interested, and deals with objects independent of itself. Its "fringes" — the felt penumbra of relations, tendencies, and meanings around clear foci — matter as much as its bright centers. James's self is layered: the material self, the social selves (as many as there are audiences), and the spiritual self, unified only as a passing Thought that appropriates its predecessors.

### The James–Lange theory of emotion

With the Danish physiologist Carl Lange (independently), James proposed that emotion *is* the perception of bodily change: we do not tremble because we are afraid; we are afraid because we tremble — the bodily reverberation comes first, and the feeling of it is the emotion. Stripped of its bodily symptoms, an emotion would be a cold, neutral perception. Modern affective science treats the strong version as untenable (bodily patterns are too similar across distinct emotions — Cannon's critique), but the theory permanently redirected emotion research toward the body, and subtler descendants (somatic-marker hypotheses) are its heirs.

### The varieties of religious experience

The *Varieties* (1902) treats religion as, first of all, individual experience — "the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine" — and mines conversion narratives, mystical states, and saintliness as psychological data. James distinguishes the healthy-minded ("once-born") from the sick soul (the "twice-born," who must pass through despair), catalogues mysticism's marks (ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity), and proposes judging religion pragmatically, by its fruits for life rather than its roots. His own tentative "over-belief" — that there is a "More" continuous with our subconscious selves — is offered as personal conclusion, not demonstrated doctrine.

### Radical empiricism and pluralism

His late metaphysics held that relations are themselves given in experience, not imposed on it by mind or Absolute: a "pure experience" prior to the subject/object split is the world's basic stuff. Against the Hegelian Absolute of his colleague Josiah Royce, James defended a *pluralistic universe*: reality unfinished, "in the making," rather than a block universe in which everything is eternally decided. This pluralism underlies his meliorism — the world can get better, partly through our acts, and is guaranteed neither way.

## Key works

- *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) — The founding text of American psychology; chapters on habit, the stream of thought, and the self are still read.
- *The Will to Believe and Other Essays* (1897) — Contains the title address and his defense of passional decision in undecidable, momentous options.
- *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) — The Gifford Lectures (1901–02): psychology and philosophy of religion via case study.
- *Pragmatism* (1907) — Eight lectures; the popular statement of the method and the truth theory (Lectures 2 and 6 are the core).
- *The Meaning of Truth* (1909) — His replies to critics of the truth theory.
- *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909) — The late metaphysics against absolute idealism.
- "The Moral Equivalent of War" (1910) — His most reprinted essay: the martial virtues of discipline, sacrifice, and collective effort redirected from war against nations to a "war against nature" — poverty, drudgery, and social ills.

## Contributions

James gave American philosophy its public voice and psychology its first great synthesis. He added the stream of consciousness, the pragmatic theory of truth (however contested), the will-to-believe doctrine, and radical empiricism to the permanent stock of philosophy, and he made the study of religious experience a respectable empirical enterprise. He is also, sentence for sentence, among the most readable great philosophers in English in any century — a contribution to clarity that should not be underrated.

## Impact and legacy

Through Dewey, pragmatism flowed into education, law, and democratic theory; through the *Varieties*, into psychology of religion; through the *Principles*, into functionalist psychology and, via the stream of consciousness, into modernist literature. His serious readership in Russell and Wittgenstein as well as in continental figures like Bergson places him at a crossroads of traditions — one of very few philosophers read carefully by all of them.

## Criticisms and debates

Russell's objection remains the sharpest: the pragmatic truth theory, he argued, confuses a belief's truth with its effects, and would make the existence of (his example) Santa Claus a matter of the belief's pleasant consequences. James's defenders reply that his constraints — agreement with reality, coherence with the stock of truths, verifiability in leading — were always part of the doctrine, and point to *The Meaning of Truth* as evidence; the dispute over whether the reply succeeds is still live. The will to believe invites the charge of wishful thinking; James's restriction to live, forced, momentous, undecidable options is the standing answer, accepted by some as principled and by others as unstable. Peirce's own distance — he renamed his doctrine "pragmaticism" to escape Jamesian "kidnappers" — opens the scholarly question of whose pragmatism is the genuine article.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Pragmatic truth is not "whatever is comforting is true"; verification, reality's resistance, and consistency with prior truths are built in.
- The will to believe covers only a narrow class of options (live, forced, momentous, evidentially undecidable) — not belief against evidence.
- James was not a crude anti-intellectual; he was a working scientist-philosopher policing the borders of both.
- The *Varieties* is a study of experience, not an endorsement of every doctrine experienced.

## What to read first

1. *Pragmatism*, Lectures 2 ("What Pragmatism Means") and 6 ("Pragmatism's Conception of Truth").
2. "The Will to Believe" — the doctrine in its author's most careful form.
3. *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, the lectures on conversion and saintliness, in any order.
