# Niccolò Machiavelli

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1469–1527
- **Place:** Florence, Italy
- **School/tradition:** Renaissance humanism; founder of modern political realism and a major republican theorist
- **Known for:** Analyzing politics as it actually works rather than as it ought to work — virtù against fortuna, the "effectual truth," and a deep argument, in the *Discourses*, that republics with armed, participating citizens outlast and outfight principalities

**Responds to:** [Plato](../01-ancient/plato.md) · **Answered by:** [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md), [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md)

## Biography

Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469, into a respectable but not wealthy family, at the height of the Italian Renaissance. He received a humanist education — Latin, rhetoric, the Roman historians — and grew up in a peninsula divided among competing city-states, the Papal States, and foreign powers (France, Spain, the Empire) that treated Italy as their battlefield.

His career began in catastrophe. In 1494 the Medici were expelled from Florence and a republic restored; in 1498 Machiavelli, aged twenty-nine, was appointed secretary of the Second Chancery and of the Ten of War — effectively a senior civil servant and roving diplomat. For fourteen years he was sent on missions to the courts of France, the Empire, the Papacy, and Cesare Borgia, the ruthless son of Pope Alexander VI whose campaign to carve out a state in the Romagna Machiavelli watched at close quarters in 1502–03. These years were his real education: he saw power exercised by men who lied, struck first, and survived, and by men who trusted to law and luck and fell.

His most practical project was a citizen militia. Convinced that mercenary soldiers — Italy's norm — were useless or treacherous, he persuaded the republic to raise a Florentine militia and became its secretary in 1506. It had early successes (notably against Pisa in 1509), but in 1512 Spanish troops shattered it at Prato, the republic collapsed, and the Medici returned.

Machiavelli was dismissed, then arrested in 1513 on suspicion of conspiracy, tortured with the strappado, and released in a general amnesty when a Medici pope was elected. Exiled to his small farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina, he wrote the works that made him immortal. In a famous letter to his friend Francesco Vettori (10 December 1513) he described his days of idleness and his evenings, when he changed into court dress and entered "the ancient courts of ancient men" — his books — conversing with the Roman historians. Out of that enforced leisure came *The Prince* (written 1513, dedicated first to Giuliano de' Medici, then, after Giuliano's death, to Lorenzo di Piero de' Medici, Duke of Urbino) and the *Discourses on Livy* (written c. 1513–19), addressed to young Florentine republicans. He spent his remaining years angling, with partial success, for rehabilitation: he wrote the *Art of War* (1521), a history of Florence on Medici commission, and comedies including *The Mandrake*. In 1527 the Medici were expelled again and the republic restored — but Machiavelli, now tainted as a Medici collaborator, was passed over for his old post. He died a few weeks later, on 21 June 1527. *The Prince* was published only after his death, in 1532.

## Major theories and concepts

### The effectual truth

In Chapter 15 of *The Prince* Machiavelli announces his break with the tradition: he will follow "the effectual truth of the thing" (*verità effettuale della cosa*) rather than its imagination. Many writers, he says, have imagined republics and principalities that have never been seen or known to exist; but how we live is so far from how we ought to live that a ruler who studies only the *ought* "learns his ruin rather than his preservation." This is not a celebration of wickedness. It is a methodological claim: political theory must start from human beings as they are — "ungrateful, fickle, pretenders and dissemblers, evaders of danger, eager for gain," as Chapter 17 puts it — and from the pressures of necessity, not from ideal commonwealths like Plato's.

### Virtù and fortuna

The central pair of his thought. **Fortuna** is fortune: the stream of events no one controls — floods, invasions, the deaths of popes, the accidents of timing. In Chapter 25 of *The Prince* he estimates fortune governs roughly half our actions, leaving the other half to us. **Virtù** is not "virtue" in the moral sense; it is the excellence that answers fortune — energy, skill, decisiveness, adaptability, the capacity to found, to strike at the right moment, to read the times and change with them. His image is hydraulic: fortune is a flooding river; in calm weather men can build dikes and embankments, so that when the flood comes it is channelled rather than catastrophic. The political agent cannot abolish contingency, but preparation and boldness shift the odds. A recurring tragedy in Machiavelli is the man whose virtù fits one set of times and not another: the cautious succeed while caution suits the moment and are ruined when boldness is needed, because, as he says, men cannot easily change their natures.

### The Prince: cruelty, fear, and the fox and the lion

*The Prince* asks how a *new* prince — one without hereditary legitimacy — acquires and holds a state. Its notorious advice is more analytical than gleeful. A prince must learn "how not to be good" and use that knowledge as necessity requires, while taking care to *appear* merciful, faithful, and religious, because men judge by appearances and outcomes. He should be both lion and fox: the lion to frighten wolves, the fox to recognize snares. Cruelty, if used at all, should be committed "at a stroke" at the beginning, for reasons of security, and then converted into benefits; injuries repeated over time keep hatred alive. On the famous question, Machiavelli concludes that if one must choose, it is safer to be feared than loved — love is held by obligation, which men break; fear is held by dread of punishment, which never abandons — provided the prince avoids being *hated*, above all by keeping his hands off his subjects' property and women. Underneath the shock tactics is a functional argument: in a world of armed rivals, the qualities that make a man good in private life can destroy a state and everyone depending on it.

### The Discourses: republicanism and productive conflict

The *Discourses on the First Ten Books of Livy* is the longer, arguably greater work, and its politics differ in emphasis: not how one man seizes power, but how a republic stays free and strong for centuries, as Rome did. Its most original claim is that Rome's internal conflicts were a source of strength, not weakness. In Book I, Chapter 4, Machiavelli argues that the tumults between the plebs and the Senate produced the laws and institutions that guarded Roman liberty — the disunion everyone condemns was the first cause of Rome's freedom. Where *The Prince* treats the multitude as material to be managed, the *Discourses* hold that the people, properly ordered by laws, are wiser and more constant than a prince, and are the proper guard of liberty because their interest is simply not to be dominated.

Other key themes follow: republics need periodic "returns to beginnings" — renewals of founding fear and discipline — to purge corruption; religion is politically indispensable as social cement (he praises Numa, Rome's second king, above Romulus for this); and a free city must expand or decay, because standing still invites attack.

### Citizen arms

Running through both works is the maxim that good laws require good arms, and good arms must be one's own. Mercenaries and auxiliaries are "useless and dangerous" (*The Prince*, Chapters 12–13). The citizen militia of the *Discourses* and the *Art of War* is the military form of republican politics: a people that defends itself cannot be ruled against its will.

## Key works

- *The Prince* (written 1513; published 1532) — Short, dense, shocking advice to a new prince; read alongside, not instead of, the *Discourses*.
- *Discourses on Livy* (written c. 1513–19; published 1531) — The republican masterpiece: Rome as model, conflict as productive, liberty as non-domination.
- *The Art of War* (1521) — The only major prose work published in his lifetime; dialogue arguing for citizen armies, drawing on Roman practice.
- *Florentine Histories* (written 1520–25; presented 1525, some accounts May 1526) — Commissioned by the Medici; a brilliant, pointed history of Florence's factionalism.
- *The Mandrake* (performed c. 1518) — A comedy of deception and desire that mirrors his political themes; still performed.
- *Letters*, especially to Vettori (10 December 1513) — The single best short route into his mind and working method.

## In their own words

> Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less. I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous.

— Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince* XXV, trans. W. K. Marriott (1908; public domain)

Here is the fortuna/virtù pair in Machiavelli's own image. Fortune governs half our actions — but notice the practical turn: a river in flood cannot be resisted *now*, yet dikes and barriers can be built in fair weather. Virtù is exactly that preparation — foresight and energy applied before the crisis. The passage is the antidote to reading Machiavelli as a fatalist: chance is real, and still not an excuse.

## Contributions

Machiavelli effectively founded the modern study of politics as an autonomous activity, with its own logic, distinct from ethics and theology — Leo Strauss called him the founder of modern political philosophy, and even those who resist that verdict grant the rupture. He gave political theory a durable vocabulary (virtù, fortuna, the effectual truth), relaunched republicanism as a serious research program, insisted that institutions and conflict — not just rulers' character — determine political outcomes, and made the armed citizen central to the theory of free states. His separation of political analysis from moral prescription, whatever one thinks of it, is the move political science still lives with.

## Impact and legacy

The reception was immediate and violent. The Church placed his works on the Index of Prohibited Books (1559); Elizabethan drama made "Machiavel" a stock villain, and "Old Nick" — a nickname for the Devil — was popularly linked to his first name. A counter-tradition began just as early: Spinoza called him "most wise," and James Harrington, Algernon Sidney, and the English republicans mined the *Discourses*. Rousseau declared that *The Prince*, read properly, was "the book of republicans" — a satire or a warning exposing how princes rule. The American founders read him, directly and through the republican tradition, on factions, citizen arms, and the corruption of standing armies. In the twentieth century, Gramsci recast the Prince as the modern political party; Isaiah Berlin argued Machiavelli exposed a genuine clash between Christian and pagan moral worlds; Quentin Skinner and J. G. A. Pocock's *The Machiavellian Moment* (1975) restored the *Discourses* to the center of his thought; and contemporary republican theorists of freedom as non-domination (Pettit, Skinner) claim direct descent. "Machiavellian" meanwhile entered psychology as a personality trait — a fate the theorist of appearances might have appreciated.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Is *The Prince* sincere, satirical, or a job application?** It was written by a dismissed republican official hoping for Medici employment. Readings range from straightforward counsel, to a trap exposing tyranny, to a republican warning disguised as advice. The dedication's shift and the book's final chapter — an exhortation to liberate Italy from foreign domination — keep all three readings alive.
- **One thinker or two?** Whether the authoritarian *Prince* and the republican *Discourses* express one coherent theory (different remedies for different situations: founding vs. maintaining) or a real tension in Machiavelli is the classic scholarly debate; the current consensus leans to coherence, with the *Discourses* as the fuller statement.
- **The moral verdict.** Bertrand Russell called *The Prince* "a handbook for gangsters"; others reply that Machiavelli's harsh advice is bounded by the aim of stable, lawful order and that he consistently condemns mere cruelty and Agathocles-style criminal success. Whether his realism is an honest reckoning with necessity or a licence for evil remains genuinely contested.
- **Strauss vs. the contextualists.** Strauss read him as a deliberate teacher of evil who lowered politics' aim from virtue to comfort and glory; the Cambridge School reads him within Renaissance humanist conventions. The dispute is as much about how to read old texts as about Machiavelli himself.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"The end justifies the means"** is not a quotation and flattens him: Machiavelli judges some means (sustained cruelty, seizing subjects' property) as self-defeating, and in the *Discourses* repeatedly distinguishes excusable founding violence from tyranny.
- ***The Prince* alone is not his political philosophy.** Reading it without the *Discourses* is like knowing Rousseau only by a pamphlet; his considered preference is for republics.
- **Virtù does not mean virtue.** Translating it as moral goodness makes Chapters 15–18 of *The Prince* sound hypocritical rather than analytical.
- **He was not a theorist of dictatorship for its own sake.** Even in *The Prince*, success is measured by a stable state that outlives its founder — and he treats glory, not domination, as the great prize.

## What to read first

1. The Vettori letter (1513) and *The Prince*, Chapters 15–18 and 25 — the effectual truth, fear and love, fortune (an hour's reading, in Mansfield's or Bull's translation).
2. *Discourses*, Book I, Chapters 2–6 and 58 — Rome's constitution, the productive tumults, the wisdom of the multitude.
3. *The Prince* entire, then *Discourses* Book I in full — now the two halves of the thought are visible.
4. Skinner's *Machiavelli: A Very Short Introduction* or Viroli's biography — to place him in the humanist and Florentine context.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the *virtù*/*fortuna* argument from the river image in *The Prince* XXV: fortune governs roughly half our actions, yet dikes built in calm weather change the outcome. Why is this neither fatalism nor a claim about moral virtue?
2. Press the *Prince*-versus-*Discourses* objection: how can one thinker advise a prince to be feared and praise the people as liberty's guard? Build the coherence reply — different remedies for founding and maintaining — and test it against both texts.
3. Compare Machiavelli's “effectual truth” with Plato's imagined republic: what does each think political theory loses by starting from how people ought to live?
4. Apply *Discourses* I.4 to a modern state suppressing noisy protests in the name of order: when, by Machiavelli's criteria, do tumults produce liberty-guarding laws rather than corruption, and what institutional channels would he demand?
