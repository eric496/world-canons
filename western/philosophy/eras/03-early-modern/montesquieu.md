# Montesquieu

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1689–1755
- **Place:** Born at the Château de La Brède, near Bordeaux, France; long periods in Paris
- **School/tradition:** Enlightenment; comparative political sociology and liberal constitutional theory
- **Known for:** The separation of powers — legislative, executive, and judicial — as the institutional condition of political liberty, and for founding a comparative science of laws shaped by climate, commerce, religion, and custom

**Responds to:** Roman history and the English constitution, his comparative materials — no single predecessor figure has an entry in this repo · **Answered by:** [Hume](david-hume.md), [Adam Smith](adam-smith.md)

## Biography

Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de La Brède et de Montesquieu, was born in 1689 into the *noblesse de robe*, the judicial aristocracy of Bordeaux. Orphaned of his mother at seven, he was educated by Oratorian fathers at Juilly, studied law, and in 1714 became a counsellor in the Parlement of Bordeaux; two years later he inherited from his uncle the office of *président à mortier*, one of the parlement's senior judgeships, together with the Montesquieu name and estates. He sold the office in 1726, having found judicial routine dull — but the decade inside a great law court gave him an insider's knowledge of institutions that few philosophers have possessed.

His literary fame came first, and anonymously. The *Persian Letters* (1721), published in Amsterdam with a false imprint, purported to be the correspondence of two Persian travelers in Paris, Usbek and Rica, whose baffled reports on French customs — the Pope as a magician, the king as another, the stock-jobbing mania of John Law's System then collapsing — turned the foreigner's gaze into an instrument of satire and comparative sociology. It was a sensation, and it opened the salons to him; election to the Académie française followed in 1728, after some diplomatic repair of its author's reputation for impiety.

Montesquieu then did what he advised: he traveled. From 1728 to 1731 he toured Austria, Italy, the German states, the Netherlands, and England, where he spent some eighteen months observing a political system that fascinated him — attending Parliament, frequenting opposition circles around Bolingbroke, and filling notebooks. Back at La Brède, among his vines (he took the wine trade seriously, and lobbied for it), he wrote in deliberate stages: *Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and of Their Decline* (1734), a masterpiece of compressed historical explanation; then twenty years of drafting, dictating (his eyesight failed progressively), and revising the enormous work of his life, *The Spirit of the Laws*, published at Geneva in 1748 — also anonymously, and promptly placed on the Church's Index (1751), despite which, or partly because of which, it went through edition after edition. He defended it in print (1750) with wit rather than retreat. He died in Paris in 1755, blind and celebrated, leaving the *Spirit* to become the most influential work of political theory of the century.

## Major theories and concepts

### The spirit of the laws: a science of variety

*Laws*, Montesquieu begins, "in their broadest meaning, are the necessary relations deriving from the nature of things." Every society's laws are a *system of relations*: to its form of government, its climate and terrain, its people's livelihood, religion, wealth, numbers, commerce, manners (*moeurs*), and customs. The "spirit of the laws" is the whole web of these relations — hence the book's method of explaining institutions comparatively rather than deducing them from abstract principles or a state of nature. A law wise in Holland may be ruinous in Turkey; the legislator's art is fit, not uniformity. This is why he is often called a founder of sociology and of comparative law: explanation precedes prescription, and institutions are understood by their causes.

### The typology of governments

Book II classifies governments by their *nature* (who holds power) and their *principle* (the passion that keeps them moving):

- **Republic** — power in the people (democracy) or a part of them (aristocracy); principle: **virtue**, the love of laws and country that makes citizens put the public good first. Montesquieu is candid that this is a demanding, almost monastic requirement.
- **Monarchy** — rule by one according to fixed laws; principle: **honor**, the code of rank and distinction that makes ambition serve the public good "even when it seems to pursue only its own." Monarchy needs intermediary powers — the nobility, the courts — as channels and brakes.
- **Despotism** — rule by one without law, by will and caprice; principle: **fear**. It is corruption built into a structure, and Montesquieu treats it as the standing alternative into which decayed regimes slide.

Each type has laws appropriate to it — education, criminal law, luxury, inheritance — and each has its characteristic mode of corruption: democracy dies by the loss of virtue or by the "spirit of extreme equality"; monarchy by the destruction of its intermediary bodies; despotism is corrupt by nature from the start.

### Liberty and the separation of powers

Political liberty, for Montesquieu, is not doing what one wants; in his famous formulation (XI.3), liberty is "the right to do everything the laws permit" — and, psychologically, "that tranquillity of spirit which comes from the opinion each one has of his security." Security requires that power not be abused, and "constant experience shows us that every man invested with power is apt to abuse it... To prevent this abuse, it is necessary from the very nature of things that power should be a check to power."

Book XI, Chapter 6, "Of the Constitution of England," draws the consequence: in a free state the **legislative** power (making laws), the **executive** (executing them, including foreign affairs), and the **judicial** power (punishing crimes and judging disputes) must not be united in the same hands. If the legislator executes, laws become tyrannical instruments; if the judge legislates, the subject's life and liberty are exposed to arbitrary control. The judiciary in particular should be, as nearly as possible, invisible and null as a standing body — drawn from the people, temporary, bound to the letter of the law — so that citizens fear the magistracy's office, not the magistrate. The executive, unitary and swift, checks the legislature by veto; the legislature checks the executive by oversight and by controlling supply; the legislature itself is bicameral, with a hereditary or aristocratic body and a representative body balancing each other. This was an idealized England — Montesquieu knew its cabinet and parties imperfectly disentangled — but the analytical point outgrew the example: liberty is an *engineering* problem, solved by dividing and counterweighting power.

### Climate, terrain, and the general spirit

Books XIV–XVIII argue that climate and soil shape physiology, temperament, and hence law: cold climates (he claimed) brace the fibers and favor vigor and liberty; hot climates relax them and favor passivity and despotism; Asia's vast plains sustain empires, Europe's divided geography sustains moderate states; barren Attica bred a republic, fertile Laconia a different regime. These chapters were the most criticized part of the book even in his lifetime, and their crude physiological mechanism has not aged well. Read carefully, though, the theory is probabilistic and multi-causal, and its deepest claims are institutional: whatever nature proposes, "mankind are influenced by various causes: by the climate, by religion, by the laws, by the maxims of government, by precedents, morals, and manners; whence is formed a **general spirit**" (XIX.4) — and bad legislators are those who encourage the vices of the climate rather than counteract them. He also drew on these books for a striking argument against slavery (XV.5), ironically dismantling its justifications one by one: "I do not know if my spirit or my heart is dictating this paragraph" — the irony is the condemnation.

### Commerce and douceur

Montesquieu is an early theorist of *doux commerce*: commerce "cures destructive prejudices," polishes manners, and inclines nations to peace, because trading partners need each other (XX.1–2). Republics and commercial states can sustain liberty without the heroic virtue of ancient republics, since interest does part of virtue's work — an idea that runs forward to Hume, Smith, and the liberal tradition. He is nobody's simple free-trader, though: luxury, land banks, and demographic policy are all assessed by fit with the regime type and its principle.

## Key works

- *Persian Letters* (1721) — Epistolary satire and comparative social observation; the foreigner's-eye view as method. Delightful and still sharp.
- *Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and of Their Decline* (1734) — Institutions, not heroes, explain Rome's rise and fall; the *Spirit* in embryo.
- *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) — The masterwork: typology of governments, separation of powers (Book XI), climate and the general spirit, commerce, slavery, and law reform. Vast, digressive, and designed to be raided as much as read straight through.
- *Defense of The Spirit of the Laws* (1750) — His reply to critics; a model of polemical irony and a useful guide to the book's intentions.
- *Pensées* (posthumous notebooks) — Aphorisms and drafts showing the workshop.

## Contributions

Montesquieu founded comparative politics as an empirical discipline: laws explained by the total system of a society's conditions, the ancestor of sociology (Durkheim wrote his Latin thesis on him) and of comparative constitutional law. Politically, his separation of powers is the single most consequential institutional idea of the modern age — the proximate source, more than Locke, for the architecture of written constitutions. He also reframed liberty itself: not collective self-rule alone, but individual security under law, protected by structures that work even when virtue fails.

## Impact and legacy

The *Spirit of the Laws* was everywhere in the second half of the eighteenth century. Catherine the Great plagiarised it in her *Nakaz*; Beccaria absorbed its penal moderation (punishments proportioned to crimes, torture attacked); the Scottish Enlightenment — Hume, Smith, Ferguson — worked in its comparative mode. Its most famous afterlife is American: the Federalists cited Montesquieu more than any other modern author; Madison in *Federalist* No. 47 calls him "the oracle who is always consulted and cited on this subject" while refining the doctrine (partial agency and mutual control rather than total separation), and the Constitution's three branches, bicameral legislature, veto, and independent judiciary are the doctrine made durable. Through the American and then the French revolutionary debates, separation of powers became the default grammar of constitutionalism worldwide. The [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/)'s assessment matches the record: his account of the distribution of powers had an enormous impact on liberal political theory and on the framers in particular.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Was the "English constitution" a fantasy?** Critics then and since note that eighteenth-century England fused executive and legislature through the ministry far more than XI.6 admits. Defenders reply that the chapter is a model, not a report — but how much of the doctrine survives the correction is a live question in constitutional theory.
- **Climate determinism.** Books XIV–XVIII risk reducing politics to latitude; Voltaire and others mocked, and later readers have found in them a machinery for ranking peoples. The debate: crass determinism, or an early, hedged environmental sociology whose real weight falls on counteracting legislation? The text supports parts of both verdicts.
- **Conservative or reformer?** A baron defending intermediary bodies, venal offices, and the parlements can read as an apologist for aristocratic privilege against royal (and popular) reform; against this stands his assault on slavery, torture, and religious persecution, and his theory was taken up by revolutionaries. Which Montesquieu is the real one remains a scholarly industry.
- **Separation of powers in practice.** Modern critics (parliamentary systems, the administrative state) argue the doctrine describes no working government and was never meant to forbid overlap; Madison's own version — checks through shared powers — may be the more defensible inheritance, and attribution between the two is still argued.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **Separation of powers is not his only, or even his largest, theme.** Most of the *Spirit* is the comparative sociology of law; the separation-of-powers argument is a single chapter (XI.6) of a 31-book work.
- **He is not a democrat in the modern sense.** Democratic republics rest on a virtue he admired and thought rare and fragile; his practical hopes lay with moderated monarchy and, increasingly, the English model.
- **"Climate made them do it" caricatures the theory.** Causes combine into a general spirit, and legislation exists precisely to counteract bad natural tendencies.
- **He does not preach revolution.** Reform, for Montesquieu, must respect the spirit of an existing society; sudden total change is a legislator's classic error.

## What to read first

1. *Persian Letters*, Letters 11–14 (the Troglodytes), 24, and 37 — the method and the wit in a few pages.
2. *The Spirit of the Laws*, Books I–III (the definitions and the typology) and Book XI entire, especially Chapter 6 — the core doctrine, in Cohler/Miller/Stone's Cambridge translation.
3. Book XIX (the general spirit) and XV.5 (the ironic chapter on slavery) — to see the sociology and the moral edge together.
4. *Considerations on the Romans*, first half — if you want his historical mode before tackling the whole *Spirit*.
5. Judith Shklar's *Montesquieu* (Past Masters) or the SEP entry — orientation on the debates above.
