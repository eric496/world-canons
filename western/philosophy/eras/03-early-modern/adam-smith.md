# Adam Smith

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1723–1790
- **Place:** Scotland; held chairs of logic and moral philosophy at Glasgow
- **School/tradition:** Moral philosopher of the Scottish Enlightenment
- **Known for:** *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), which grounds morality in sympathy disciplined by an "impartial spectator," and *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776), the founding text of classical economics — which Smith himself understood as one part of a larger science of human nature and society

**Responds to:** [Hume](david-hume.md), [Montesquieu](montesquieu.md) · **Answered by:** [J. S. Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

## Biography
Smith was born in Kirkcaldy, a small Fife port town, baptized in June 1723, a few months after his father's death. Raised by his mother, he studied at the University of Glasgow under Francis Hutcheson, whose lectures in moral philosophy set his direction, and then spent six years at Balliol College, Oxford, which he found intellectually deadening and left without a degree. Returning to Scotland, he lectured in Edinburgh before being appointed professor at Glasgow in 1751, first of logic, then of moral philosophy — the chair he held while writing the *Theory of Moral Sentiments*, warmly received on publication in 1759.

In 1764 he resigned to tutor the young Duke of Buccleuch on a European tour, a post carrying a generous lifetime pension that bought him independence. In Paris he met Voltaire, Quesnay, and Turgot; the tour ended abruptly in 1766, and Smith spent most of the next decade at home in Kirkcaldy drafting the *Wealth of Nations*. Published in 1776, it made him famous across Europe. He spent his last years as a customs commissioner in Edinburgh, revising both books — the sixth edition of the *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1790) contains major additions written in full knowledge of the *Wealth of Nations*. On his deathbed he ordered his unfinished manuscripts burned, a loss scholars still regret; the surviving lecture notes, published posthumously, show that his Glasgow course had unified ethics, law, government, and economics in a single framework.

## Major theories and concepts

### Sympathy and fellow-feeling
The *Theory of Moral Sentiments* opens with a claim against pure egoism: however selfish man may be supposed, "there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others." Smith's mechanism is **sympathy** — not pity, but the imaginative fellow-feeling by which we place ourselves in another's situation and feel, in weaker form, what we conceive they feel. Moral judgment arises from a match or mismatch: we approve of someone's grief, anger, or joy when, imagining ourselves in their place, we find our own sympathetic emotion coincides with theirs. Because sympathy is projective, it is fallible — we sympathise more readily with joy than sorrow, with the near than the distant, and with the rich and powerful more than the poor, a deformation Smith flags as "the great and most universal cause of the corruption of our moral sentiments."

### The impartial spectator
To correct these biases, Smith introduces his central device: the **impartial spectator**, the imagined standpoint of a fully informed, disinterested observer within the self — "the man within the breast." Conscience is the habit of judging our own conduct as this spectator would. We learn the standpoint socially, by seeing ourselves through others' eyes, then internalize it so that we can judge ourselves even when actual spectators are fooled or absent. General moral rules, for Smith, crystallize out of repeated spectator judgments. The device lets him explain how morality can be both socially formed and genuinely self-critical, and why the desire at morality's root is not merely to be praised but to be *praiseworthy*.

### Division of labor, markets, and the invisible hand
The *Wealth of Nations* begins with the division of labor: a pin factory's workers, each specializing in one operation, produce vastly more than the same workers making whole pins. Specialization raises productivity through dexterity, saved time, and the invention of machinery — but Smith also warns, in Book V, that a worker confined to a few simple operations risks becoming "as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become," an evil public education must counter. Exchange arises from a propensity to "truck, barter, and exchange," and markets coordinate self-interested action: it is not from the benevolence of the butcher, brewer, or baker that we expect dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. Three times across his works Smith uses the phrase **invisible hand** for such unintended social benefits of individual action. His policy conclusions follow: restrict monopoly and mercantile privilege, let prices and wages settle competitively, and confine the state to defense, justice, public works, and education. Crucially, his subject throughout is not greed but the whole institutional order — law, custom, and government — within which self-interest operates.

## Key works
- *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759; 6th edition 1790) — sympathy, the impartial spectator, virtue, and the corruption of morals by wealth and rank.
- *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776) — division of labor, price formation, the critique of mercantilism, taxation, colonies, and the duties of the state.
- *Lectures on Jurisprudence* (student notes, published 1896/1978) — the Glasgow lectures linking property, government, and the four-stages theory of society.
- *Essays on Philosophical Subjects* (1795, posthumous) — including his "History of Astronomy," a study of how scientific systems arise to soothe the imagination.

## Contributions
Smith created modern economics as a systematic discipline while simultaneously producing one of the eighteenth century's most sophisticated moral psychologies. The impartial spectator remains a live model of conscience and moral learning, and his analysis of unintended social order — how institutions channel private action into public outcomes — shaped sociology and political theory as much as economics.

## Impact and legacy
The *Wealth of Nations* became the canonical text of classical economics, shaping nineteenth-century liberal policy and every subsequent debate about markets and the state; Ricardo, Mill, and Marx all built on or against it. The *Theory of Moral Sentiments* was eclipsed for a century by its sibling's fame, then revived by twentieth-century scholarship as a major work in its own right, influencing virtue ethics, moral psychology, and Amartya Sen's work on justice and identity.

## Criticisms and debates
The most famous debate is the so-called **"Adam Smith problem"** (*das Adam Smith Problem*), posed by nineteenth-century German scholars: the *Theory* seems to found morality on sympathy and benevolence, the *Wealth* on self-interest — contradiction? The modern consensus largely dissolves the problem: self-interest in Smith is one motive among several, sympathy is a mechanism of judgment rather than benevolence itself, both books were revised together across his life, and the *Wealth* presupposes the legal and moral framework the *Theory* describes. Genuine debates remain: how far his invisible hand was a considered doctrine or a passing metaphor; whether his labor theory of value survives his own qualifications; and how egalitarian his economics really is, given his sharp remarks on landlords, merchants' conspiracies against the public, and the stunting effects of repetitive labor.

## Misreadings to avoid
- Smith did not teach that selfishness is good or sufficient: markets in his account run inside justice — the "laws of justice" are the pillars without which society crumbles — and he distrusted businessmen's political influence.
- The invisible hand is a minor phrase (used once in each major work), not the title of a theory.
- The *Wealth of Nations* is not a brief for a minimal state: Smith assigns government defense, justice, infrastructure, and education, and supports some interventions, including public schooling and usury laws.

## What to read first
Start with *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part I, for sympathy and propriety, then *Wealth of Nations* Book I, chapters 1–3 (division of labor and the extent of the market) and Book IV's attack on mercantilism. Reading the two books together is the surest cure for the caricature.
