## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1668–1744
- **Place:** Naples, Kingdom of Naples (then under Spanish, then Austrian, rule)
- **School/tradition:** Anti-Cartesian humanism; founder of the philosophy of history and an early theorist of culture
- **Known for:** The principle *verum factum* — we can truly know only what we have made — applied to the "civil world" of nations: since humans made history, history can be known from inside, through language, myth, law, and custom. His *New Science* traces all nations through three ages — of gods, of heroes, of men — in cycles (*corsi e ricorsi*)

**Responds to:** [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Francis Bacon](francis-bacon.md) · **Answered by:** [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

## Biography

Giambattista Vico was born in Naples in 1668, above his father's bookshop on the Via San Biagio dei Librai, the seventh of eight children. A childhood fall fractured his skull and left him, the doctors said, likely to die or grow up an idiot; he instead grew up, by his own account, with a melancholic temperament and a ferocious appetite for self-education. He attended Jesuit schools irregularly, read law at the University of Naples, and spent the years 1686–95 as a private tutor in a castle at Vatolla in the Cilento — his real university, where he worked through Latin poetry, Roman law, and the history of philosophy, and formed his lifelong heroes: Plato and Tacitus, and among the moderns Francis Bacon and Grotius (he later added the jurist Gravina as a fourth master).

In 1699 he won, by competition, the chair of rhetoric at the University of Naples — a modest post he held for over forty years, badly paid, while supporting a growing family and hoping vainly for the more prestigious chair of law. His inaugural orations (1699–1708) defended the union of eloquence and wisdom against the new fashion for Cartesian method. In *On the Most Ancient Wisdom of the Italians* (1710), written against Descartes, he first stated his great principle, *verum ipsum factum* — the true is precisely what is made. His *Universal Law* (1720–22) tried to derive law and history from first principles; the project failed commercially but generated his breakthrough.

The *New Science* first appeared in 1725 — a compressed, difficult version, issued after a grander work was abandoned when funding fell through. Vico rewrote it wholesale for the second edition of 1730 and again for the third (1744), which he was correcting when he died in January 1744. The book was received locally with respect but made little impression abroad; its author died an admired, marginal professor in a provincial capital of European thought. His *Autobiography* (written 1725–28, at the request of Venetian patrons of learning, and continued to 1731) — a history of his own mind modelled on, and against, Descartes's *Discourse on Method* — remains one of the great intellectual autobiographies, and a sly act of self-canonization by a man convinced posterity would need a guide to him.

It did. Vico was rediscovered in the nineteenth century: Michelet translated the *New Science* into French in 1827 and declared he had no other master; Italian idealists (Croce above all) rebuilt his reputation; and in the twentieth century Isaiah Berlin made him, for English readers, the great counter-Enlightenment founder of historicism and pluralism. The man who wrote almost unread in 1744 is now routinely placed among the founders of the human sciences.

## Major theories and concepts

### Verum factum: we know what we make

Descartes had argued that clear and distinct ideas are the mark of truth, and mathematics — transparent to the mind — the model of knowledge. Vico inverted the ranking with an old scholastic-sounding principle: **verum ipsum factum**, "the true is the made itself." To *know* something completely is to know how it is put together, the elements and the manner of their combination — and only the maker has that knowledge. God, who made nature, alone knows nature fully; we dissect it from outside. Mathematics we *can* know demonstratively, precisely because we invented its objects and rules. But the inversion has a decisive second half: the **civil world** — languages, laws, institutions, myths, customs — "has certainly been made by men, and its principles are therefore to be found within the modifications of our own human mind" (*New Science*, §331). The world of nations, disdained by philosophers as a chaos of accident, is in fact the thing most knowable by us. History is not a lesser knowledge than physics; by the strictest criterion, it is better placed. This is the founding charter of the humanities as sciences in their own right.

### The New Science and the ideal eternal history

The *Scienza Nuova* (1744) proposes a science of the "common nature of nations": every people, independently, runs through the same sequence of institutions — Vico calls it an **ideal eternal history** (*storia ideal eterna*), a pattern "in time" traced by actual histories. He claims to find it by combining **philosophy** (reasoning about the true) with **philology** (the study of the certain: languages, texts, artefacts, records) — each certifying the other. His evidence is characteristically concrete: etymologies, Roman law, Homer, burial customs, marriage rites, heraldry. And his governing intuition is **providence**: men intend narrow ends — ferocity, avarice, ambition — yet are led, through institutions, to outcomes none intended: "men mean to gratify their bestial lusts and abandon their offspring, and thereby they establish the chastity of marriages, whence the families arise" (§1108). The resemblance to later doctrines of unintended order (Smith's invisible hand, Hegel's cunning of reason) is not an accident of my summary; Vico is their acknowledged ancestor.

### The three ages: gods, heroes, men

Every nation passes through three ages, each with its own nature, customs, language, law, and mode of thought:

1. **The age of gods** — primitive humans, terrified by thunder, imagine the sky as a great animated body, Jove, and govern by divine auspices and oracles. Families are ruled by fathers who are priests and kings; thought is entirely **poetic**, reality read as signs from gods.
2. **The age of heroes** — aristocratic commonwealths: a noble order (the "heroes," claiming divine descent) monopolises land, marriage, law, and religion against the plebeian *famoli*. Its language is emblematic and symbolic — heraldry, heroic song; its law is secret, oral, and savagely exact.
3. **The age of men** — popular commonwealths and then monarchies (which Vico treats as equalising all subjects under one ruler): reason recognizes all men as equal by nature; language becomes fully articulate prose; law becomes public, written, and rational.

Correspondingly, the first men were "poets" in the Greek sense — makers. Because they could not form abstract genera, they thought in **poetic characters** (*universali fantastici*), imaginative universals: Achilles is not an illustration of valor, he *is* valor, a concrete image into which all brave deeds are poured. Myth, on this account, is not decorative falsehood but the first form of knowledge — "the first science to be learned," the civil histories of the first peoples (§51, §779). Homer, for Vico, is not an individual genius but the Greek peoples themselves, singing their own institutions into existence across centuries — an anticipation by decades of the "Homeric question" that Wolf made famous in 1795.

### Corsi e ricorsi

History runs in **courses** (*corsi*) — and **recourses** (*ricorsi*). When the age of men decays — through the "barbarism of reflection," Vico's chilling phrase for a learned, calculating cruelty worse than primitive savagery, and through faction, skepticism, and luxury — a society may collapse back into a new barbarism: long centuries of rusticity from which it must climb again, as Europe did after Rome into the Middle Ages, which Vico reads as a *ricorso*: feudal lords as new heroes, Dante as the new age's Homer. Monarchy is the providential brake that can arrest decay before collapse. The pattern is neither a circle of eternal return nor a line of progress: the *ricorso* never repeats the first course exactly, because nations carry memories, languages, and religions (above all Christianity) across the fall.

### The critique of Descartes and of his own age

Vico's polemical target was the Cartesian program: the scorn for history, rhetoric, probability, and erudition; the reduction of education to critical method alone. In *On the Study Methods of Our Time* (1709) he argued that training only the critical faculty starves **ingegno** — ingenium, the inventive, analogical, topic-finding capacity — and produces minds sharp at refuting and sterile at creating. The young, he warned, need the ancients' languages, poetry, history, and eloquence *first*, critique later. It is among the earliest and still among the best statements of the case for humanistic education against a purely analytical curriculum.

## Key works

- *On the Study Methods of Our Time* (1709) — The defense of humanistic education against Cartesian method; the most accessible Vico.
- *On the Most Ancient Wisdom of the Italians* (1710) — First statement of *verum factum*; metaphysics drawn from Latin etymology.
- *Universal Law* (*De universi iuris*, 1720–22) — The transitional system of law and history.
- *New Science* (1725; rewritten 1730; definitive third edition 1744) — The masterwork: principles of the science of nations, the three ages, poetic wisdom, the discovery of the true Homer. Dense, aphoristic, organized in numbered paragraphs — read with help.
- *Autobiography* (1725–31) — His account of his intellectual formation; both evidence and interpretation.

## Contributions

Vico gave the human sciences their epistemological charter (*verum factum* applied to the civil world), invented the philosophy of history as a discipline — before Voltaire, Herder, and Hegel — and modelled a method that fuses philosophy with philology: concepts tested against words, myths, laws, and things. He saw that myth and language are primary evidence about minds unlike our own, that institutions evolve in intelligible sequences, and that historical agents produce orders they never intended. Each of these is now common property of history, anthropology, and social theory; in 1725 all of them were his alone.

## Impact and legacy

The influence was delayed but immense. Michelet's French translation (1827) fed romantic historiography; Marx read him — a footnote in *Capital* cites Vico's insight that human history differs from natural history because we made the one and not the other; Croce's *Philosophy of Giambattista Vico* (1911) made him central to Italian idealism; Joyce built *Finnegans Wake* on the *corsi e ricorsi*. In English, Isaiah Berlin's essays (*Vico and Herder*, 1976) cast him as the first modern thinker to argue that cultures are plural, intelligible from inside, and not rankable on one scale of progress — making Vico a founding father of value pluralism and of the interpretive social sciences. Collingwood absorbed his doctrine that history is re-enactment of past thought; historicism, hermeneutics, and the sociology of knowledge all acknowledge the debt.

## Criticisms and debates

- **System or suggestiveness?** The *New Science*'s pattern is powerful but elastic; critics ask whether any evidence could falsify the three ages, and whether Vico's etymologies — often fanciful — can bear the weight put on them. Defenders reply that the pattern is a heuristic ideal type, not a law, and that his errors are separable from the method.
- **Providence and orthodoxy.** Vico, a sincere Catholic, exempted Hebrew history from his scheme as guided directly by God, and presented providence as theological. Whether his providence is real doctrine, protective camouflage in Inquisition Naples, or an early theory of unintended consequences in religious dress is genuinely unsettled — and changes how modern the system looks.
- **How original, and how read?** Some scholars stress his debts (Bacon, Grotius, Roman law, Neapolitan investigators of nature); Berlin's portrait of a solitary prophet of pluralism has itself been accused of importing twentieth-century liberal concerns into 1744. The corrective literature is now large.
- **Eurocentrism of the pattern.** The "common nature of the nations" was generalized from Graeco-Roman and biblical materials; applying the sequence to China, India, or the Americas strains it — Vico himself hesitated over peoples outside the Mediterranean story.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **Vico is not a relativist for whom every age is as good as another.** He has strong norms — the full humanity of the third age, law's publicity, monarchy's equalising justice — and he ranks the barbarism of reflection below primitive barbarism.
- **The ricorsi are not cycles of eternal return.** History spirals; it does not repeat, because language and religion transmit gains across collapses.
- **Verum factum does not say truth is whatever we invent.** It is a criterion of *knowledge*: we know demonstrably only where we grasp the making. Fiction, which we also "make," fails precisely because its maker knows it as his own arbitrary construction.
- **He was not a neglected madman in his own city.** He held his chair for four decades, had patrons and pupils, and was Naples' official historiographer; the isolation was European, not local.

## What to read first

1. *On the Study Methods of Our Time* (1709) — short, lucid, and the best doorway into his quarrel with Descartes.
2. *New Science*, Book I ("Of the Elements" — the axioms) and §§1–2, 331, 342–45 in the Bergin–Fisch translation — the principles, including *verum factum* and the three ages, in Vico's own numbered paragraphs.
3. The *Autobiography* — to see the method applied to one life, his own.
4. Then *New Science* Books II ("Poetic Wisdom") and IV ("The Course the Nations Run") — with Isaiah Berlin's essay "Vico's Concept of Knowledge" or the [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/) entry open beside you.
