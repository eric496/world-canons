# Émilie du Châtelet

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1706–1749 (Gabrielle Émilie Le Tonnelier de Breteuil, marquise du Châtelet)
- **Place:** France
- **School/tradition:** Mathematician, physicist, and philosopher working at the intersection of Newtonian science and Leibnizian metaphysics
- **Known for:** Her *Institutions de Physique* (1740), her defense of *vis viva* (living force, proportional to mass times velocity squared) against the Cartesian measure of force, and the French translation of Newton's *Principia* — with her own commentary — that remained the standard French version for centuries

**Responds to:** [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md) · **Answered by:** [Kant](immanuel-kant.md)

## Biography
Émilie de Breteuil grew up in a wealthy Parisian household where her father, a court official, unusually encouraged his daughter's education: she learned Latin, mathematics, and several modern languages, alongside the fencing and riding she enjoyed. At eighteen she married the Marquis du Châtelet, a soldier frequently away on campaign, and after an initial period of court life she turned seriously to study, taking lessons from leading mathematicians including Pierre-Louis Maupertuis and Alexis Clairaut.

Around 1733 she began her long relationship with Voltaire. The couple settled at Cirey, her husband's château in Champagne, where they built a laboratory and theater and worked side by side on physics and the popularization of Newton. Both entered the Paris Academy of Sciences' 1738 prize competition on the nature of fire; neither won, but both essays were published by the Academy, and hers included an early suggestion that light and heat rays might differ in kind — a glimpse beyond the visible spectrum.

Her *Institutions de Physique* appeared in 1740, framed as lessons for her son but in fact a systematic treatise engaging Newton, Leibniz, and Descartes. A public dispute with the Academy's secretary, Dortous de Mairan, over the measure of force followed, in which she defended the Leibnizian position with published experiments. In the 1740s she undertook her most demanding project: translating Newton's *Principia Mathematica* into French and adding an algebraic commentary, drawing on Clairaut's help for the mathematics. She completed it while pregnant by a new lover, the poet Saint-Lambert, and died in September 1749, days after giving birth to a daughter — reportedly driven by the premonition that she would not survive, she had worked punishing hours to finish. The translation was published posthumously in 1759.

## Major theories and concepts

### Foundations of physics: hypotheses, certainty, and metaphysics
The opening chapters of the *Institutions* are philosophy of science, not physics. Du Châtelet argues that physics needs metaphysical foundations — principles such as non-contradiction and sufficient reason — because experience alone underdetermines our theories. Against a crude Newtonian suspicion of hypotheses, she defends them as legitimate and necessary tools: a hypothesis earns its keep by explaining phenomena systematically and surviving tests, though it never becomes certain merely by being useful. She distinguishes grades of certainty and warns against both dogmatism and the opposite vice of rejecting every unproven idea. This methodological middle path — empiricism disciplined by rational principles — is her characteristic position, and it lets her combine Newton on gravity with Leibniz on the foundations of mechanics where pure partisans chose sides.

### The vis viva controversy
The sharpest technical dispute of the period asked what quantity is conserved in collisions: the Cartesian measure, mass times velocity (mv, roughly what we now call momentum when direction is handled properly), or Leibniz's *vis viva*, "living force," mass times velocity squared (mv²). Du Châtelet, following Leibniz, argued for mv², and crucially recruited experiment: she publicised Willem 's Gravesande's results in which brass balls dropped into clay sank to depths proportional to the square of their velocity, not to velocity itself — evidence that the "force" of a moving body scales with v². Her published exchange with de Mairan made the case to a wide audience. The modern resolution is that both sides tracked real quantities — momentum and kinetic energy (our ½mv²) — and the dispute partly dissolved once the concepts were distinguished; but du Châtelet's insistence on deciding the question empirically, and her grasp of energy-like conservation, are now seen as genuinely forward-looking.

### The Principia translation and commentary
Du Châtelet's *Principia* is not a bare translation. She rendered Newton's difficult geometric demonstrations into the algebraic language of continental calculus, making the work usable by French mathematicians, and added a commentary summarizing the system of the world and key astronomical results, including work by Clairaut on the shape of the Earth and lunar motion. In her hands translation was an act of scholarship and transmission: she decided what French science needed to assimilate Newton, and built it.

## Key works
- *Dissertation sur la nature et la propagation du feu* (Academy essay of 1738; published in the Academy recueil 1739, separately 1744) — her prize-essay on fire, notable for speculating about radiation beyond visible light.
- *Institutions de Physique* (1740; revised 1742) — her systematic treatise: metaphysical foundations, method, and the physics of matter, motion, gravity, and force.
- *Réponse de Madame la Marquise du Châtelet* (1741) — her published reply to de Mairan in the vis viva dispute.
- *Principes mathématiques de la philosophie naturelle* (1759, posthumous) — her translation of Newton with commentary; the standard French *Principia*.
- *Discours sur le bonheur* (published 1779) — a candid essay on happiness, women, and the passions, written in the 1740s.

## Contributions
Du Châtelet helped move the vis viva debate toward experiment and energy conservation, gave French readers their Newton, and produced one of the period's most philosophically sophisticated defenses of hypotheses and of rational principles within empirical science. She also modelled — in print and in life — rigorous scientific work by a woman in institutions closed to women.

## Impact and legacy
Her Newton anchored French Newtonianism; Kant read and cited the *Institutions*; Lagrange and Laplace worked in a France her translation had equipped. Then her reputation narrowed: through the nineteenth and much of the twentieth century she was remembered mainly as Voltaire's companion, her physics treated as derivative. Scholarship since the late twentieth century has reversed this, restoring her as an original natural philosopher — a reassessment still under way as her manuscripts and correspondence are edited.

## Criticisms and debates
The standing debate concerns originality. Older accounts cast her as translator and popularizer, her metaphysics borrowed from Leibniz via Christian Wolff, her mathematics dependent on Clairaut and Maupertuis. Revisionist scholars reply that collaboration was normal in the period, that her synthesis of Leibnizian principles with Newtonian phenomena is itself an original philosophical position, and that her treatment of hypotheses and of vis viva shows independent judgment — including where she parts from her teachers. A balanced current view credits her as a creative systematizer and critic rather than a mere conduit, while acknowledging her dependence on collaborators for some advanced mathematics.

## Misreadings to avoid
- She was not "Voltaire's mistress who dabbled in science"; the scientific work is substantial, technical, and was recognized by the leading mathematicians of her day.
- Her Newton translation is not a transparent window onto Newton; it is an interpretation, recast in calculus and supplemented with commentary — which is precisely its value.
- She was not simply a Leibnizian or simply a Newtonian; her project was to test each against the other.

## What to read first
Begin with the opening chapters of the *Institutions de Physique* (available in English translation) on method and hypotheses, then her vis viva exchange in excerpt. A modern biography gives the life; her *Discours sur le bonheur* is short and vivid.
