## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1713–1784
- **Place:** Born in Langres, France; lived and worked in Paris
- **School/tradition:** French Enlightenment; materialism and the *Encyclopédie*
- **Known for:** Editing the *Encyclopédie*, the Enlightenment's great machine of useful knowledge, and for a daring materialist philosophy — matter that senses, thinks, and transforms — developed mostly in dialogues he never dared to publish

**Responds to:** [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Locke](john-locke.md) · **Answered by:** [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md)

## Biography

Diderot was born in 1713 in Langres, a cathedral town in Champagne, the son of a prosperous master cutler whose trade he was expected to inherit. Educated by the Jesuits, he went to Paris as a teenager, took a master's degree in 1732, toyed with the law and the priesthood, and instead chose the bohemian life: tutoring, sermon-writing for hire, translation piecework, and hunger. His first substantial job, translating Robert James's *Medicinal Dictionary* (1746–48), trained him in exactly the skills — technical vocabulary, order, cross-reference — that his life's work would need. In 1743 he secretly married Antoinette Champion, a devout seamstress his family disapproved of; of their children only a daughter, Angélique, survived, and her memoirs remain a key source for his life.

His own writing began cautiously and escalated fast. The *Philosophical Thoughts* (1746), still deist in temper, was condemned and burned by the Parlement of Paris. The *Letter on the Blind* (1749) went much further — towards atheism and materialism — and the authorities responded: Diderot spent over three months of 1749 imprisoned in the fortress of Vincennes. It was during his confinement that Rousseau visited him and, on Rousseau's own later account in the *Confessions*, discussed the Dijon Academy's essay question that produced the *Discourse on the Sciences and Arts* — the founding scene, as Rousseau tells it, of both men's careers.

From 1747 Diderot's energies were swallowed by the *Encyclopédie*. The project began modestly, as a French translation of Chambers's *Cyclopaedia*, but with Jean le Rond d'Alembert as co-editor it swelled into a "reasoned dictionary of the sciences, arts and trades" — some 72,000 articles from around 140 contributors across 17 volumes of text (1751–1765) and 11 of plates. The state and Church struck repeatedly: the first two volumes were suspended in 1752, and in 1759, after the scandal over Helvétius and d'Alembert's article "Genève," the royal privilege was revoked; d'Alembert had already resigned, leaving Diderot to finish the text volumes in clandestine defiance. His reward included betrayal: in 1764 he learned that his own publisher, Le Breton, had secretly toned down or cut the boldest passages in the final proofs. Diderot called it a massacre of twenty years' work.

Salvation, of a kind, came from Russia. In 1765 Catherine the Great bought Diderot's library for 15,000 livres — while leaving it in his possession in Paris and paying him an annual salary as its librarian, a famous act of enlightened patronage that secured his daughter's dowry and his old age. He acted as her informal agent in Paris, recommending the sculptor Étienne Maurice Falconet, who went to St Petersburg in 1766 to create the great equestrian statue of Peter the Great; the two men's correspondence ranges over art, glory, and posterity. In 1773–74 Diderot finally made the journey to St Petersburg, talking daily with Catherine about law, education, and reform — conversations both enjoyed, though she concluded that his schemes, magnificent on paper, ignored the realities of governing. He came home through Holland, worked on, and died in Paris in 1784, his most original books still waiting, unpublished, in his desk.

## Major theories and concepts

### The Encyclopédie as a philosophical weapon

The *Encyclopédie* was never a neutral reference work. Its stated aim, in Diderot's own article "Encyclopédie" (1755), was nothing less than "to change the common way of thinking." Knowledge was to be gathered from everywhere — above all from the workshops, whose trades the plates dignified as liberal arts — and arranged alphabetically, a form that quietly demoted theology from queen of the sciences to one heading among others. Diderot's sharpest instrument was the cross-reference (*renvoi*): officially a finding aid, in practice a satirical device that sent readers from safe articles to subversive ones, letting the dictionary argue with itself. Against censorship's letter, the book waged war by structure. Its collaborative scale made a point too: knowledge is a collective, improvable enterprise, not church or academy property.

### Materialism: matter that senses and thinks

Diderot's mature philosophy, developed from the *Letter on the Blind* through the *Thoughts on the Interpretation of Nature* (1754) to the dialogues of 1769, is a thoroughgoing materialism. Against Descartes's two substances, he asked why thought should require an immaterial soul if matter itself can be sensitive. His key move was to treat sensitivity as a general property of matter — inert in a stone or a statue, active in organized living flesh — so that mind is what sufficiently organized matter *does*, not a second thing added to it. Feed a statue, he suggested in the opening dialogue of the *Dream*, grind it, mix it with humus, grow peas in it and eat them, and you have made the passage from marble to feeling a problem of organization, not of magic.

### D'Alembert's Dream: life, mind, and transformation

The fullest statement is *D'Alembert's Dream* (written 1769, published 1830), a trilogy of dialogues in which the physician Théophile de Bordeu explains Diderot's system to a feverish, dreaming d'Alembert and to Julie de Lespinasse. Its famous images still organize the argument: the fertilised egg, mere warm matter that becomes a sensing animal; and the swarm of bees clinging together, Bordeu's model for how a colony of tiny living units can form one sensing organism — the brain, correspondingly, is a web of threads with a spider (the self) poised at its center, itself part of the web. Species, too, are provisional: nature experiments endlessly, monsters are its drafts, and what exists is what proved viable — an evolutionary *intuition* a century before Darwin, though not a theory of natural selection, and presented here as the scholarship warrants: as anticipation, not science. The dialogue form is itself the doctrine: truths about a changing, self-organizing nature arrive through conversation, dream, and delirium rather than system.

### Rameau's Nephew and the dialogue of the self

*Rameau's Nephew* (composed c. 1761–74; first published in Goethe's German translation, 1805) stages "Moi," the philosopher, against "Lui," the parasite-musician nephew of the composer Jean-Philippe Rameau — a brilliant failure who lives off the rich and knows exactly what his abasement costs and buys. The Nephew's pantomimed confession — that society is a theater of mutual deception in which the fool sees clearest — is never simply refuted and never simply endorsed. The dialogue has been read as satire on the Nephew, as satire on the Philosopher's complacency, and (by Hegel, who made it central to the *Phenomenology of Spirit*) as the portrait of a culture's consciousness torn apart. Which side Diderot takes remains the work's productive open question.

### Determinism and morality: Jacques the Fatalist

*Jacques the Fatalist and His Master* (written c. 1765–80, published 1796) turns its hero's creed — "everything that happens to us down here, good or bad, was written up above" on a great scroll — into a narrative experiment. The novel constantly interrupts, withholds, and re-routes its own story, so that the reader experiences the very problem Jacques asserts: if all is written, what are freedom, responsibility, and storytelling itself? Diderot's ethics elsewhere is naturalistic and social: virtue is what conduces to human happiness and the general good, judged by consequences, not by revelation — a morality fitted to the materialist universe, though how his determinism and his moral indignation (against tyranny, slavery, and colonial plunder, as in the *Supplement to Bougainville's Voyage*) fit together is a tension he explored rather than solved.

### Aesthetics: the Salons and the theory of art

From 1759 Diderot wrote art criticism for Grimm's manuscript journal, the *Correspondance littéraire*, reviewing the Paris Salon exhibitions — the celebrated *Salons* (1759–81). Writing for an audience that would never see the pictures, he invented a criticism of description, imagination, and moral response: praising Chardin's truthfulness and Vernet's landscapes, savaging Boucher's prettiness as artifice, demanding that painting act like drama — composed, expressive, morally serious. He is often called a founder of modern art criticism. His theater theory (the *Discourses on Dramatic Poetry*, 1758) likewise proposed a serious bourgeois drama between tragedy and comedy, and his posthumous *Paradox of the Actor* argues, scandalously, that the great actor feels nothing: mastery is technique, not sensibility.

## Key works

- *Philosophical Thoughts* (1746) — Aphorisms towards deism; burned by the Parlement. The apprentice work that announced him.
- *Letter on the Blind* (1749) — Epistemology through blindness; the turn to materialism that sent him to Vincennes.
- *Encyclopédie* (1751–1772), co-edited with d'Alembert — 17 volumes of text, 11 of plates, c. 72,000 articles. The Enlightenment's flagship.
- *Thoughts on the Interpretation of Nature* (first published 1753 as *De l'interprétation de la nature*; enlarged edition under this title 1754) — Aphoristic program for an experimental, conjectural science against mathematical system-building.
- *Salons* (1759–1781) — The exhibition reviews; the birth of modern art criticism.
- *Rameau's Nephew* (written c. 1761–74; published 1805, in German) — The great dialogue of parasite and philosopher.
- *D'Alembert's Dream* (written 1769; published 1830) — Materialism's masterpiece: egg, swarm, spider, and the transformation of species.
- *Jacques the Fatalist* (written c. 1765–80; published 1796) — The anti-novel of determinism.

## Contributions

Diderot built the Enlightenment's infrastructure: the *Encyclopédie* organized the knowledge of an age into a form that spread it, and its editorial methods — collaboration, cross-reference, the dignity of the mechanical arts — are a permanent model of public philosophy. As a philosopher proper, he gave materialism its most imaginative early-modern form, anticipating (in the careful, non-anachronistic sense) themes of emergent organization, embodied mind, and species transformation. As a critic he effectively founded modern art criticism, and his dialogues remain the standing proof that philosophy can be done as literature without loss of rigor.

## Impact and legacy

The *Encyclopédie* shaped the century that read it and the revolutions that followed. The unpublished dialogues had a stranger afterlife: *Rameau's Nephew* reached Europe through Goethe's translation and entered German philosophy through Hegel's *Phenomenology*; Marx admired Diderot's materialism and recommended *Rameau's Nephew* as a masterpiece; the French materialist line running through d'Holbach to the nineteenth century descends from the *Dream*. The *Salons* set the terms of art writing for Baudelaire and beyond. Catherine's library purchase became the emblematic anecdote of Enlightenment's bargain with enlightened despotism — debated ever since.

## Criticisms and debates

- **System or improvisation?** Because his boldest philosophy stayed in manuscript, scholars debate how systematic Diderot's materialism is: a coherent emergentist monism, some argue; others, a shifting set of brilliant experiments whose contradictions (determinism beside moral passion, atheism beside nature-piety) are the point. The texts support serious work on both sides.
- **The Encyclopédie as propaganda.** Revisionist historians note the dictionary's uneven quality — many articles are competent compilation or scissors-and-paste — and warn against reading every page as coded subversion. Defenders reply that the *renvois*, the plates, and the framing articles do the radical work, and that unevenness is the nature of a 72,000-article collective enterprise.
- **Catherine and complicity.** Was the library purchase a lifeline honorably accepted, or did the St Petersburg journey expose the philosophe as courtier, drafting unrealisable plans for an autocrat who collected intellectuals as ornaments? Diderot's own journey home — he wrote frankly about Russian realities — complicates both verdicts.
- **D'Alembert's departure.** The split of 1758 is still argued: prudence versus steadfastness, and whether the *Encyclopédie* after d'Alembert lost its mathematical spine or gained its freedom.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **He was not "just the editor."** The *Encyclopédie* consumed him, but the philosophy — materialism, aesthetics, the dialogues — is original work of the first rank, most of it written for the drawer because print was dangerous.
- **He was not born an atheist materialist.** The *Philosophical Thoughts* of 1746 are deist; the materialism hardens across the 1740s. Flattening the development hides the interest of the *Letter on the Blind* as a turning point.
- **The Nephew is not Diderot's spokesman — and neither, simply, is the Philosopher.** The dialogue's refusal to settle is its method; readings that award the victory to either voice have to explain away the other's best pages.
- **The *Encyclopédie* is not Wikipedia with wigs.** Its alphabetical order, cross-references, and plates were argumentative choices in a censored kingdom, not neutral packaging.

## What to read first

1. *Rameau's Nephew* — short, funny, unsettling; the ideal entry point (Margaret Mauldon's Oxford translation, or the Penguin edition).
2. *D'Alembert's Dream* — the materialist dialogues, in the Penguin *Selected Writings* or a standalone edition; read the egg and the bee-swarm slowly.
3. The article "Encyclopédie" and a handful of famous articles and plates — to see the machine working, not just described.
4. *Jacques the Fatalist* (first third) — determinism as comedy; then the *Supplement to Bougainville's Voyage* for the moral edge.
