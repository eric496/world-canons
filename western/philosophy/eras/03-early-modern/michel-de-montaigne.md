## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1533–1592
- **Place:** Château de Montaigne, near Bordeaux, France
- **School/tradition:** Renaissance humanism; Pyrrhonian skepticism revived for the modern world
- **Known for:** Inventing the essay as a literary-philosophical form; radical skepticism summed up in his motto "Que sçay-je?" ("What do I know?"); and turning the study of himself — body, moods, ignorance and all — into a way of doing philosophy

## Biography

Michel Eyquem de Montaigne was born in 1533 at the family estate in the Dordogne, heir to a recently ennobled merchant family (his great-grandfather had traded in wine, herring, and woad; his father was the first to live as a gentleman). His education was a deliberate experiment: his father had him raised by a German tutor who spoke only Latin, so that Latin was literally his first language, and woke him each morning to music. He then attended the Collège de Guyenne in Bordeaux, one of the finest humanist schools in France, and studied law, probably at Toulouse.

From 1557 he served as a counsellor in the Bordeaux Parlement, a sovereign court where he formed the defining friendship of his life, with the humanist and political thinker Étienne de La Boétie, author of a famous discourse against tyranny. La Boétie died of dysentery in 1563, aged thirty-two. Montaigne's essay "Of Friendship" is his memorial: "Because it was he, because it was I." He married in 1565; of his six daughters, only one survived infancy.

In 1571, on his thirty-eighth birthday, Montaigne retired from public life to his tower at Saint-Michel-de-Montaigne, inscribing on the wall that he consecrated his remaining years to "freedom, tranquillity, and leisure." There, surrounded by a library of about a thousand books (large for the time) with maxims painted on the ceiling beams, he began to write the *Essays*. Retirement was not withdrawal from history: France was tearing itself apart in the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots, and Montaigne — a moderate Catholic with Protestant relatives and friends — was repeatedly drawn back as a negotiator. He served two terms as mayor of Bordeaux (1581–85), kept the city loyal and plague-stricken at once, and acted as a trusted intermediary between Henri III and Henri of Navarre (the future Henri IV).

In 1580 he published Books I and II of the *Essays* and set out on a seventeen-month journey through Germany and Italy — partly for his health (he suffered agonising kidney stones, which he wrote about with unmatched frankness), partly to take the waters, partly to see Rome. Book III followed in 1588, and he spent his last years annotating and expanding his own book: the handwritten additions in the "Bordeaux copy" were published posthumously in 1595 by his devoted editor Marie de Gournay, the young scholar he called his "daughter by alliance." He died at home in 1592, reportedly during Mass.

## Major theories and concepts

### The essay: trying out the self

*Essai* means a trial, an attempt, a weighing (like assaying metal). Montaigne invented the form to match an epistemic situation: if knowledge of the great questions eludes us, what we *can* study honestly is the one specimen always at hand — oneself. "I study myself more than any other subject. That is my metaphysics, that is my physics." The essays ramble, quote the ancients, contradict themselves across editions, and treat digestion, thumbs, coaches, smells, and fear with the same seriousness as God and the soul. This is not self-indulgence; it is method. The self is a laboratory where claims about human nature can be tested against evidence: "I have no being except in halves... I do not portray being: I portray passing." The resulting portrait, he insists, is of everyone: "Each man bears the entire form of the human condition."

### "Que sçay-je?": Pyrrhonian skepticism revived

Montaigne had the motto *Que sçay-je?* — "What do I know?" — struck on a medallion with a balance, and in the longest essay, the "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (II.12), he mounted the most powerful case for ancient Pyrrhonian skepticism since antiquity. The timing mattered: Sextus Empiricus's *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, the main surviving source for Pyrrho's school, had been printed in Latin in 1562, just as confessional violence made rival certainties lethal. Montaigne's arguments are systematic skeptical machinery: the senses deceive and differ between species and between our own moods; reason argues in circles, needing proof of its own premises; dreams and madness show that conviction is no mark of truth; and the dizzying disagreement of philosophers about every first principle is itself evidence that none has been reached. Animals, he argues provocatively, may reason as well as we do — human presumption, "our natural and original disease," is the real target. The conclusion is suspension of judgment (*epochē*), held not as despair but as suppleness: "To learn that we have not learned is the highest achievement."

### Faith, custom, and the critique of certainty

What does the skeptic *do*? Montaigne's answer made him controversial in every direction. Outwardly he conformed to the Catholicism of his country — skepticism about reason, he argued, leaves faith to rest on grace rather than on shaky human arguments (this is the *fideist* reading: reason humbled so faith can be pure). But the same arguments dissolve every party's claim to kill for doctrine: "It is rating one's conjectures at a very high price to roast a man alive for them." Custom, he saw, is the real sovereign: we call barbarous whatever is not our own practice, and laws of conscience we attribute to nature are usually just habits absorbed in childhood. Skepticism thus becomes a technology of tolerance and intellectual humility — and a standing invitation to inquiry.

### "Of Cannibals": turning the mirror on Europe

In "Of Cannibals" (I.31), drawing on reports from Brazil, Montaigne describes the Tupinambá, who ate slain enemies ritually — and then asks who the barbarians are. Europeans, who torture living men over theological opinions, have more barbarity, he argues, than people who eat men already dead. The essay does not idealize its subjects into noble innocents; it uses them as an instrument to expose the provincialism of European judgment: "We have no other test of truth and reason than the example and pattern of the opinions and customs of the country we live in." It is a founding text of cultural relativism and of the essay as anthropology.

### The body, death, and learning to live

Against the philosophical tradition he is ambivalent about its most famous maxim: Cicero said philosophising is learning how to die, but Montaigne's mature view is that death deserves no such tyranny over life — nature will teach us when the time comes; in the meantime "the most savage of our maladies is to despise our being." His late essays celebrate the body and its pleasures without embarrassment — "On the loftiest throne in the world, we still sit only on our own behind" — and treat experience itself as the final school: "Of Experience" (III.13), his last essay, closes the book not with doctrine but with the art of living "loyally," enjoying one's being properly. Self-knowledge, at the end, is not a theory but a practice of acceptance.

## Key works

- *Essays*, Books I–II (1580), Book III and expanded edition (1588); posthumous Bordeaux-copy edition (1595) — The work. Always evolving; cite by book and chapter (e.g. II.12).
- "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (II.12) — The longest essay; the manifesto of his skepticism, ostensibly defending a work of natural theology by showing reason can defend nothing, including itself.
- "Of Cannibals" (I.31) and "Of Coaches" (III.6) — The New World essays: cultural relativism and a fierce critique of conquest.
- "Of Friendship" (I.28) — On La Boétie; the classic account of perfect friendship.
- "Of Experience" (III.13) — The summation: body, law, custom, self.
- *Travel Journal* (written 1580–81; published 1774) — His secretary's and partly his own record of the Italian journey; Montaigne unbuttoned.

## In their own words

> Now, to return to my subject, I find that there is nothing barbarous and savage in this nation, by anything that I can gather, excepting, that every one gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country. As, indeed, we have no other level of truth and reason than the example and idea of the opinions and customs of the place wherein we live: there is always the perfect religion, there the perfect government, there the most exact and accomplished usage of all things.

— Michel de Montaigne, "Of Cannibals," *Essays* I.30, trans. Charles Cotton (1685; public domain)

Writing about the Tupinambá of Brazil — who did, in fact, eat their enemies — Montaigne turns the mirror on Europe. The key term is *custom*: his sceptical point is that our standard of "truth and reason" is usually just the habits of home dressed up as nature. It is one of the earliest and sharpest statements of cultural relativism, delivered a century before anthropology existed.

## Contributions

Montaigne created a genre. The essay — personal, provisional, digressive, testing ideas against lived experience — became a permanent instrument of thought, carried forward by Bacon, Locke, Hume, Hazlitt, Emerson, and Woolf. Philosophically, he reintroduced Pyrrhonian skepticism into European thought at full strength, and that injection is one of the triggers of modern philosophy: Descartes's method of doubt, and his project of finding an indubitable foundation, are best read as a response to the crisis Montaigne had made vivid. He also pioneered a naturalistic, first-person study of mind and body — self-observation as evidence — that anticipates both the memoir and moral psychology.

## Impact and legacy

Pascal argued with him compulsively (the *Pensées* treat Montaigne as the great adversary and secret partner: "It is not in Montaigne, but in myself, that I find everything I see in him"). Descartes does not name him, but the skeptical scenarios of the *Meditations* are Montaigne's inheritance systematized. Shakespeare read him in Florio's 1603 translation — Gonzalo's commonwealth speech in *The Tempest* paraphrases "Of Cannibals." Rousseau's confessional project, Emerson's self-trust, and Nietzsche's praise ("That such a man wrote has truly augmented the joy of living on this Earth") all descend from him. In the twentieth century he was claimed variously as a fideist, a crypto-skeptic subversive, and the first modern individual; the "Que sçay-je?" tradition runs on into contemporary fallibilism and the philosophy of tolerance.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Fideist or subversive?** Did Montaigne sincerely hold that skepticism serves Catholic faith, or is the piety a protective coloring for a corrosive naturalism? Scholars (from Villey and Popkin, who made him the hinge of a "Pyrrhonian crisis," to more recent readers) still divide; the text supports strategic ambiguity.
- **Quietism.** Critics from Pascal — who charged him with a "lazy" indifference — to modern political readers have asked whether suspension of judgment in an age of massacre was wisdom or evasion. His active diplomacy complicates the picture of the withdrawn tower-dweller.
- **The self-indulgence charge.** That the *Essays* are narcissistic self-portraiture was already a seventeenth-century complaint (Malebranche called him a pedant of the self). Defenders reply that the self is his instrument, not his idol — the point is the test, not the portrait.
- **Which Montaigne?** The book exists in layers (1580, 1588, 1595) that sometimes qualify or reverse earlier claims; whether we should read for development, for a stable core, or accept contradiction as the doctrine is a standing interpretive debate.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **He is not a relativist about everything.** Suspension of judgment targets dogmatic *knowledge-claims*; Montaigne keeps firm commitments — to loyalty, to tolerance, against cruelty — and treats custom as a fact to reckon with, not a truth to celebrate.
- **"Que sçay-je?" is not a shrug.** It names a disciplined method (equipollence of arguments, suspension, continued inquiry), not indifference or laziness.
- **The *Essays* are not failed treatises.** The digressions, quotations, and self-contradictions are the form doing its work; reading him for extractable doctrine misses the invention.
- **He did not "refute" religion.** His skepticism cuts against every party's rational certainty, including atheist certainty — which is precisely why all sides have tried to claim or condemn him.

## What to read first

1. "Of Cannibals" (I.31) and "Of Friendship" (I.28) — short, famous, and immediately distinctive. (Screech's Penguin translation is the modern standard; Frame's is the classic alternative.)
2. "Of Experience" (III.13, selections) — the mature voice.
3. "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (II.12, selections) — the skeptical engine room; read with patience.
4. Then simply browse: open the *Essays* anywhere. The book was designed for grazing as much as for marching through.
