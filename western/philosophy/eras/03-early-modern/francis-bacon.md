## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1561–1626
- **Place:** England
- **School/tradition:** The new experimental science — its great herald; lawyer, courtier, and statesman who became Lord Chancellor
- **Known for:** Diagnosing the "idols of the mind" that distort inquiry, and proposing a reformed method of induction meant to replace scholastic logic with systematic observation — "knowledge itself is power"

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md) · **Answered by:** [Vico](giambattista-vico.md), [J. S. Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md)

## Biography
Bacon was born in London to a high-ranking family: his father, Sir Nicholas Bacon, was Lord Keeper of the Great Seal under Elizabeth I, and his mother, Anne Cooke Bacon, was a learned translator of religious works. He entered Trinity College, Cambridge, at twelve, and came away unimpressed — he later said the Aristotelian philosophy taught there produced only barren disputation. After legal training at Gray's Inn and a diplomatic posting in France, he entered Parliament in 1584 and began a long, careful climb through the courts of Elizabeth and then James I.

His rise was spectacular and his fall matched it. Knighted in 1603, he became Attorney General (1613), Lord Keeper (1617), and Lord Chancellor and Baron Verulam (1618), then Viscount St Alban (1621). The same year, Parliament charged him with taking bribes from suitors in cases before him. Bacon admitted accepting gifts — a common, if corrupt, practice — while denying they had swayed his judgments; he was fined, briefly imprisoned in the Tower, and barred from office. Historians still debate how corrupt he was by the standards of his day, but the disgrace ended his political career.

Retirement gave him five years to write. His philosophical project, the *Instauratio Magna* ("Great Instauration" or Great Renewal), was planned in six parts and never completed; it aimed at nothing less than a total rebuilding of human knowledge on new foundations. He died in 1626. According to John Aubrey's later account — the famous story, anecdotal in its details — he was traveling in snow near Highgate when he bought a chicken and stuffed it with snow to test whether cold could preserve flesh; he caught a chill, developed pneumonia, and died. Later ages half-seriously called him a martyr to experiment. His essays, histories, and the utopian fragment *New Atlantis* were published and republished long after his death, and his reputation as the philosopher of the Scientific Revolution grew steadily through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

## Major theories and concepts

### The idols of the mind
Before method can be reformed, Bacon argues, the mind itself must be cleared of systematic sources of error — the "idols" (*idola*), false notions that "beset" human understanding. He lists four. **Idols of the Tribe** are built into human nature generally: our senses deceive, we see patterns and purposes that are not there, and we prefer confirming instances to counterexamples. **Idols of the Cave** are individual: each person's upbringing, reading, and temperament creates a private cave that refracts the light of nature. **Idols of the Marketplace** come from language — words that name things that do not exist, or badly defined terms that generate empty disputes. **Idols of the Theatre** are received philosophical systems, which Bacon compares to stage plays: elegant invented worlds mistaken for the real one. The point is diagnostic before it is methodological: error is not random but structured, and inquiry must be designed to counteract it.

### The new induction
Bacon's positive method, set out in the *Novum Organum* (1620) — the title announces a "new instrument" to replace Aristotle's *Organon* — rejects the scholastic syllogism, which he says can compel assent but cannot grip nature. Instead, the investigator compiles natural histories: organized tables of instances where a phenomenon (his example is heat) is present, absent, or present in degrees. By comparing these tables and progressively *excluding* candidate explanations, the inquirer narrows in on the "form" of the phenomenon — roughly, its underlying nature or law. Only after this slow ascent from particulars may one cautiously generalize. Bacon is candid that this is laborious; he compares his method to a compass that lets even an unsteady hand draw a true circle, and he insists science should be collective, cumulative work rather than the feat of lone geniuses.

### Knowledge and power
Bacon's most famous claim is that human knowledge and human power "meet in one": nature, he writes in the *Novum Organum*, "is not conquered except by obeying" her. Understanding causes is what makes reliable action — medicine, engineering, agriculture — possible, and the true test of a theory is its fruitfulness in works. This utilitarian aim scandalized some contemporaries and still shapes debate about Bacon: he recast natural philosophy from contemplation of truths into a project for "the relief of man's estate," and imagined it organized institutionally — the research house of Salomon's House in *New Atlantis* is often read as a blueprint for the Royal Society, founded a generation after his death.

## Key works
- *Essays* (1597; expanded 1612 and 1625) — short, shrewd pieces on ambition, studies, friendship, and revenge; his most-read work in his own lifetime.
- *The Advancement of Learning* (1605) — a survey and defense of learning, arguing for the dignity and expansion of knowledge; Book II maps the sciences and diagnoses their gaps.
- *Novum Organum* (1620) — the methodological core of his project: the idols, the rejection of syllogism, and the new induction. Written in aphorisms, deliberately provisional.
- *New Atlantis* (published posthumously, 1627) — an unfinished utopian tale whose Salomon's House depicts a state-funded research institute.
- *History of the Reign of King Henry VII* (1622) — written in disgrace; long admired as a model of political history in English.

## Contributions
Bacon gave the emerging science a philosophy and a rhetoric: a taxonomy of cognitive error, a program of organized fact-gathering and exclusion-based induction, and a justification for science as a public, collaborative enterprise aimed at human welfare. Even where practicing scientists did not follow his method literally, his vision helped legitimate experimental natural philosophy against the universities' Aristotelian curriculum.

## Impact and legacy
The founders of the Royal Society (1660) claimed Bacon as their inspiration; Thomas Sprat's official *History* (1667) treats him as the movement's prophet. The French *philosophes* revered him — d'Alembert praised him at length in the *Preliminary Discourse*, and the *Encyclopédie* organized its tree of knowledge on Baconian lines. Voltaire called him "the father of experimental philosophy" (1734) — the herald who mapped a continent others explored. His influence runs onward through Locke and the empiricists to John Stuart Mill's logic of induction.

## Criticisms and debates
Scientists and historians have long noted that Bacon's own method, followed strictly, would rarely have produced the discoveries of Galileo, Kepler, or Newton, who relied on mathematics and bold hypothesis — tools Bacon undervalued. William Harvey reportedly said Bacon wrote philosophy "like a Lord Chancellor." Whether Bacon was a genuine methodologist or mainly a brilliant publicist for science remains debated. His inductive program also faces the problem later sharpened by Hume: no amount of tabulated instances logically guarantees the next one. Defenders reply that Bacon never claimed mechanical certainty, and that his stress on exclusion, negative instances, and institutional checking anticipates later accounts of scientific method.

## Misreadings to avoid
- Bacon did not invent "the scientific method" single-handed; he codified and championed tendencies also present in craftsmen, navigators, and earlier natural philosophers.
- The idols are not a list of other people's biases; the Tribe and Cave idols apply to every inquirer, including Bacon's readers.
- "Knowledge is power" is not a cynical slogan about domination; in context it means knowledge of causes enables beneficial works — though critics dispute how innocent that program was.

## What to read first
Start with the *Essays* for his voice, then *Novum Organum* Book I (the idols and the critique of existing philosophy) before attempting Book II's method in detail. *New Atlantis* is a quick, enjoyable way into his institutional vision.
