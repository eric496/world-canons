## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1588–1679
- **Place:** England, with long exile in Paris
- **School/tradition:** Materialist and founder of modern social-contract theory
- **Known for:** Deriving absolute authority from fear and rational self-preservation in *Leviathan*

**Responds to:** [Machiavelli](niccolo-machiavelli.md) · **Answered by:** [Locke](john-locke.md), [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md), [Spinoza](baruch-spinoza.md)

## Biography

Thomas Hobbes was born in Westport, Wiltshire, on 5 April 1588, prematurely, as news arrived of the approaching Spanish Armada. He later joked that “fear and I were born twins together” — his own quip, and a fitting emblem for a philosophy centered on fear of violent death.

Educated at Magdalen Hall, Oxford, Hobbes became tutor to the Cavendish family, earls of Devonshire. That post gave him income, libraries, travel, and political access for life. He met Galileo and moved in Mersenne’s Paris circle with Descartes and Gassendi, but built his system late: after translating Thucydides (1629), his philosophy emerged from his forties onward.

In 1640, as conflict between Charles I and Parliament intensified, Hobbes fled to Paris ahead of the Civil War — by his account the first royalist exile to leave — and stayed eleven years. There he wrote *De Cive* (1642) and tutored the exiled Prince of Wales, the future Charles II. He returned in 1651, when *Leviathan* was published, submitting to the Commonwealth — a move that angered former royalist allies.

*Leviathan* made him famous and suspect. Its materialism and subordination of church to state brought accusations of atheism; in 1666 a parliamentary committee investigating atheism and profaneness created a climate in which Hobbes feared prosecution and reportedly burned papers. Charles II protected him personally, but he was barred from publishing further controversial works in England. He wrote into extreme old age — history, law, and a long, ridiculed feud with John Wallis over squaring the circle — and died at Hardwick Hall on 4 December 1679, aged 91.

## Major theories and concepts

### Materialism: everything is matter in motion

For Hobbes, the universe contains only bodies — matter in motion. Sensation, imagination, memory, reasoning, desire, and thought itself are motions within the body: objects press on the senses, and those motions continue inside us. Reasoning is calculation, adding and subtracting consequences of agreed names. There is no separate immaterial soul and no purposes built into nature; “good” and “evil” name what each person desires and avoids.

Example: deliberating whether to flee danger is, for Hobbes, alternating appetites and aversions until the last appetite — the will — issues in action. Tension: if all is caused motion, what is freedom? Hobbes is a compatibilist: liberty is absence of external impediment to doing what you will, not freedom of the will from causes.

### The state of nature and the war of all against all

Imagine people with no common power to keep them in awe. This state of nature is a thought experiment, not mainly a claim about prehistory. People are equal in the sense that matters — even the weakest can kill the strongest, by stealth or alliance. From equality, scarcity, and uncertainty come three causes of quarrel: competition, diffidence (fear for safety), and glory.

The result is a “war of all against all”: not constant fighting, but a known disposition to fight, with no security for industry, trade, or knowledge. Life would be “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.” There is no injustice here, because there is no law. Tension: has Hobbes described human nature, or only human nature stripped of trust? Critics ask whether cooperation is just as natural as fear.

### Laws of nature as rational precepts

Reason discovers laws of nature — rational precepts about self-preservation. The first: seek peace so far as peace can be had, otherwise use the advantages of war. The second: lay down your unlimited right to all things if others will too. A third: keep covenants made. Until a sovereign enforces them, these bind in conscience as conclusions of reason; acting on them alone, when others will not, is self-destruction. Government must close that gap.

### Covenant and authorization of the sovereign

Each subject covenants with every other: I authorize and give up my right of governing myself to this person or assembly, on condition you do the same. The multitude thus becomes one artificial person, the Commonwealth or Leviathan, and the sovereign acts in the subjects’ name.

Two points are essential. First, the contract is among subjects, **not with the sovereign**: the sovereign is not a party, cannot breach it, and cannot lawfully be deposed for breach. Second, the sovereign need not be a king — an assembly can be Leviathan. Hobbes preferred monarchy in practice, but the theory requires only that sovereignty be unified and absolute: undivided and final. Absoluteness is the point, because divided sovereignty, in his diagnosis, is civil war by institutional means.

### The residual right of self-defense

Absolutism is not quite unlimited. No one can covenant away the right to resist immediate violence to their own body, to refuse self-destruction, or to confess to a capital crime without pardon. The covenant’s whole purpose was preservation, so a command to destroy oneself defeats its reason. This right is narrow — no general right of revolution, no collective mechanism to judge the sovereign — but it shows the theory rests on individual self-preservation throughout, not divine right. Its tension with absoluteness has occupied commentators ever since.

### Religion under state control

Half of *Leviathan* concerns religion. Hobbes argues from Scripture that the church must be subordinate to the civil sovereign: public worship, doctrine, and appointments fall under sovereign authority, because rival spiritual power invites civil war. Inward belief cannot be commanded, but public profession can. For Hobbes, clerical claims to independent jurisdiction were a chief cause of England’s wars — which is why contemporaries investigated him for heresy, and why modern readers should not skip these chapters.

## Key works

- *The Elements of Law, Natural and Politic* (1640) — First systematic statement of his psychology and politics, circulated in manuscript before his flight.
- *De Cive* (1642) — Compact Latin statement of the political theory, written in Paris; often clearer than *Leviathan* on the covenant.
- *Leviathan* (1651) — The masterpiece: materialist psychology, state of nature, sovereignty, and a theological second half subordinating church to state.
- *De Corpore* (1655) — His systematic philosophy of body and motion, the metaphysical foundation of the system.

## Contributions

Hobbes invented the modern template: start from individual psychology, imagine away authority, and derive the state as the rational solution. He gave contract theory its grammar — state of nature, covenant, authorization, sovereignty — which Locke and Rousseau would invert. His sovereignty is artificial, made by people rather than handed down by God, a foundation stone of modern political and legal theory. He also gave modern philosophy one of its first fully materialist accounts of mind.

## Impact and legacy

Locke kept consent but made government a dissoluble trust; Rousseau kept popular authorization while rejecting Hobbes’s natural man. Spinoza absorbed his naturalism and realism. In the twentieth century Hobbes was revived by international-relations realists, by game theorists reading the state of nature as a prisoner’s dilemma, and by legal positivists. His reputation has shifted from “philosopher of total power” toward “first great theorist of the modern secular state” — now a leading scholarly reading, though theological readings of Hobbes remain a live rival.

## Criticisms and debates

Contemporaries charged atheism, reduction of morality to self-interest, and excuse of tyranny. Enduring debates include: egoism — can all action reduce to self-preservation, and what of benevolence? Obligation — are laws of nature mere prudential advice, or also God’s commands that genuinely oblige? Resistance — does the residual right quietly reopen a door Hobbes shuts? And evidence — stateless societies have often been orderly, though Hobbes’s device does not strictly require otherwise. These are live debates, not settled refutations.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **“Hobbes defended divine-right kingship.”** No: authority rests on covenant, and an assembly can be sovereign.
- **“Hobbes thought people are simply evil.”** His argument is structural: under uncertainty, even fearful moderates are driven to conflict.
- **“The sovereign signed a contract and can be fired.”** The covenant is among subjects only — hence no right of deposition.
- **“The state of nature is his history of early humans.”** It is chiefly an analytical device: remove the common power and see what follows.

## What to read first

1. *Leviathan*, chapters 13–17 — state of nature, laws of nature, and covenant.
2. *Leviathan*, chapters 6 and 11 — appetite, aversion, and the restless desire of power.
3. *De Cive*, Preface and chapters 1–3 — a shorter restatement.
4. *Leviathan*, chapters 21 and 29 — liberty of subjects, and how commonwealths dissolve.
