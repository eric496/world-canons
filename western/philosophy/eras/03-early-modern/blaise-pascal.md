# Blaise Pascal

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1623–1662
- **Place:** France (Clermont-Ferrand, Rouen, Paris)
- **School/tradition:** Mathematician, physicist, and Jansenist Christian thinker
- **Known for:** The *Pensées*: fragments on reason’s limits, human wretchedness and greatness, diversion, and the Wager

**Responds to:** [Montaigne](michel-de-montaigne.md), [Descartes](rene-descartes.md), [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md) · **Answered by:** [Kierkegaard](../04-19th-century/soren-kierkegaard.md), [William James](../04-19th-century/william-james.md)

## Biography

Blaise Pascal was born on 19 June 1623 in Clermont-Ferrand. His mother died when he was three; his father Étienne, a tax administrator and able mathematician, educated the children himself, deciding Blaise should not study mathematics until fifteen. Curiosity defeated the plan: Blaise re-derived Euclid alone, joined Mersenne’s circle, and at sixteen wrote a treatise on conic sections so fine Descartes reportedly suspected the father of writing it.

In Rouen, where the family moved in 1640, Pascal invented a mechanical calculating machine, the Pascaline, to aid his father’s tax work. In his twenties he was an experimental scientist of the first rank: experiments on atmospheric pressure (including the 1648 Puy-de-Dôme test) defended the vacuum against Aristotelian orthodoxy, and his 1654 correspondence with Fermat founded probability theory.

In 1646 the family came under the influence of Jansenism, the austere Augustinian movement centered on Port-Royal, stressing sin, grace, and suspicion of Jesuit casuistry. Pascal’s sister Jacqueline became a Port-Royal nun. His decisive experience came on the night of 23 November 1654 — the “night of fire,” about two hours of intense religious rapture recorded in the *Memorial*, beginning “Fire,” which he sewed into his coat lining and carried for life.

Thereafter Pascal largely withdrew from mathematics and society (a return during toothache in 1658 produced work on the cycloid). He became Port-Royal’s brilliant public defender: the pseudonymous *Provincial Letters* (1656–57), written during proceedings against the Jansenist Antoine Arnauld, skewered Jesuit casuistry so wittily they became French prose classics — and were condemned, indexed, and publicly burned in 1660, amid escalating persecution of Jansenists. His last years, in poor health, were spent on charity, on Paris’s first public transport system, and on notes for a great apology for Christianity he never completed. He died in Paris on 19 August 1662, aged thirty-nine.

Those notes are the *Pensées*: hundreds of fragments found bundled after his death. Port-Royal published an edited, softened edition in 1670; modern editions (numbering systems — Brunschvicg, Lafuma, Sellier — still divide scholarship) restore a rawer text. Fragment order is itself contested.

## Major theories and concepts

### The limits of reason: “the heart has its reasons”

“The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know” is not irrationalism. By “heart” Pascal means immediate apprehension: we know first principles — that we exist, that we are awake, the starting points of proof — not by demonstration but by direct grasp, and all reasoning depends on such unproved foundations. Reason is real but limited, and can show its own limits.

Pascal distinguishes the *esprit de géométrie*, deducing from clear principles, from the *esprit de finesse*, perceiving many fine principles at once, as in judging character. A complete mind needs both. His complaint against Descartes is not that he reasoned but that he treated geometry as the whole of knowledge. Tension: is this demotion of demonstration, from a mathematical genius, profound humility or special pleading for faith? Readers have split ever since.

### Human greatness and wretchedness

Man is a paradox — “a thinking reed,” nature’s weakest thing, yet a reed that knows it dies, which the universe crushing it does not. Our wretchedness is real: inconstancy, anxiety, mortality. Our greatness is equally real: we alone *know* our wretchedness; a tree does not. Any honest account, Pascal argues, must explain both; philosophies teaching only greatness (Stoic pride) or only wretchedness (skeptical despair) suppress half the evidence. Christianity’s Fall and Redemption, he proposes, map the paradox exactly — whether that fit counts as evidence is his standing question.

### *Divertissement*: diversion

Why is a king with every pleasure miserable if left alone in a room? *Divertissement* — diversion. Hunting, gambling, intrigue, business, even science: much busyness keeps us from thinking about death and dependence. The hunter does not really want the hare (given it as a gift, he would be disappointed); he wants the chase, which occupies him. The compulsive checking of a phone in any unfilled moment is the modern instance Pascal predicts. Diversion is also, in his compassionate treatment, a mercy: it makes bearable a condition we cannot face steadily. The tension is that his remedy — face the condition and seek God — is exactly what the diagnosis says we are built to avoid.

### The two infinities and man’s disproportion

In the fragment on man’s disproportion, Pascal sets us between two infinities: the infinitely large of the new astronomy, where Earth is a point, and the infinitely small, where a mite contains limbs and veins and worlds within worlds, descending without end. Suspended between nothing and infinity, comprehending neither extreme, we occupy a permanent middle — *le milieu*. The science that reveals nature’s scale reveals that we have no fixed standpoint within it: “the eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me.” The argument does not prove Christianity; it clears away the pretense that unaided reason has made us at home in the universe.

### The Wager

Where reason cannot decide whether God exists, Pascal argues, prudence can still decide how to act — “you are embarked,” the choice is forced. Wager that God exists and live accordingly: if God exists, you gain an infinity of infinitely happy life; if not, you lose finite pleasures. Wager against, and if wrong you risk infinite loss. Expected gain favors wagering for God, however small the probability, if it is not zero.

The nuance stressed by the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/) is essential. Pascal wrote as a believer, addressing a skeptical libertine, to show belief is *not unreasonable* — that supposedly neutral detachment is itself a choice with stakes. He did **not** think the argument could manufacture saving faith. In his Jansenist theology, faith is a gift of God’s grace, not a conclusion calculation can produce. His practical advice — follow believers’ practices, and habit will quiet the passions blocking belief — aims to remove obstacles and dispose a person toward a gift, not replace it. Presenting the Wager as a conversion machine, a proof of God, or Christian insurance reverses his theology: it makes faith a human work, close to the error he fought in the *Provincial Letters*.

## Key works

- *Provincial Letters* (1656–57) — Eighteen pseudonymous letters defending Jansenism and demolishing Jesuit casuistry; masterpieces of irony that shaped French prose.
- *Pensées* (posthumous, 1670) — The unfinished apology: fragments on diversion, disproportion, greatness and wretchedness, and the Wager. Unfinished notebooks, not a finished book.
- Scientific works (1640s–50s) — Treatises on the vacuum and fluids, and the 1654 Fermat correspondence founding probability theory.

## In their own words

> Let us then examine this point, and say, "God is, or He is not." But to which side shall we incline? Reason can decide nothing here. There is an infinite chaos which separated us. A game is being played at the extremity of this infinite distance where heads or tails will turn up. What will you wager? […] "The true course is not to wager at all." Yes; but you must wager. It is not optional. You are embarked. […] Let us weigh the gain and the loss in wagering that God is. Let us estimate these two chances. If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then, without hesitation that He is.

— Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §233, trans. W. F. Trotter (1910; public domain)

This is the Wager, and its most radical line is "You are embarked": abstaining is itself a bet, since you must live one way or the other. Notice Pascal concedes the sceptic's premise — reason cannot settle whether God exists — and then changes the question from *what is true?* to *what is it rational to stake?* With an infinite gain on one side and only finite losses on the other, he argues, the calculation is not close.

## Contributions

Pascal created a mode of philosophy that barely existed before him: mathematically rigorous, experimentally grounded, and existentially intimate. He gave thought a durable vocabulary — heart, diversion, disproportion, thinking reed — and an early philosophy of demonstration’s limits: reasoning rests on unproved principles grasped otherwise. With Fermat he co-founded probability theory, and the Wager is a founding document of decision theory.

## Impact and legacy

The *Provincial Letters* turned French opinion against casuistry and remain a model of polemic. The *Pensées* anticipated themes central to Kierkegaard — anxiety, the limits of system, faith beyond proof — and Pascal is read by believers and unbelievers alike as the anatomist of distraction and dread. Voltaire attacked him while conceding his genius; Nietzsche admired his honesty. The Wager still anchors debates on pragmatic arguments for belief, from William James to modern decision theorists. Pascal’s law and the unit of pressure keep his scientific name in daily use.

## Criticisms and debates

The Wager draws the sharpest objections. The “many gods” problem: the argument cannot specify *which* god to wager on, and rival infinities may cancel the calculation. Others object that God could not reward mercenary belief — a point Pascal partly concedes via his theology of grace, which is why the conversion-machine reading matters. Debates continue over his picture of wretchedness (insight or Jansenist pathology?) and over how far the fragments cohere into one apology. None is closed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **“The Wager proves God exists.”** It is prudential reasoning under undecidability; Pascal denies reason can settle the question.
- **“Use the Wager, get faith.”** Faith, for Pascal, is grace’s gift; the argument at most removes obstacles and shows belief is not unreasonable.
- **“‘The heart’ means feelings over facts.”** The heart apprehends first principles — a cognitive faculty, in a life devoted to exact reasoning.
- **“Pascal abandoned science for religion.”** He largely withdrew after 1654, but his 1658 cycloid work unites both halves.

## What to read first

1. *Pensées*, diversion fragments — the king alone in his room; the hunter who does not want the hare.
2. *Pensées*, the disproportion / two infinities fragment — the longest sustained piece, and the best.
3. *Pensées*, the Wager fragment — read whole, with the interlocutor’s objections and Pascal’s advice on practice.
4. *Provincial Letters*, Letters 4–5 — the satirist at work.
