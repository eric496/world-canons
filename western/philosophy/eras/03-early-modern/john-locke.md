## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1632–1704
- **Place:** Somerset, Oxford, and London, England; exile in the Netherlands, 1683–89
- **School/tradition:** Empiricism; early liberal political theory
- **Known for:** All ideas come from experience; personal identity rests on consciousness; legitimate government rests on consent and protects natural rights to life, liberty, and property

**Responds to:** [Hobbes](thomas-hobbes.md) · **Answered by:** [Berkeley](george-berkeley.md), [Hume](david-hume.md), [Leibniz](gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz.md)

## Biography

Locke was born in 1632 in Wrington, Somerset, son of a country attorney who fought for Parliament in the Civil War. Educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, he stayed on to teach Greek, rhetoric, and moral philosophy, but grew dissatisfied with the scholastic curriculum. He turned to the new experimental science and to medicine, working alongside Robert Boyle and the physician Thomas Sydenham. His medical and scientific associations shaped his intellectual style: observe carefully, and distrust grand systems built ahead of the evidence.

In 1667 he entered the household of Anthony Ashley Cooper, later Earl of Shaftesbury — first in a medical capacity, then as secretary and political adviser. Through Shaftesbury he was drawn into Restoration politics, colonial administration, and trade. Philosophy and politics were never separate for him: authority, consent, property, and toleration were live party questions.

When Shaftesbury fell and was implicated in plots against Charles II, Locke’s position became dangerous. In 1683 he fled to the Netherlands, living in exile until 1689 under threat of extradition, and bringing much of his major work to final form. He returned after the Glorious Revolution brought William and Mary to the throne. That year, 1689, saw the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, the *Two Treatises of Government*, and the *Letter Concerning Toleration* all published. Despite chronic asthma, he remained active — serving on the Board of Trade and writing on education, money, and Christianity — spending his last years at Oates in Essex, in Sir Francis Masham’s household, where he died in 1704.

## Major theories and concepts

### No innate ideas: the mind as “white paper”

Book I of the *Essay* attacks the doctrine that certain ideas or principles — logical maxims, moral rules, the idea of God — are innate, stamped on the mind from birth. If they were, Locke argues, they would command universal assent; yet children and many whole peoples show no awareness of supposed innate maxims. And if “innate” merely means “appearing when reason matures,” then everything discoverable by reason would count as innate, emptying the doctrine of content.

His alternative, in Book II: suppose the mind to be “white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas” — how is it furnished? “From experience.” Note the label: **“tabula rasa” (blank slate) is a later shorthand label, not Locke’s own image here**, and it misleads if taken to mean a mind with no structure at all.

For Locke denies innate *ideas and principles*, not innate *faculties*. Perception, memory, reasoning, desire, and natural temperaments are part of our equipment. His quarrel is with ready-made knowledge, not with human nature.

### Sensation and reflection

All ideas come from two sources. *Sensation* supplies ideas of external things: colors, sounds, solidity, motion. *Reflection* is the mind’s notice of its own operations — perceiving, doubting, believing, willing. Reflection is not an innate stock; it is experience turned inward.

From *simple* ideas received through these sources, the mind actively builds *complex* ideas by combining, comparing, and abstracting. This yields Locke’s “historical, plain method”: for any contested concept — substance, cause, infinity — ask how the idea could have been assembled from experience. If no such history can be given, suspect a word being used without a clear idea, a diagnosis he applies relentlessly to scholastic jargon.

### Primary and secondary qualities

In *Essay* II.8, Locke develops a distinction associated with Galileo and Boyle. *Primary qualities* — solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, number — are really in objects whether perceived or not, and our ideas of them *resemble* them: the idea of a shape is genuinely like the shape.

*Secondary qualities* — colors, sounds, tastes, smells — do not resemble anything in the object as they appear to us. They are *powers* in objects, grounded in the primary qualities of their minute parts, to produce ideas in us. The same water feels warm to one hand and cold to the other, so warmth as felt cannot simply reside in the water as it appears. The distinction was meant to reconcile everyday experience with the new corpuscular science — though Berkeley argued Locke had not followed it far enough.

### Material substance: “something, I know not what”

We experience collections of qualities — hard, white, cold, round — and suppose a *substratum* in which they inhere. Strip away all the qualities, and what remains? Locke is disarmingly honest: substance in general is “something, I know not what,” a *supposed* support whose own nature we do not grasp. He does not deny substance; he confesses our idea of it is obscure. It is a consequential loose thread: Berkeley discarded material substance while keeping spiritual substance, and Hume pressed the question against both.

### Personal identity: consciousness and memory

*Essay* II.27, added in the second edition, founds the modern debate. A *person* is a thinking being that can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, across different times and places — and it does so by *consciousness*. Sameness of person is neither sameness of soul-substance nor of body, but the backward reach of consciousness: I am the same person who did a past deed insofar as I can remember it from the inside.

His thought experiments still structure the field. If a prince’s consciousness entered a cobbler’s body, the resulting person would be the prince. If Socrates waking and Socrates sleeping shared no consciousness, they would not be the same person — which matters because “person” is, Locke insists, a *forensic* term: identity is what praise, blame, and punishment properly track. Critics objected that memory presupposes identity rather than constituting it (Butler), and that overlapping memory chains break transitivity (Reid’s brave-officer case). These remain live debates, not settled refutations.

### Natural rights and the state of nature

The *Second Treatise* begins before government, in a *state of nature* where people are free and equal, bound by a law of nature — reason — teaching that no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions. These *natural rights* are held independently of government. Locke’s state of nature is not Hobbesian war, but it has “inconveniences”: no settled known law, no impartial judge, no reliable executive. Everyone must enforce the law of nature themselves, and partiality predictably corrupts that arrangement.

### Property: labor mixing

If the earth was originally given to mankind in common, how can anything become privately owned? Locke’s answer in chapter V is *labor*. Each person owns his own person, and therefore his labor. By mixing that labor with something unowned — gathering acorns, tilling a field — he joins to it something that is his and makes it his property, provided (the famous provisos) that “enough, and as good” is left for others and nothing spoils in his possession. Money, introduced by tacit consent, later allows accumulation beyond the spoilage limit. Whether labor-mixing justifies extensive private property, and how the provisos fare amid scarcity, has been debated ever since — including sharply critical readings of the theory’s use in justifying seizure of Indigenous land in America.

### Consent, limited government, and dissolution

Political power can arise legitimately only from *consent*: free individuals compact to form a society and entrust government with power to protect their rights. Government is therefore *limited* and *fiduciary* — a trust, not an ownership. Even the supreme legislative power cannot rule arbitrarily, take property without consent, or transfer lawmaking to others.

If government breaks the trust, who shall judge? “The people shall be judge.” Government may be *dissolved*, and power reverts to society, which may erect a new one. Locke distinguishes dissolving a *government* from dissolving a *society*, and answers the charge that his doctrine invites constant rebellion: peoples are slow to rouse, and revolutions follow long trains of abuse, not light provocations. Written during the Exclusion Crisis, the argument was published in 1689 as a justification of the revolution just completed.

### Toleration

The *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) argues the magistrate’s business is civil interests — life, liberty, property — not salvation. True religion is inward persuasion, which force cannot produce: a church is a voluntary society, and coercion manufactures hypocrisy, not faith. Locke’s own limits are revealing and much debated: he excluded atheists, whose oaths he thought could not bind them, and distrusted groups he believed owed allegiance to a foreign power — limits his own principles have since been used to criticize and extend beyond.

## Signature arguments

**The argument against innate ideas (*Essay*, Book I)**

1. A truth counts as innate only if it is universally assented to, at least once it is understood.
2. Children and people with severe cognitive impairments assent to no speculative or practical principles — not even to the principle of non-contradiction or to basic moral rules.
3. What is assented to only later, after teaching and reasoning, is acquired in the ordinary way, not discovered already stamped on the mind.
Therefore, no ideas or principles are innate, and the mind begins as "white paper," furnished thereafter by experience.

**Objection.** Ideas could be innate dispositionally: present not as conscious assent but as capacities or tendencies, assented to when reason matures. This is the line Leibniz later developed against Locke in the *New Essays*, comparing the mind to veined marble predisposed to one shape rather than another.

**Reply.** Locke answers that a disposition to acquire an idea from experience is just the mind's general capacity to learn, which nobody denies. If every idea we are capable of forming thereby counts as innate, the doctrine loses its content: it no longer marks off a special stock of God-given truths needing no experience. The dispute is partly about what "innate" must mean to be worth defending, and Leibniz's more sophisticated dispositional version — tendencies and inclinations rather than ready-made thoughts — is generally treated as a genuine advance in the debate, not a mere restatement of the position Locke attacked.

**Personal identity as consciousness (*Essay* II.xxvii)**

1. A person is a thinking being that can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, at different times and places.
2. Personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness: I am the same person as the doer of a past action insofar as I can remember that experience or action as my own, from the inside.
3. Consciousness can in principle accompany a different body, or fail to connect stages of the same body or immaterial substance.
Therefore, identity of person can diverge both from identity of body and from identity of immaterial substance; being the same person is a separate question from being the same living organism or soul.

**Objection.** Memory looks circular and gappy. Butler charged that consciousness of personal identity presupposes the very identity it is meant to constitute, since genuine remembering already assumes the experience was mine. Reid's "brave officer" case adds that memory-chains can break transitivity: the old general remembers the officer, the officer remembered the boy, but the general cannot remember the boy — yet identity, unlike remembered connection, must be transitive.

**Reply.** Lockeans distinguish the evidence for identity from what constitutes it, arguing that apparent memory can misfire without undermining consciousness as the criterion. Others move to psychological continuity with overlapping memory chains — the modern descendant of Locke's view — so direct recall of every stage is not required. On the brave-officer case, that continuity view answers Reid by chaining the general to the boy through the officer, even though no single memory spans the whole distance. Both moves are best presented as a debate Locke started rather than settled, since each revision moves some distance from the letter of II.xxvii.

## Key works

- *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (first published Dec 1689, title page dated 1690) — Against innate ideas; the sources of ideas; words and their abuses; knowledge and probability.
- *Two Treatises of Government* (1689, but written c. 1679–83 during the Exclusion Crisis) — The *First* refutes Filmer’s divine-right monarchy; the *Second* gives the positive account of rights, property, consent, and dissolution.
- *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) — First published in Latin in the Netherlands; the classic argument separating civil authority from religious coercion.
- *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693) — Habit, example, and esteem over rote and the rod; widely influential.
- *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695) — A minimalist account of Christian belief, controversial in its day.

## In their own words

> Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas; how comes it to be furnished? Whence comes it by that vast store which the busy and boundless fancy of man has painted on it with an almost endless variety? Whence has it all the materials of reason and knowledge? To this I answer, in one word, from EXPERIENCE. In that all our knowledge is founded; and from that it ultimately derives itself.

— John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.i.2 (1690; public domain)

Locke's answer to where ideas come from became the charter of empiricism. Notice the care in the image: he says the mind is *supposed* to be white paper "as we say" — the point is not the metaphor but the challenge it issues. If any idea cannot be traced back to sensation or to reflection on our own mental operations, Locke will conclude it is empty noise rather than knowledge.

## Contributions

Locke set empiricism’s research program: trace every idea to experience, and suspect concepts that cannot survive that audit. He made personal identity as psychological continuity a central, still-open problem. He articulated the grammar of modern liberalism — pre-political rights, government by consent, power as trust, and a principled right to replace a government that betrays it. And he gave toleration a systematic defense grounded in the nature of belief itself, not merely political convenience.

## Impact and legacy

The *Essay* dominated British and French philosophy for a century. Berkeley and Hume each claimed to complete Locke’s project more consistently than he had; Leibniz thought the book worth a book-length reply; Voltaire popularized him in France as a patron of Enlightenment reform. His political theory fed eighteenth-century liberalism and the American Founding — the language of unalienable rights, consent of the governed, and justified revolution in the Declaration of Independence is recognisably Lockean in structure, whatever debates remain about direct influence. His educational writings shaped modern ideas of childhood formation.

## Criticisms and debates

Berkeley argued the primary/secondary distinction collapses — extension and figure vary with perspective just as color does — and that material substance is an idea Locke’s own principles cannot license. Hume pressed the empiricist audit further, to causation, the self, and induction. On politics, scholars debate whether Locke’s *tacit* consent — inferred from merely living under a government — is too thin to legitimise anything. Critical historians read the property chapter as ideology for enclosure and colonialism; others stress the provisos as real constraints much modern accumulation violates. Twentieth-century scholarship also transformed the *Two Treatises* themselves, showing they were Exclusion Crisis works, not a post-1688 afterthought.

## Misreadings to avoid

- “Tabula rasa” is later shorthand; Locke’s image is “white paper,” and he denies innate *ideas*, not innate faculties or tendencies.
- Locke is not a behaviorist or crude blank-slate determinist; reflection, reasoning, and active combination are built into his account.
- The state of nature is primarily an analytical device about authority and obligation, not simply a claim about prehistory.
- The right of revolution is not a licence to rebel at any grievance; Locke stresses long abuse, a broken trust, and the people as judge.

## What to read first

1. *Essay* II.1 — the sources of ideas, short and programmatic.
2. *Essay* II.8 — primary and secondary qualities.
3. *Essay* II.27 — personal identity, his most influential single chapter.
4. *Second Treatise*, chapters II, V, VII–IX — state of nature, property, and political power.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Locke's primary/secondary quality distinction using the water that feels warm to one hand and cold to the other: which qualities resemble features of the object, and which are merely powers to produce ideas in us?
2. Press Berkeley's collapse objection — extension, figure, and motion vary with perspective just as color does — and assess whether anything of Locke's distinction survives it.
3. Reconstruct Locke's consciousness criterion through the prince-and-cobbler case, explaining why “person” is a forensic term. Then press Butler's circularity charge and Reid's brave-officer case: does the psychological-continuity reply save Locke, or revise him?
4. Apply Locke's criterion to a fresh case: a defendant who genuinely cannot remember a crime committed years earlier. Is he, for Locke, the same person who acted, and how do body-based and soul-based criteria answer differently?
5. Compare Leibniz's veined-marble dispositions with Locke's attack on innate ideas: is a predisposition to form a truth an innate idea, or exactly the general capacity Locke says makes “innate” empty?
