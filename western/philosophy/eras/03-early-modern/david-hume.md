## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1711–1776
- **Place:** Edinburgh, Scotland
- **School/tradition:** Empiricism and mitigated skepticism
- **Known for:** Showing that beliefs about cause, self, and morality rest on custom and sentiment, not reason — and for waking Kant from his "dogmatic slumber"

**Responds to:** [Locke](john-locke.md), [Berkeley](george-berkeley.md), [Montesquieu](montesquieu.md) · **Answered by:** [Kant](immanuel-kant.md), [Adam Smith](adam-smith.md), [Parfit](../05-20th-century/derek-parfit.md)

## Biography

David Hume was born on 7 May 1711 in Edinburgh, son of a modest landowner who died when Hume was two. Raised at Ninewells, he entered Edinburgh University at about eleven and left without a degree. Family expectations pointed to law; Hume found an "insurmountable aversion to everything but the pursuits of Philosophy and general Learning."

Intense study in his twenties produced his masterpiece. Retreating to France — chiefly at La Flèche, Descartes's old school town — he wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40, anonymous), attempting to introduce "the Experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects."

It failed. Hume's verdict in *My Own Life* was that it "fell dead-born from the press." He recast the material in clearer forms. The *Treatise* is now widely regarded as the greatest work of philosophy in English — a complete inversion of its reception.

He was a public man of letters, not a professor: he was twice blocked from university chairs (Edinburgh 1745, Glasgow 1752) by opponents of his religious views. He supported himself as a tutor, secretary to General St Clair, librarian in Edinburgh (where he wrote his *History of England*), and diplomat in Paris and London.

The recast works made his reputation. The *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) reworked Book I of the *Treatise*, adding miracles. The *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751) he considered "incomparably the best" of his writings. In Paris he was lionised as "le bon David," a fame complicated by a public quarrel with Rousseau. Essays on suicide and immortality were suppressed, and the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* appeared only after his death.

Hume died in Edinburgh on 25 August 1776, with a composure that became famous. The *Dialogues* appeared in 1779.

## Major theories and concepts

### Impressions, ideas, and the copy principle

Hume distinguishes **impressions** from **ideas**. Impressions are perceptions in full force: seeing red, feeling pain, experiencing an emotion. Ideas are the "less lively perceptions" we have in remembering or imagining them. His **copy principle** states that all simple ideas copy corresponding simple impressions. We can combine materials impressions supply — a golden mountain joins gold and mountain — but cannot invent a new simple idea with no experiential source: a blind man has no idea of color. His own exception, the "missing shade of blue," he candidly offers and dismisses as too singular to overturn the principle.

The principle is an instrument: confronted with a suspect term — substance, necessary connection, the soul — Hume asks, *from what impression is this idea derived?* If none can be produced, the term may be empty noise.

### Hume's fork

The objects of human reason divide into two kinds. **Relations of ideas** — mathematics and logic — are demonstratively certain; their denial implies a contradiction. **Matters of fact** are known only from experience; their contrary is always conceivable. That the sun will rise tomorrow is a matter of fact: a world in which it did not is perfectly thinkable.

This is **Hume's fork**. A book containing neither abstract reasoning about number nor experimental reasoning about fact should be committed, he concludes, "to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion." The fork's tension is self-application: which prong is the fork itself? Defenders treat it as a methodological proposal; critics have pressed the question.

### The problem of induction

Reasoning beyond present perception rests on the expectation that the future will resemble the past. What justifies it? Not **deduction**: the contrary — that nature's course might change — is conceivable without contradiction. Not **induction**: arguing induction has worked before assumes exactly what is to be proved. The argument is circular.

This **problem of induction** has never received a universally accepted solution. Hume is not saying induction is unreliable, or that we should stop expecting bread to nourish. He is saying the inference is not performed by reason: nature has determined us, by **custom or habit**, to expect it — skeptical about *justification*, naturalistic about *practice*. A burned child expects fire to burn, without any proof.

### Causation: constant conjunction and custom

Watching one billiard ball strike another, we perceive contiguity, priority, and — after repetition — **constant conjunction**: balls of that kind have always been followed by motion of that kind. We never perceive a further item, a power or necessary tie *in the objects*. Yet we have an idea of necessity. Hume locates its source inside us: after repetition, the mind is carried by custom from one object to a lively idea of the other, and that *felt determination of the mind* is the origin of the idea of necessary connection. Necessity, provocatively, exists in the mind, not in objects.

Hence two definitions of cause: an object followed by another, where all similar objects are followed by similar objects (regularity); and an object whose appearance conveys the mind, by custom, to the idea of the second (projection). Whether Hume believed there are no objective necessities, or merely that we can form no idea of them beyond regularity, is the "New Hume" debate below. The secure point: our evidence never exceeds constant conjunction plus habit.

### The bundle theory of the self — and the Appendix admission

When I introspect, Hume reports, "I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch *myself* at any time without a perception." There is no impression of a simple, identical self behind the flow; therefore, by his test, there is no such idea. The mind is **a bundle or collection of different perceptions** in perpetual flux. Personal identity is a construction: memory and the imagination's smooth transitions among linked perceptions lead us to *feign* an identical self.

Then comes a famous act of honesty. In the **Appendix** to the *Treatise*, Hume found himself "involv'd in such a labyrinth" that he could neither correct his opinions nor render them consistent. Roughly: perceptions are distinct existences, and the mind perceives no real connection among them — so what unites the bundle into *one* mind? He confessed he could not solve it. Scholars still debate the exact inconsistency.

### Is and ought

In *Treatise* Book III, Hume notes that moral authors proceed with *is* and *is not* propositions, then imperceptibly the connections become *ought* and *ought not* — "some new relation or affirmation" — without explaining how it could be deduced from the old. Since the change is "of the last consequence," he asks for a reason "for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it."

Crucially, Hume **asks for an explanation of the move**; whether he meant an absolute ban on *all* derivations of ought from is, or a challenge to rationalist confidence, is **debated**. The passage is a demand for an argument, not a formal theorem. It fits his positive view: moral distinctions rest on sentiment, not reason.

### Morality as sentiment, sympathy, and reason as slave of the passions

Hume holds that **moral distinctions are not derived from reason**. Discovering that an act occurred does not pronounce it virtuous or vicious — people can agree on all the facts and still divide morally, and moral judgments *motivate* us, which inert beliefs alone do not. Morality rests on **sentiment**: approbation or blame felt when contemplating a character from a general point of view.

**Sympathy** makes this social: ideas of others' feelings are converted, through resemblance and association, into the feelings themselves in us, more faintly. It explains why others' joy pleases and misery pains us, and why moral judgment can converge.

The famous slogan belongs here: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them." This concerns **motivation**, not irrationality. Reason informs us of facts and means and corrects false beliefs, but cannot by itself supply an end or move us; only a passion can. Reason keeps full authority over truth: a passion founded on a false belief dissolves when the belief is corrected.

### Miracles

In Section X of the first *Enquiry*, Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature established by firm experience. His argument concerns **testimony**, not metaphysical possibility. A wise man "proportions his belief to the evidence." Testimony's force comes from its usual reliability; a law's force from uniform experience without exception. When testimony reports a miracle, the two collide, and belief should follow the stronger — which, he argues, it never can in practice, because evidence for the law is maximal, while testimony fails through credulity and love of the marvellous. His maxim: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish." He adds that miracle reports cluster in "ignorant and barbarous nations" and cancel each other across rival religions. Whether this is an a priori barrier or a strong evidential presumption — and how it fares under Bayesian reconstructions — is actively debated.

### Compatibilism on free will

Hume held the dispute about liberty and necessity to be largely verbal. Everyone presupposes that actions show the same uniformity and constant conjunction with motives and character as nature does. **Liberty**, as actually meant, is "a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will." This is compatible with necessity as regularity, and requires it: an action disconnected from character would be chance, not freedom, and could not be praised or blamed. This **compatibilism** became the template for most later versions.

His overall stance is **mitigated skepticism**: the doubts are real, but nature cures what reason cannot — we dine, play backgammon, and believe — and awareness of reason's limits should make us modest rather than paralyzed.

## Signature arguments

**The problem of induction (*Enquiry* IV)**

1. All reasoning about unobserved matters of fact rests on the relation of cause and effect.
2. Knowledge of cause and effect comes only from experience, never from reasoning alone.
3. Inferring the unobserved from the observed therefore presupposes that the future will resemble the past — that nature's course will continue uniformly.
4. That presupposition cannot be proved demonstratively, since its denial is conceivable without contradiction, nor proved from experience, since that would assume exactly what is in question.
Therefore, inductive inference has no rational justification; it is the work of custom or habit, not of reason.

**Objection.** This sets the bar for justification too high. A practice need not be provable, demonstratively or otherwise, to be reasonable: reliabilists argue that induction is justified if it in fact reliably produces true beliefs, and pragmatic vindications argue it is the best strategy available whatever nature does.

**Reply.** Hume largely agrees about practice. His "sceptical solution" is that nature, through custom, cures what reason cannot justify: we cannot help expecting the future to resemble the past, and we should go on doing so. The argument targets rational foundations — a proof reason could give itself — not whether to rely on induction in life or science. Whether reliabilist and pragmatic replies answer Hume, or instead change the subject from justification to usefulness, is itself part of the continuing debate the argument started.

**The is–ought passage (*Treatise* 3.1.1)**

1. Moral systems proceed, for a while, from propositions about what is or is not the case.
2. They then switch, often imperceptibly, to propositions about what ought or ought not to be.
3. This "ought" expresses a new relation or affirmation, entirely different from the relations stated in the earlier propositions.
Therefore, anyone who derives an "ought" must explain how it follows from premises containing only "is"; until then, the deduction seems altogether inconceivable.

**Objection.** Hume may not be banning all derivations of ought from is. Naturalists — and some readings of Hume himself — argue that some oughts do follow from facts about human nature: claims about virtues as useful or agreeable to ourselves and others, for instance, are factual claims from which Hume's own moral conclusions appear to flow.

**Reply.** The enduring point, on this reading, is a demand for an explanation of the inference, not necessarily proof of an unbridgeable gap. Which reading is Hume's — a strict barrier or a challenge to explain the transition — remains a scholarly debate, and his own sentimentalist ethics can be recruited on both sides of it. What is not in dispute is the passage's effect: after Hume, a moral argument that slides from facts to values without marking the step looks unfinished, whatever one's theory says about whether the step can be justified.

Fuller reconstructions of the argument on induction are in the study guide: [*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*](../../study/hume-enquiry.md).

## Key works

- *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) — Anonymous; the masterpiece that "fell dead-born from the press."
- *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) — The clearer recasting of *Treatise* Book I; the best entry point.
- *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751) — His ethics recast; the work he considered his best.
- *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (posthumous, 1779) — On arguments for God's existence, especially design; who speaks for Hume is debated, by design.
- *Essays* (1741–42 and later) and *History of England* (1754–62) — The writings that made him famous in his lifetime.

## In their own words

> Every one will readily allow, that there is a considerable difference between the perceptions of the mind, when a man feels the pain of excessive heat, or the pleasure of moderate warmth, and when he afterwards recalls to his memory this sensation, or anticipates it by his imagination. These faculties may mimic or copy the perceptions of the senses; but they never can entirely reach the force and vivacity of the original sentiment.

— David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* §II (1748; public domain)

Hume opens the *Enquiry* with the distinction everything else depends on: impressions (live perceptions) versus ideas (their fainter copies in memory and imagination). Notice the deliberately homely test — the felt difference between pain and the thought of pain is something every reader can verify at once. From this modest starting point Hume will build his copy principle, and then use it as a detector: any philosophical term that cannot be traced to an impression is suspect.

## Contributions

Hume formulated the **problem of induction** canonically; reanalysed **causation** as regularity plus habit; dissolved the **self** into a bundle of perceptions; posed the **is–ought** challenge; founded modern **sentimentalist ethics**; gave **compatibilism** its modern form; and set the terms for assessing **miracle testimony** and the **design argument**. Underlying all is a method: test every concept by the impressions it copies, and where reason overreaches, give a natural history of why we believe anyway.

## Impact and legacy

Kant's testimony is most famous: Hume interrupted his "dogmatic slumber," and Kant's critical philosophy is largely an answer to Hume. The logical positivists adopted Hume's fork as a criterion of meaning. Philosophy of science still teaches induction through Hume; moral psychology inherits his sentimentalism (also through Adam Smith); Derek Parfit revives the bundle theory. The *Dialogues* and miracles essay remain standard obstacles for natural theology.

## Criticisms and debates

Approach these as open. First, the **"New Hume" debate**: did Hume merely say we cannot *know* objective necessary connections while believing they exist, or did he reduce causation to regularity and projection? Passages support both. Second, whether **is–ought** states a strict barrier or demands an explanation remains contested. Third, Thomas Reid charged that Hume escapes skepticism only by inconsistency, trusting "nature" where his arguments forbid trust; defenders reply the naturalism *is* the position. Fourth, Kantians argue causation as a condition of experience answers Hume; Humeans argue it relocates the problem. Fifth, scholars debate who speaks for Hume in the *Dialogues*.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Hume disproved causation."** He argued necessary connection has no impression-source in objects and inference rests on custom — while insisting we rightly rely on it.
- **"Hume said don't trust science."** He practiced experimental reasoning; his stance is mitigated skepticism, not rejection.
- **"Reason as slave of the passions means be irrational."** It is about motivation: reason alone cannot move us; it still governs truth.
- **"Is–ought bans all moral reasoning from facts."** It demands an explanation of the transition; whether it bans every derivation is debated.
- **"Hume proved miracles impossible."** His argument concerns testimony and evidence, not possibility.

## What to read first

1. *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Sections 2, 4–5 — impressions, the fork, and induction.
2. *Enquiry*, Sections 7, 10, and 12 — necessary connection, miracles, mitigated skepticism.
3. *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, Sections 1–5 — his own favorite.
4. *Treatise* 1.3, 1.4.6 with the Appendix, and Book III, Part I — causation, personal identity, is–ought.
5. *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Parts II and X–XII — the design argument.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the problem of induction premise by premise: inference to the unobserved presupposes that the future will resemble the past; that presupposition can be proved neither demonstratively nor from experience without circularity. Then assess one reply — reliabilist or pragmatic: does it justify induction, or change the subject?
2. Using the billiard balls, reconstruct causation as constant conjunction: what is actually perceived, and where does the idea of necessary connection come from, on Hume's account? How does this differ from saying causes are merely one thing after another?
3. Apply the miracles maxim to a fresh case: well-attested witnesses report an event violating a firmly established law of nature. Weigh the falsehood of the testimony against the fact reported, as Hume directs, and state what a wise person should believe.
4. Take a policy report establishing only that a program reduces suffering. Using the is–ought passage, explain what further step its author needs before concluding the program ought to be adopted — and how Hume's own appeal to sentiment supplies, or complicates, that step.
