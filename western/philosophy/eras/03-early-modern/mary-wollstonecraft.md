# Mary Wollstonecraft

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1759–1797
- **Place:** England
- **School/tradition:** Writer and philosopher of the Enlightenment and the French Revolutionary era
- **Known for:** *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), the founding text of modern feminism, which argues that women's apparent inferiority is the product of denied education, not nature, and demands equal schooling so women can become rational, independent citizens

**Responds to:** [Rousseau](jean-jacques-rousseau.md) · **Answered by:** the suffrage and women's-education movements and the twentieth-century feminists who recovered her — no single successor figure has an entry in this repo

## Biography
Wollstonecraft was born in London into a family sliding down the social scale: her father squandered his inheritance and drank, and violence at home marked her childhood — she later wrote of sleeping outside her mother's door to intercept his rages. Largely self-educated, she worked in the few employments open to respectable women: companion, schoolteacher, governess. In 1784 she founded a school at Newington Green with her sisters and her friend Fanny Blood, whose early death in Lisbon, where Wollstonecraft had traveled to nurse her, haunted her writing. The school failed, and a miserable governess post in Ireland followed — but she had begun to write, encouraged by the radical publisher Joseph Johnson, who published her *Thoughts on the Education of Daughters* (1787) and employed her as a reviewer and translator for his *Analytical Review*. Through Johnson's circle she met Thomas Paine, William Godwin, and other reformers, and argued religion and education with the dissenting minister Richard Price.

The French Revolution made her a public combatant. Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) provoked her *Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) — published anonymously, then under her name in the second edition, among the first and fiercest replies to Burke. Two years later, provoked partly by Talleyrand's proposal that French girls receive only domestic schooling, she wrote the *Rights of Woman* in a matter of weeks, dedicating it to him in open challenge. She then traveled to revolutionary Paris alone, witnessed the Terror, and wrote a history of the Revolution's first years. Her relationship with the American adventurer Gilbert Imlay, by whom she had a daughter, Fanny, collapsed; she twice attempted suicide. Recovering, she traveled in Scandinavia on business for Imlay, and the published *Letters Written during a Short Residence in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark* (1796) — part travel book, part meditation — became her most popular work in her lifetime. She married Godwin in 1797 and died that September, aged thirty-eight, of puerperal fever days after giving birth to a second daughter — Mary Shelley, the future author of *Frankenstein*. Godwin's well-meant *Memoirs* (1798), candid about her relationships and suicide attempts, scandalized the age and wrecked her reputation for a century.

## Major theories and concepts

### Reason, education, and manufactured inferiority
The *Vindication*'s engine is a single argument applied relentlessly. Women's minds appear frivolous and weak, Wollstonecraft concedes, but the cause is educational and social, not natural: girls are trained from infancy in cunning, softness, and "a preposterous desire to please," denied serious study and bodily exercise, and rewarded for beauty rather than understanding — then their resulting character is cited as proof they merit nothing better. If women share with men the God-given faculty of reason — and her theology insists they do, since virtue requires rational self-government — then justice and consistency demand the education that develops it. She puts the wager plainly: let women's faculties unfold, and if they truly are weaker, the case against equality can be remade; until then, arguing from produced incapacity to natural incapacity begs the question.

### Against Rousseau
Her sharpest theoretical target is Rousseau's *Émile* (1762), whose Book V designs Sophie's education entirely around pleasing and serving Émile. Wollstonecraft answers that an education aimed at attractiveness makes women artificial, dependent, and ultimately worse wives and mothers — the very roles conservatives claimed to protect. Her alternative is co-educational national schooling: boys and girls taught together in day schools, in the same subjects, with physical exercise, so that women become companions to their husbands rather than "alluring mistresses," and mothers capable of educating their own children. Equality of education, she argues, benefits the whole society: it produces better marriages, better citizens, and women who, if widowed, can support themselves rather than sinking into dependence.

### Rights, virtue, and independence
From the *Rights of Men* onward, Wollstonecraft grounds her politics in natural rights against Burke's reverence for inherited custom: inherited property, primogeniture, and standing armies corrupt, and a society is judged by the reason and virtue it cultivates in all its members. In the *Rights of Woman* she extends the logic: virtue is one standard for both sexes — a direct attack on the double standard of sexual morality — and women need civil and political standing, including a voice in representation, because duties cannot be demanded of beings denied the independence that makes duty possible. Her key term is independence: not isolation, but the material and intellectual capacity to govern oneself, without which "virtue" is obedience rebranded.

## Key works
- *Thoughts on the Education of Daughters* (1787) — her first book; practical and already critical of the accomplishments-curriculum for girls.
- *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) — the reply to Burke defending the Revolution, Price, and natural rights against prescription.
- *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) — the major work: education, Rousseau, sexual double standards, national co-education, and women's civil standing.
- *An Historical and Moral View of the Origin and Progress of the French Revolution* (1794) — her history of the Revolution's early phase, written in Paris.
- *Letters Written during a Short Residence in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark* (1796) — travel letters blending landscape, commerce, and reflections on society and motherhood.

## Contributions
Wollstonecraft founded modern feminist philosophy: she first systematically applied Enlightenment universalism — reason, rights, education — to sex, exposing the contradiction of proclaiming universal rights while excluding half of humanity. She also contributed to revolutionary-era debates on Burke, property, and education, and pioneered a confessional, first-person mode of philosophical travel writing.

## Impact and legacy
After Godwin's *Memoirs* made her name a byword, nineteenth-century reformers quietly used her arguments while avoiding her name; the suffrage and women's-education movements recovered her openly, and twentieth-century feminism canonized the *Vindication* as a founding document. Her daughter Mary Shelley's career, and the Wollstonecraft-Godwin circle's role in radical publishing, extend her influence through Romantic literature as well as political thought.

## Criticisms and debates
Critics from her day to ours have pressed several points. She often argues instrumentally — educate women to make better wives and mothers — and scholars debate how far her equality is intrinsic rather than justified by social utility, and how far her address to middle-class women limits the argument's reach. Her remarks on modesty and sexuality are stricter than modern readers expect, and her case rests partly on theological premises (an immortal soul accountable to God) that secular feminists have had to rebuild on other foundations. Others note the *Vindication*'s haste: it is repetitive and unfinished in plan, with a promised second volume never written. Defenders reply that the core argument — produced inferiority is no evidence of natural inferiority — stands independent of these framings, and that the instrumental appeals were the price of being heard in 1792.

## Misreadings to avoid
- She did not argue that women are identical to men in every respect; she argued that differences in character are unproven while education is unequal — the experiment has not been run.
- She is not only a feminist writer: the *Rights of Men*, the Revolution history, and the *Letters* make her a major voice in the wider radical Enlightenment.
- The *Vindication* is not a polite request for accomplishments; it demands institutional change — national co-education — and challenges men's power directly.

## What to read first
The *Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, chapters 1–3, gives the argument's core; the dedication to Talleyrand is a model statement of intent. Then sample the *Letters from Sweden* for her voice at its most personal, and pair the *Rights of Men* with Burke for the debate in full.
