# Gotthold Ephraim Lessing

*Era: Early Modern Philosophy (c. 1600 – 1800) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1729–1781
- **Place:** Born in Kamenz, Saxony; worked in Leipzig, Berlin, Breslau, Hamburg; ended as ducal librarian at Wolfenbüttel
- **School/tradition:** German Enlightenment (*Aufklärung*); dramatist, critic, and controversialist
- **Known for:** The ring parable in *Nathan the Wise* (1779), the aesthetics of *Laocoön* (1766), the "ugly broad ditch" between historical and rational truths, and *The Education of the Human Race* (1780)

**Responds to:** Lutheran orthodoxy and its biblical literalism, French classical drama, the rationalist theology of his century · **Answered by:** [Kant](immanuel-kant.md), [Schleiermacher](../04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md), [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md)

## Biography

Lessing was born in 1729 in Kamenz, the eldest surviving son of a Lutheran pastor — a household of solid learning and modest means. At the Fürstenschule St. Afra in Meissen and then Leipzig University he began in theology and drifted, to his father's dismay, toward the theater, medicine, and everything except a settled profession. What he chose instead was a new and precarious existence in Germany: the free professional writer, living by his pen. Berlin journalism, reviewing, and translation filled the 1750s; the Seven Years' War gave him five comparatively secure years as secretary to General von Tauentzien in Breslau, among officers and gamblers rather than scholars.

His plays made him the founder of modern German drama: *Miss Sara Sampson* (1755), the first German domestic tragedy; *Minna von Barnhelm* (1767), the great comedy of the war's aftermath; *Emilia Galotti* (1772), a tragedy of court corruption. From 1767 he was dramaturg of the Hamburg National Theatre, whose failure he converted into the *Hamburg Dramaturgy* (1767–69), still the sharpest sustained work of dramatic criticism in German. In 1770 he accepted the post that gave him a desk and a salary for the rest of his life: librarian of the Herzog August Bibliothek at Wolfenbüttel, one of Europe's great collections.

The librarian's chair became a pulpit. From 1774 to 1778 Lessing published, anonymously and under his editorial protection, the *Fragments of an Anonymous Author* — extracts from the vast apologetic-critical manuscript of the Hamburg orientalist Hermann Samuel Reimarus, which attacked the historical credibility of the resurrection narratives and of revealed religion generally. The resulting Fragments Controversy (*Fragmentenstreit*) with the Hamburg pastor Johann Melchior Goeze became the century's defining public fight over the Bible: when the Duke forbade him further theological polemic in 1778, Lessing answered that he would return to his "old pulpit, the theatre" — and wrote *Nathan the Wise* (1779). Worn by work and by his wife Eva König's death in 1778, he died at Braunschweig in 1781.

## Major theories and concepts

### The ugly broad ditch

Lessing's most quoted philosophical argument is in the short essay *On the Proof of the Spirit and of Power* (1777). Miracles and fulfilled prophecies, he grants, might have compelled belief for eyewitnesses; but reports of them, centuries later, have no such force. "Accidental truths of history can never become the proof of necessary truths of reason." Between the two yawns what he called an "ugly broad ditch" (*der garstige breite Graben*) "across which I cannot get, much as I have often and earnestly tried to make the leap." No amount of reliable testimony about contingent past events can ground the timeless, necessary truths on which doctrine claims to rest. The argument is not that history is unknowable, but that it is the wrong *kind* of thing to bear that weight — a category distinction that would structure German theology from Kant to Kierkegaard and beyond.

### The ring parable

*Nathan the Wise*, set in Crusader Jerusalem, dramatizes the answer Lessing's ditch argument makes room for. Asked by Saladin which of the three monotheisms is true, the Jewish merchant Nathan tells of a father with a miraculous ring, who, loving his three sons equally, has two perfect copies made; after his death the sons quarrel over the genuine ring, and the judge's verdict is that the true ring's power — to make its wearer beloved of God and men — must prove itself in each son's life, over "thousands and thousands of years." Lessing adapted the tale from Boccaccio's *Decameron* (where it answers a trick question and earns the teller his safety) and gave it its modern form: the competing revealed religions share one descent; their truth cannot be settled by pedigree or document; it is to be shown, if anywhere, in ethical fruitfulness — and meanwhile the sons are told to live as if their own ring were true, in humility and tolerance.

### Laocoön and the limits of the arts

*Laocoön: An Essay on the Limits of Painting and Poetry* (1766) takes its occasion from the famous Hellenistic statue of the Trojan priest and his sons in the serpents' grip: the sculpted Laocoön does not scream, as Virgil's does — why not? Against Winckelmann's explanation (Greek nobility of soul), Lessing argues from *medium*: the visual arts arrange signs side by side in space and can therefore properly represent bodies at a single moment; poetry arranges signs successively in time and therefore properly represents actions. Visual art must choose the "pregnant moment" (*fruchtbarer Augenblick*) — the instant that leaves the imagination free play, never the climax, which freezes and disgusts (a screaming mouth fixed forever in marble). Hence Homer shows Helen's beauty only through its effect on the Trojan elders, and describes Achilles' shield by narrating its making. Aesthetics here becomes semiotics: each art does well what its signs can do, and "sister arts" rhetoric that confuses them corrupts both.

### The Education of the Human Race

His last prose work (1780) recasts revelation as pedagogy: "What education is to the individual man, revelation is to the whole human race." The Old Testament is the primer for humanity's childhood — a child learns first by rewards and punishments; the New Testament is the better primer of youth, teaching the immortality of the soul and purity of motive; and a "third age" is coming, prefigured by Joachim of Fiore's "eternal gospel," in which men will do right because it is right, without need of either book. The scheme is simultaneously a defense (revelation is not false, only graduated) and a dissolution (what is graduated can be completed and outgrown). Whether Lessing meant it as sincere theology, as a strategic shelter under censorship, or as something between is still genuinely disputed — he published the first fifty-three paragraphs as editor of another man's manuscript, a characteristic indirection.

## Key works

- *Laocoön* (1766) — The limits of painting and poetry argued from the nature of their signs; the founding text of medium-specific aesthetics.
- *Hamburg Dramaturgy* (1767–69) — Reviews turned philosophy of drama: Aristotle's *Poetics* recovered against French classicism, Shakespeare vindicated, the bourgeois tragedy theorized.
- *Emilia Galotti* (1772) — Tragedy of a prince's desire and a father's desperate defense; political drama under absolutism.
- *On the Proof of the Spirit and of Power* (1777) — The "ugly broad ditch": historical contingency cannot prove rational necessity.
- *Nathan the Wise* (1779) — The ring parable; the Enlightenment's canonical plea for religious tolerance, written after the censor closed his theological pulpit.
- *The Education of the Human Race* (1780) — Revelation as the progressive education of humanity, pointing beyond itself.

## Contributions

Lessing created the modern German literary public sphere almost single-handedly: the independent critic, judging by reason rather than court or church, was his invention as a social role. Philosophically he bequeathed three durable instruments — the ditch argument against historical apologetics, the medium argument in aesthetics, and the idea of religious truth proved ethically and historically rather than dogmatically. His *Dramaturgy* fixed the German reading of Aristotle and Shakespeare for a century, and the ring parable remains the standard parable of inter-religious ethics.

## Impact and legacy

The largest legacy arrived posthumously, and detonated. In 1785 Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi published his *Letters on the Doctrine of Spinoza*, reporting a conversation of 1780 in which Lessing had confessed himself a Spinozist — "there is no philosophy other than the philosophy of Spinoza" — and endorsed the *hen kai pan*, the One-and-All. Moses Mendelssohn, Lessing's closest friend, rushed to defend a "purified" Lessing; Jacobi insisted on the confession; the **Pantheism Controversy** (*Pantheismusstreit*) consumed German letters, drew in Kant's essay on orientation in thinking (1786), and made Spinoza — hitherto philosophy's great heretic — unavoidable for the generation of Herder, Goethe, Schelling, and Hegel. Whether Lessing was really a Spinozist systematician, a provocative talker, or something subtler is still argued; that the argument happened is his doing. His philosophy-of-history sketch in the *Education* fed directly into Herder and into Hegel's lectures; Kierkegaard's "leap" and his whole attack on historical Christianity reprise Lessing's ditch, as Kierkegaard knew and said. *Nathan* has never left the stage.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Was Lessing a Spinozist?** Jacobi's report is our main evidence, and Jacobi had a fideist agenda: to show that all rational philosophy ends in Spinozism and atheism, hence one must leap to faith. Mendelssohn countered with a refined, enlightened Spinozism compatible with theism. Modern scholarship is split between readers who take the confession at face value, those who stress Lessing's irony and love of paradox in conversation, and those who find his considered view in the *Education*'s progressive theism — the texts support each partially.
- **Provocation or program?** Lessing called himself a seeker: in a famous passage, given the choice between God's eternal truth and the eternal striving for it, he would choose the striving. Critics from his day to ours ask whether the ditch argument and the ring parable express a philosophy or a brilliant skepticism that never lands; defenders answer that the landing place *is* the ethical-pedagogical scheme of the *Education*.
- **The parable's even-handedness.** Some readers, then and now, object that the ring parable dissolves real doctrinal differences into etiquette; others that it covertly favors a rationalised, post-dogmatic religion — a fourth ring. Both complaints register its actual force.
- **Tolerance's limits.** *Nathan* preaches tolerance among the three monotheisms; whether Lessing's scheme extends further, and his era's blind spots about those outside the family of religions altogether, are live critical questions.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **The ring parable does not say all religions are equally true.** It says the genuine ring cannot be identified by inspection, only proved in conduct over generations — and the sons are each told to strive as if his own were genuine. Agnosticism about pedigree, not relativism about truth.
- **The "ditch" is not an argument that historical claims are false.** Lessing grants the reports may be as true as historical reports get; his point is that truths of that *type* cannot found necessary truths of reason.
- **Lessing was not simply a deist or an atheist.** Both labels were weapons in the Pantheism Controversy, wielded by parties who needed him one way or the other; his published positions are more mobile than either camp's portrait.
- **Laocoön is not only about a statue.** The sculpture is the test case for a general semiotic theory of artistic media.

## What to read first

1. *Nathan the Wise*, Act III (the ring parable in context) — twenty pages that contain the man.
2. *On the Proof of the Spirit and of Power* (1777) — the ditch argument in his own brief compass.
3. *Laocoön*, the first sixteen chapters — the medium argument with its examples.
4. *The Education of the Human Race* (complete) — short, strange, and the seed of a century of philosophy of history.
