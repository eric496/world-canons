# Jean-Paul Sartre

## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1905–1980
- **Place:** Paris, France
- **School/tradition:** Existentialism — its leading public figure; philosopher, novelist, playwright, and political activist
- **Known for:** “Existence precedes essence,” radical freedom, and the analysis of bad faith

**Responds to:** [Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md) · **Answered by:** [Merleau-Ponty](maurice-merleau-ponty.md), [Beauvoir](simone-de-beauvoir.md), [Murdoch](iris-murdoch.md)

## Biography

Sartre was born in Paris in 1905. His father, a naval officer, died when Sartre was fifteen months old, and he was raised in his maternal grandfather’s book-lined household — an upbringing he later anatomised in his memoir *Words* (1964), where the gifted child performs precocity for admiring adults. A childhood eye condition left him with a pronounced squint he would joke about for the rest of his life.

At the École Normale Supérieure he met Simone de Beauvoir in 1929, when both were preparing for the *agrégation* in philosophy; Sartre placed first, Beauvoir second, the youngest person ever to pass at that time. Their lifelong partnership — intellectually intense, deliberately non-marital, and by agreement open to other relationships, with consequences for third parties that later readers have judged more critically — became one of the famous intellectual alliances of the century. Each read and criticized everything the other wrote. How influence ran between them is discussed below; neither was the other’s pupil.

Sartre taught in lycées through the 1930s, spending 1933–34 in Berlin studying Husserl’s phenomenology — the toolkit he would fuse with Hegel, Heidegger, and Descartes. His novel *Nausea* appeared in 1938. Drafted in 1939, he was captured in 1940 and spent nine months in a prisoner-of-war camp, where captivity gave him an experience of solidarity he later said turned his thinking from individual freedom towards social responsibility. Released in 1941, he returned to occupied Paris, taught, wrote, and joined a small, largely ineffective resistance group — a wartime record later debated: neither collaborator nor heroic resister, but a writer who became a symbol of resistance largely through post-war prominence.

His major treatise, *Being and Nothingness*, appeared in 1943. After the Liberation he became, almost overnight, the emblematic intellectual of the Left Bank. With Beauvoir, Merleau-Ponty, and others he co-founded the journal *Les Temps modernes* in 1945, and his lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism* that year packed a hall and made “existentialism” a household word — to his own later regret about its simplifications.

The post-war decades were political as much as literary. Sartre supported the French Communist Party as a fellow traveler from 1952 to 1956, broke with it over the Soviet invasion of Hungary, later took Maoist-aligned positions around 1968, and campaigned against the French war in Algeria — his preface to Frantz Fanon’s *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) endorsed anti-colonial violence in terms that remain controversial. He also condemned the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968. In 1964 he refused the Nobel Prize in Literature, saying a writer must not be turned into an institution. Failing eyesight ended his writing in the 1970s. He died in 1980; tens of thousands followed his coffin through Paris.

## Major theories and concepts

### Existence precedes essence

For a manufactured object, Sartre says, essence precedes existence: a paper knife is designed — its purpose and nature fixed in the maker’s mind — before it is made. Traditional religion and much philosophy treated humans the same way: God, or human nature, supplies the blueprint first. Sartre’s atheistic existentialism reverses the order: “existence precedes essence.” A person first exists — is thrown into the world — and only then makes themselves through choices. There is no pre-written nature to discover, excuse oneself by, or fail to live up to.

This is not the claim that we choose everything about ourselves (see facticity below). It is the claim that what our given characteristics *mean*, and what becomes of them, is settled by what we do. Two people with the same timid temperament can make of it a life’s handicap, a discipline of caution, or material for comedy. The temperament is given; its significance in a life is not.

### The in-itself, the for-itself, and nothingness

*Being and Nothingness* divides being into two regions. The *in-itself* (*en-soi*) is the being of things: a stone is fully, solidly what it is, with no gap between itself and itself. The *for-itself* (*pour-soi*) is consciousness, and it is the opposite: it exists by *not* coinciding with itself. To be conscious is to be aware of a table *and*, in the same moment, non-thetically aware of oneself perceiving it; to be able to ask what one is, and thereby already stand apart from any answer.

Sartre expresses this by saying the for-itself introduces *nothingness* into the world. Negation is not found in things — the in-itself contains no absences — but a person entering a café looking for Pierre experiences his *absence* as a real feature of the scene. Imagination, questioning, and freedom all rest on this power to hold what is at a distance and set against it what is not, might be, or ought to be.

### Radical freedom, facticity, and anguish

Because consciousness is this gap, Sartre concludes that we are radically free — “condemned to be free.” Condemned, because we did not choose to be free and cannot resign from it: even refusing to choose is a choice, and even obedience hands responsibility to the one who obeys. Freedom here does not mean getting what you want or escaping constraint. A prisoner is unfree in circumstance but, for Sartre, still free in the stance he takes and the meaning his situation has within his projects. Critics have always pressed this example hard, asking whether such interior freedom honors or trivialises real oppression; Sartre’s later Marxist work, with its emphasis on scarcity and structures, is partly an attempt to answer them.

Freedom is always *situated*. Sartre calls the given side *facticity*: your body, birthplace, past, social position, and eventual death. Freedom is what you make of what was made of you, to use his later summary. Awareness of the total responsibility this entails is *anguish*: not fear of a particular danger, but dizziness before the fact that nothing — not character, not orders, not passion — finally decides in your place. Most of social life, Sartre thinks, is organized flight from anguish.

### Bad faith

The flight takes a characteristic form: *bad faith* (*mauvaise foi*), a self-deception in which a person treats themselves as a thing with a fixed nature when it suits them, or as pure unconstrained freedom when that suits better. His famous example is the café waiter who plays at being a waiter too completely — movements a little too precise, identity poured into the role as if a waiter were a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell. The opposite evasion also counts: “I couldn’t help it, that’s just how I’m wired” collapses freedom into facticity; “circumstances don’t touch the real me” pretends facticity away. Both are bad faith because each denies one pole of a being that is always both.

Sartre is careful that bad faith is not ordinary lying: liar and lied-to are the same person, which is precisely the puzzle his account of consciousness as divided, self-distancing is built to explain. Whether it fully succeeds — how can the deceiver hide from himself what he must know in order to hide it? — remains a standard objection, and a productive one in philosophy of self-deception ever since.

### The Look and the Other

Other people are not simply objects in my world, because they can *look back*. In Sartre’s analysis of *the Look*, catching another’s gaze — or even hearing footsteps in a park and realizing I am seen — transforms my experience: I become aware of myself as an object in someone else’s world, fixed by judgments I cannot control. Shame is his key example: I am ashamed of myself *as I appear to the Other*. This is the real sense of the famous line from his play *No Exit* (1944), “Hell is other people”: three dead characters, locked together, torture each other eternally by being mirrors in which each is frozen as what they were. It is not a claim that company is unpleasant or that solitude is salvation; without others, Sartre argues, self-knowledge itself would be impossible, and love, language, and pride all depend on the same structure that makes shame possible.

### From existentialism to Marxism

The *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) asks what the early philosophy missed: history and material constraint. Its key ideas include *scarcity*, the background that makes people rivals, and the *practico-inert* — past action hardened into institutions and market forces that then constrain their makers like alien things. People waiting for a bus form a “series” whose unity is imposed from outside. The later Sartre does not retract freedom, but the reconciliation with Marxist structural explanation is strained, and readers still argue over whether the two Sartres form one philosophy or two.

## Key works

- *Nausea* (1938) — Novel; its diarist Roquentin discovers the sheer, gratuitous existence of things — the famous chestnut-root scene. Philosophy as lived nausea at contingency.
- *Being and Nothingness* (1943) — The systematic treatise: in-itself/for-itself, nothingness, bad faith, the Look. Long and difficult; most readers use selections.
- *No Exit* (1944) — One-act play; three dead strangers in a room that is hell. Source of “Hell is other people,” best read whole — it takes an evening.
- *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946; lecture 1945) — The short public defense of existentialism against Catholic and Communist critics. Lucid, quotable, and later judged by Sartre himself to be oversimplified.
- *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) — The vast attempt to ground Marxism in individual praxis under scarcity. Admired, rarely read cover to cover.
- *Words* (1964) — Autobiography of his childhood; also a farewell, at the time, to literature’s pretensions. Among his most beautifully written books.

## Contributions

Sartre gave phenomenology concepts — bad faith, the Look, facticity — that name experiences readers recognize immediately and that passed into ordinary language. He showed philosophy could be done in novels and plays without dilution. His accounts of self-deception, shame, and interpersonal conflict remain working tools in philosophy of mind, social philosophy, and existential psychotherapy, and his prefaces materially amplified anti-colonial thought, notably Fanon’s.

## Impact and legacy

Post-war French culture ran through Sartre for two decades: existentialism set the terms for Merleau-Ponty, early Camus (before their bitter 1952 rupture), Beauvoir’s independent philosophy, and a generation of writers worldwide. Fanon and postcolonial theory took up his analyses of the colonized person fixed by the colonizer’s gaze. Structuralists and post-structuralists then defined themselves against his humanism — Foucault’s “death of man” is unintelligible without Sartre as foil — so even his eclipse transmitted his influence.

## Criticisms and debates

The coherence of radical freedom is the perennial objection: if situation genuinely constrains, in what sense is the prisoner or the oppressed person free, and does the doctrine quietly blame victims? Sartre’s defenders point to facticity and to the *Critique*; critics reply that the early rhetoric outran those qualifications. His political judgments draw separate fire: fellow-traveling in 1952–56, and defenses of revolutionary and anti-colonial violence, are criticized as subordinating evidence and victims to commitment, while supporters note his breaks over Hungary and Czechoslovakia and his anti-colonial record when it carried real personal risk in France. Finally, the Beauvoir question: older scholarship treated Sartre as the system-builder and Beauvoir as popularizer. Current scholarship documents extensive mutual influence — shared drafts, concepts developed in conversation and in her earlier works — and increasingly holds that some ideas credited to Sartre were developed jointly or first by Beauvoir. The exact division is debated; the one-way picture is not.

## Misreadings to avoid

- “Hell is other people” is not misanthropy. It is about being fixed under the Other’s Look — and others are equally necessary to selfhood.
- Freedom is not licence or optimism. For Sartre it is a burden experienced as anguish, exercised always inside a situation not chosen.
- Bad faith is not simply lying, hypocrisy, or having a job role. It is the specific self-deception of denying either freedom or facticity.
- Sartre did not think you can become anything whatsoever by willing it. Essence is made, but from materials, and within a world, you did not make.

## What to read first

1. *Existentialism Is a Humanism* — one sitting; the map of the territory, with the caveat about simplification kept in mind.
2. *No Exit* — the Look made theatrical; pairs directly with the lecture.
3. *Nausea* — the feel of contingency that the concepts formalize.
4. Selections from *Being and Nothingness* on bad faith and freedom, ideally with a commentary; save the *Critique* for a later, Marx-focused pass.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct "existence precedes essence" through the paper-knife contrast: for the artefact, a maker's concept comes first; why, on Sartre's atheism, can no blueprint precede a human life — and what fills the gap left by the absent blueprint?
2. Analyze the café waiter and the claim "I couldn't help it — that's just how I'm wired" as opposite forms of bad faith. Which pole of the human condition — freedom or facticity — does each deny, and why is bad faith not simply lying?
3. Press the standard objection: if a prisoner remains free in the stance he takes, does radical freedom trivialise real oppression? Then give Sartre's best reply, drawing on facticity and the later turn toward scarcity and the practico-inert.
4. A student says: "I'm just not a maths person, so there's no point in trying." Analyze the remark in Sartre's terms: what belongs to facticity, where bad faith enters, and what would anguish consist in here?
