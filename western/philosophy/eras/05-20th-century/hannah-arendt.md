# Hannah Arendt

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1906–1975
- **Place:** Born in Linden (Hanover), Germany; exiled to Paris then New York
- **School/tradition:** Political theory, phenomenology
- **Known for:** Politics as public speech and action among distinct equals; totalitarianism as a new form of rule; and the "banality of evil" — great crimes committed by thoughtless bureaucrats

**Responds to:** [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Jaspers](karl-jaspers.md) · **Answered by:** Jürgen Habermas and later theorists of the public sphere — outside this repo's entries

## Biography

Hannah Arendt was born Johanna Cohn Arendt in 1906 into a secular Jewish family in Linden, near Hanover, and grew up largely in Königsberg, Kant's city. Her father died when she was seven; she was raised by her mother, Martha. Precocious, she read Kant and Kierkegaard as a teenager and chose philosophy early.

At Marburg in 1924 she studied with Martin Heidegger, whose teaching electrified a generation. She and Heidegger — married, seventeen years older — had a brief affair she kept private for life; their correspondence appeared only after her death. Reducing her work to a reply to Heidegger misses its independence. She studied a semester with Edmund Husserl at Freiburg and took her doctorate at Heidelberg in 1929 under Karl Jaspers, on love in St. Augustine. Jaspers, who valued communication and public reason over closed systems, remained a lifelong friend and model.

Her study of Rahel Varnhagen, a 19th-century Jewish salon hostess, became her route into a lifelong question: what does it mean to be offered assimilation at the price of self-erasure? Hitler's rise in 1933 ended any academic future in Germany. Briefly arrested by the Gestapo while researching antisemitic propaganda for the Zionist Federation, she fled to Paris, worked for Jewish relief organizations helping young Jews emigrate to Palestine, and married Heinrich Blücher, a former communist and self-taught philosopher who became her closest interlocutor. When France fell in 1940 she was interned at Gurs as an "enemy alien," escaped, and reached New York in 1941, effectively stateless — a condition she would analyze as losing not a home but a place in the world where one's actions and opinions matter.

In New York she wrote for the German-Jewish newspaper *Aufbau*, held research and editorial jobs, and wrote the book that made her reputation. *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951) put Nazism and Stalinism in one analytical frame early in the Cold War and argued both demanded explanation, not just condemnation. *The Human Condition* (1958) followed. Suspicious of academic schools, including any "Arendtian" one, she held secure posts only late — at the University of Chicago and the New School — after becoming the first woman appointed to a full professorship at Princeton, as a visiting professor.

In 1961 *The New Yorker* sent her to Jerusalem to report the trial of Adolf Eichmann, the SS officer who organized the transport of Jews to extermination camps, captured by Israeli agents in Argentina. Her report, published as *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963), caused her career's largest controversy (below). She spent her last decade on revolution, civil disobedience, and violence, and on the unfinished *Life of the Mind*. She died in New York in December 1975, the first page of its final volume in her typewriter.

The Eichmann controversy needs factual care. In court Eichmann did not present, to Arendt, as a fanatical monster or sadist, but as a careerist speaking in clichés and officialese, seemingly unable to articulate another's viewpoint. From this came her subtitle. Two further moves ignited outrage. She criticized the prosecution's staging of the trial and parts of its legal framing — while explicitly endorsing Eichmann's death sentence. And she wrote harshly about some Jewish Councils (*Judenräte*), arguing their cooperation in compiling lists and administering ghettos had made deportation run more smoothly, and asking whether deaths might have been fewer without it — which many readers heard as counterfactual blame of people acting under terror with no good options. Gershom Scholem, in an open exchange, charged that she lacked *Ahavat Israel*, love of the Jewish people. Friendships ended; campaigns denounced the book. Arendt replied that she reported what she saw, that love could not require falsifying judgment, and that understanding is not forgiving. Later historians, notably Bettina Stangneth using Eichmann's Argentina writings, have argued he was a committed ideological antisemite performing ordinariness in Jerusalem. Most scholars treat that evidence seriously while debating whether it refutes or qualifies her broader claim about thoughtlessness.

## Major theories and concepts

### Totalitarianism as a new form of rule

In *Origins*, totalitarianism is not dictatorship turned up louder. A tyrant breaks laws to seize power; a totalitarian movement aims to remake human beings. Its tools are ideology — one idea, race or History, from which everything is deduced — and terror, which continues after all opposition is destroyed. Its characteristic institution is the camp, where inmates are stripped of legal personhood, then moral personhood, then individuality, until people are made *superfluous*: killable without consequence.

Her path ran from imperialism and the post-1918 collapse of nation-states to mass statelessness. Stateless people found supposedly universal "rights of man" unenforceable without a political community to guarantee them. Hence the *right to have rights*: before any particular right, a person needs membership in a world where rights can be claimed. Written about interwar refugees, the argument is why *Origins* still frames debates on refugees today. Its tension: historians debate how well one category fits both Nazism and Stalinism, and whether she neglects economics and local collaboration. She revised the book herself and never claimed it explained every dictatorship.

### The vita activa: labor, work, and action

*The Human Condition* distinguishes three activities in the *vita activa*:

- **Labor** keeps bodies alive: cyclical, necessary activity — cooking, cleaning — that leaves nothing durable. Its condition is life.
- **Work** fabricates a durable world: a table, a house, a book that outlasts its making. Its condition is *worldliness*.
- **Action** — speech and deeds among others — neither makes nor survives. In it a person discloses *who* they are, distinct from *what* they are (talents, roles). Its conditions are plurality and natality.

Example: a hospital cleaner labors — the ward is dirty again tomorrow. The hospital's builder did work — the building stands for decades. A nurse who speaks at a public inquiry into unsafe care, risking her standing to begin a changed practice, acts: no one can control what follows, and who she is is revealed in the doing.

Arendt's provocation is that modernity inverted the hierarchy: we became a society of laborers and consumers, glorifying jobholding while the durable world decays into consumables and public action shrinks. She is not simply romanticising Athens, whose public realm rested on slavery and the exclusion of women — a fact she acknowledges but, critics argue, underweights. The diagnosis stands: if everything becomes labor, nothing lasts and no one truly appears.

### Plurality and natality

*Plurality*: humans are equal enough to understand one another and distinct enough that no one repeats anyone else. Politics exists because of it — if we were identical, speech would be unnecessary; if wholly different, impossible.

*Natality* is its twin. Against Heidegger, who organized existence around being-toward-death, Arendt organized hers around birth: each newborn is a new beginning, and action is possible because beginners keep arriving. "The miracle that saves the world... is ultimately the fact of natality." Forgiveness and promises answer action's hazards — irreversibility and unpredictability: forgiveness releases us from what was done, promises stabilise what is to come.

### The banality of evil

Her most misunderstood phrase came from watching Eichmann. She did *not* claim his crimes were banal, evil trivial, or Eichmann innocent or a mere cog — she agreed he should hang. Her claim concerned the *doer*: a perpetrator of enormous crimes could lack diabolical depth or much ideological reflection and instead display *thoughtlessness* — not stupidity, but failure to think from another's standpoint, armored by clichés, ambition, and regime language. "The longer one listened to him," she wrote, "the more obvious it became that his inability to speak was closely connected with an inability to *think*, namely, to think from the standpoint of somebody else."

The mechanism she generalized is bureaucratic: evil administered through timetables, euphemisms ("evacuation," "final solution"), and divided responsibility, each participant handling a fragment. No monster is required; advancement and conformity can suffice when judgment is switched off. The tension, sharpened by later archival work, is how far the portrait fits Eichmann himself versus how far it names a real mechanism in others.

### Thinking and judging

Her late work asked what resists thoughtlessness. Not a rulebook, but activities: *thinking* as internal dialogue — the "two-in-one" of talking with oneself — whose by-product is conscience, since you must live with your internal partner; and *judging*, drawn from Kant's aesthetics, for particular cases no rule covers. Judging exercises an "enlarged mentality," considering imagined standpoints of others without abandoning one's own — for Arendt, the political faculty par excellence.

## Key works

- **The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951)** — Antisemitism, imperialism, and totalitarian rule; source of the "right to have rights." The third part is most read.
- **The Human Condition (1958)** — Her central philosophy: labor, work, action; plurality and natality.
- **On Revolution (1963)** — American versus French revolutions; founding a durable space for freedom as revolution's real achievement.
- **Eichmann in Jerusalem (1963)** — Trial report; source of "the banality of evil." Read with its postscript answering early critics.
- **Between Past and Future (1961)** — Essays including "What Is Freedom?"; her most accessible prose.
- **Men in Dark Times (1968)** — Portraits including Jaspers, Rosa Luxemburg, and Walter Benjamin.
- **The Life of the Mind (posthumous, 1978)** — Unfinished work on thinking and willing; judging survives as notes and lectures.

## Contributions

Arendt created concepts that had no usable prior form: totalitarianism as a distinct regime type; the labor/work/action diagnostic; natality set against mortality; the "right to have rights" as human rights' foundation problem; and the banality of evil as a question about judgment in bureaucratic systems. She also modelled political theory done from historical events and stories rather than systems — theory accountable to what happened.

## Impact and legacy

She reshaped debates on the public sphere (in dialogue and disagreement with Jürgen Habermas), democratic action, and civil disobedience, and distinguished power from violence sharply: power arises when people act together; violence signals power's failure. Beyond academia, *Origins* frames scholarship and journalism on refugees and rightlessness, while "banality of evil" entered common language — often in the sloganised form she resisted. Renewed attention after 2016, amid debates on authoritarianism and lying in politics — a subject she analyzed acutely — returned her books to public argument.

## Criticisms and debates

The Eichmann debate remains open. Evidence of Eichmann's ideological antisemitism (Stangneth; David Cesarani) leads many historians to conclude Arendt misread the man, whether because he performed for the court or because she generalized too fast. Defenders reply her claim concerned a *type* of perpetrator and a failure of thinking, not a complete biography, and that testimony is itself a source to read critically. No verdict settles both the history and the concept.

On the Jewish Councils, many historians argue the councils had no meaningful choices, that she relied on early sources, and that her tone blamed victims. Others argue she asked a genuine, if painful, question about cooperation and responsibility that inquiry cannot rule out. The argument concerns evidence, counterfactuals, and the ethics of judgment.

Her 1959 essay "Reflections on Little Rock," opposing federally enforced school desegregation on the ground that education was social rather than political, has been widely criticized — including by admirers — as failing her own insight about public appearance when applied to racial equality. She privately conceded to Ralph Ellison that she had not understood the sacrifice involved. Her totalitarianism account is also charged with underplaying economics and differences between Nazi and Soviet rule, her Greek model with romanticising a sphere built on exclusion, and her sharp distinctions (labor/work, social/political) with rigidity: real activities mix all three.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Banality of evil" does **not** mean the Holocaust was banal, perpetrators are excused, or Eichmann had no choices. Arendt insisted on his guilt and supported his death sentence.
- She did not celebrate violence or revolution as such; she separated power from violence.
- She resists classification as left or right, liberal or conservative — and resisted "philosopher" itself, preferring "political theorist."
- Her praise of action is not advice to be an activist about everything; action is rare and risky and needs institutions and promises to stabilise it.

## What to read first

1. The essays "What Is Freedom?" and "What Is Authority?" in *Between Past and Future*.
2. *The Human Condition*, Chapters I and V, slowly; the Sputnik-era prologue sets the stakes.
3. *Eichmann in Jerusalem* in full, postscript included, paired with a recent historical treatment of Eichmann — take away the debate, not the slogan.
4. The "Decline of the Nation-State" chapter of *Origins*, for the refugee question running through her life.
