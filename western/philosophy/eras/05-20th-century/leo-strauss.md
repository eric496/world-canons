# Leo Strauss

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1899–1973
- **Place:** Germany, then the United States; professor at the University of Chicago from 1949
- **School/tradition:** Political philosophy; history of political thought
- **Known for:** Esoteric writing and the art of careful reading, the quarrel of the ancients and the moderns, the three waves of modernity, *Natural Right and History* (1953)

**Responds to:** [Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md) (whose lectures formed and alarmed him), [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md), the classical political philosophy of Plato, Xenophon, Maimonides and Alfarabi, which he sought to recover · **Answered by:** a large school of students and grand-students in political science, and a comparably large body of critics — both continuations are part of the record

## Biography

Leo Strauss was born in Kirchhain, in Hesse, in 1899, into an Orthodox Jewish family, and took his doctorate at Hamburg in 1921 with a dissertation on Jacobi supervised by Ernst Cassirer. In Freiburg he attended Husserl's lectures and, decisively, heard Heidegger, whose interpretation of Aristotle he later said showed a brilliance beside which his other teachers seemed to fade — and whose political trajectory became for Strauss the standing proof of philosophy's modern crisis. He worked at the Academy for the Scientific Study of Judaism in Berlin, publishing *Spinoza's Critique of Religion* (1930), a study of Hobbes in 1936, and left Germany in 1932 on a fellowship that took him to Paris and Cambridge — a departure that became permanent exile when the Nazis took power the following year. In 1937 he emigrated to the United States, teaching at the New School for Social Research before moving in 1949 to the University of Chicago, where he remained until 1969, building the most consequential teaching practice in twentieth-century American political science: generations of students trained to read a small canon of great books slowly, literally and whole. He spent his last years partly at St. John's College, Annapolis, and died there in 1973.

At Chicago he published the books that define him: *On Tyranny* (1948), a minute reading of Xenophon's *Hiero* (later editions add his exchange with Alexandre Kojève); *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952); *Natural Right and History* (1953); *Thoughts on Machiavelli* (1958); *The City and Man* (1964); and *Socrates and Aristophanes* (1966). Strauss wrote no system and founded his authority on interpretation: the recovery, as he put it, of political philosophy as the quest for the right order of human life, against a social science that had declared such questions unscientific.

## Major theories and concepts

### Persecution and the art of writing

Strauss's most famous thesis, stated in *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952), is that many pre-modern philosophers wrote **esoterically**: where a society punishes heterodoxy, a thoughtful writer addresses two audiences at once — an exoteric surface teaching that upholds the opinions on which the city rests, and a teaching **between the lines**, recoverable by attentive readers through deliberate contradictions, conspicuous silences, repetitions, and the placement of key statements at the center of a work. Strauss's exemplary case is the medieval Jewish and Islamic falāsifa — Alfarabi and Maimonides — writing under revealed law; he extended the practice, in varying degrees, to figures from Xenophon to Spinoza and Locke. The thesis is partly historical (persecution was real, and writers admitted accommodating to it) and partly hermeneutic: it licenses a method of reading in which the surface of a text is evidence about its author's prudence, not a transparent window on his thought. How far the licence extends is the central scholarly dispute about Strauss, taken up below.

### The quarrel of the ancients and the moderns

Strauss reframed the history of political thought as a **quarrel** that modernity has forgotten it lost. Classical political philosophy — Socratic in origin — asked how human beings ought to live and what regime is best by nature, treating the city as answerable to a standard it does not create: **natural right**, the just and noble known by unassisted reason. Modern political philosophy begins, on his account, when Machiavelli deliberately lowered the standard to what is effective — how men do live, not how they ought to — trading the best regime for the reliable one, virtue for the conquest of fortune and then of nature. The quarrel is not antiquarian: Strauss held that the modern project, worked out across centuries, ends in the crisis of our time — a West that can no longer justify its own deepest commitments (freedom, equality, human rights) in the rational terms it demands, because it has dismantled the idea of nature that once grounded them.

### The three waves of modernity

His essay "The Three Waves of Modernity" gives the crisis a plot. The **first wave** — Machiavelli, then Hobbes and Locke — founds modernity on the effective truth: politics reoriented from ends to beginnings, rights from duties, nature from standard to material for conquest. The **second wave** begins with Rousseau, who saw that the first wave's bourgeois had lost both virtue and nature, and tried to recover both through history and the general will — a turn Kant and Hegel complete, replacing nature with history as the horizon of meaning. The **third wave** begins with Nietzsche, who saw that history itself dissolves every standard it was meant to ground, and drew the conclusion: nihilism, the will's self-assertion in a valueless world, radicalized in Heidegger's thought. Modernity is thus not one error but a sequence of self-corrections, each more radical than the last and each ending where it began — in the loss of any ground for the values modernity proclaims. Strauss's own response was deliberately austere: not a doctrine but the reopening of the classical alternative, and with it the permanent tension between philosophy and the city.

### The theologico-political problem and the return to the classics

Beneath the method lies what he called the **theologico-political problem**: the unresolved contest between biblical revelation and Greek philosophy — Jerusalem and Athens — each claiming to know how to live, neither able to refute the other on its own terms. Strauss regarded the impossibility of settling this contest as the vitality of the West, and modernity's attempts to dissolve it (through Spinoza's critique of religion, through historicism) as evasions that produced the crisis. His scholarly practice followed: immensely slow readings of Xenophon (whose *Hiero* and Socratic works he raised from neglect), Plato read through Aristophanes and the medieval commentators, Maimonides read as a philosopher rather than a theologian, Machiavelli read as a teacher of evil whose break with the classics must be understood from inside before it is judged. Whether these readings recover their authors or construct them is, again, the standing dispute.

## Key works

- *Spinoza's Critique of Religion* (1930) — the early reckoning with the Enlightenment's critique of revelation.
- *On Tyranny* (1948) — Xenophon's *Hiero* read line by line; expanded editions include the Kojève exchange on tyranny and recognition.
- *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952) — the esotericism thesis and its method.
- *Natural Right and History* (1953) — the quarrel stated systematically: Weber, historicism, Burke, and the classical doctrine of natural right.
- *Thoughts on Machiavelli* (1958) — Machiavelli as the founder of modernity, read esoterically.
- *The City and Man* (1964) — Aristophanes, Plato and Thucydides: political philosophy and its poetic and historical rivals.
- *Socrates and Aristophanes* (1966) — the comic evidence for the young Socrates and the city's view of philosophy.
- "The Three Waves of Modernity" (essay, first published posthumously in *Political Philosophy: Six Essays by Leo Strauss*, 1975; reprinted in *An Introduction to Political Philosophy*, 1989) — the crisis narrative in miniature.

## Contributions

Strauss changed how political philosophy is studied in the United States. Against a discipline dividing itself between behavioral social science and analytic theory, he restored the history of political thought as philosophy itself — the great texts read as live answers to a permanent question — and he recovered whole authors for the canon: Xenophon above all, and the medieval falāsifa and Maimonides as philosophers. Concepts he sharpened — esoteric writing, the ancients/moderns quarrel, the waves of modernity — are now standard vocabulary even among scholars who reject his conclusions, and his diagnosis of modernity's self-undermining remains the strongest conservative statement of that case, cited by readers who share none of his politics.

## Impact and legacy

His teaching line is unusually traceable: students at Chicago and the New School, and their students in turn, staffed political science departments across America and formed a self-conscious school — "Straussians," internally divided between so-called East Coast and West Coast branches that dispute his meaning much as other traditions dispute their founders. Beyond the academy, some of his students and their associates entered public service, which later made his name a public controversy (see below). In scholarship proper, his legacy is double: a thriving subfield that practices his method, and a generation of classicists and historians of philosophy — Myles Burnyeat's review "Sphinx without a Secret" (1985) is the famous statement — who regard the method as licensed speculation. Both reactions are evidence of the same fact: no account of twentieth-century political thought can simply pass him by.

## Criticisms and debates

The scholarly criticism is methodological. Esoteric reading, its critics argue, risks unfalsifiability: if silence is evidence and contradiction is design, no text could count against a proposed hidden teaching; Quentin Skinner and the Cambridge School objected that meaning must answer to what an author could have been doing in his historical moment, not to signals only initiates detect. Strauss's defenders reply that the method is constrained — by an author's own statements about writing, by the literary character of the works, by the demand that every detail be explained — and that the alternative (assuming pre-modern authors wrote like modern professors) is its own anachronism. The dispute is unresolved and is best presented as such.

The political controversy is separate and louder. Shadia Drury's *Leo Strauss and the American Right* (1997) argued that Strauss taught an illiberal doctrine — rule by wise elites sustained by salutary myths — and that his students carried it into American conservatism; after 2003, journalistic accounts linked Straussian ideas, via officials who had studied with his students, to the advocacy of the Iraq War. Against this, scholars including Steven B. Smith, Peter Minowitz, and Catherine and Michael Zuckert have argued that the political portrait caricatures a thinker whose writings defend liberal democracy against its totalitarian enemies while criticizing its theoretical foundations, that the lines of influence on policy are thin and contested, and that Strauss's own stated allegiance was to philosophy, which he insisted is not a party. The honest summary is that the esotericism debate is a genuine scholarly question, the political-influence debate mixes scholarship with polemic on all sides, and neither is settled — an entry on Strauss must report both without adjudicating either.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Strauss did not teach that all old books have secret doctrines. Esotericism, on his account, belongs to specific conditions — persecution, and authors who signal their practice — and most of his readings turn on ordinary, if extreme, attentiveness to argument and structure.
- "Straussian" is not a political position. His students include liberals and conservatives, scholars of every party and none; the school's unity is a method and a canon, not a platform.
- He was not a nostalgic who thought we could simply return to Athens. The return he proposed was to the *question* the classics posed — he held that we cannot recover their answers as answers, only reopen the quarrel modernity closed prematurely.
- The Iraq War connection is a claim in a contested debate, not a biographical fact. What is documented is a teaching lineage into some policy circles; what it caused is disputed by the scholars closest to the evidence.

## What to read first

1. "The Three Waves of Modernity" — the crisis of modernity in twenty pages; the clearest entry to his diagnosis.
2. *Natural Right and History* (1953), chapters I–II and V — the attack on positivism and historicism, and the statement of classical natural right.
3. *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952), the title essay — the esotericism thesis in his own terms, to be read with one critical response (Burnyeat's "Sphinx without a Secret" is the classic) so the method and its chief objection arrive together.
