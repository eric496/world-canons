# Michel Foucault

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1926–1984
- **Place:** Poitiers and Paris; posts in Sweden, Poland, Tunisia, and the United States
- **School/tradition:** French philosophy, history of systems of thought
- **Known for:** Showing that power produces knowledge, institutions, and kinds of people — not only represses — through discipline, surveillance, and biopolitics

**Responds to:** [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md), [Bentham](../04-19th-century/jeremy-bentham.md) · **Answered by:** [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md)

## Biography

Michel Foucault was born Paul-Michel Foucault in Poitiers in 1926, son of a prominent surgeon who expected him to enter medicine. He did not. A brilliant, unhappy schoolboy, he entered the École Normale Supérieure in Paris in 1946 to study philosophy and psychology. His student years were marked by severe depression and at least one suicide attempt — experiences later readers connect, without reducing the work to biography, to his enduring interest in how societies draw lines between reason and madness, normal and abnormal.

Teachers mattered more than the fashionable existentialism of the day. From Georges Canguilhem he took the question of how the "normal" and "pathological" are defined; from Jean Hyppolite, history's weight; from Nietzsche, a method and a suspicion — that our noblest concepts have low, contingent origins. In the 1950s he taught in Sweden, Poland, and Germany; Poland's authoritarian state sharpened his sense of how power organizes everyday life.

His doctorate, published in 1961 as *Folie et déraison* (abridged in English as *Madness and Civilization*), argued that modern "madness" was made, not simply discovered, when the mad were confined and then handed to medicine. *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963) examined how medical seeing changed around 1800. *The Order of Things* (1966), on how economics, natural history, and linguistics were reorganized between eras, became an unexpected bestseller and briefly made him a public intellectual — associated, to his irritation, with a "structuralism" he rejected.

May 1968 and a post in Tunisia during student unrest pushed his work toward direct politics. At the experimental University of Vincennes, then from 1970 as professor at the Collège de France in a chair he named "History of Systems of Thought," he co-founded the Groupe d'Information sur les Prisons, which did not speak *for* prisoners but created conditions for prisoners to speak about intolerable conditions themselves. That was his model of the "specific intellectual": working on a concrete problem within one's competence, not pronouncing on everything like Sartre's "universal intellectual."

*Discipline and Punish* (1975) and *The History of Sexuality Vol. 1* (1976) are this period's influential books. His Collège lectures — on psychiatric power, government, liberalism and neoliberalism — drew crowds and have been published since his death. He was an activist as well as an archivist: for prisoners, for Polish Solidarity, for Vietnamese boat people — evidence against the charge that he thought resistance pointless. In his last years the history of sexuality turned to Greek and Roman ethics: how people made themselves subjects through care of the self. Volumes 2 and 3 appeared in 1984, weeks before his death in Paris on 25 June 1984, of an AIDS-related illness, aged 57. A fourth volume on early Christianity, which he had not wanted published unfinished, appeared in 2018 with his estate's permission.

## Major theories and concepts

### Archaeology: the rules behind what can be said

Foucault's first method, *archaeology*, digs for unspoken rules: not whether a past statement was true, but what made it possible, in a period, for certain statements to count as serious knowledge at all.

Around 1800, he argued in *The Order of Things*, naturalists classifying by visible similarities gave way to biologists explaining by hidden functions and histories. Neither side was simply "more scientific" in its own terms; the underlying grid — an *episteme* — had changed. *The Birth of the Clinic* gives a second case: doctors learned a new "gaze," relocating disease from the patient's story to lesions found at autopsy. Archaeology's internal tension is its silence about causes: it describes transformations brilliantly but is coy about why they happen. His second method partly answers that gap.

### Genealogy: history that makes the familiar strange

*Genealogy*, adapted from Nietzsche, traces how a practice or identity that looks natural emerged from contingent struggles and impositions of power. It had a beginning, so it could have been — and could be — otherwise.

His best-known case is "the homosexual." Before the 19th century, law and theology condemned sodomy as an *act* anyone might commit; 19th-century medicine and psychiatry produced the homosexual as a *species* — a type of person with a nature and a case history. The claim is not that same-sex desire was invented in 1870, a common caricature, but that a new classification came into being, with real effects: pathologization, but also a later "reverse discourse" claiming the category for liberation. Critics press a tension: if accounts of origins are shaped by present power, what secures Foucault's own histories? His practical reply was archival detail — genealogies stand or fall as empirical history, open to correction.

### Power/knowledge

The hyphenated *power/knowledge* (*pouvoir/savoir*) is his signature claim: the two are not external to each other. Knowledge is not pure, corrupted from outside by power; nor is truth merely a mask for power. Institutions exercising power produce knowledge — the prison produces criminology, the clinic clinical medicine, the confessional and the therapist's couch sciences of sexuality — and that knowledge extends power by defining what is normal, risky, or treatable.

A school learning-analytics dashboard shows the point without conspiracy: it generates genuine knowledge about who is falling behind, and it quietly defines success, flags children, and shapes teaching. You cannot analyze the knowledge without the institution, nor the institution without the knowledge. Each society, Foucault said, has a "regime of truth" — procedures separating true from false and attaching truth to power. Studying the regime is not denying that statements can be true.

### Discipline and the Panopticon

*Discipline and Punish* opens with two scenes eighty years apart: the public execution of the regicide Damiens in 1757, and a minute-by-minute timetable for young prisoners in 1838. Between spectacle and schedule, punishment changed its target from the body to the "soul" — habits, character, risk. *Disciplinary power* trains people in enclosed institutions — prison, barracks, school, hospital, factory — through hierarchical observation, *normalizing judgment* (constant comparison with a norm), and the *examination*, which turns each person into a documented "case."

His emblem is Bentham's *Panopticon*: cells ring a central tower whose occupancy inmates cannot verify, so they must assume they may always be watched — and watch themselves. Power becomes automatic and internal. Foucault did not claim every society is literally a Panopticon; it is a diagram of a power that spreads into offices, performance reviews, fitness tracking, and platform metrics. If discipline is so total, where is freedom? His answer: power, technically, is exercised only over subjects who could act otherwise; where no resistance is possible there is not power but physical determination. "Where there is power, there is resistance" is an analytic claim, not optimism.

### Biopower and biopolitics

From the 18th century a second power joined discipline. *Biopower* takes the *population* as its object: birth rates, life expectancy, epidemics, hygiene. Sovereign power had "made die and let live" by the sword; biopower "makes live and lets die," fostering populations' health and productivity. Vaccination, censuses, social insurance, and sanitation are biopolitics in this descriptive sense; at the dark extreme, state racism is biopower deciding which part of a population may be exposed to death for the rest. Scholars have extended the analysis to pandemics, borders, and health data — extensions Foucault did not himself make.

### The repressive hypothesis and technologies of the self

Victorian sexuality, Foucault argued, was not simply silenced. Against the *repressive hypothesis* — power mainly says "no" to sex, so liberation means speaking freely — he showed an explosion of discourse *about* sex: medical, pedagogical, psychiatric. Power is *productive*: it produces categories, pleasures, and selves. His late work turned to *technologies of the self*: practices such as self-examination, letter-writing, and care of the self (*epimeleia heautou*) by which people shape their own conduct — not a retreat from politics, but a search for how subjects might form themselves less governably: ethics as a practice of freedom.

## Signature arguments

**The disciplinary-power argument**

1. Pre-modern punishment displayed sovereign power on the body: public execution, as in the spectacle with which *Discipline and Punish* opens, made the ruler's vengeance visible to all.
2. Modern institutions work differently: prisons, schools, barracks, hospitals, and factories train bodies through surveillance, timetables, examinations, and normalizing judgment — constant comparison of each person against a norm.
3. Bentham's Panopticon is the diagram of this power: inmates in cells around a central tower are visible at any moment but cannot tell when they are being watched, so they come to police themselves.
Therefore, modern power works less by repression from above than by producing disciplined, self-monitoring subjects whose habits and capacities it has itself formed.

The claim is about a technology of power, not only about prisons. The examination, the dossier, and the timetable travel: what was perfected in enclosed institutions becomes a general way of managing people in offices, clinics, and classrooms.

**Objection.** The history is one-sided, critics object: public and sovereign violence did not vanish with modernity, and discipline itself long predates the modern prison — monasteries and armies trained bodies by rule and schedule centuries earlier, a point Foucault himself notes.

**Reply.** The reply is that Foucault's claim concerns the dominant diagram of power and its spread, not the total disappearance of sovereign violence. In his fuller account, sovereign, disciplinary, and later security mechanisms coexist and overlap, with discipline becoming the characteristic modern technology as it migrates across schools, hospitals, factories, and prisons. Read as a claim about a shift in emphasis and reach, rather than a clean replacement, the argument survives the counterexamples.

**The argument against the repressive hypothesis**

1. The received story — the *repressive hypothesis* — says that Victorian power dealt with sex mainly by silencing it, so that liberation would consist in speaking freely about a natural secret.
2. The record examined in *The History of Sexuality, Vol. I* shows instead a multiplication of discourses about sex: medicine, psychiatry, pedagogy, and demography talked about it incessantly, classifying perversions and measuring populations.
3. That talk was productive rather than merely censoring: it created "sexuality" as an object of knowledge and a core of personal identity, in the pattern Foucault calls power/knowledge.
Therefore, sexuality is not simply a natural secret repressed by power; it is partly an effect of the very apparatuses that claim only to police it or to liberate it.

**Objection.** Nancy Fraser, Jürgen Habermas, and others press a charge of normative confusion: if power is everywhere and productive of everything, Foucault seems to give no standpoint from which domination is objectionable. His critique appears to rely on values — freedom, resistance to subjugation — that his own framework cannot avow.

**Reply.** Defenders reply that genealogy criticizes by showing contingency: arrangements presented as natural or necessary are shown to be historical, and therefore changeable, which reopens space for action without needing a universal foundation. Foucault also points to resistance as internal to every power relation, not imported from outside it. Whether this answers the normative charge, or restates it in other terms, is a standing debate in the literature.

## Key works

- **Madness and Civilization (*Folie et déraison*, 1961; abridged version 1964, English 1965)** — Confinement of the mad and the birth of psychiatry; the book that launched him.
- **The Birth of the Clinic (1963)** — How the medical gaze reorganized around 1800. Dense but short.
- **The Order of Things (1966)** — Archaeology at full stretch; famous, difficult, not the place to start.
- **The Archaeology of Knowledge (1969)** — His methodological self-explanation; read after the histories, not before.
- **Discipline and Punish (1975)** — The birth of the prison, discipline, and the Panopticon. His most widely read book.
- **The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1 (1976)** — Power/knowledge, biopower, and the critique of repression in under 200 pages.
- **The History of Sexuality, Vols. 2–3 (1984)** — Greek and Roman ethics and care of the self; Vol. 4 appeared in 2018.
- **Collège de France lectures (1970–84)** — Including *Society Must Be Defended* and *The Birth of Biopolitics*; essential on government and neoliberalism.

## Contributions

Foucault gave the humanities and social sciences a new object — the historical production of apparently natural categories: madness, delinquency, sexuality, the abnormal — and a new picture of power as dispersed, productive, and bound to knowledge, rather than a thing held at the top and used only to forbid. His tools — archaeology, genealogy, discipline, biopolitics, governmentality — remain standard. And he showed critique need not rest on a fixed human nature: if identities were made in history, they can be remade; studying limits tests which of them are contingent.

## Impact and legacy

Few modern thinkers traveled further outside philosophy. Judith Butler's account of gender performativity develops his account of how norms produce subjects. Readers of Edward Said used his idea of discourse while criticizing his Eurocentrism. Giorgio Agamben took biopolitics toward the camp and the state of exception. His work underpins surveillance studies, critical criminology and prison-abolition debates, histories of medicine and psychiatry, education research, and queer theory. In public argument "panoptic" is now default vocabulary for digital surveillance, and pandemic-era debates about public-health power were conducted, often unknowingly, in his terms.

## Criticisms and debates

First, *normative grounding*: Habermas and Nancy Fraser pressed that Foucault condemns domination while refusing to state the standard — if norms are effects of power, why is discipline bad and resistance good? Defenders call his critique internal and experimental, testing limits from within practices; critics find that evasive. Second, *empirical accuracy*: historians dispute details of his accounts of confinement and of Bentham's influence. His books argue from archives and parts have been corrected — which his method invites. Third, his 1978–79 reporting on the Iranian Revolution, showing interest in its "political spirituality," was criticized then and since for underestimating the theocratic outcome; it remains contested in assessing his political judgment. Fourth, feminist critics are divided: his tools have been indispensable to feminist theory, yet his histories center male institutions and say little about women's specific subjection. Fifth, Derrida's early critique of his Descartes reading opened a long dispute about reason and its outside. None of these debates is closed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Foucault did **not** argue that "truth is fake" or that any claim is as good as another; his own books make checkable historical claims.
- "Everything is power" is not his thesis. Power names specific relations; he distinguished mobile power relations from frozen *states of domination*.
- He did not preach that resistance is futile — his activism and his analysis say the opposite.
- He was not, by his own description, a structuralist, Marxist, or postmodernist; treat those labels as loose shelving, not summaries.

## What to read first

1. Do **not** start with *The Order of Things*. Start with the opening of *Discipline and Punish* — Damiens's execution, then the prison timetable: the method in two scenes.
2. Read *The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1* next: short, argumentative, the source for power/knowledge and biopower.
3. Pair both with the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/) article on Foucault.
4. Then sample a lecture course — *Society Must Be Defended* — to hear him thinking aloud.
