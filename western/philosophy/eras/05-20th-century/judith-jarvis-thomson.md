# Judith Jarvis Thomson

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Judith Jarvis Thomson, 1929–2020.
- **Place:** Born in New York City; educated at Barnard and Cambridge; taught at MIT for most of her career.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic moral philosophy, especially the theory of rights.
- **Known for:** "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) and its famous violinist thought experiment, and for naming and shaping the trolley problem — philosophy's most famous moral dilemma.

**Responds to:** [Anscombe & Foot](anscombe-and-foot.md) · **Answered by:** the trolley-problem literature she launched — debate ongoing, no single entry in this repo

## Biography

Judith Jarvis Thomson was born on 4 October 1929 in New York City. She studied philosophy at Barnard College, graduating in 1950, and then at Cambridge, where she took a second BA and, in 1959, a PhD at Clare College. After a brief period teaching at Boston University, she joined MIT in 1964 and spent the rest of her career there, becoming one of the most influential moral philosophers in the analytic tradition. She was married to the philosopher James F. Thomson, with whom she also wrote.

Thomson's reputation rests on a series of papers of unusual clarity and argumentative daring. "A Defense of Abortion," published in 1971 in the very first issue of *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, transformed the abortion debate within a few years of *Roe v. Wade* becoming a live legal question. In the 1980s she took up a puzzle case devised by Philippa Foot and, in "The Trolley Problem" (1985) and related papers, turned it into a research program that is still running. Her systematic work on rights appeared in *The Realm of Rights* (1990), and she continued to refine — and sometimes reverse — her positions, notably returning to the trolley cases in "Turning the Trolley" (2008) and revising a view she had held for thirty years. She died on 20 November 2020, aged ninety-one.

## Major theories and concepts

### The violinist: a defense of abortion that grants the premise

Thomson's famous move is to concede, for the sake of argument, the strongest opposing claim: suppose the foetus is a person with a full right to life. She then asks whether abortion would still sometimes be permissible, using an analogy. You wake in a hospital bed to find a famous unconscious violinist plugged into your circulatory system: a society of music lovers has kidnapped you because only your blood type can keep him alive for nine months. He has a right to life — but, Thomson argues, it does not follow that he has a right to *use your body*, or that you are obliged to stay plugged in. A right to life is not a right to everything needed to live.

The argument reframes the debate: the question is not only what the foetus *is*, but what one person may demand of another's body. Thomson distinguishes what morality *requires* from what would be kind or decent — unplugging yourself may sometimes be callous, she allows, without being unjust. The argument has been debated for fifty years, but it permanently shifted abortion ethics away from the single question of foetal personhood.

### The trolley problem: Foot's case, Thomson's name and variant

The trolley problem has two authors, and getting the credit right matters. In 1967 **Philippa Foot** introduced a case involving a runaway tram: a driver must choose between letting it kill five workmen on the track ahead or steering it onto another track where it will kill one. Foot used the case to explore the doctrine of double effect — the difference between harm intended and harm merely foreseen.

Thomson coined the label "the trolley problem" (in her 1976 paper on killing and letting die, and in the 1985 paper of that name) and, crucially, changed the case. In her **bystander variant**, you are not the driver but a bystander at a switch: you may divert the trolley from five to one. Most people judge diverting permissible for the bystander as for the driver. But in a further variant — pushing one large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path to stop it, also saving five at the cost of one — most judge it impermissible. Why is turning the switch different from pushing the man? That contrast, which Foot's original case did not isolate, became the problem proper: a standing challenge to find the moral principle that explains both judgments at once.

Thomson's own answers changed over time. She long defended a rights-based explanation; then, in "Turning the Trolley" (2008), she argued — against her earlier self — that the bystander may *not* in fact turn the trolley, because doing so imposes a death on the one to save the five in a way the driver, already forced to choose between two harms his vehicle will do, is not. The reversal is a model of philosophical intellectual honesty, and the debate it reopened continues.

### Rights as the structure of morality

Across *The Realm of Rights* (1990) and her papers, Thomson defended a morality built from rights and claims rather than from maximizing good outcomes. Rights, on her account, are not decorative: to have a right is for others to be under obligations, and moral reasoning is largely about which claims people have against one another and what infringing a claim requires (as opposed to merely *violating* a right, which she distinguished as infringement without wrongdoing — sometimes an infringement is justified, and compensation is then owed). This framework drives both the abortion argument (a right to life does not automatically include a right to another's body) and the trolley analyses (the one on the side track has a claim not to be killed to save others — the question is its exact shape). She also wrote influentially on self-defense, arguing that even an innocent threat may sometimes be permissibly killed, and on goodness and reasons in *Goodness and Advice* (2001).

## Key works

- "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) — The violinist argument; arguably the most reprinted philosophy paper of the twentieth century.
- "The Trolley Problem" (1985) — Names the problem and sets out the bystander and footbridge contrasts.
- "Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem" (1976) — Her first treatment of Foot's case, on the moral weight of the doing/allowing distinction.
- *The Realm of Rights* (1990) — Her systematic theory of rights and claims.
- "Turning the Trolley" (2008) — The late paper in which she argues the bystander may not turn after all.

## Contributions

Thomson invented a method as much as a set of views: the precisely engineered hypothetical case, varied one feature at a time, used as an instrument for testing moral principles. The trolley problem — her development of Foot's tram — became the standard test-bed for the ethics of harm, absorbed into law, psychology, and now machine ethics, where "trolley problems" frame debates about self-driving cars. Her abortion argument showed that conceding an opponent's premise can be more powerful than denying it, and her rights theory gave non-consequentialist ethics a rigorous analytic form.

## Impact and legacy

Few philosophers have shaped public argument as directly. The violinist appears wherever abortion is debated; the trolley problem is a cultural fixture — taught in introductory courses worldwide, dramatized on television, run as a mass online experiment (MIT's Moral Machine), and invoked in debates about autonomous vehicles and military targeting, sometimes to the dismay of philosophers who find the popular versions crude. Within the discipline, trolleyology — critics' half-ironic name for the literature she launched — produced decades of work by Frances Kamm, Jeff McMahan, and many others, and Thomson's own late reversal ensured the problem stayed open rather than becoming a solved puzzle.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The violinist's disanalogies.** Critics argue pregnancy differs from the kidnapping case in morally relevant ways: the foetus is usually not a stranger, the mother's relation to it is special, and (except in rape) she may bear responsibility for its dependence. Debate turns on whether these differences defeat the argument or only limit its scope — Thomson herself restricted parts of it.
- **Is there a unified trolley answer?** Frances Kamm and others propose principles (such as the Principle of Permissible Harm) that preserve the bystander's permission to turn; Thomson's 2008 reversal denies that permission outright. Whether any principle captures the switch/footbridge contrast without ad hoc machinery remains contested.
- **Rights inflation.** Some critics hold that Thomson's framework multiplies claims and rights beyond what moral experience supports; consequentialists press that her bystander conclusions, old or new, sacrifice lives to distinctions without sufficient reason.
- **The method itself.** A standing debate asks whether intuitions about fantastic cases (kidnapped blood donors, footbridges) are evidence about real morality at all — a question Thomson's work made unavoidable rather than settled.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **Thomson did not invent the trolley case from nothing** — the runaway tram is Philippa Foot's (1967). Thomson coined the name, created the decisive bystander/footbridge contrast, and built the literature.
- **The violinist argument does not deny the foetus is a person** — it grants personhood for argument's sake and denies that a right to life includes a right to use another's body.
- **"Thomson's view" on the trolley switch changed.** Her famous early position allowed the bystander to turn; "Turning the Trolley" (2008) argues he may not. Cite her with a date.
- **She was not a consequentialist about the five and the one** — the whole point of her case pairs is that numbers alone do not settle permissibility.

## What to read first

Read "A Defense of Abortion" first — it is short, self-contained, and shows the method at its best. Then read Foot's original 1967 tram passage alongside Thomson's "The Trolley Problem" (1985) to see exactly what she added, and finish with "Turning the Trolley" (2008) for her reversal. *The Realm of Rights* is for after the papers have made you want the theory behind them.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the violinist argument, noting its granted premise: Thomson concedes the foetus is a person with a right to life. Why does she deny that this right includes a right to use another's body — and how does her distinction between what morality requires and what decency asks limit the conclusion?
2. Critics press disanalogies: the foetus is not a stranger, and the mother may be responsible for its dependence. State the strongest version of this objection and Thomson's likely reply. Do the differences defeat the argument, or only narrow its scope?
3. Foot's 1967 tram case asked the driver to choose between five deaths and one. What contrast did Thomson's bystander and footbridge variants isolate that Foot's case did not, and why has no agreed principle yet explained both judgments?
4. Apply Thomson's rights framework to a fresh case: a surgeon could save five dying patients by taking organs from one healthy patient. What, on her view, blocks the numbers from settling the matter — and how would her 2008 reversal on the switch bear on a bystander version?
