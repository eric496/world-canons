# Derek Parfit

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Derek Parfit, 1942–2017.
- **Place:** Born in Chengdu, China, to British medical missionaries; educated in England and based at Oxford for his entire career.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic moral philosophy and metaphysics of persons.
- **Known for:** Arguing that personal identity is not what matters in survival — what matters is psychological continuity — and for founding modern population ethics through the non-identity problem and the Repugnant Conclusion.

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Bernard Williams](bernard-williams.md) · **Answered by:** a generation of population and applied ethicists — debate ongoing

## Biography

Derek Parfit was born on 11 December 1942 in Chengdu, China, where his parents worked as medical missionaries and researchers in preventive medicine. The family returned to England, and Parfit was educated at the Dragon School and Eton College, and then Balliol College, Oxford, where he read history before turning to philosophy. In 1967, still in his twenties, he was elected to a fellowship at All Souls College, Oxford — a research post with no teaching duties — and he remained there, in one form or another, for the rest of his life, later holding visiting positions at Harvard, NYU, and Rutgers.

Parfit was famous for a monastic style of work: he revised obsessively, circulated drafts for years, and treated philosophy as a collaborative enterprise conducted by correspondence. His first book, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), grew out of articles from the early 1970s — "Personal Identity" (1971) had already made his reputation — and immediately became one of the most discussed works of postwar moral philosophy. Its final part, on future generations, opened a field that had barely existed.

His second great project took even longer. *On What Matters* began as the Tanner Lectures he gave at Berkeley in 2002 and circulated in draft for nearly a decade, accumulating detailed comments from colleagues; when it finally appeared in 2011 it ran to two volumes, with a third following in 2017. Parfit married the philosopher Janet Radcliffe Richards in 2010 (they had met in about 1982). He died on 2 January 2017, aged seventy-four, having worked on the final volume's proofs almost to the end. Obituaries in both the philosophical and the general press treated him as the most important moral philosopher of his generation — a judgment his critics largely shared, even when disputing his conclusions.

## Major theories and concepts

### Reductionism about persons, and why identity is not what matters

Parfit's central claim about persons is **reductionist**: a person is not a separately existing subject, over and above a body and a stream of experiences. A person's existence just consists in physical and psychological continuity; there is no further fact — no soul, no Cartesian ego — that makes you the same person tomorrow.

From this he draws his most famous conclusion, using cases where identity breaks down but nothing important seems lost. In **My Division**, imagine your brain's two hemispheres, each containing full copies of your memories and character, are transplanted into two different bodies. Both successors are fully psychologically continuous with you. They cannot *both* be you — identity is one-to-one — yet it seems absurd to say you have simply died. Parfit's answer: what fundamentally matters in ordinary survival is not identity but **Relation R** — psychological connectedness and continuity with the right kind of cause — and Relation R can take a branching form that identity cannot. Identity is not what matters.

### Teletransportation and the spectrum cases

In the **teletransportation** cases, a scanner records your exact physical and psychological state, destroys your body, and builds a perfect replica on Mars. Is the replica you, or are you dead, replaced by a copy? Parfit argues the case exposes that our concept of identity has no sharp answer to press on: in the "branch-line" version, where the original is not destroyed and briefly meets the replica, we see clearly the replica is someone else — yet the only difference in the main case is that the original dies. He supports this with **spectrum cases**, in which psychological and physical change is increased grain by grain between "clearly me" and "clearly not me," arguing there is no non-arbitrary line where identity must stop — the question "will that person be me?" can be *empty*, fully answerable in every factual detail yet with nothing left to decide.

### The non-identity problem

*Reasons and Persons* Part IV turns to future people, and to a problem Parfit discovered. Many of our choices — about energy policy, resources, or an individual's decision to conceive now rather than later — determine *which* people will exist. A policy of depletion may give future people lives that are barely worth living. But those people cannot complain they were harmed: had we chosen conservation, *different* people would have existed instead, and they would never have lived at all. Since a life worth living is not worse than never existing, the depleting choice seems to wrong no one — yet Parfit insists it is still worse. This is the **non-identity problem**: how can an act be wrong if it makes things worse for no one? It has structured debates about climate ethics, procreation, and intergenerational justice ever since.

### The Repugnant Conclusion and population ethics

Comparing populations, Parfit showed that seemingly innocent principles collide. If a large population of people with very good lives is better than a small one whenever total welfare is greater, and if adding people with lives worth living never makes an outcome worse, we are driven toward the **Repugnant Conclusion**: that a vast population whose lives are barely worth living could be better than a smaller population living in bliss. Parfit found the conclusion genuinely repugnant and spent decades looking for a "Theory X" that avoids it without falling to opposite absurdities (such as the reverse conclusion that small ecstatic populations beat any world like ours). The search continues; population ethics is widely regarded as a field where every proposed solution costs something implausible — a state of affairs many trace directly to Parfit's way of setting the problems.

### The Triple Theory: climbing the same mountain

*On What Matters* defends an ambitious hope: that the great moral traditions are converging. Parfit argues that the best versions of **Kantian ethics** (act only on principles everyone could rationally will to be universal laws), **Scanlonian contractualism** (act only on principles no one could reasonably reject), and **rule consequentialism** (follow the code whose general acceptance would make things go best) turn out to require the same acts. Adherents of rival theories, he writes, have been "climbing the mountain on different sides" — disagreeing about the route, not the summit. Much of the book is a painstaking, clause-by-clause revision of Kant's formulas intended to make the convergence exact rather than rhetorical.

## Key works

- *Reasons and Persons* (1984) — The masterwork: self-defeating theories of rationality, personal identity, and Parts III–IV on future generations that founded modern population ethics.
- *On What Matters*, vols. 1–2 (2011) and vol. 3 (2017) — The convergence project: reasons, Kant, contractualism, and consequentialism; vol. 1 includes responses by Barbara Herman, T. M. Scanlon, and others.
- "Personal Identity" (1971) and "Later Selves and Moral Principles" (1973) — The early papers that launched the identity arguments.

## Contributions

Parfit more or less created population ethics as a rigorous field, and the non-identity problem is now unavoidable in climate and procreative ethics. His reductionism reshaped the metaphysics of persons and, strikingly, he drew a moral upshot from it: if the boundaries between selves are less deep than we think, egoism becomes harder to justify and concern for others easier — a link he connected to Buddhist thought, which he cited with respect. His account of reasons and his convergence argument set the agenda for twenty-first-century normative ethics, and his habit of endless draft-sharing made him a model of philosophy as collective inquiry.

## Impact and legacy

Parfit's influence runs through both academia and practice. The non-identity problem is standard equipment in environmental philosophy and bioethics; the Repugnant Conclusion anchors debates over population and long-term futures, including in the effective altruism movement, many of whose leading figures (Toby Ord, William MacAskill) worked in his orbit at Oxford. Philosophers as different as Peter Singer and T. M. Scanlon treated him as the field's central figure; Singer called *Reasons and Persons* a work of genius. His death in 2017 prompted assessments comparing his effect on moral philosophy to Russell's on logic.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Is survival really separable from identity?** Bernard Williams, Parfit's great predecessor in this area, resisted some of the thought experiments, holding that bodily continuity and first-personal fear of future pain track something Parfit's criteria miss. Critics of fission cases question whether intuitions about science-fiction surgery can carry metaphysical weight.
- **Reductionism and responsibility.** If persons are less unified than we thought, what happens to desert, blame, and compensation across a life? Parfit accepted that reductionism weakens some retributive attitudes; opponents argue this shows the view must be wrong, or at least costlier than he admitted.
- **Population ethics remains unsolved.** Many now argue the non-identity problem shows person-affecting morality is untenable; others (for example, defenders of "wide" person-affecting views) resist. Whether Parfit's own search for Theory X was well-posed is itself debated — some hold the Repugnant Conclusion should simply be accepted.
- **The Triple Theory.** Critics, including some of the book's own respondents, argue the convergence is achieved by revising each theory so heavily that the agreement is partly manufactured; Kant scholars debate whether Parfit's Kant is Kant.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Parfit proved we don't survive death/sleep/travel."** He argued identity is not what *matters*; ordinary identity and ordinary survival remain perfectly real on his view.
- **"The Repugnant Conclusion is Parfit's recommendation."** It is a conclusion he named because he found it repugnant — a problem to escape, not a policy.
- **"The non-identity problem means climate change harms no one, so anything goes."** Parfit's point is the opposite: the conclusion that such choices wrong no one is a puzzle showing our moral principles need revision.
- **"Teletransportation is a claim about future technology."** The cases are intuition pumps about what survival consists in, not engineering forecasts.

## What to read first

Start with the excerpted chapters of *Reasons and Persons* on personal identity (Part III, chapters 12–13) — the teletransportation and division cases are gripping and self-contained. Then read Part IV's opening on future generations for the non-identity problem and the Repugnant Conclusion. Tackle *On What Matters* afterward, beginning with its first chapters on reasons; the full two volumes are a commitment best made once the earlier book has convinced you the project matters.
