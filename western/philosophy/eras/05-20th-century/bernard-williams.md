# Bernard Williams

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1929–2003.
- **Place:** Born in Westcliff-on-Sea, England; taught at Cambridge (Knightbridge Professor), Oxford, and Berkeley.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy in a humanist, historically minded key — the most formidable internal critic of systematic moral theory in late twentieth-century Anglophone philosophy.
- **Known for:** Moral luck, the integrity objection to utilitarianism ("one thought too many"), internal reasons, and *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985), his argument that moral theory itself distorts ethical life.

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md) · **Answered by:** [Singer](peter-singer.md), [Parfit](derek-parfit.md)

## Biography

Bernard Arthur Owen Williams was born in 1929 in Westcliff-on-Sea, Essex, the son of a civil servant. He won a scholarship to Chigwell School and then to Balliol College, Oxford, where he took a celebrated first in Greats (classics and philosophy) in 1951 after wartime-shadowed schooling and national service in the RAF, during which he learned to fly. He became a fellow of All Souls and then New College, held chairs at Cambridge and Oxford, and spent substantial periods at Berkeley, forming a transatlantic reputation unusual even for his generation.

Williams was famously quick — colleagues described the experience of arguing with him as exhilarating and slightly alarming — and his writing has the same quality: compressed, ironic, densely argued, hostile to piety of every kind, including philosophical piety. He served on public bodies (including the royal commission on gambling and the committee on obscenity and film censorship, whose report he largely shaped), and brought the same unsentimental intelligence to both.

His central philosophical project, from *Morality: An Introduction to Ethics* (1972) through the papers collected in *Moral Luck* (1981) to *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985), was an attack on what he called **"the morality system"** — the peculiar modern institution, built by Kantian and utilitarian theory, that treats morality as a special set of obligation-demands overriding all other reasons. Against it he set an older and broader question, the question of Socrates as he read it: **how should one live?** His later work turned to truthfulness (*Truth and Truthfulness*, 2002, a genealogy of accuracy and sincerity), Descartes, and a lifelong engagement with Nietzsche. He married twice; his second wife, Patricia Williams, was a senior civil servant. He died of cancer in Rome in June 2003, aged 73, still working.

## Major theories and concepts

### Moral luck

In his 1976 paper "Moral Luck" (Thomas Nagel's companion paper gave the phenomenon its taxonomy), Williams argued that whether our lives and decisions turn out well — and how we are judged for them — depends pervasively on **luck**: luck in outcomes, in circumstances, in the character we happen to have. Kantian morality insists that the moral realm must be immune to luck, since luck is the antithesis of control and responsibility. Williams's central example is the painter **Gauguin** (as a stylised figure): he abandons his family for Tahiti and painting. Whether his decision can be justified — even retrospectively, even to himself — depends on whether he *succeeds* as a painter, something not within his rational control at the moment of decision, and partly a matter of whether he is knocked down by a cart on the way to the docks. If moral justification were what theory says it is, this dependence on luck would be unintelligible; in real life it is unavoidable. The conclusion is not that anything goes, but that the Kantian ideal of a luck-proof morality misdescribes the ethical. Tension: how much of blame and responsibility survives once luck is admitted this deeply is the debate the paper started.

### Integrity, ground projects, and "one thought too many"

Against utilitarianism Williams pressed the **integrity objection**, first through two famous cases. Jim, a traveler in a South American town, is offered a "deal": shoot one Indian and the other nineteen prisoners go free. George, a chemist out of work, is offered a job in a chemical-weapons laboratory that a less scrupulous man will take if he refuses. Utilitarian calculation may direct both to comply, but Williams argued it treats the agent's own deepest commitments — his **ground projects**, the projects and relationships around which a life is built and which give it meaning — as just more entries in an impersonal ledger, demanding that the agent regard his own actions and responsibilities with an alien detachment ("negative responsibility" for everything he could have prevented). His later formulation is the most quoted sentence in the debate: a husband who, before rescuing his drowning wife rather than a stranger, pauses to check that impartial morality *permits* preferring her has had "**one thought too many**." The point is not that the calculation gives the wrong answer, but that needing it reveals a corruption of the ethical motive itself — the thought that should move him is simply that it is his wife.

### Internal and external reasons

Williams's most influential technical thesis: a statement that an agent **has a reason** to act is true only if the action connects, by a sound deliberative route, to something in the agent's existing **subjective motivational set** — desires, commitments, loyalties, projects. These are **internal reasons**. **External reasons** — reasons alleged to apply to an agent regardless of anything he cares about, which rational argument alone obliges him to act on — Williams argued are either disguised internal reasons or empty: a claim that someone "has reason" to do what nothing in his psychology could lead him to do explains nothing and motivates nothing. Example: the cruel husband who has no trace of concern for his wife — saying he has a reason to treat her better may express our condemnation, but if no deliberative path leads from his actual motivations to kindness, the "reason" floats free of his agency. The thesis reshaped debates about normativity and remains contested ground between realists and Humeans.

### Against the morality system; thick concepts

*Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* argues that ethical knowledge, where it exists, lives in **thick concepts** — concepts like *cowardly*, *brutal*, *grateful*, which are simultaneously world-guided and action-guiding — rather than in the thin concepts (*right*, *ought*, *good*) on which theory builds. A community can simply *know* that an act was cowardly, in a way inseparable from knowing what happened; abstracting to thin principles loses that knowledge without gaining objectivity. Reflection can destroy ethical knowledge as well as create it (his unsettling example is the confidence of an earlier, "hyper-traditional" society measured against our reflective sophistication), and philosophy's proper ambition is therefore modest: not to found morality but to help us understand the ethical outlooks we actually inhabit. Hence his preference for genealogy, history, and the question "how should one live?" over system-building.

## Key works

- *Morality: An Introduction to Ethics* (1972) — Short, sharp: the case that something is wrong with "morality" as philosophers conceive it.
- *Problems of the Self* (1973) — Papers on personal identity, the body, and free will, including the reduplication problem for identity criteria.
- *Moral Luck* (1981) — The classic papers: "Moral Luck," "Persons, Character and Morality" (integrity and ground projects), and the internal-reasons paper "Internal and External Reasons."
- *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985) — His masterpiece: thick concepts, the morality system, and the limits of theory.
- *Shame and Necessity* (1993) — Greek ethics (shame vs guilt) as a mirror for modern assumptions.
- *Truth and Truthfulness* (2002) — A genealogy of the virtues of accuracy and sincerity, answering postmodern suspicion of truth from within.

## Contributions

Williams changed what moral philosophy argues about. After him, no defense of utilitarianism or Kantianism could ignore the costs of impartiality for integrity and personal projects; "moral luck" became a permanent category of the subject; and the internal-reasons thesis set the terms for metaethical debate about normativity. He also restored the history of ethics — the Greeks, Nietzsche — as a live philosophical resource inside the analytic tradition, and demonstrated that analytic rigor and literary irony could inhabit the same prose.

## Impact and legacy

His influence is visible wherever ethics is done with suspicion of systems: in the particularist and anti-theory movements, in Nagel's and Scheffler's work on agent-relative reasons and prerogatives, in debates about partiality in love and friendship, and in the revival of Nietzsche within Anglophone philosophy (he was a central figure in making Nietzsche respectable reading for analytic philosophers). *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* is routinely described as the most important work of moral philosophy of its decade — including by many who think it is wrong. Outside the academy, his public service and his example of the philosopher as unillusioned citizen left a distinct mark on British intellectual life.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Does integrity prove too much?** Utilitarians reply that Jim should shoot, that squeamishness about one's own hands at the price of nineteen lives is itself a kind of self-indulgence, and that ground projects cannot be wielded as trumps without argument. Williams's defenders answer that the objection targets the *structure* of utilitarian deliberation, not only its conclusions. The Jim and George cases remain a standard undergraduate battleground precisely because both responses have force.
- **Internal reasons and the unreachable agent.** Critics (notably Christine Korsgaard and other Kantians) argue that external reasons can be grounded in the structure of rational agency itself, so the cruel husband's indifference shows not that he lacks a reason but that he is failing as an agent. Williams's rejoinder — that this smuggles in the motivational connection under the name of rationality — is still argued line by line.
- **Anti-theory or quietism?** Some readers take the critique of the morality system to leave nothing standing: if theory distorts and reflection can destroy knowledge, what is ethics *for*? Williams's own answer (understanding, genealogy, vigilance about truthfulness) strikes supporters as bracing honesty and detractors as an elegant shrug.
- **The Gauguin example.** Nagel and others have questioned whether retrospective, success-dependent justification is a *moral* phenomenon or an aesthetic one improperly imported; Williams held that the importation is the point — real justification of a life does not sort itself into the theory's compartments.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Williams showed consequences don't matter."** He showed that one moral theory's *way* of counting consequences erases the agent; he was not licensing disregard of outcomes.
- **"Moral luck means nobody is responsible for anything."** The argument targets the ideal of a luck-free morality, not responsibility as such — Williams's own treatment of regret (the lorry driver who faultlessly kills a child and rightly feels agent-regret) is about responsibility's real texture.
- **"Internal reasons means reasons are whatever you feel like."** The subjective motivational set includes commitments and loyalties the agent may not be able to abandon, and deliberation can extend it by sound routes — imagination, correction of false belief — not by whim.
- **"'One thought too many' is an argument for acting on instinct."** It is an argument about which thoughts constitute the right motive in personal life, addressed to a theory that inserts an impartial check where none belongs.

## What to read first

Start with "Moral Luck" and "Persons, Character and Morality" in *Moral Luck* — the Gauguin, Jim, and George cases and the integrity argument in their original form. Then *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*, chapters 1, 6, and 10, for the systematic statement. *Morality* (1972) is the quickest way in if time is short. Finish with *Truth and Truthfulness* chapters 1–2 to see the late method — genealogy in defense of truth — at work.
