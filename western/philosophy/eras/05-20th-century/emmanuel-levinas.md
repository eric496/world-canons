# Emmanuel Levinas

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Emmanuel Levinas, 1906–1995.
- **Place:** Born in Kaunas (Kovno), Lithuania; educated in Strasbourg and Freiburg; settled in Paris, where he taught at the Sorbonne and Paris Nanterre.
- **School/tradition:** Phenomenology, transformed into an ethics; also a major figure in modern Jewish thought.
- **Known for:** The thesis that **ethics is first philosophy** — prior to ontology and knowledge: the face of the Other (*le visage d'Autrui*) commands "thou shalt not kill" before any theory, and the self is responsible for the Other before it is free.

**Responds to:** [Husserl](edmund-husserl.md), [Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md) · **Answered by:** [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md), [Ricoeur](paul-ricoeur.md)

## Biography

Levinas was born on 12 January 1906 (30 December 1905, Old Style) in Kaunas, then in the Russian Empire, into a Lithuanian Jewish family — his father ran a bookshop, and the household combined Jewish observance with Russian literary culture. The First World War displaced the family to Kharkov, where the boy witnessed the revolutions of 1917; they returned to an independent Lithuania in 1920. In 1923 Levinas left for the University of Strasbourg and began the studies that made his career: he was among the first to carry phenomenology into France. His 1930 thesis, *The Theory of Intuition in Husserl's Phenomenology*, introduced Husserl to French readers — Sartre read it in the early 1930s — and in 1928–29 he spent two semesters at Freiburg attending Husserl's last lectures and Heidegger's seminars, an encounter he never ceased to regard as formative even after it became, for him, a wound.

He worked from 1931 at the Alliance Israélite Universelle in Paris, which ran Jewish schools across the Mediterranean — the start of a lifelong double career in philosophy and Jewish education. Naturalized French in 1939, he was mobilised that year, served as an interpreter and was captured in 1940, spending the war as a prisoner of war near Hanover, assigned to forestry work. His status as a French officer protected him from deportation; his wife Raïssa and daughter Simone survived in hiding near Orléans, helped by Maurice Blanchot. His family in Lithuania was not protected: his parents and brothers were murdered after the German invasion of 1941. The fact stands at the center of his biography — he dedicated *Otherwise than Being* (1974) partly to the memory of those closest among the six million — but he almost never wrote about the Shoah directly and resisted reducing his philosophy to a response to it.

After the war he directed the École Normale Israélite Orientale, training teachers for Alliance schools, while publishing the works of his maturity: *Time and the Other* (1948), *Existence and Existents* (1947), and, after fourteen years of preparation, *Totality and Infinity* (1961), which made his name. Chairs followed: Poitiers (1961), Paris Nanterre (1967), and the Sorbonne (1973). In parallel he led, from 1957, the Talmudic readings at the Colloquium of French Jewish intellectuals — published as *Difficult Freedom* (1963) and *Nine Talmudic Readings* — arguing that Judaism's texts belong to universal philosophical culture. *Otherwise than Being* (1974) restated his ethics in a new, deliberately difficult idiom. He retired in 1976 and died in Paris on 25 December 1995.

## Major theories and concepts

### Ethics as first philosophy

Western philosophy, Levinas argues, has almost always subordinated the Other to the Same: to know something is to comprehend it, to fit it into my categories — an activity he calls **totality**, whose theoretical form is **ontology**, the philosophy of Being. Ontology is thus not innocent: comprehension is a mode of power, and its political shadow is the totalising state. Against this tradition Levinas inverts the order. Before I know, choose, or even exist as a free ego, I am already claimed by the Other: philosophy's first truth is not "I think" but an obligation I did not contract. **Ethics is first philosophy** — not a branch applied to conduct, but the condition of meaning itself. The claim is argued phenomenologically, not merely preached: Levinas tries to *describe* an experience — the encounter with the face — whose structure resists the knowing relation.

### The Face of the Other

The **face** (*visage*) is Levinas's central phenomenon, and the most misread image in his work: it is not the physical face as an object of sight, not a colored surface to be described. The face is the Other's way of presenting himself *in person*, exceeding every idea I form of him: in seeing a face I do not merely register features; I am addressed. Its "first word," Levinas writes, is **"thou shalt not kill"** — not because the Other is strong (he is precisely vulnerable: the face is naked, exposed, mortal) but because this destitution itself commands. The face forbids murder by an ethical, not physical, impossibility — I *can* kill, and the face is exactly what makes killing *murder* rather than an event in nature. Hence his vocabulary: the Other is **Autrui**, the personal Other; the relation is **asymmetrical** — my obligation does not wait for reciprocity; and the encounter comes from a height (the Other as teacher) and from destitution (the Other as stranger, widow, orphan — the Biblical triad he constantly cites).

### Substitution, responsibility, and the hostage self

In *Otherwise than Being* the account deepens and darkens. Responsibility is not something the self *assumes*; it precedes freedom, an election I undergo: I am responsible for the Other "before" any choice, even — in Levinas's most extreme formulation — to the point of being a **hostage**. The self is defined by **substitution**: I can put myself in the Other's place, answer for him, expiate for him; this "one-for-the-other" is subjectivity's very structure, the self as accusative ("here I am," *me voici*) before it is nominative ("I am"). He calls the condition **an-archic** — without an origin I could point to — and marks it with the trace of **illeity**, the "he" of a God not present as a being but witnessed in the obligation itself. Critics ask whether infinite, unchosen responsibility is an ethics or a pathology of guilt; Levinas replies that he is describing the birth of the ethical, not legislating its measure — measures come only with the next concept.

### The third party, justice, and the Said and the Saying

My responsibility to one Other collides with my responsibility to another: the **third party** (*le tiers*) is also an Other, and with him enters comparison, measure, law — **justice**. Philosophy, the state, and knowledge are thus *necessary betrayals* of the face-to-face: necessary, because the third demands them; betrayals, because the singular obligation always exceeds them, and justice must be perpetually corrected by the charity it administrates. His mature method matches this structure: the **Saying** (*le Dire*) — my exposure to the Other — must be expressed in the **Said** (*le Dit*), the propositions and systems that inevitably fix and betray it; philosophy is the endless task of unsaying the Said. This, not carelessness, is why the later prose is so difficult.

### The critique of Heidegger and the il y a

Levinas ranked *Being and Time* among the century's indispensable books; his thought is nonetheless a sustained counter-argument to its author. Where Heidegger makes death *my ownmost* possibility and Being the horizon of all meaning, Levinas answers with the death of the *Other* as the first death, and with experiences ontology cannot digest — insomnia, fatigue, the anonymous rustling of existence he calls the **il y a** ("there is"), being without beings, from which the encounter with the Other is a deliverance. His 1951 question "Is Ontology Fundamental?" became, as the extent of Heidegger's Nazism and postwar silence grew clear, a moral as well as theoretical charge: a philosophy that subordinates the Other to Being cannot hear the command that precedes Being. Scholars debate the charge's fairness to Heidegger's texts; the quarrel itself became one of the defining axes of postwar French philosophy.

## Key works

- *The Theory of Intuition in Husserl's Phenomenology* (1930) — His thesis; the book that introduced Husserl to France.
- *Existence and Existents* (1947) and *Time and the Other* (1947/48; lectures delivered 1946/47) — The early original work: the *il y a*, insomnia, eros, fecundity, and time as the relation to the Other.
- *Totality and Infinity* (1961) — The major statement: totality vs infinity, the face, and ethics as first philosophy.
- *Difficult Freedom* (1963) — Essays on Judaism for a general reader; the most accessible Levinas.
- *Otherwise than Being, or Beyond Essence* (1974) — The second magnum opus: substitution, the hostage, Saying and Said.
- *Ethics and Infinity* (1982) — Interviews with Philippe Nemo; short, clear, the best first book by Levinas himself.
- *Nine Talmudic Readings* (English ed. 1990) — The Talmud lectures, engaging universal questions of law, forgiveness, and the other.

## Contributions

Levinas reversed the priority of ontology and ethics that had governed Western philosophy since Parmenides, and gave the reversal phenomenological rigor rather than leaving it as exhortation. He introduced a vocabulary now unavoidable — the Other, the face, asymmetry, responsibility before freedom — and a standing argument that any theory of knowledge or politics beginning with the self's powers has begun too late. He also modelled how a thinker can write simultaneously as a philosopher and as a reader of Scripture and Talmud without confusing the registers.

## Impact and legacy

In France, Levinas's influence matured slowly, then became structural: Derrida's "Violence and Metaphysics" (1964) put him at the center of the phenomenological debate; Ricoeur's *Oneself as Another* answers his asymmetry with a dialectic of self and other; Jean-Luc Marion developed his "saturated phenomenon" partly from Levinasian analyses. Beyond France: moral philosophers use the face in debates on partiality and demandingness; theologians read him on transcendence without onto-theology; political theorists press the third party into theories of justice and human rights; and Levinasian motifs inform care ethics and medical ethics, where "the face of the other" checks proceduralism. His Talmudic readings reshaped postwar French Jewish thought. Within phenomenology, the standing assessment is that he effected its "ethical turn": after Levinas, the description of experience itself came under ethical interrogation.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Derrida's objection (1964):** To accuse ontology, Levinas must speak its language; the face, described, becomes a phenomenon, and the critique of totality is itself totalised. *Otherwise than Being*'s Saying/Said machinery is widely read as his reply — whether it succeeds is the central scholarly question about the late work.
- **Demandingness and asymmetry.** If my responsibility is infinite and non-reciprocal, does ethics become self-erasure? Critics (including sympathetic ones like Ricoeur) argue reciprocity must return at the level of the self's own standing; Levinasians reply that asymmetry describes obligation's *source*, not a rule that others owe me nothing.
- **Justice and politics.** The move from the face to the third party is terse; readers dispute whether Levinas has a political philosophy at all, or only a prophetic limit on politics. His remarks on Israel and his reticence after the 1982 Sabra and Shatila massacres — when he declined in a radio interview to call the Palestinian simply "the Other" — have drawn serious criticism about his ethics' universality in application.
- **Fairness to Heidegger.** Heidegger scholars contest whether *Being and Time* subordinates the Other as Levinas claims, pointing to *Mitsein* (being-with); Levinas's defenders answer that being-with grounded in *my* Being is precisely the subordination at issue.
- **Phenomenology or theology?** Dominique Janicaud charged Levinas with a "theological turn" smuggling faith into description; Levinas maintained a strict separation between his philosophical and confessional writings — the boundary's credibility remains debated.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The face is not a literal face, and the doctrine is not "be kind to people you meet": it is a claim about the structure of obligation, stated through a deliberately strained image.
- Levinas does not deny ontology, knowledge, or the state; he demotes them — they are necessary, and answerable to what precedes them.
- His ethics is not derived from the Holocaust as an argument; he presents the obligation as belonging to the human as such, and treated the Shoah as the historical exposure of ontology's limits, not as a premise.
- "Responsibility before freedom" does not abolish freedom in his account; it redefines the self that freedom belongs to.

## What to read first

1. *Ethics and Infinity* (1982) — the interviews: Levinas explaining himself plainly, in a hundred pages.
2. *Totality and Infinity*, Section I ("Same and Other") and the opening of Section III ("Exteriority and the Face") — the argument in its canonical form.
3. "Ethics as First Philosophy" (1984 essay, in *The Levinas Reader*) — the thesis in twenty pages.
4. *Difficult Freedom*, the title essay and "A Religion for Adults" — the Jewish thought in accessible form.
5. Then *Otherwise than Being*, chapters I and IV, with a commentary (Simon Critchley's *The Ethics of Deconstruction* introduction, or the relevant chapters of Colin Davis's *Levinas: An Introduction*) at hand.
