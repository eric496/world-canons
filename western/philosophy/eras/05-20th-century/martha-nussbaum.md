# Martha Nussbaum

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** b. 1947
- **Place:** United States; American philosopher at Harvard, Brown and the University of Chicago
- **School/tradition:** Aristotelian liberalism; capabilities approach
- **Known for:** The capabilities approach to justice and development, *The Fragility of Goodness* (1986), emotions understood as judgments of value in *Upheavals of Thought* (2001), *Anger and Forgiveness* (2016)

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Rawls](john-rawls.md), [Williams](bernard-williams.md) · **Answered by:** her critics in the debates over the capabilities list, the moral role of literature, and anger — most sharply Richard Posner on literature and ethics

## Biography

Martha Nussbaum was born Martha Craven in New York City in 1947, into a wealthy East Coast family — her father was a Philadelphia tax lawyer — and educated at the Baldwin School in Bryn Mawr. At New York University she first studied theater, then classics, taking her BA in 1969. She moved to Harvard for graduate work in philosophy (MA 1972, PhD 1975), studying Greek philosophy with G. E. L. Owen; her doctoral dissertation became her first book, on Aristotle's *De Motu Animalium* (1978). She took the surname Nussbaum from her first marriage, to the linguist Alan Nussbaum.

Nussbaum taught at Harvard through the 1970s and early 1980s. Her denial of tenure there in 1982 was controversial and much discussed at the time; she moved to Brown University in 1984, and in 1995 to the University of Chicago, where she is Ernst Freund Distinguished Service Professor of Law and Ethics, appointed jointly in the Law School and the Philosophy Department. Chicago has been her base ever since, and her work there is deliberately interdisciplinary — law, classics, political science, development studies and animal ethics all feed the same set of questions.

A decisive collaboration began in the 1980s with the economist Amartya Sen, at the World Institute for Development Economics Research in Helsinki, where the two developed the "capabilities" framework for thinking about development and quality of life. Nussbaum's version of that framework, defended across *Women and Human Development* (2000), *Frontiers of Justice* (2006) and *Creating Capabilities* (2011), made her one of the most influential living philosophers working on global justice. Her honors include the Kyoto Prize (2016), the Berggruen Prize (2021) and the Holberg Prize (2021).

## Major theories and concepts

### The capabilities approach

The capabilities approach evaluates societies not by wealth or reported satisfaction but by what people are actually able to do and to be — their *capabilities*, the real opportunities open to them. A person may formally have the right to vote, to be educated, or to move about freely, yet lack the internal capacities and external conditions that make those freedoms usable. Justice, on Nussbaum's account, requires bringing every citizen up to a threshold level of each central capability; below that threshold, a life is not fully human in the sense her Aristotelian framework specifies, and its absence is a failure of justice, not merely of charity.

Her version is deliberately more determinate than Sen's. Where Sen declines to fix a list, Nussbaum defends ten **central capabilities**: (1) life; (2) bodily health; (3) bodily integrity; (4) senses, imagination and thought; (5) emotions; (6) practical reason; (7) affiliation; (8) other species; (9) play; (10) control over one's environment, political and material. The list is presented as the content of a *political* liberalism: a set of entitlements each citizen can endorse from within very different religious and philosophical worldviews, in something like [Rawls](john-rawls.md)'s overlapping consensus. In *Frontiers of Justice* she presses the approach against three problems contract theories handle badly: justice for people with disabilities, justice across national borders, and justice toward non-human animals — the last developed further in *Justice for Animals* (2023).

### The fragility of goodness

*The Fragility of Goodness* (1986), her study of Greek tragedy and Greek ethics, argues against a longstanding philosophical ambition — visible in Plato — to make the good human life invulnerable to luck. Reading Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides alongside Plato and Aristotle, Nussbaum insists that central human values (love, friendship, civic commitment) are constitutively exposed to fortune: to value things outside your control is to be at risk, and a life insured against that risk would no longer contain the goods that matter. Aristotle, on her reading, accepts this fragility; Plato's attempt to transcend it purchases safety at the cost of recognisable humanity. Genuine ethical conflicts, too, can be real and tragic, without either side being simply mistaken — a claim that runs against theories on which correct reasoning always dissolves conflict.

### Emotions as judgments of value

In *Upheavals of Thought* (2001), developed from her Gifford Lectures, Nussbaum defends a neo-Stoic, cognitive-evaluative theory: emotions are not blind surges opposed to reason but *judgments* — appraisals that ascribe great value to things and people outside the agent's control. Her grief at her mother's death, the book's running example, embodies the judgment that someone of enormous value to her flourishing has been lost. Because emotions register what we care about, they are indispensable data for ethics rather than noise to be suppressed.

The Stoic analysis is accepted, the Stoic therapy refused. Where the Stoics concluded that emotions rest on false beliefs about the value of externals and should be extirpated, Nussbaum argues the beliefs are largely true — externals really do matter — so a life without grief, fear, love and compassion would be impoverished, not purified. Politically, she defends **compassion** as a public emotion a decent society should cultivate and design institutions around, while subjecting disgust and shame to sharp criticism as bases for law in *Hiding from Humanity* (2004).

### Cosmopolitanism

Nussbaum is a leading contemporary defender of cosmopolitanism, beginning with her 1994 essay "Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism" and the exchange it provoked (published as *For Love of Country*, 1996). Drawing on the Stoic image of concentric circles of allegiance — self, family, neighbors, city, humanity — she argues that education should teach students to see their primary loyalty as owed to the world community of human beings, with local attachments understood as valuable but not ultimate. *The Cosmopolitan Tradition* (2019) traces this idea through Cicero, Grotius, Kant and Adam Smith, and presses a point she thinks the tradition ducked: duties to distant strangers are not only duties of respect but can include duties of material aid.

### Anger and forgiveness

*Anger and Forgiveness* (2016), from her John Locke Lectures, argues that anger is normatively problematic almost to its core. Ordinary anger, she claims, conceptually involves a wish for payback — either the magical thought that the offender's suffering somehow restores what was lost, or a status-obsession in which the injury is read as a humiliation to be reversed by putting the offender down. The one useful form is what she calls **Transition-Anger**, whose entire content is forward-looking: "how outrageous — this must not happen again." Against retributive instincts in criminal law and personal life alike, she recommends a forward-looking generosity modelled on figures such as Mandela and Gandhi, and treats forgiveness with suspicion when it is *transactional* (conditioned on apology and penance), preferring unconditional forgiveness understood as a form of generosity. The book's reception was lively and divided: many readers resisted the claim that even anger at injustice is corrupt, and the debate over whether anger can be apt, motivating and even virtuous continues.

## Key works

- *The Fragility of Goodness: Luck and Ethics in Greek Tragedy and Philosophy* (1986) — tragedy and Aristotle on luck, vulnerability and conflicting values.
- *Love's Knowledge* (1990) and *Poetic Justice* (1995) — essays on literature's contribution to moral perception and public reasoning.
- *Upheavals of Thought: The Intelligence of Emotions* (2001) — the systematic theory of emotions as value-judgments.
- *Frontiers of Justice: Disability, Nationality, Species Membership* (2006) — capabilities against the contract tradition on three hard cases.
- *Creating Capabilities: The Human Development Approach* (2011) — the clearest short statement of the capabilities approach and its ten capabilities.
- *Anger and Forgiveness: Resentment, Generosity, Justice* (2016) — the case against anger and retribution.
- *The Cosmopolitan Tradition: A Noble but Flawed Ideal* (2019) — Stoic cosmopolitanism and its material duties.
- *Justice for Animals: Our Collective Responsibility* (2023) — the capabilities approach extended across species.

## Contributions

Nussbaum gave the capabilities approach its most philosophically complete form: a determinate list, a threshold conception of justice, and a grounding in political liberalism, turning a development-economics framework into a full theory of social justice that now shapes real policy discussion (the UN's human development measures are its partial, Sen-flavoured cousins). She restored the emotions to the center of moral philosophy with a worked-out cognitivism that neither romanticises nor dismisses them, and she made the study of literature a live methodological question for ethics. Across all of it, she reconnected analytic moral philosophy to its Greek sources without antiquarianism: Aristotle and the Stoics are treated as live options, argued with rather than merely expounded.

## Impact and legacy

Few living philosophers have comparable reach across disciplines. Development economists and policy-makers use capability language; legal scholars argue with her accounts of disgust, anger and punishment; classicists still work through *The Fragility of Goodness*; and her cosmopolitanism set the terms of the 1990s patriotism debate. The exchange with Judge Richard Posner over *Poetic Justice* — Posner's skeptical reviews and the "Against Ethical Criticism" debate in *Philosophy and Literature* (1997–98) — became the standard exhibit in arguments about whether reading novels makes anyone morally better: Nussbaum held that certain literary forms train moral attention in ways abstract theory cannot; Posner replied that the evidence that literature improves conduct is thin and that morally serious reading cuts no obvious ethical figure. Both positions still have their partisans.

## Criticisms and debates

The capabilities list attracts the most sustained fire. Critics charge it with essentialism and paternalism — who authorized this catalogue of the human good, and why should it bind cultures that order values differently? Nussbaum's reply is that the list is abstract, revisable and politically (not metaphysically) grounded; whether that fully answers the charge is debated. Sen's refusal to endorse any fixed list marks a genuine fork within the approach, not a detail. On emotions, cognitivists and their opponents still dispute whether judgment is necessary and sufficient for emotion (infants, animals, recalcitrant feelings are the standard test cases). On anger, critics argue she underrates anger's role in protest and self-respect, and that "Transition-Anger" may be anger in name only — a dispute about both psychology and the concept itself. Her readings of Greek texts, once heterodox, are now better described as contested classics within classics itself.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The capabilities approach is not a development metric or a fancier GDP. The list specifies entitlements of justice; collapsing them into a single index number is exactly what Nussbaum resists.
- Her view is not identical to Sen's. The fixed list, the threshold, and the Aristotelian grounding are hers, and Sen declines all three.
- *Anger and Forgiveness* does not say "never feel anything at wrongdoing." Transition-Anger — outrage wholly directed at future prevention — is explicitly exempted; the target is the payback wish.
- *The Fragility of Goodness* is not nostalgia for Greek wisdom. Its claims about luck and conflict are meant as standing challenges to Plato and to modern theory alike.

## What to read first

1. *Creating Capabilities* (2011) — short, direct and complete: the approach, the list, and the contrast with rival measures of development.
2. *Upheavals of Thought*, chapter 1 — the theory of emotions stated through the case of grief; the rest of the book can be sampled.
3. *The Fragility of Goodness*, introduction and the Aristotle chapters — for the luck-and-tragedy argument in its original form, before the later system's vocabulary arrives.
