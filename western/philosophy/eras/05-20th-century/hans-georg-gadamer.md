# Hans-Georg Gadamer

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1900–2002.
- **Place:** Born in Marburg, Germany; professor at Leipzig, Frankfurt, and for most of his career Heidelberg.
- **School/tradition:** Philosophical hermeneutics — the theory of interpretation and understanding — developed from Heidegger, Dilthey, and the classical tradition.
- **Known for:** *Truth and Method* (1960) and the **fusion of horizons**: understanding as an event in which the interpreter's historically formed outlook and the text's or tradition's outlook merge into a new one.

**Responds to:** [Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md), [Schleiermacher](../04-19th-century/friedrich-schleiermacher.md) · **Answered by:** [Derrida](jacques-derrida.md), [Ricoeur](paul-ricoeur.md)

## Biography

Hans-Georg Gadamer was born in Marburg in 1900 and grew up in Breslau, where his father was a professor of pharmaceutical chemistry who hoped his son would follow him into science. The son chose the humanities. Struck by polio in 1922, which left him with lasting physical limitations, he studied at Marburg under the neo-Kantians and then under Martin Heidegger, whose charismatic teaching in the 1920s set the course of his life. His formation, however, was at least as much classical: Greek philosophy, above all Plato and Aristotle, remained his home ground, and his early work was on Plato's dialectical ethics.

His career under National Socialism was quiet and, by the standards of the time, cautious rather than resistant: he held posts at Marburg and Leipzig, signed what academics had to sign, avoided party prominence, and kept his distance from the regime's ideological projects — a record that has been examined, and both defended and criticized, since. After the war he served as rector of Leipzig University in the Soviet zone, then moved west to Frankfurt and in 1949 to Heidelberg, succeeding Karl Jaspers. There he remained, the center of a wide circle, long after formal retirement.

Gadamer published his masterwork, *Truth and Method*, only in 1960, at the age of sixty — the fruit of a lifetime's teaching. It made him, with Paul Ricoeur, the leading figure of hermeneutics as a general philosophy rather than a technique for interpreting texts. His later decades were remarkably productive and public: dialogues and debates with Habermas, Derrida, and many others, lecture tours on several continents, and a stream of essays on art, poetry, medicine, and practical philosophy. He died in Heidelberg in 2002, aged 102, having taught and written into his late nineties.

## Major theories and concepts

### Philosophical hermeneutics

Hermeneutics began as the art of interpreting texts — Scripture, law, classics. Schleiermacher and Dilthey generalized it: understanding is what the human sciences *do*, in contrast to the natural sciences' explaining. Gadamer, following Heidegger, radicalized the claim: understanding is not one method among others but the basic mode of human being itself — we always already find ourselves understanding the world from within a history. *Truth and Method*'s title is deliberately ironic: the truth available in art, history, and philosophy is **not** the product of method in the scientific sense, and Gadamer's target is the monopoly of scientific method on the concept of truth. Tension: if truth outruns method everywhere, what disciplines interpretation against mere projection? Gadamer's answer — the text's otherness, the logic of question and answer — is the theory's most contested joint.

### Prejudice and effective history

Gadamer's most notorious rehabilitation is of **prejudice** (*Vorurteil*, pre-judgment). The Enlightenment, he argued, held a "prejudice against prejudice": the demand to discard all inherited assumptions and begin from pure reason. But our assumptions are largely supplied by tradition, language, and upbringing, and they are not merely obstacles — they are the condition of understanding anything at all, the first orientation that lets a text address us. The task is not to eliminate prejudice (impossible) but to let the encounter with the subject matter test and revise it. Closely linked is **effective history** (*Wirkungsgeschichte*): we stand inside the history of effects of what we study — Plato has been shaping our categories for two millennia — and understanding includes becoming aware of that situatedness. Example: a modern reader cannot approach a medieval text with genuinely medieval eyes, nor with no eyes at all; both facts must be owned.

### The fusion of horizons

A **horizon** is the range of vision that includes everything visible from a standpoint — our own present horizon of assumptions, and the historical horizon we project for a past text or culture. Understanding happens when these are not kept separate (the fantasy of pure historical reconstruction) nor collapsed (the fantasy that the past is just like us), but **fuse**: in a genuine encounter, our horizon is enlarged and altered by the text's horizon, producing a new, shared one from which the subject matter can be seen. Fusion of horizons is not empathy and not agreement; it is the event in which a text's claim on us becomes intelligible and our questions become answerable by it. Tension: critics ask whether fusion is a description of all reading or a norm for good reading, and whether Gadamer's model underplays horizons that refuse to fuse.

### Application and practical philosophy

Borrowing from Aristotle's *phronesis* (practical wisdom), Gadamer argued that understanding always involves **application**: to understand a text, a law, or a moral norm is to apply it to one's own concrete situation, as a judge applies a statute to a case that the statute's authors never envisioned. Meaning is not first recovered in the abstract and then applied; application is internal to meaning itself. This grounds his model of the humanities on law and theology as much as on literature, and his ethics on Aristotle rather than on rule-systems.

### Language as the medium of understanding

"Being that can be understood is language." Understanding happens in language — not as translation of pre-linguistic thoughts into words, but because our world is disclosed linguistically in the first place. Dialogue is therefore his model of all understanding: a real conversation is one in which neither partner controls the outcome and both are changed by the logic of the subject matter itself.

## Key works

- *Truth and Method* (1960) — The masterwork: art, history, language, and the fusion of horizons. Dense but the whole theory is here.
- *Philosophical Hermeneutics* (essays, collected in English 1976) — Shorter, clearer statements, including "The Universality of the Hermeneutical Problem."
- *Dialogue and Dialectic* (English ed. 1980) — Essays on Plato showing his classical roots in action.
- *Reason in the Age of Science* (English ed. 1981) — Essays on method, expertise, and practical reason.
- *The Relevance of the Beautiful* (English ed. 1986) — His aesthetics in brief compass: art as play, symbol, and festival.
- *Philosophical Apprenticeships* (English ed. 1985) — A memoir of his teachers, including Heidegger — valuable biography as well as philosophy.

## Contributions

Gadamer turned hermeneutics from a scholarly technique into a general philosophy of human understanding, rehabilitated tradition and authority as epistemic categories (without traditionalism), and gave the humanities a self-understanding that does not borrow its standards from physics. His recovery of Aristotelian practical philosophy fed directly into the late twentieth-century revival of virtue and practice — Alasdair MacIntyre's work is unthinkable without it.

## Impact and legacy

*Truth and Method* reshaped literary studies, theology, legal theory (interpretation of constitutions and statutes), historiography, and translation studies; "fusion of horizons" entered common intellectual vocabulary. In philosophy he stands as the chief heir and civilizer of Heidegger: where Heidegger's account of understanding is tied to individual authenticity and Being, Gadamer's is social, linguistic, and oriented by the classical tradition. His debates with Habermas and Derrida (below) defined two of the major fault lines of late twentieth-century European philosophy, and his influence persists wherever disciplines argue about interpretation — from biblical scholarship to the theory of medical practice.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The Gadamer–Habermas debate.** Habermas charged that Gadamer's rehabilitation of tradition leaves no room for **ideology critique**: tradition can be systematically distorted by power, and reflection must be able to break its spell — psychoanalysis being his model of an emancipatory science. Gadamer replied that the critic also stands within tradition and language; critique is itself a hermeneutical act, and the claim to stand outside is the Enlightenment prejudice again. The exchange (1967 onwards) remains the standard statement of the hermeneutics/critical-theory standoff, with many concluding that each exposed the other's genuine blind spot.
- **The Gadamer–Derrida encounter.** At their 1981 Paris meeting, Derrida questioned whether Gadamer's appeal to good will and the desire for consensus in dialogue presupposed exactly the metaphysics of presence that deconstruction contests; Gadamer defended dialogue as openness to the other rather than assimilation. Many observers judge that the two largely talked past each other — an irony not lost on either side — though later exchanges were more productive.
- **Relativism and conservatism.** Critics from both left and right have charged that if all understanding is prejudice-bound, Gadamer cannot distinguish better from worse interpretations, or reform from mere drift. His defenders point to the subject matter's resistance and the testing of prejudices in application. Whether that suffices is a live debate, as is the charge that his wartime caution exemplifies the political quietism his philosophy is accused of.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Prejudice is good, so bias is fine."** Gadamer's claim is that pre-judgments are unavoidable and *enabling*; they must still be risked and revised in encounter with the text. He distinguishes enabling from blinding prejudices precisely by this test.
- **"*Truth and Method* is a manual of method."** The title is polemical: the book argues the most important truths in the humanities are not produced by method at all.
- **"Fusion of horizons means agreeing with the past."** It means achieving a standpoint from which the past's claims become intelligible — including claims one then rejects with better understanding of what is being rejected.
- **"Gadamer was simply Heidegger applied to texts."** His sources are as much Plato, Aristotle, Hegel, and Dilthey, and his conclusions about tradition and dialogue diverge sharply from Heidegger's.

## What to read first

Begin with the short essay "The Universality of the Hermeneutical Problem" (in *Philosophical Hermeneutics*) for the whole outlook in twenty pages. Then *Truth and Method*, Part II — the sections on prejudice, effective history, and the fusion of horizons — before attempting Part I on art. *The Relevance of the Beautiful* shows the theory at its most graceful. For the debates, read the Habermas exchange after Gadamer's own position is clear.
