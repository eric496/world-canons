# Gilbert Ryle

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1900–1976
- **Place:** Born in Brighton, England; spent his career at Oxford
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy; central figure of postwar "ordinary language" philosophy
- **Known for:** *The Concept of Mind* (1949) — the attack on Descartes's "ghost in the machine" and the distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that

**Responds to:** [Descartes](../03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md) · **Answered by:** later philosophers of mind who defined themselves against him — no single entry in this repo

## Biography

Ryle was born in Brighton in 1900 and won scholarships to Queen's College, Oxford, where he read classics and philosophy. He became a lecturer at Christ Church in 1924 and, despite publishing relatively little in the 1930s (a noted paper, "Systematically Misleading Expressions," 1932, and a fine review-essay on Heidegger's *Being and Time* in 1929 showing unusual openness to Continental work), rose steadily. During the Second World War he served in the Welsh Guards and rose to major in intelligence work. In 1945 he succeeded Collingwood as Waynflete Professor of Metaphysical Philosophy at Oxford — the most influential philosophy chair in Britain — and from 1947 to 1971 he edited the journal *Mind*, a post he used to set standards for a generation.

*The Concept of Mind* appeared in 1949 and made him the public face of Oxford philosophy: witty, demotic, and ruthlessly anti-pompous. Later work was broader than the caricature suggests — *Dilemmas* (1954), two volumes of *Collected Papers* (1971), and *Plato's Progress* (1966), a serious work of Plato scholarship. He retired in 1968 and died in 1976.

## Major theories and concepts

### Category mistakes

Ryle's diagnostic tool. A **category mistake** occurs when we present facts belonging to one logical category in the idioms of another. His famous example: a visitor is shown the colleges, libraries, and offices of Oxford and then asks, "But where is the *University*?" The visitor treats "the University" as one more institution alongside the others, when it names the way they are all organized. Ryle argues philosophy's big pseudo-problems often have this shape: we mis-assign a concept's logical type and then puzzle over the phantom leftover. Medicine is not a sixth thing beside doctors and hospitals — and, Ryle argues, mind is not a hidden thing beside intelligent behavior.

### The ghost in the machine

Descartes's dualism — mind as a non-spatial, non-mechanical inner theater, body as a machine it somehow steers — Ryle labels **"the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine"** and, more technically, "Descartes' myth." The "official doctrine," as he reconstructs it, creates an insoluble double problem: how a ghostly mind can causally move a material body, and how anyone can know anything about other minds sealed inside their machines. Ryle's proposal is deflationary: mental-conduct words — *intelligent, careful, vain, knows how* — do not report ghostly inner episodes; they signify abilities, tendencies, and dispositions manifested in what people do. "Minds are not bits of clockwork, they are just bits of not-clockwork" — the point being that talk of mind was never the right grammar for a mechanism at all.

### Knowing-how and knowing-that

The book's most durable distinction. "The intellectualist legend" says intelligent action requires first performing an inner act of considering propositions — knowing-*that*. Ryle replies with a regress: if executing an action intelligently required a prior theoretical operation, that operation would itself have to be done intelligently or stupidly, requiring a further prior operation, and so on forever. So knowing how to ride a bicycle, argue a case, or cook cannot be reduced to consulting internalized rules; it is an ability exercised directly. Reflection on rules can improve performance, but it is a second skill, not the secret engine of the first.

### Dispositions and the mental vocabulary

To say glass is brittle, or a person is vain, is not to report an occult inner state but to license a bundle of hypothetical statements — what the glass *would* do if struck, what the person *does* do, in tendencies and patterns. Ryle extends this treatment across much of the mental vocabulary. He explicitly denied being a behaviorist: dispositions are not single behaviors, feelings and sensations are acknowledged as real, and his target was a *logical* picture of mind-talk, not the denial of inner experience. The debate over whether the position nonetheless collapses into "logical behaviourism" is one of the live disputes about him.

## Key works

- "Systematically Misleading Expressions" (1932) — early statement of his method: grammatical form can mislead us about logical form.
- *The Concept of Mind* (1949) — the masterpiece: category mistakes, the ghost in the machine, knowing-how.
- *Dilemmas* (1954) — the Tarner Lectures: accessible case studies of conceptual conflicts.
- *Plato's Progress* (1966) — Plato's development reconstructed; evidence Ryle was no mere linguistic technician.
- *Collected Papers*, 2 vols (1971) — including essays on thinking, sensation, and the concept of a person; "The Thinking of Thoughts" introduces the idea Clifford Geertz made famous as "thick description."

## Contributions

Ryle gave philosophy a method and a target: a way of dissolving problems by mapping the logical geography of concepts, and the most famous attack on Cartesian dualism ever written — one framed in argument rather than mere scientism. The knowing-how/knowing-that distinction restructured philosophy of mind and epistemology and has a second career in education theory, expertise research (the Dreyfus model of skill draws on it), and debates about whether artificial intelligence "knows." His editorial and professorial stewardship made postwar Oxford the center of analytic philosophy.

## Impact and legacy

*The Concept of Mind* shaped everyone who came after in philosophy of mind — even those, like the identity theorists and functionalists who followed, who defined themselves against it. The ghost-in-the-machine phrase entered general culture. Ordinary-language method declined as a program (Strawson, then the post-Kripke revival of metaphysics, moved elsewhere), but Ryle's therapeutic suspicion of pseudo-entities persists as a discipline of thought. Geertz's borrowing of "thick description" carried a Rylean tool into anthropology, and contemporary enactivist and embodied approaches to mind often claim him as a forerunner.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Is dispositionalism enough?** Strawson and others objected that pains, images, and occurrent thoughts are not plausibly analysable as tendencies to behave; the rise of the causal theory of mind (Armstrong, Fodor) argued we need inner states with causal roles, not just patterns — functionalism is in part Ryle's insight re-mechanized.
- **Cause and agency:** does Ryle explain *why* intelligent performances happen, or merely redescribe them? Critics say dispositions name the explanandum, not the explanation.
- **Behaviorism, yes or no:** textbooks long classified him as a logical behaviorist; scholars dispute this, pointing to his denials and his treatment of sensations. Both readings remain in print.
- **Method:** the charge that conceptual analysis settles too little — that science, not linguistic geography, should answer questions about the mind — defined the next generation's turn; Ryleans reply that category confusion survives every scientific advance unless policed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Ryle did not deny that minds, thoughts, or feelings exist; he denied a specific theory of what mental talk refers to.
- The "ghost" is Descartes's *myth* as Ryle reconstructs it — scholars debate how fairly it fits the historical Descartes.
- Knowing-how is not mindless habit: skilled performance is intelligent through and through; that is precisely Ryle's point.

## What to read first

*The Concept of Mind*, chapters 1–2 (the ghost, category mistakes, knowing-how) is the essential read and genuinely entertaining. Then *Dilemmas* for the method at work on smaller problems, and "Systematically Misleading Expressions" if you want the early manifesto.
