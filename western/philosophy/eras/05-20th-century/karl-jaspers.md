# Karl Jaspers

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1883–1969
- **Place:** Germany (Heidelberg), later Basel, Switzerland
- **School/tradition:** Existenzphilosophie; existential phenomenology
- **Known for:** Boundary situations, the Encompassing, ciphers of transcendence, philosophical faith, the Axial Age thesis, and *The Question of German Guilt* (1946)

**Responds to:** [Husserl](edmund-husserl.md) (whose phenomenology he redirected from consciousness to existence), Kierkegaard and Nietzsche (read as the great exceptions who shattered system), [Heidegger](martin-heidegger.md) · **Answered by:** [Arendt](hannah-arendt.md) (his doctoral student), [Ricoeur](paul-ricoeur.md) (who read him closely in a prisoner-of-war camp)

## Biography

Karl Jaspers was born in Oldenburg, Germany, in 1883, and trained first, briefly, in law and then in medicine, taking his medical degree at Heidelberg in 1909. He worked at the Heidelberg psychiatric clinic, where dissatisfaction with the speculative systems of contemporary psychiatry produced his first major work, *General Psychopathology* (1913) — a methodological classic that distinguished understanding meaningful connections in a patient's life from explaining them causally, and which is still in print in psychiatry. Habilitated in psychology in 1913, he moved across to philosophy, becoming professor at Heidelberg in 1921 without a conventional philosophical training. *Psychology of Worldviews* (1919), which treated worldviews as shells within which human beings shelter from the extremity of existence, already contained his mature program, formalized in the three volumes of *Philosophie* (1932).

The Nazi years tested the philosophy in its author. Jaspers's wife Gertrud, whom he married in 1910, was Jewish; he refused to divorce her. He was removed from university administration in 1933, banned from teaching in 1937 and from publishing in 1938, and lived in Heidelberg under escalating threat — the couple survived, by their later account, keeping poison against the deportation they expected. After the war he helped refound the University of Heidelberg, published *The Question of German Guilt* (1946) from his lectures to a shattered student body, and wrote increasingly as a public philosopher of the new republic. Disillusioned with German politics, he moved in 1948 to Basel, where he taught until 1961; he took Swiss citizenship in 1967 and died in Basel in 1969. His friendship and correspondence with Hannah Arendt, his former doctoral student, lasted from her Heidelberg days until his death; his early friendship with Heidegger did not survive 1933.

## Major theories and concepts

### Existenz and the Encompassing

Jaspers distinguishes what we are as objects of study — empirical existence (*Dasein*), consciousness as such, spirit — from **Existenz**: the unobjectifiable selfhood that each person *can* be, actualized only in freedom, decision and communication, never given as a fact. Philosophy, accordingly, does not deliver a system of the world; it **illuminates Existenz** (*Existenzerhellung*), appealing to the reader's possible freedom rather than compelling assent like a proof. Behind both stands **the Encompassing** (*das Umgreifende*): that which always recedes as we try to make it an object — being itself, which we never encounter whole, only within horizons that point beyond themselves. Every attempt to totalise knowledge breaks on the Encompassing, and the breaking is not a defect but philosophy's positive result.

### Boundary situations

The route to Existenz runs through **boundary situations** (*Grenzsituationen*): limits that cannot be changed, avoided or seen past — that I must die, that suffering is woven into life, that I am thrown into struggle, that I inevitably incur guilt, that everything particular is hostage to chance. Ordinary life evades them with busyness and doctrine. Confronted honestly, they shipwreck our self-sufficient plans — Jaspers speaks of foundering (*Scheitern*) — and in that shipwreck Existenz first becomes aware of itself and of transcendence. The boundary situation is thus not a problem to be solved but an experience to be entered: philosophy begins where our constructions fail us.

### Ciphers of transcendence and philosophical faith

Transcendence — being itself — never appears directly. It speaks in **ciphers** (*Chiffren*): objects, myths, works of art, doctrines, nature itself, read not as literal statements but as a script in which transcendence is present without becoming knowledge. A cipher read literally hardens into superstition or dogma; read as cipher, it leaves freedom intact. This grounds Jaspers's **philosophical faith**: a faith whose content is given in ciphers and historical depth, held in opposition to *revealed* faith's claim to possess exclusive, propositional truth — yet equally opposed to the scientistic denial that there is anything to have faith in. His late work on the great philosophers and on the biblical tradition tries to hold the space open: the ciphers of the religions are to be honored without being believed literally, and philosophy and religion, he argued against Barth and Bultmann alike, must learn to communicate without either surrendering.

### The Axial Age

In *The Origin and Goal of History* (1949) Jaspers proposed the most influential single idea he gave the humanities: the **Axial Age** (*Achsenzeit*), roughly 800–200 BCE (with a peak around 500 BCE), in which, independently and simultaneously, the spiritual foundations of humanity were laid in China (Confucius, Laozi, the schools), India (the Upanishads, Buddha), Iran (Zarathustra), Palestine (the prophets) and Greece (Homer's heirs, the philosophers, the tragedians). In this age human beings became conscious of being as a whole, of themselves and their limits, and posed the radical questions to which — Jaspers claimed — we still live off the answers. The thesis was meant as an empirical-historical alternative to both Hegel's Christian philosophy of history and nationalist historiographies: a common axis for a common human history, and so a basis for mutual understanding across civilizations.

### The four guilts

*The Question of German Guilt* (1946) distinguishes four kinds of guilt, each with its own jurisdiction. **Criminal guilt** concerns violations of law, before a court. **Political guilt** — the liability of citizens for the deeds of their state — lies before the power of the victors and one's fellow citizens. **Moral guilt** lies before one's own conscience. **Metaphysical guilt** — the failure of human solidarity, surviving where one should have acted — lies before God alone. Conflating them, Jaspers argued, produces both self-righteous condemnation and evasive self-pity; separating them let a German audience face what could be faced. The distinctions were widely adopted in post-war discussion, and contested too — especially the reach of political and metaphysical guilt.

## Key works

- *General Psychopathology* (1913) — the psychiatry classic: understanding versus explaining in the study of mental illness.
- *Psychology of Worldviews* (1919) — worldviews as protective shells; boundary experiences introduced.
- *Philosophie*, 3 vols. (1932) — the system-that-is-not-one: world orientation, illumination of Existenz, metaphysics of ciphers.
- *Reason and Existenz* (1935) — five lectures; the Encompassing stated compactly.
- *The Question of German Guilt* (1946) — the four guilts; post-war moral reckoning.
- *The Origin and Goal of History* (1949) — the Axial Age thesis.
- *The Great Philosophers* (1957 and posthumous volumes) — Socrates, Buddha, Confucius and Jesus as the "paradigmatic" founders, then the "seminal" and "creative" thinkers.
- *Philosophical Faith and Revelation* (1962) — ciphers and faith in their late form.

## Contributions

Jaspers contributed at three levels. To psychiatry he gave a method — the disciplined distinction of understanding and explaining — that outlasted his move to philosophy. To philosophy he gave Existenzphilosophie its most systematic and least theatrical form: where Heidegger's analytic of Dasein and Sartre's existentialism became movements, Jaspers built an austere framework of limits, communication and transcendence, and — unusually among existential thinkers — paired it with an ethics of **loving struggle** (*liebender Kampf*): communication in which each party wills the other's selfhood rather than victory. To public life he gave the four guilts and the Axial Age: two durable instruments, one for moral reckoning, one for comparative history, each still in use far beyond his readership.

## Impact and legacy

His influence ran through people as much as texts. Arendt took from him the primacy of communication and a model of thinking without banisters; Ricoeur, reading him as a prisoner of war, found the philosophy of existence he would later take through hermeneutics. The Axial Age thesis, taken up by sociologists and historians — Shmuel Eisenstadt's "axial civilisations" research program, Robert Bellah's *Religion in Human Evolution* (2011) — became a standing framework in comparative civilizational analysis. In Germany he remains a figure of the post-war reconstruction: the philosopher who stayed, kept his Jewish wife, and told his students the truth about guilt when silence would have been easier. Outside Germany his eclipse by Heidegger and Sartre was near-total in the Anglophone world, though translations and the Arendt connection have kept him in view.

## Criticisms and debates

The technical charge is vagueness at the center. The Encompassing cannot be stated, ciphers cannot be decoded by any checkable method, and Existenz cannot be described — critics from the analytic side have asked whether a philosophy whose key terms systematically resist objectification has content or only atmosphere; defenders reply that the resistance is the content, a principled account of why being is not a possible object. The Axial Age thesis has been pressed harder by historians: the simultaneity is loose (Zarathustra's dates are uncertain by centuries; Homer belongs at the period's edge), the selection privileges literate, "questioning" traditions in ways some call a secularised Protestant template, and the common axis may homogenise civilizations whose breakthroughs were profoundly different. Jaspers's post-war politics also drew criticism — his skepticism about German reunification and his self-distancing from the Federal Republic struck some compatriots as a philosopher's ingratitude. His break with Heidegger, and Heidegger's silence toward him after the war, is usually read to Jaspers's credit; his own 1933 record — he signed, then withdrew from, university politics more slowly than his later self-presentation sometimes implied — has also been examined by scholars, and remains part of the honest record.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Jaspers is not a vaguer Heidegger. The two projects share sources (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche) and a vocabulary of existence, but diverge fundamentally: communication and openness against resoluteness, philosophical faith against Heidegger's silence on ethics — and the personal breach of 1933 tracks the philosophical one.
- Boundary situations are not a technique for self-improvement. They cannot be sought or engineered on Jaspers's account; they befall us, and what matters is whether we evade them or enter them.
- Philosophical faith is not watered-down religion. It is a determinate rival to revelation: ciphers held without literal belief, in deliberate refusal of both dogma and scientism.
- The Axial Age is a thesis, not a fact. It is a fruitful, contested periodization proposed in 1949 — to be used with its critics in view, not recited as settled chronology.

## What to read first

1. *The Question of German Guilt* (1946) — short, lucid, and historically momentous: the four guilts in his clearest prose.
2. *Reason and Existenz* (1935), Lecture I — the Encompassing and boundary situations in fifty pages.
3. *The Origin and Goal of History* (1949), Part I — the Axial Age argued in full; read it alongside one critical discussion to keep the thesis in its proper, debated place.
