# Hanna Pitkin

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1931–2023
- **Place:** Born in Berlin, Germany; emigrated to the United States as a child; career at the University of California, Berkeley
- **School/tradition:** Political theory; Wittgensteinian conceptual analysis applied to politics
- **Known for:** *The Concept of Representation* (1967) — still the standard philosophical map of what representation is — and *Wittgenstein and Justice* (1972)

**Responds to:** [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md), and the democratic theory she found conceptually confused · **Answered by:** The contemporary literature on political representation (Mansbridge, Rehfeld, Dovi and others), which works almost entirely inside the framework she built

## Biography

Hanna Fenichel Pitkin was born in Berlin in 1931, the daughter of Otto Fenichel, a prominent psychoanalyst in Freud's circle. The family fled the Nazis, reaching the United States in 1938, and she grew up in California. She took her doctorate at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1961; the dissertation became her first and most famous book. She spent her career at Berkeley, where she became a professor of political science and a famously demanding teacher of political theory, retiring as professor emerita. Later books turned the same close conceptual attention onto Machiavelli and gender (*Fortune Is a Woman*, 1984) and onto Hannah Arendt (*The Attack of the Blob*, 1998). She died in Berkeley in 2023.

## Major theories and concepts

### The four views of representation

*The Concept of Representation* (1967) begins from an embarrassment: everyone favors representation and no one can say, without contradiction, what it is. Pitkin's method — learned from the later [Wittgenstein](wittgenstein-late.md) — is to map how the concept is actually used, in political argument and ordinary language, and to let its structure emerge from the uses rather than impose a definition. Four families of views emerge:

- **Formalistic views** locate representation in arrangements *before and after* the representative acts: **authorization** (a representative is someone granted the right to act, as in Hobbes) and **accountability** (a representative is someone who must answer afterwards, and can be sanctioned or removed). Pitkin shows each captures something institutional and misses the substance: an authorized tyrant "represents" on the first view; accountability alone says nothing about what should be done between elections.
- **Descriptive representation** judges by resemblance: the assembly should be a portrait in miniature of the people, matching them in class, race, sex and opinion. Its strength is symbolic and epistemic; its weakness, she argues, is that it treats representatives as standing *for* people by *being like* them, while saying nothing about acting — a mirror does not deliberate.
- **Symbolic representation** works through belief and acceptance: a flag, a monarch, or a leader "represents" by being taken to embody the nation. Pitkin treats it as real but dangerous, since it can be manufactured and asks for faith rather than activity.
- **Substantive representation** — "acting for" others — is where the concept's center lies: the representative acts in the interest of the represented. But that phrase immediately generates the book's central problem.

Two further features of her account matter for everything written since. First, she insists that representation is not exhausted by the dyad of one representative and one constituency: whether someone's activity counts as representing depends on the surrounding **institutional arrangement** — electoral rules, parties, publicity, the possibility of removal — which is why formally identical acts can represent in one system and dominate in another. Second, she distinguishes the *object* of representation: what gets represented is not primarily opinions (which constituents may not have, on most issues, until asked) nor brute interests (which they may misjudge), but people in their capacity to form and revise judgments — which is why a representative who merely mirrors today's polled wishes can fail at representing just as surely as one who ignores them.

### The mandate–independence controversy

If a representative must act in constituents' interest, must she follow their wishes or her own judgment of their good? **Mandate** theorists say wishes; **independence** theorists (Burke's address to the electors of Bristol is the classic text) say judgment. Pitkin argues the controversy is irresolvable as posed because each side is half of the concept: representation requires a representative who is genuinely responsive to constituents *and* genuinely capable of independent action — a person, not a transmission belt, yet not an autonomous ruler either. Her resolution turns on the object of action: over time, and across the system, the representative's activity must be justifiable to the represented in terms of *their* interests — she must act "in a manner responsive to them", not systematically against their wishes without compelling reasons they could in principle accept. Representation, on her account, is thus less a property of a single act than of an ongoing institutional arrangement in which responsiveness and independence are held in working tension.

### Wittgenstein and justice

*Wittgenstein and Justice* (1972) asks what the later Wittgenstein's philosophy of language can do for political theory. Her answer: it dissolves pseudo-problems generated by treating words like "justice" as names of essences, and it relocates political philosophy in our shared forms of life — what we can say to one another and still be understood. The book is both an exposition of Wittgenstein for political scientists and a methodological manifesto: attend to use, suspect abstractions that have floated free of the practices that give them sense, and expect political concepts to remain essentially open-textured rather than awaiting their final definition.

### The blob

In *The Attack of the Blob: Hannah Arendt's Concept of the Social* (1998), Pitkin turned her method on an ally. Arendt's category of "the social" — the blurring of household and economy into public life — had swollen, Pitkin argues, into a **blob**: an amorphous, expanding explanatory mass that swallows everything it touches, blaming an abstraction for what particular people and institutions do, and licensing resignation (nothing can resist the social) where Arendt herself wanted action. The critique generalizes: political theory's own categories can become blobs, and conceptual vigilance is a political duty, not a pedantic one.

## Key works

- *The Concept of Representation* (1967) — the four views, the mandate–independence controversy, and representation as an institutional arrangement; still the field's reference point.
- (Editor) *Representation* (1969) — the Nomos X yearbook volume, gathering the debate her book had set off.
- *Wittgenstein and Justice: On the Significance of Ludwig Wittgenstein for Social and Political Thought* (1972).
- *Fortune Is a Woman: Gender and Politics in the Thought of Niccolò Machiavelli* (1984) — Machiavelli's politics read through his figures of fortune, virtue and gender.
- *The Attack of the Blob: Hannah Arendt's Concept of the Social* (1998) — a sympathetic critic's case that Arendt's "social" became an unaccountable abstraction.

## Contributions

Pitkin gave political science its working concept of representation. The distinctions of her 1967 book — formalistic, descriptive, symbolic, substantive — remain the vocabulary in which debates about legislatures, quotas, descriptive representation and populism are conducted, including by writers who revise them. Her treatment of the mandate–independence problem reframed it from a puzzle about individual legislators into a question about systems and time. And *Wittgenstein and Justice* was among the earliest serious attempts to bring ordinary-language philosophy to bear on political theory's foundations rather than its decorations.

## Impact and legacy

Representation studies is a subfield built on her foundations. Jane Mansbridge's influential expansion of the concept (promissory, anticipatory, gyroscopic and surrogate representation) explicitly works from Pitkin's map; Andrew Rehfeld's work on constituency and Michael Saward's "representative claim" likewise define themselves against her account, and Suzanne Dovi's work on good representation takes up her normative questions. The rise of descriptive-representation research, of gender and ethnic quotas, and of citizens' assemblies chosen by lot has kept her distinctions in daily use — sortition's defenders argue in her vocabulary, claiming for a descriptively accurate mini-public a kind of representation her framework both illuminates and was not built to settle. Her death in 2023 prompted retrospectives treating the 1967 book as that rare thing: a conceptual analysis that permanently reorganized an empirical discipline.

## Criticisms and debates

Her framework is so dominant that the criticisms define the field's frontier. One line holds that the four views, drawn from mid-century usage, miss forms of representation that matter now — self-appointed advocates, NGOs and celebrities who claim to speak for others without election (the "representative claim" literature), and non-human or future interests. Another worries that her resolution of the mandate–independence controversy is more a description of the tension than a resolution of it: saying the representative must be responsive *and* independent, within an arrangement that keeps both alive, tells us what to preserve rather than what to do in a hard case — supporters reply that a demand for more was exactly the conceptual confusion the book diagnosed. Her Wittgensteinian method itself has critics, who ask whether mapping usage can settle normative disputes about how representation *ought* to work. Pitkin herself returned to the book late in her career (notably in "Representation and Democracy: An Uneasy Alliance", 2004), conceding that its picture was drawn largely from electoral representation in stable democracies, and worrying that large-scale, mediated politics had stretched the concept's conditions — her characteristic move of treating even her own framework as provisional before the phenomena.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Pitkin did not define representation once and for all. Her book argues the concept has an internal structure and permanent tensions; quoting a single sentence of hers as "the definition" misses the method.
- Descriptive representation is not her recommendation. She treats resemblance as one partial view with real but limited force, not as the goal of institutional design.
- The mandate–independence discussion is not a verdict for Burke. Independence unqualified ceases, on her account, to be representation at all.
- "The blob" is not an attack on Arendt from outside. It is an insider's warning, extended to political theory generally, about categories that explain everything and so nothing.

## What to read first

1. *The Concept of Representation* (1967), chapters 1–3 and the concluding chapters — the four views and the mandate–independence problem in her own terms.
2. *Wittgenstein and Justice* (1972), opening chapters — the clearest statement of what conceptual attention can do for political thought.
3. *The Attack of the Blob* (1998), introduction and first chapter — the method turned, entertainingly and severely, on a single swollen concept.
