# John Dewey

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1859–1952
- **Place:** Born in Burlington, Vermont, USA; worked mainly at Chicago and Columbia University, New York
- **School/tradition:** American pragmatism (with Peirce and James the third classical pragmatist)
- **Known for:** Rebuilding philosophy around experience and inquiry — and turning that into the most influential philosophy of education and of democracy of his century

**Responds to:** [Hegel](../04-19th-century/g-w-f-hegel.md), [Peirce](../04-19th-century/charles-sanders-peirce.md), [William James](../04-19th-century/william-james.md) · **Answered by:** Richard Rorty and later democratic and embodied-cognition theorists — outside this repo's entries

## Biography

Dewey was born in Burlington, Vermont, in 1859, the year Darwin published *On the Origin of Species* — a coincidence he liked, since Darwin's rethinking of fixed species shaped his own rejection of fixed essences and eternal truths. After studying at the University of Vermont, he took his PhD at Johns Hopkins in 1884, in an intellectual atmosphere still dominated by German idealism; the young Dewey wrote as a Hegelian.

The Hegelianism gradually fell away as Darwin, William James's *Principles of Psychology* (1890), and his own experimental work took hold. At the University of Chicago (1894–1904) he founded the Laboratory School, where his educational ideas were tested on real children rather than merely theorized, and published the landmark 1896 paper "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology," an early shot in his campaign against carving mind and world into separate pieces. A quarrel with the university president pushed him out; he moved to Columbia University in New York (1904–1930) and became the most famous public intellectual in America: lecturing in China (1919–21, where he influenced a generation of reformers) and Japan, chairing the 1937 commission that investigated the Moscow Trials' charges against Trotsky, and writing on nearly every public question of the age.

He retired from Columbia in 1930 but kept writing — *Art as Experience* and the *Logic* came after — and died in New York in 1952, aged 92, still at work.

## Major theories and concepts

### Experience and the critique of the "spectator theory"

Dewey's starting point is a complaint: philosophy since the Greeks, he argued, has treated knowledge as a spectator's view of a finished world — a mind standing outside reality, trying to copy it accurately. Against this he puts *experience* understood actively: an organism doing things to its environment and undergoing the consequences. Knowing is one mode of this transaction, not a separate realm. A child who touches a flame does not first form a private inner picture and then infer; the doing and the undergoing are bound together, and meaning is forged in that loop. The test of an idea is not whether it mirrors an antecedent reality but what difference it makes when acted on.

### Inquiry

Dewey's theory of knowledge is a theory of *inquiry*. Its pattern (set out fully in the 1938 *Logic*) begins with an **indeterminate situation** — something unsettled, obstructive, confusing. Inquiry first converts that felt difficulty into a definite *problem*, then develops a possible solution as a hypothesis, reasons out its consequences, and finally tests it in action. If the situation is transformed into a unified, settled one, inquiry has succeeded: the result is what Dewey calls **warranted assertibility**, his replacement for the quest for absolute certainty. Scientific method is simply this pattern refined and made self-conscious — which is why he insisted it should be the model for thinking in ethics, politics, and everyday life too.

### Habit and impulse

In *Human Nature and Conduct* (1922), morality is rebuilt on **habits**: not mechanical routines but flexible, socially formed dispositions that constitute the self. Character is "the interpenetration of habits." Because habits are formed in social environments, moral improvement is less a matter of private willpower than of changing the conditions — schools, laws, workplaces — that form habits. Intelligence enters when habits conflict or fail: deliberation is a "dramatic rehearsal" in imagination of competing lines of action.

### Education as growth

*Democracy and Education* (1916) argues that education is not preparation for life but life itself: growth is its only end ("the educational process has no end beyond itself"). Hence learning by doing, curricula organized around occupations and problems rather than isolated subjects, and hostility both to rote traditional schooling and to undisciplined "child-centred" romanticism (he criticized both in *Experience and Education*, 1938). The school should be an embryonic democratic community.

### Democracy and the public

Democracy, for Dewey, is "more than a form of government; it is primarily a mode of associated living." *The Public and Its Problems* (1927), written partly in response to Walter Lippmann's pessimism about ordinary citizens, diagnoses the "eclipse of the public": modern society's consequences are so indirect and complex that citizens cannot identify themselves as a public. The remedy is not rule by experts but better communication and local community, with experts serving inquiry rather than replacing it.

## Key works

- *The School and Society* (1899) — short manifesto from the Laboratory School years.
- *How We Think* (1910; revised 1933) — his account of reflective thought, for teachers and general readers.
- *Democracy and Education* (1916) — the classic: education, experience, and democracy as one project.
- *Human Nature and Conduct* (1922) — ethics and social psychology built on habit.
- *Experience and Nature* (1925) — his metaphysics of experience; dense but central.
- *The Public and Its Problems* (1927) — democracy, publics, and expertise.
- *The Quest for Certainty* (1929) — the Gifford Lectures; his crispest attack on philosophy's traditional quest for absolute certainty.
- *Art as Experience* (1934) — aesthetics: art as heightened everyday experience, not a museum category.
- *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938) — the mature statement of inquiry; technical.
- *Experience and Education* (1938) — brief correction of both traditional and progressive schooling.

## Contributions

Dewey gave pragmatism its most complete systematic form, extending a theory of meaning into logic, ethics, aesthetics, education, and politics. He effectively founded modern philosophy of education as an academic field, was a founding member of the NAACP (1909), the first president of the American Association of University Professors (1915), and a co-founder of the New School for Social Research (1919), and helped shift American psychology toward functionalism. His naturalism — treating mind, values, and inquiry as continuous with nature — remains a live option against both supernaturalism and reductive scientism.

## Impact and legacy

Progressive education worldwide drew on Dewey, sometimes faithfully, often in diluted forms he himself disowned. In China his lectures influenced educational reform and May Fourth-era thinkers such as his student Hu Shih. After mid-century eclipse by analytic philosophy, Dewey was revived by Richard Rorty and others; contemporary work in embodied cognition, democratic theory, and social epistemology regularly cites him as a forerunner. UNESCO-era and contemporary democratic-education movements still work his side of the street.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Vagueness of "experience" and "growth":** critics from Bertrand Russell onward charged that Dewey's key terms stretch until they explain everything and nothing, and that he slides between describing inquiry and defining truth by it.
- **Truth and warranted assertibility:** Russell and others objected that Dewey's account confuses the *meaning* of truth with the *method* of finding it — a belief might work in inquiry and still, intuitively, be false.
- **Progressive education backlash:** mid-century critics blamed Dewey for perceived anti-intellectualism in schools; defenders reply that he explicitly attacked the practices blamed on him — a debate about both the philosophy and its social transmission.
- **Instrumentalism about values:** whether treating ends as "ends-in-view," always revisable, dissolves genuine moral commitment or instead describes how commitment actually works.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Dewey did **not** say children should just do whatever they like; he criticized laissez-faire classrooms as firmly as rote ones.
- "Learning by doing" does not mean activity without content — the doing must be organized around genuine problems with real subject matter.
- Pragmatism in Dewey's hands is not "whatever works for me": inquiry is social, experimental, and answerable to consequences anyone can check.

## What to read first

Start with *Experience and Education* (short, crisp) or the middle chapters of *Democracy and Education*, then *Human Nature and Conduct* for the ethics. Save *Experience and Nature* and the *Logic* for later — they reward effort but punish haste.
