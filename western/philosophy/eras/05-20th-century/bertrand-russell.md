# Bertrand Russell

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Bertrand Arthur William Russell, 1872–1970 — a life spanning Victorian Britain to the nuclear age.
- **Place:** Born at Trellech, Wales; raised at Pembroke Lodge, Richmond, England; worked mainly at Cambridge, with periods of lecturing and activism in Britain and the United States.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy — one of its founders, alongside Frege, Moore, and Wittgenstein.
- **Known for:** Russell's paradox, the theory of descriptions, and logicism — and, to the wider public, a century of lucid, controversial writing on religion, war, education, and politics.

**Responds to:** [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [G. E. Moore](g-e-moore.md), [Bergson](henri-bergson.md) · **Answered by:** [Wittgenstein (early)](wittgenstein-early.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md), [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md), [Whitehead](alfred-north-whitehead.md)

## Biography

Bertrand Russell was born on 18 May 1872 into the British aristocracy; his grandfather, Lord John Russell, had twice been Prime Minister. His radical parents — advocates of birth control and atheists — died young (1874 and 1876), and Russell was raised at Pembroke Lodge by his grandmother, Countess Russell, in strict Victorian piety he experienced as lonely and oppressive. Educated at home, he found two rescues: Euclid, discovered at eleven and experienced as dazzling as first love, and the secret loss of his religious faith in adolescence, recorded in a journal in Greek letters to hide it.

At Trinity College, Cambridge, from 1890, Russell found his intellectual home, took first-class honors in mathematics and the Moral Sciences, and fell in with G. E. Moore. With Moore he led the revolt against British Idealism (Bradley, McTaggart), which held reality is a single Absolute. Russell's revolt was pluralist and realist: the world consists of many independent things, knowable piece by piece, and logic is the instrument for understanding its structure — a founding moment of analytic philosophy.

The decade after 1900 brought extraordinary technical achievement, driven by the attempt to show that mathematics rests on logic alone. In 1901 he discovered the paradox that bears his name, which undermined Gottlob Frege's system just as Frege was completing it. *The Principles of Mathematics* appeared in 1903. Then came "On Denoting" (1905), his most famous short paper, and the monumental *Principia Mathematica* (1910, 1912, 1913), written with Alfred North Whitehead — three vast volumes deriving mathematics from logical axioms, so austere that the proof that 1 + 1 = 2 arrives hundreds of pages in, with the dry comment that "the above proposition is occasionally useful."

Russell married four times (Alys Pearsall Smith, 1894; Dora Black, 1921, with whom he ran a school; Patricia Spence, 1936; Edith Finch, 1952). He stood unsuccessfully for Parliament as a women's suffrage candidate in 1907. Opposition to the First World War cost him his Trinity lectureship in 1916, and in 1918 he was sentenced to six months in Brixton Prison for a pacifist article, serving about four months of it (May–September) — composing the *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* there.

Between the wars he became a famous public intellectual — books on education, marriage, religion, and politics, and lecture tours of America and China. In 1940 his appointment at the City College of New York was revoked by a court after a campaign against his views on sexual morality — a cause célèbre for academic freedom. He returned to Trinity in 1944 and to public honor: the Order of Merit in 1949 and the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950. His most commercially successful book, *A History of Western Philosophy* (1945), is also among his most disputed by scholars.

His final decades were dominated by politics. With Einstein he issued the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955), leading to the Pugwash Conferences; he was a founding figure of the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (1958) and was imprisoned briefly in 1961, at eighty-nine, for civil disobedience. He opposed the Vietnam War and convened the Russell Tribunal (1966–67) with Sartre — supporters saw moral witness, critics a show trial; his political legacy is still argued between them. He died in Wales on 2 February 1970, aged ninety-seven.

## Major theories and concepts

### Logicism

*Logicism* is the thesis that mathematics is, at bottom, logic: mathematical concepts can be defined in purely logical terms and mathematical theorems derived from purely logical axioms. If true, it would explain mathematics' certainty — logic is certain — without Kant's appeal to a special faculty of intuition.

Russell's route ran through Peano's notation (Paris, 1900) and Frege, who had pursued logicism independently. *Principia Mathematica* was the great attempt. Two tensions dogged it. First, axioms such as infinity and reducibility look more like substantive assumptions than pure logic. Second, Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed any consistent system strong enough for arithmetic cannot prove all arithmetical truths, limiting the *Principia* project. Logicism in its original form is generally considered to have failed; in revised "neo-logicist" forms it remains a live program.

### Russell's paradox and the theory of types

In 1901 Russell considered the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. Is that set a member of itself? If it is, then by its defining condition it is not; if it is not, then it is. This is *Russell's paradox*, a contradiction inside the naive idea that every condition determines a set. Frege's system rested on a principle (Basic Law V) that generated the paradox, as Russell informed him in a famous letter of 1902, received just as Frege's second volume was in press. Frege's shattered appendix to that volume is one of the most moving documents in the history of logic.

Russell's remedy was the *theory of types*: entities are stratified — individuals, sets of individuals, sets of sets — and a set can only contain members of the next lower type, so "a set being a member of itself" is not false but ill-formed. The paradox is blocked by grammar. The costs were real: the ramified version, needed to block related paradoxes, made ordinary mathematics awkward to recover and forced the controversial axiom of reducibility. Modern set theory took a different route — the cumulative hierarchy of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory — but type theory enjoyed a second life in computer science, where typed programming languages and proof assistants are its direct descendants.

### The theory of descriptions

"On Denoting" (1905) — which Frank Ramsey called a paradigm of philosophy — addresses *definite descriptions*: phrases of the form "the so-and-so." The puzzle case: "The present King of France is bald." France has no king. Is the sentence true, false, or nonsense? If words mean by standing for things, we seem forced either to call it meaningless or to populate reality with a non-existent king for it to refer to — the route taken, in different ways, by Alexius Meinong.

Russell's dissolution: a description is not a genuine referring expression. It is an "incomplete symbol" that disappears under logical analysis. "The present King of France is bald" unpacks as a conjunction: there is at least one present King of France; there is at most one; and whatever is a present King of France is bald. The first clause is false, so the whole is false — meaningful, false, and committed to no shadowy king.

The payoffs were wide, and the method mattered as much as the result: *show that a problem arises from surface grammar, and dissolves when logical form is displayed* — analytic philosophy's signature move. Tensions remain: Strawson's "On Referring" (1950) argued ordinary uses of "the" *presuppose* rather than assert existence, so Russell mistook presupposition failure for falsity — opening a debate, continued by Donnellan, still taught as unresolved in its details.

### Logical atomism and logical construction

In lectures of 1918, Russell developed *logical atomism*: the world, and language fully analyzed, resolve into logical atoms — particulars, qualities, and relations — combined in atomic facts, pictured by atomic propositions. It paralleled Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*; each man influenced the other, and assigning priority on individual doctrines is contested.

A companion method was *logical construction*: replace inferred entities with constructions out of things directly known. Tables are constructions from sense-data; numbers are classes of classes; matter, in his later *neutral monism*, is constructed from events neither purely mental nor purely physical. Its tension is fruitful: constructions are only as secure as their data, and Russell changed his mind repeatedly about what those data are.

### Knowledge by acquaintance and by description

In *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), Russell distinguished *knowledge by acquaintance* — direct awareness of sense-data and universals — from *knowledge by description*, of things picked out as "the so-and-so," such as Bismarck for those who never met him. Most knowledge of the physical world is by description, anchored in acquaintance. The distinction gave analytic epistemology its vocabulary, even as later philosophers rejected its sense-data foundation.

## Signature arguments

**The theory of descriptions**

1. Sentences containing definite descriptions, such as “the present King of France is bald,” seem to refer to a present King of France — yet France has no king, and there is no such object to refer to.
2. If referring required an object, we would face a bad choice: call the sentence meaningless, or populate reality with nonexistent objects for descriptions to stand for.
3. On Russell's analysis in “On Denoting” (1905), a description is not a referring expression at all, but part of a quantificational structure: the sentence says that there is exactly one present King of France, and that whatever is one is bald.
4. Since the existence and uniqueness clauses are false, the whole sentence is false.
Therefore, no mysterious nonexistent entities are needed, and such sentences are simply false — meaningful, but false.

**Objection.** P. F. Strawson, in “On Referring” (1950), argued that Russell confuses sentences with their uses. Uttering “the King of France” does not assert that a king exists; it presupposes it, and when there is no king the utterance fails to make a true-or-false statement at all, rather than making a false one.

**Reply.** Russellians reply that the quantificational analysis captures the logic needed for inference and mathematics, where descriptions must combine, take scope, and enter proofs. Presupposition, they argue, can be layered on top of that logical form as a fact about use, without displacing the analysis. The Russell–Strawson dispute remains a standard reference point in the philosophy of language, presented as a live disagreement rather than a settled victory for either side.

**Russell's paradox**

1. Naive set theory assumes unrestricted comprehension: any property or condition defines a set — the set of all things satisfying that condition.
2. Apply that assumption to the property of not being a member of oneself, and consider the set of all sets that are not members of themselves.
3. Ask whether that set is a member of itself: if it is, then by its defining condition it is not; and if it is not, then by that same condition it is.
4. Both answers yield a contradiction.
Therefore, unrestricted comprehension is false, and set theory — and with it Frege's logicist foundations, to whom Russell reported the paradox in a letter of 1902 — must be rebuilt; Russell's own answer was the theory of types.

**Objection.** Type theory can look ad hoc. It bans the paradox by grammatical fiat, stratifying entities so that self-membership is ill-formed, and in doing so it also bans apparently harmless forms of self-reference that mathematics and language seem to need.

**Reply.** Defenders reply that the restrictions are independently motivated by the iterative conception of set: sets are formed in stages, each stage collecting only objects already available at earlier stages. On that picture, there is no stage at which a set could contain itself, and “all sets” cannot be treated like one set among others. The paradox, on this reading, taught a genuine lesson about sets, not merely a need for a technical patch.

## Key works

- ***The Principles of Mathematics*** (1903) — The first statement of his logicism, confronting the paradox's consequences.
- **"On Denoting"** (1905), in *Mind* — Seventeen pages that founded the modern philosophy of language; read it before any commentary.
- ***Principia Mathematica***, with A. N. Whitehead, 3 vols (1910, 1912, 1913) — The logicist monument. Consult it, marvel at it, do not try to read it through.
- ***The Problems of Philosophy*** (1912) — His short, lucid introduction to epistemology and metaphysics; still a standard first text.
- ***Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy*** (1919) — The accessible *Principia*, written in prison; the best route into his philosophy of mathematics.
- ***The Analysis of Mind*** (1921) and ***The Analysis of Matter*** (1927) — His mature, shifting philosophy of mind and science, including neutral monism.
- ***Why I Am Not a Christian*** (lecture 1927; collection 1957) — The public controversialist at his clearest on religion.
- ***A History of Western Philosophy*** (1945) — Sweeping, witty, and partisan; read for orientation and pleasure, with the [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/) at hand for adjudication, not as a neutral textbook.
- ***Autobiography***, 3 vols (1967–69) — A great intellectual autobiography; its prologue is his own summary.

## Contributions

1. **Modern logic as a philosophical instrument.** With Whitehead he gave the Fregean revolution its most influential textbook form and proved, by example, what formalization could do.
2. **A paradox that rebuilt foundations.** Russell's paradox forced the reconstruction of set theory and logic and still introduces every foundations course.
3. **The theory of descriptions.** A single analysis that became the model of philosophical analysis itself and founded modern philosophy of language.
4. **A bridge between technical and public philosophy.** No comparable logician has also been a Nobel laureate in literature and a global political campaigner; he created the modern figure of the public philosopher, for better and for worse.

## Impact and legacy

Inside philosophy, Russell's influence is structural: he taught Wittgenstein (and was then displaced by him), inspired Carnap and the Vienna Circle, and set the problems Ayer imported into British logical positivism. Much later work, from Strawson to Kripke, is written in explicit argument with him. In computer science, type theory descends from his remedy for the paradox. Outside philosophy, his popular books shaped generations of readers, and the Russell–Einstein Manifesto and Pugwash gave scientists a durable model for organized political responsibility.

## Criticisms and debates

**Technical.** The axioms of infinity and reducibility strain the claim that *Principia* mathematics is pure logic, and Gödel limited the whole formalist ambition. Strawson and Donnellan contest the theory of descriptions as an account of ordinary language, though its standing as an account of logical form remains strong. His sense-data epistemology was largely abandoned, including, in pieces, by himself.

**Historical.** Scholars dispute many verdicts in the *History of Western Philosophy* — on Nietzsche, on the medievals, on pragmatism — treating it as brilliant advocacy rather than reliable scholarship. Use it accordingly.

**Political and personal.** His tribunals, his shifting positions on preventive war in the late 1940s (disputed by biographers), and aspects of his private life have drawn fierce criticism. Balanced assessments distinguish the logician, whose results are secure, the public moralist, and the 1960s activist, about whom debate continues.

**The Wittgenstein verdict.** Russell judged the later Wittgenstein's influence pernicious; the profession mostly disagreed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Russell proved mathematics is logic."** He mounted the greatest attempt; its original form is generally held to have failed, for internal reasons and because of Gödel. Neo-logicism revives a modified claim.
- **"Start with *Principia Mathematica*."** Do not. Start with *The Problems of Philosophy* and the *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy*.
- **"The *History* is a neutral survey."** It is readable, opinionated, and frequently disputed. Enjoy it; check it.
- **"The theory of descriptions says 'the King of France' is meaningless."** It says the opposite: the sentence is meaningful and false, and the phrase has no meaning in isolation — it contributes to sentences without referring.
- **"Russell was simply an atheist gadfly."** The popular image omits the technical work on which his philosophical reputation rests.

## What to read first

1. ***The Problems of Philosophy*** (1912) — short, lucid, and still the best single-volume entry to his epistemology; chapters 1 and 5–7 first.
2. **"On Denoting"** (1905) — with a guide at hand (the Stanford Encyclopedia article on descriptions is ideal) for the King of France analysis and the Strawson debate.
3. ***Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy***, early chapters — for logicism, the paradox, and types, without *Principia*'s notation barrier.
4. **"What I Have Lived For"** (prologue to the *Autobiography*) and a few essays from *Why I Am Not a Christian* — the public voice, in his own self-understanding.
5. **Then, selectively, the *History of Western Philosophy*** — for pleasure and orientation, cross-checked against reference sources where it provokes disagreement.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Russell's analysis of "The present King of France is bald" as existence, uniqueness, and predication clauses. Why does this make the sentence false rather than meaningless, and why does it need no non-existent king?
2. Analyze "The first person to set foot on Mars will be famous" twice: as Frege would, the description a term with sense and possibly no reference; as Russell would, quantificational structure. Which analysis better explains why the sentence is already meaningful, and plausibly true?
3. State Strawson's objection: "the King of France" presupposes rather than asserts existence, so the utterance is neither true nor false. Then give the Russellian reply about logical form and inference. Which side better handles the sentence inside a proof?
4. "The present King of France is not bald" has two Russellian readings. State both, using scope, say which is true, and what the ambiguity shows about surface grammar.
5. Reconstruct the paradox from unrestricted comprehension, then evaluate the charge that type theory merely bans it by grammatical fiat: does the iterative conception of set answer that charge?
