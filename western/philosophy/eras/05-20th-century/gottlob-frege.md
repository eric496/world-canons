# Gottlob Frege

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Friedrich Ludwig Gottlob Frege, 1848–1925.
- **Place:** Born in Wismar, on the Baltic coast of Germany; spent nearly his whole career at the University of Jena.
- **School/tradition:** Founder of modern mathematical logic and a founding figure of analytic philosophy and the philosophy of language.
- **Known for:** The *Begriffsschrift* (1879) and modern predicate logic; the distinction between sense and reference; logicism — the attempt to found arithmetic on logic alone; and the paradox, discovered by Russell, that brought that project down at the moment of its completion.

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [J. S. Mill](../04-19th-century/john-stuart-mill.md) · **Answered by:** [Russell](bertrand-russell.md), [Wittgenstein (early)](wittgenstein-early.md), [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md)

## Biography

Frege was born on 8 November 1848 in Wismar, then part of the Grand Duchy of Mecklenburg-Schwerin. His father ran a girls' school; both parents were devout Lutherans. Frege studied mathematics, physics, chemistry, and philosophy at Jena and then Göttingen, where he took his doctorate in mathematics in 1873. He returned to Jena as a lecturer in 1874 and remained there for over forty years, rising slowly — he was made an honorary ordinary professor only in 1896, and his salary was modest enough that he lived quietly and somewhat frugally.

In 1879 he published the *Begriffsschrift* ("Concept-Script"), a slim book presenting the first complete system of predicate logic. It was largely ignored or misunderstood: reviewers found its two-dimensional notation forbidding, and the logicians of the day — working in the algebraic tradition of Boole and Schröder — saw little need for it. Frege pressed on regardless. In *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), written in prose rather than symbols, he argued that arithmetic is analytic and reducible to logic, attacking psychologism — the view that numbers are mental ideas — with lasting arguments. His essays "Function and Concept" (1891), "On Sense and Reference" (1892), and "On Concept and Object" (1892) laid the foundations of the modern philosophy of language.

The summit of the project was the *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (*Basic Laws of Arithmetic*), volume I appearing in 1893 and volume II in 1903. Just as the second volume was in press, Frege received a letter from the young Bertrand Russell: the class of all classes that are not members of themselves — is it a member of itself? Either answer yields a contradiction. Frege's system rested on his Basic Law V, which licensed exactly the formation of that class. His response, in an appendix written in haste, is one of the great documents of intellectual honesty: hardly anything more unfortunate, he wrote, can befall a scientific writer than to have a foundation of his edifice shaken after the work is finished. His attempted repair did not succeed, and he never published the planned third volume.

Frege's personal life darkened alongside the professional blow. His wife Margarete died in 1904; years later Frege took in and eventually adopted a son, Alfred. Politically he was a man of the nationalist right, and a diary from 1924, published only in 1994, records anti-Semitic and anti-democratic views that scholarship now states plainly rather than excusing. He retired in 1918 and died at Bad Kleinen on 26 July 1925, largely unrecognised: his fame came posthumously, through Russell, Wittgenstein (who visited him in 1911), and Rudolf Carnap, who had attended his Jena lectures.

## Major theories and concepts

### The Begriffsschrift and predicate logic

Aristotelian logic, essentially unchanged for two thousand years, could analyze "Socrates is mortal" but choked on relational and multiply general statements: "every farmer who owns a donkey beats it," or "for every number there is a larger one." Frege's breakthrough was to import the mathematical idea of a **function** into logic. Instead of subject and predicate, a proposition is analyzed as a function taking arguments: "x beats y" is a two-place relation awaiting saturation by names. Onto this he built the **quantifier** — a notation for "for all x" and "for some x" that nests inside itself, so statements with *scope* (the two readings of "everyone loves someone") can finally be written exactly. The result is modern predicate logic, the working tool of philosophy, mathematics, linguistics, and computer science. Almost everything in it is still Frege's design, though his two-dimensional notation was abandoned for the linear notation of Peano and Russell.

### Sense and reference

In "On Sense and Reference" (1892) Frege asks why "the Morning Star is the Evening Star" is informative while "the Morning Star is the Morning Star" is trivial, when both are true and both sides refer to Venus. His answer: a sign has a **reference** (*Bedeutung*) — the object it stands for — and a **sense** (*Sinn*) — the *mode of presentation* of that object. "Morning Star" and "Evening Star" share a reference but differ in sense, which is why the identity is a discovery. Sense is objective and shareable, not a private mental image. The thought expressed by a sentence is its sense; in indirect contexts ("Galileo believed that…") words shift to refer to their usual senses, dissolving puzzles about substitution in belief reports. The distinction remains a load-bearing wall of the philosophy of language.

### Logicism and the Foundations of Arithmetic

Frege's logicism holds that arithmetic is not synthetic knowledge grounded in Kantian intuition, nor empirical generalization (Mill), nor a description of mental processes, but **analytic**: derivable from logic plus definitions. The *Foundations* (1884) clears the ground with three methodological principles — separate the psychological from the logical, ask for the meaning of a word only in the context of a proposition (the **context principle**), and keep concept and object distinct — and then defines the number belonging to a concept as the extension of "equinumerous to that concept": two concepts have the same number when their instances can be paired one-to-one (the insight now called **Hume's Principle**). Zero is the number of the concept "not identical with itself"; each successor is defined in turn, and the infinity of the number series becomes a theorem rather than an intuition. The *Grundgesetze* then undertook the formal derivation. Modern "neo-Fregean" logicists have shown that Hume's Principle, added to second-order logic, suffices to derive the Peano axioms — a result known as **Frege's Theorem** — so the program, minus Law V, has been partly revived.

### Russell's paradox and Frege's response

**Basic Law V** said, roughly, that two concepts have the same extension just when they apply to the same objects — it let every concept determine a set-like object. Russell's paradox showed this permits the set of all non-self-membered sets, which must both contain and not contain itself. Frege saw at once that the law, and with it his derivations, could not stand unamended. His appendix proposed restricting Law V, but the patch was later shown to fail. Frege himself, late in life, abandoned logicism and toyed with a geometrical foundation for arithmetic. The paradox did not destroy his logic: predicate logic and the sense/reference framework are independent of Law V. Type theory (Russell) and axiomatic set theory (Zermelo) were built largely as alternative responses to the same crisis — responses possible only because Frege had made the problem precise.

## Key works

- *Begriffsschrift* (1879) — The first complete predicate logic, presented in Frege's daunting two-dimensional notation; few read it, everyone inherited it.
- *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884) — The prose masterpiece: anti-psychologism, the context principle, and the logicist definition of number. The best entry point.
- "On Sense and Reference" (1892) — Morning Star / Evening Star; the sense–reference distinction and a theory of indirect discourse.
- *Basic Laws of Arithmetic*, vols. I–II (1893, 1903) — The formal logicist derivation, with the famous appendix on Russell's paradox.
- *Philosophical Writings* (posthumous selections) — Includes "The Thought" (1918), his late defense of objective, timeless thoughts — a "third realm" neither physical nor mental.

## Contributions

Frege single-handedly created modern logic: quantifiers, variables, functions in logical analysis, and the axiomatic treatment of inference. The context principle and the sense/reference distinction founded the philosophy of language as a systematic discipline. His campaign against psychologism — the insistence that logic and arithmetic describe objective structures, not mental episodes — set methodological terms for Husserl (whom a Frege review helped convert on the point) and for analytic philosophy generally. Even his failure was fertile: Russell's paradox, which his system made discoverable, drove set theory, type theory, and a century of foundational work.

## Impact and legacy

Frege's influence ran through conduits: Russell publicised him; Wittgenstein absorbed his questions ("to whose great works I owe in large part the stimulation of my thoughts"); Carnap carried the Jena lectures into the Vienna Circle; and through those channels his framework reached Michael Dummett, who argued in *Frege: Philosophy of Language* (1973) that Frege, not Descartes or Kant, founded modern philosophy — because he made language, not mind, the first object of analysis. Computer science inherits his function–argument analysis and quantifier logic; linguistics inherits his compositionality principle (the meaning of a sentence is built from the meanings of its parts). The neo-logicist revival of Frege's Theorem shows the foundational project still generating new mathematics.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The concept horse problem.** Frege insisted concepts are "unsaturated" and cannot be referred to by noun phrases; yet his own sentence "the concept *horse* is a concept" seems, by his lights, to fail to say what it means. Frege acknowledged the awkwardness — language forces a "pinch of salt" — and philosophers still debate whether the doctrine is coherent or a reductio of the concept/object distinction.
- **Did Law V's failure sink logicism?** The mainstream verdict was yes, until neo-Fregeans showed the derivation works from Hume's Principle alone. Critics reply that Hume's Principle is not pure logic either, and that "bad company" principles formally similar to it are inconsistent — the debate continues.
- **Sense: objectivity vs usefulness.** Russell rejected senses as needless intermediaries; Kripke's causal theory of reference challenged the descriptivism often built on Frege; Dummett and Evans defended revised Fregean senses. Where the boundary lies is live.
- **The man and the work.** The 1924 diary's bigotry is documented and indefensible; the standing question is the general one of how a thinker's vices bear on his technical work — here the near-consensus is that predicate logic stands untouched, but the diary has permanently ended the era of presenting Frege as a pure, disembodied intellect.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Frege did not "fail at everything." The logic and the philosophy of language succeeded completely; only the logicist derivation via Law V collapsed.
- Sense is not a mental image or private idea — Frege explicitly opposed that conflation; senses are objective and communicable.
- Russell's paradox did not show that sets are impossible; it showed one naive principle governing them is inconsistent, which set theory then repaired.
- Frege was not a member of the Vienna Circle or a logical positivist; he died in 1925, before its manifesto, and his Platonism about thoughts sits awkwardly with positivist doctrine.

## What to read first

1. *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884) — read §§1–69 for the demolition of rival views, then the definition of number; short, witty, and largely symbol-free.
2. "On Sense and Reference" (1892) — the Morning Star puzzle and its solution, in under twenty pages.
3. *The Basic Writings of Bertrand Russell* or Russell's *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* (1919), chs. I and XVII, for a friendly account of what the paradox destroyed and what survived.
4. Then Michael Dummett's *Frege: Philosophy of Mathematics* or Anthony Kenny's *Frege* (in the Routledge Philosophers series) for a guided tour of the logic itself.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Frege's solution to the puzzle of identity: why is "the Morning Star is the Evening Star" a discovery while "the Morning Star is the Morning Star" is trivial, when both are true? Which work is done by sense, and which by reference?
2. Russell's paradox contradicts Basic Law V. Reconstruct the contradiction, then assess the neo-Fregean reply that Hume's Principle alone can carry the derivation of arithmetic: has logicism been rescued, or has the assumption just been relocated?
3. Frege rejects both Kant's synthetic a priori arithmetic and Mill's empirical generalizations. Reconstruct his case against each. Which opponent is more directly hit by the context principle, and why?
4. A headline reads: "The author of the next bestselling novel is already rich." Give the Fregean analysis: the sense and (if any) reference of the description, and whether the sentence can express a true thought before any such novel exists.
