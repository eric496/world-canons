# Alfred North Whitehead

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1861–1947
- **Place:** England (Cambridge and London), then the United States; professor at Harvard from 1924
- **School/tradition:** Process philosophy; speculative metaphysics
- **Known for:** *Principia Mathematica* (1910–13, with Russell), the metaphysics of *Process and Reality* (1929) — actual occasions, prehension, concrescence — and the fallacy of misplaced concreteness

**Responds to:** [Russell](bertrand-russell.md), [Bergson](henri-bergson.md), Plato (whose dialogues he rereads as a process thinker) · **Answered by:** [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md) (his doctoral student, who took the training and rejected the metaphysics), and the process theologians who developed his idea of God

## Biography

Alfred North Whitehead was born in Ramsgate, Kent, in 1861, the son of a clergyman and schoolmaster, and educated at home until he was fourteen, then at Sherborne School, before winning a scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge. He stayed at Trinity as a fellow in mathematics, wrote *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898), and taught the young Bertrand Russell — beginning the collaboration that produced *Principia Mathematica* (1910–13), the three-volume attempt to derive mathematics from logic, and one of the most consequential books of the century. Whitehead's part in it is often understated: the projected fourth volume, on geometry, was his, and its abandonment marks the point where his interests were already moving from logic toward the philosophy of nature.

In 1910 he left Cambridge for London — University College, then Imperial College — and began the second career of his intellectual life. *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Natural Knowledge* (1919), *The Concept of Nature* (1920) and *The Principle of Relativity* (1922) rebuilt the philosophy of science around events rather than matter: nature is not stuff sitting in space and time but a passing of occurrences, and the abstractions of physics mistake their own models for the concrete world. In 1924, at sixty-three, when a career would normally be closing, Harvard invited him to a chair in philosophy. He accepted, having never held a philosophy post, and produced in thirteen years the works on which his reputation now rests: *Science and the Modern World* (1925), *Religion in the Making* (1926), *Symbolism* (1927), *Process and Reality* (1929), *The Function of Reason* (1929), *Adventures of Ideas* (1933) and *Modes of Thought* (1938). He retired from Harvard in 1937, remained in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and died there in 1947. Among his doctoral students was W. V. O. Quine — the process metaphysician supervised the logician who would define the opposite temper in American philosophy.

## Major theories and concepts

### From logic to the philosophy of organism

Whitehead's mature system — he called it the **philosophy of organism**, later labeled **process philosophy** — begins from a refusal. The inherited picture of nature as matter in motion, built of substances that simply sit in space and endure through time, he regarded as an abstraction masquerading as reality. What is ultimately real are not enduring things but **occasions of experience**: momentary events of becoming, each of which takes up the world that produced it, unifies it in a novel way, and perishes into the data inherited by the next. Mountains and electrons are societies or routes of such occasions, stable patterns in the flux — real, but derivative. "The safest general characterization of the European philosophical tradition," he wrote in *Process and Reality*, "is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato" — and his Plato is the Plato of the *Timaeus*, for whom becoming is as fundamental as being.

### Actual occasions, prehension, concrescence

The technical core is austere. Each **actual occasion** (or actual entity) is a drop of experience constituted by its **prehensions** — its graspings of other occasions. A prehension is not yet consciousness: it is any taking-account-of, physical or felt, by which one occasion inherits and selectively transforms its predecessors. The process by which an occasion's many prehensions integrate into one determinate unity is its **concrescence**, ending in a **satisfaction** — the occasion achieved, after which it perishes and becomes a datum ("superject") for later occasions. **Eternal objects** — pure possibilities of definiteness, like colors, shapes and mathematical forms — "ingress" into occasions and give each its character; they are Whitehead's analogue of Platonic forms, doing their work inside process rather than above it. **Creativity** — the many becoming one, and being increased by one — is the category of the ultimate: not a thing but the form of all becoming. Even rocks, on this view, are not inert: they are highly repetitive societies of occasions, and the difference between a stone and a mind is one of complexity and novelty, not of ontological kind — a position often called panexperientialism, though Whitehead's occasions are far below anything like thought.

### The fallacy of misplaced concreteness and the bifurcation of nature

Two critical doctrines do much of the system's public work. The **fallacy of misplaced concreteness** is the error of treating an abstraction — the physicist's point-instant, the economist's rational agent, the philosopher's substance — as if it were the concrete reality it was abstracted from. Whitehead held that modern thought commits this fallacy wholesale, and that much philosophy is the business of catching it. The **bifurcation of nature**, named in *The Concept of Nature*, is its scientific form: the split between nature as it is in itself (molecules, wavelengths — the presumed causes) and nature as experienced (color, warmth, value — demoted to subjective by-products in the mind). Whitehead refuses the split: the red of the sunset and the physics of light are both abstractions from one concrete event, and a philosophy of nature must start from what is actually given — the passing occasion of experience.

### God, dipolar and persuasive

*Process and Reality* gives God a technical place in the system, and it is not the place of classical theism. God is an actual entity with two natures. In the **primordial nature**, God envisages all eternal objects in their relevance to each occasion, offering each new occasion an initial aim — a lure toward the best possibility open to it. In the **consequent nature**, God prehends the finished world, receiving every occasion into the divine life as it perishes. God therefore does not create the world from nothing, does not override occasions by coercion, and is not omnipotent as classical theology defines it: God persuades, and is in turn enriched by what the world becomes — "the fellow-sufferer who understands," in *Process and Reality*'s closing words. Whether this is a recovery of religious truth or a metaphysical construction with religious uses has been debated ever since, and Whitehead's own position — he was a lapsed Anglican who treated religion as a human phenomenon to be explained, not a creed to be defended — resists simple classification.

## Key works

- *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898) — the first career's monument.
- *Principia Mathematica*, 3 vols. (1910–13), with Bertrand Russell — logicism's founding text.
- *The Concept of Nature* (1920) — events not matter; the bifurcation of nature criticized.
- *Science and the Modern World* (1925) — the Lowell lectures; misplaced concreteness; the modern scientific worldview historicised.
- *Religion in the Making* (1926) — religion as experience, dogma as its dangerous sediment.
- *Process and Reality* (1929) — the system entire, from the Gifford Lectures of 1927–28; among the most difficult books of the century.
- *Adventures of Ideas* (1933) — the system applied to civilization: truth, beauty, adventure, peace.
- *Modes of Thought* (1938) — the late, comparatively accessible lectures.

## Contributions

Whitehead made two distinct contributions that rarely get credited to one person. With Russell he co-authored the founding document of analytic philosophy's logical turn — and then walked away from its premises, becoming the century's most systematic defender of speculative metaphysics at the moment of its eclipse. The second contribution is the system itself: the first fully worked-out metaphysics built for a post-Newtonian, evolutionary universe, in which relation is prior to substance, experience extends below mind, and novelty is a fundamental feature of the world rather than an appearance to be explained away. His critical vocabulary — misplaced concreteness above all — escaped the system and is used by people who have never read him.

## Impact and legacy

Whitehead's direct academic afterlife ran in two channels. The first is **process theology**: Charles Hartshorne, his former assistant, and John B. Cobb Jr. developed the consequent nature of God into a full theological program in which God is genuinely affected by the world — a development Whitehead made possible but did not himself carry out, and which should be distinguished from his own more austere claims. Process thought also fed ecological philosophy and, through Cobb, an unlikely dialogue with Chinese thought. The second channel is quieter: Quine's formation at Harvard, the event-ontologies and four-dimensionalisms that recur in analytic metaphysics, and periodic rediscoveries — by Deleuze, who admired him, by Isabelle Stengers, and by contemporary metaphysicians of grounding and structure — whenever substance-based pictures run into their limits. In Anglophone philosophy departments he remains a respected outlier: more cited for *Principia* than read in *Process and Reality*.

## Criticisms and debates

The standing charge is obscurity compounded by system-building. Whitehead coined dozens of technical terms, defined them through each other, and wrote *Process and Reality* at a density its author cheerfully admitted would trouble readers; to a profession forming itself around logical analysis, the whole enterprise looked like a relapse into the metaphysics it had just escaped. The logical positivists ignored him; Russell, his collaborator, found the later work uncongenial. Defenders reply that the difficulty is the price of a genuinely alternative categorial scheme, and that the shorter works state the vision plainly. Substantive critics press other points: that eternal objects re-import the Platonism the process was meant to dissolve; that occasions below the level of life "experience" anything is either metaphor or an unexplained posit; and that the dipolar God, neither creator nor omnipotent, satisfies neither classical theists nor naturalists. Whether the system can be revised piecewise — as some contemporary process metaphysicians attempt — or stands and falls as a whole remains an open question within its small literature.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Whitehead did not write *Principia Mathematica* as Russell's assistant. He was the senior collaborator; the book was a joint enterprise, and its unfinished fourth volume was to be his.
- Process theology is the reception of Whitehead, not Whitehead. Hartshorne and Cobb built a theology from his God; Whitehead himself offered a metaphysical scheme in which God has a defined function, and was markedly reticent about creeds.
- "Everything is connected" is not the philosophy. The system is a precise — arguably over-precise — categorial machinery of occasions, prehensions and ingression, not a slogan about interconnection.
- The later work is not a repudiation caused by failure in logic. Whitehead regarded logic and abstraction as genuine but partial, and his metaphysics as the correction of their overreach — the fallacy of misplaced concreteness applied to his own first career.

## What to read first

1. *Science and the Modern World* (1925) — the vision in its most readable form: how the scientific worldview formed, and what it leaves out.
2. *Adventures of Ideas* (1933), Parts I and IV — the system warming into cultural criticism, and the closing reflections on peace.
3. *Process and Reality* (1929), Part I and the final chapter — attempt the system's own statement only with the first two behind you; Part I lays out the categorial scheme that the rest presupposes.
