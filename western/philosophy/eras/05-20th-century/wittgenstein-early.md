# Wittgenstein, Early

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Ludwig Wittgenstein, 1889–1951. The "early" Wittgenstein is the philosopher of roughly 1911–1929, above all of the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*.
- **Place:** Born in Vienna, Austria-Hungary; worked mainly in Cambridge, England, with formative periods in Norway and Austria.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy, at its founding moment — logic, language, and the limits of thought.
- **Known for:** The picture theory of meaning, and the claim that what cannot be said clearly must be passed over in silence — because the most important things lie beyond factual speech.

**Responds to:** [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](bertrand-russell.md) · **Answered by:** [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md), [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md)

## Biography

Ludwig Wittgenstein was born on 26 April 1889 in Vienna, the youngest of eight children in one of the wealthiest families in the Austro-Hungarian Empire. His father Karl was a steel magnate; his mother Leopoldine was musical, and the family home was a center of Viennese cultural life visited by Brahms and Mahler. The household was also intense and demanding. Three of Wittgenstein's brothers died by suicide, and Wittgenstein himself struggled with suicidal thoughts throughout his life. Any honest biography holds both parts together: immense privilege, and a severe, restless temperament that never let him settle comfortably inside it.

Educated at home until fourteen, then at a technical school in Linz, he first pursued engineering: Berlin, then Manchester in 1908 for aeronautics research. The mathematics it required led him to its foundations. Reading Russell's *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903) and Frege changed his direction. In 1911 Frege advised him to study with Russell at Cambridge. He arrived unannounced and argued so well that Russell quickly judged him a genius — "the most perfect example I have ever known of genius as traditionally conceived, passionate, profound, intense, and dominating."

After his father's death in 1913 left him very rich, Wittgenstein withdrew to a house he had built at Skjolden in Norway. In 1914 he gave away 100,000 crowns to Austrian writers and artists (Rilke and Trakl among them); the bulk of the fortune itself he transferred to his siblings only in 1919, after the war. There, in isolation through the winter, the core ideas of the *Tractatus* took shape.

In 1914 Wittgenstein volunteered for the Austro-Hungarian army, although he could have sought exemption. He served on the Vistula, in an artillery workshop, and then — at his own request — at the front, and was decorated for bravery. His wartime notebooks, written partly in code, show a deepening preoccupation with God, ethics, and death, alongside the logic. He finished the manuscript in summer 1918, carried it into Italian captivity that November, and from a prison camp sent it to Russell.

Publication was a struggle: it finally appeared in German in 1921, and in English in 1922, translated by C. K. Ogden with Ramsey's help and an introduction by Russell — which Wittgenstein disliked, thinking Russell had misunderstood its ethical point. The title was suggested by G. E. Moore, echoing Spinoza.

Believing he had solved philosophy's problems, Wittgenstein left it. From 1920 to 1926 he taught in poor villages in Lower Austria — earnestly, and by many accounts too intensely; it ended badly, including an incident in which he struck a pupil who collapsed, after which he left teaching (he was later cleared in court, and the episode haunted him). He then worked as a gardener's assistant, and in 1926–1928 helped design a famously austere house in Vienna for his sister Margaret.

In 1929, partly after conversations with Ramsey and with members of the Vienna Circle, and partly because his confidence in the *Tractatus* had begun to crack, he returned to Cambridge. That return begins the companion entry, Wittgenstein, Late. Whether the early work should be read as a bold metaphysical system or as a ladder to be thrown away once climbed is still debated, as discussed below.

## Major theories and concepts

### The world as facts, not things

The *Tractatus* opens: "The world is all that is the case" (1). The world is not best understood as a collection of objects, like items in a drawer. It is the totality of *facts* — that things stand in certain relations. A chair is a thing; that the chair is next to the table is a fact. Objects are simple and fixed, but they combine into *states of affairs*, and a state of affairs that obtains is a fact. Language, on this view, hooks onto the world at the level of facts, not isolated things. To understand a sentence is to understand which fact it presents.

Which objects are truly simple was never settled by Wittgenstein — he gave no convincing final examples — and that gap later became a pressure point for his second philosophy.

### The picture theory of meaning

A proposition with sense is a *picture* of a possible state of affairs. A picture need not look like what it depicts. A musical score, a gramophone record, and a performed symphony share, Wittgenstein argued, an internal structure that lets one be projected onto another. In the same way, a proposition shares *logical form* with the fact it pictures. Its elements (names) correspond to objects, and their arrangement mirrors a possible arrangement of those objects in the world.

Consider "The cat is on the mat." On an early-Wittgensteinian analysis, a fully analyzed version would arrange names in a way that mirrors a possible arrangement of things. If the cat really is on the mat, the pictured state of affairs obtains and the proposition is true; if not, it is false but still meaningful, because it still pictures a possibility. This bipolarity — capable of truth and capable of falsity — is the mark of a genuine factual proposition.

Everyday language, Wittgenstein thought, disguises this structure. Philosophical confusion arises when we mistake surface grammar for real logical form. Clarification means analysis: breaking a proposition down until its picturing relation becomes perspicuous.

### Logical form: what can be shown but not said

If a proposition pictures a fact by sharing logical form with it, can a proposition *describe* that shared form? Wittgenstein's answer is no. Any attempt to state the form would itself have to stand outside the form it describes, and that is impossible. Logical form therefore *shows itself* in meaningful propositions, but cannot itself be said: "What can be shown, cannot be said" (4.1212).

This saying/showing distinction does much of the book's work. Tautologies such as "it is raining or it is not raining" say nothing about the weather, but they show the structure of language and the world. Similarly, the propositions of the *Tractatus* itself — about pictures, form, and the world — cannot, by its own standard, be factual pictures. Wittgenstein faces this in the penultimate remark, calling his own propositions a ladder: the reader who understands him must climb through them and then throw the ladder away (6.54). How literally to take that instruction is the central interpretive dispute about the book.

### The limits of language and the mystical

If only factual propositions have sense, then ethics, aesthetics, religion, and metaphysics cannot be stated as facts. "Ethics cannot be put into words. Ethics is transcendental" (6.421). The meaning of the world as a whole must lie outside the world of facts. Wittgenstein calls this dimension *the mystical*: not a set of hidden facts, but the experience of the world as a limited whole, wonder that the world exists at all.

The tone matters. In a 1919 letter to publisher Ludwig von Ficker, Wittgenstein wrote that the book's point was ethical, that it consisted of the part written and all the part he had *not* written — and that the unwritten part was the important one. Drawing a limit around factual speech protected what mattered most. Hence the final proposition: "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence" (7).

### Solipsism, the self, and death

In the 5s, Wittgenstein argues the thinking subject is not an item *in* the world but a limit of it, much as the eye is not itself in the visual field. Properly understood, solipsism coincides with a kind of realism, and death is not an event within life. These compressed, contested remarks show the *Tractatus* was never only a logic textbook.

## Key works

- **Notebooks 1914–1916** (published posthumously, 1961) — The wartime notebooks from which the *Tractatus* grew; indispensable for seeing the ethical and religious concerns alongside the logic.
- ***Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*** — German edition 1921; English book edition 1922, translated by C. K. Ogden (a later, widely used translation is by D. F. Pears and B. F. McGuinness, 1961). About 75 pages, arranged as seven main numbered propositions with decimal sub-remarks. The only book of philosophy Wittgenstein published in his lifetime.

## Contributions

Before Wittgenstein, Frege and Russell had shown that modern logic could clarify philosophical problems. Wittgenstein added, in this form, something new:

1. **A unified picture of language, thought, and world.** One theory — picturing through shared logical form — was meant to explain meaning, truth, and the limits of both at a stroke.
2. **The saying/showing distinction.** The idea that some things are manifested by language rather than stated in it reshaped debates about logic, ethics, and religion.
3. **Philosophy as clarification, not doctrine.** The *Tractatus* declares that philosophy is not a body of propositions but an activity of clarifying thoughts (4.112). That conception became a lasting analytic ideal, even for those who rejected the picture theory.
4. **A limit drawn from inside.** Earlier thinkers had limited knowledge; Wittgenstein tried to limit *sense* itself, from within language, and to honor what lay beyond the limit rather than debunk it.

## Impact and legacy

The Vienna Circle seized on the *Tractatus* in the 1920s, reading it line by line and treating it as a foundation for logical empiricism: science speaks sense; metaphysics does not. Wittgenstein cooperated only fitfully — he met Moritz Schlick and Friedrich Waismann, sometimes read poetry aloud instead of discussing logic, and insisted he was no empiricist. The Circle's use of him is a major, partly mistaken, chapter of his reception, covered in the Vienna Circle entry.

In Britain, Ramsey helped transmit Wittgenstein's ideas, and the *Tractatus* set the interwar analytic agenda: what can be meaningfully said, and where does language run out? Outside philosophy it became a cultural object, admired for its austere numbered form and closing silence.

## Criticisms and debates

**Ramsey's early objections.** Frank Ramsey pressed, among other points, that Wittgenstein's treatment of identity, of mathematics, and of color-exclusion did not work as stated. "This is red and green all over" is a problem because elementary propositions were supposed to be independent, yet colors exclude each other. That problem was one acknowledged pressure that helped push Wittgenstein back to philosophy in 1929.

**The simple objects.** Critics ask what the simple objects and elementary names actually are. Wittgenstein demanded them on theoretical grounds — without simples, sense would not be determinate — but never produced a settled example. Defenders reply that the demand is structural, not an inventory; critics reply that a theory of meaning should not rest on items no one can exhibit.

**The resolute vs. traditional reading.** On a *traditional* (or ineffability) reading, defended in different forms by P. M. S. Hacker and others, the *Tractatus* conveys genuine, if unsayable, truths about logical form and the mystical. On a *resolute* reading, associated with Cora Diamond and James Conant, there are no ineffable truths to convey: the book's propositions are plain nonsense, and therapy is complete only when the reader stops trying to keep a theory. A middle range of readings also exists. This is a live scholarly dispute, not a settled verdict.

**Self-application.** If the book's own sentences lack factual sense, why trust them? Wittgenstein embraced the paradox with the ladder metaphor; whether that is profound consistency or evasion remains contested.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Wittgenstein proved ethics and religion are worthless."** Almost the reverse. He held that what cannot be said was the important part of life; silence was reverence, not contempt.
- **"He was a logical positivist."** He influenced the Vienna Circle deeply but was not a member and not an empiricist.
- **"The picture theory is about mental images."** A picture here is a logical model sharing form with a fact, not a photograph in the mind.
- **"Russell's introduction explains the book."** Wittgenstein thought it misunderstood him. Read it, if at all, as Russell's view, not an authorized summary.
- **"The *Tractatus* is a consistent system to memorise."** Its author included instructions for discarding it. Read it as a work in productive tension with itself.

## What to read first

1. **A short guide, alongside, not after.** A concise commentary such as Michael Morris, *Wittgenstein and the "Tractatus"*, or the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/) article, to orient you.
2. **The *Tractatus* itself, slowly, in the Pears–McGuinness translation** — a few numbered remarks at a time, with the guide open. Propositions 1–4 first; then 5–7 once the picture theory is in hand.
3. **The *Notebooks 1914–1916*, selections** — to see war, ethics, and logic growing together.
4. **Then the Ficker letter and propositions 6.54–7 again** — and decide for yourself between the traditional and resolute readings before moving to the late work.

## Study questions

1. Using "The cat is on the mat," reconstruct the picture theory: what corresponds to what, what does the proposition share with the fact, and why is bipolarity — being capable of truth and of falsity — the mark of a genuine factual proposition?
2. State Ramsey's color-exclusion objection: "this is red all over" and "this is green all over" exclude each other, yet elementary propositions were meant to be independent. Why is this a problem inside the system rather than an external quibble?
3. The Vienna Circle read the *Tractatus* as licensing the dismissal of ethics as nonsense. Using proposition 6.421 and the Ficker letter, argue whether that reading captures or betrays the book's point about what cannot be said.
4. Apply the ladder (6.54) to the sentence "A proposition shares logical form with the fact it pictures." By the book's own standard, can it be said? Defend either the resolute or the traditional reading of your answer.
