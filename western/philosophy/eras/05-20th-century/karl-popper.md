# Karl Popper

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1902–1994
- **Place:** Born in Vienna, later based in New Zealand and London
- **School/tradition:** Analytic, liberal tradition; philosopher of science and political philosopher
- **Known for:** Replacing verification with falsifiability as the mark of science, and for defending the “open society” against historicism

**Responds to:** [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md) · **Answered by:** [Kuhn](thomas-kuhn.md)

## Biography

Karl Raimund Popper was born in Vienna in 1902 to a middle-class family of Jewish origin that had converted to Lutheranism. Interwar Vienna was intellectually intense and politically unstable, and the young Popper moved through several of its worlds. He briefly joined socialist and communist circles, then broke with Marxism after a 1919 incident in which workers were shot during a demonstration he had supported. He later said it taught him the danger of theories that excuse any outcome in advance.

At the University of Vienna he studied mathematics, physics, history, and philosophy, earning a doctorate in psychology in 1928, and worked as a schoolteacher while writing. He was close to, but never a member of, the Vienna Circle: they shared an interest in science and clarity; he disagreed with them about meaning and about induction.

His first major book, *Logik der Forschung* (1934, with a 1935 imprint in some listings), appeared as Austria slid toward authoritarian rule. In 1937 he emigrated to Christchurch, New Zealand, where he taught at Canterbury University College and, isolated during the Second World War, wrote *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) — partly a war effort by other means, an attack on the intellectual roots of totalitarianism.

In 1946 he moved to the London School of Economics, becoming professor of logic and scientific method. Knighted in 1965, he retired in 1969 but continued writing until his death in London in 1994. Students such as Imre Lakatos and Paul Feyerabend began as followers and became his sharpest critics — which Popper, in principle, could present as his method working as intended.

## Major theories and concepts

### Falsifiability as the demarcation of science

Popper’s central question was not “what makes a statement meaningful?” — the Vienna Circle’s question — but “what distinguishes science from non-science?” His answer: a theory is scientific insofar as it *forbids* some observable outcomes and can therefore be falsified. The riskier and more precise its predictions, the more scientific content it has.

His classic contrast came from 1919 Vienna. Einstein’s general relativity made a risky prediction — starlight would bend by a specific amount near the Sun, tested by Eddington’s eclipse expedition — and would have been abandoned if the observation went the other way. By contrast, Popper thought Marxist history as he then encountered it, and the psychoanalytic theories of Freud and Adler, could explain *any* outcome after the fact: repression, sublimation, inferiority complex — some explanation is always available. A theory that explains everything, Popper argued, explains nothing, because it takes no risks. This does not mean non-scientific theories are worthless; myths, metaphysics, and psychoanalysis might be interesting or even partly true. They simply lack the testable structure of science on his criterion.

### Conjectures and refutations: the answer to induction

David Hume had shown that induction has no logical justification: no number of past observations *proves* that the future will resemble the past. No number of white swans proves all swans are white; one black swan refutes the claim. Popper accepted Hume’s logic and turned it into a method. Science, he said, does not grow by accumulating verifications. It grows by bold *conjectures* — guesses that go well beyond the data — followed by severe attempts at *refutation*.

A theory that survives severe tests is, in Popper’s term, *corroborated*. Corroboration is a report about past performance, not a guarantee of truth or even of probability. We may rationally prefer the best-corroborated theory for practical action, Popper argued, without ever claiming we have verified it. All scientific knowledge remains fallible and revisable.

### The Duhem–Quine problem and the limits of falsification

The obvious objection, associated with Pierre Duhem and later W. V. O. Quine, is that no theory is tested alone. Tests involve auxiliary hypotheses about instruments, background conditions, and other theories. When a prediction fails, logic alone does not say whether the main theory is false or an auxiliary was wrong: astronomers who found Uranus off its predicted orbit did not abandon Newton; they posited an unseen planet — Neptune — and were right.

Popper replied with methodological rules: decide in advance what would count as falsification, and do not rescue a theory with *ad hoc* modifications adding no new testable content. Critics reply that this turns falsification into communal decision, weakening his clean logic. Lakatos developed the tension most fruitfully: research programs rationally protect a hard core for a time and are judged by whether they predict novel facts, not by single failures. The popular image — one failed test and the theory dies — is *naive falsificationism*, a position Popper himself did not quite hold.

### Historicism and the open society

Popper extended fallibilism into politics. *Historicism*, as he defined it, is the doctrine that history is governed by discoverable, inevitable laws, so that large-scale prophecy about society’s future is possible. He traced versions of it through Plato, Hegel, and Marx, and argued it leads, however well-intentioned, toward authoritarian planning: if you know history’s destination, dissent looks like ignorance or obstruction.

Against this he set the *open society*: a society in which no one — ruler, party, or philosopher — is treated as possessing final truth, institutions allow criticism, and governments can be removed without violence. Democracy mattered to Popper not because the people are wise, but because it is the system that lets us get rid of bad rulers peacefully. The corresponding method in social policy is *piecemeal engineering*: small, reversible reforms whose effects can be monitored and corrected, rather than utopian blueprints for remaking society whole.

## Signature arguments

**The demarcation argument**

1. Induction cannot justify a universal scientific claim: no number of observed white swans proves that all swans are white, because the next observation might always differ.
2. A single genuine counter-instance, by contrast, falsifies a universal claim: one black swan, if accepted, refutes "all swans are white."
3. What gives a theory scientific content is therefore that it forbids observable states of affairs and takes risks: the more it rules out, the more it says and the more severely it can be tested.
Therefore, falsifiability, not verification, is the criterion of the scientific status of a theory, and science proceeds by bold conjecture followed by attempted refutation.

Popper's contrast cases, from his experience in Vienna in 1919, were Marxist history and psychoanalysis as he encountered them. On his telling, they could explain any outcome after the fact and so risked nothing, whereas Einstein's general relativity staked itself on a precise prediction about starlight bending near the Sun. A theory that explains everything, he concluded, explains nothing in the scientific sense — though he insisted that non-scientific did not mean meaningless or worthless.

**Objection.** The Duhem–Quine problem is that no theory is tested alone: every test also assumes auxiliary hypotheses about instruments, background conditions, and other theories. A failed prediction therefore never conclusively falsifies the theory itself, since scientists can always blame an auxiliary instead.

**Reply.** Popper concedes the logical point and answers methodologically. Science, on his proposal, adopts rules against immunising stratagems: decide in advance what would count as a refutation, and do not rescue a theory with ad hoc changes that add no new testable content. The criterion is thus a proposal for honest scientific practice, not a logical guarantee that refutation will ever be forced.

**The argument against historicism**

1. The course of human history depends strongly on the growth of human knowledge, including future scientific discoveries and inventions.
2. We cannot predict today, by rational or scientific methods, what we will only know tomorrow: if we could state that future knowledge now, we would already possess it.
3. Predicting the future course of history would therefore require predicting future knowledge, which the previous step shows to be impossible in principle.
Therefore, we cannot predict the future course of history, and there can be no scientific theory of historical destiny or inevitable stages.

The practical conclusion of *The Poverty of Historicism* is negative and positive at once: no large-scale historical prophecy, but instead *piecemeal social engineering* — small, reversible reforms whose results can be observed and corrected.

**Objection.** Unpredictability of knowledge does not block all social prediction: demography and economics make useful bounded forecasts, for instance about ageing populations or the effects of a tax change. Popper's argument, the objector says, may prove too much, ruling out ordinary social science along with prophecy.

**Reply.** Popper's target is prophecy in the historicist sense — unconditional claims about laws or stages of historical development — not conditional, short-range forecasting. His own framework expressly allows piecemeal prediction tied to institutions: if we do this, under these conditions, that is likely to follow, and we can check and revise. The distinction between prophecy and piecemeal forecasting is therefore central to the argument, not an afterthought.

## Key works

- *Logik der Forschung* (1934; English as *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959) — the founding statement of falsificationism and the critique of induction.
- *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) — a two-volume attack on Plato, Hegel, and Marx as historicists, and a defense of liberal democracy.
- *The Poverty of Historicism* (articles 1944–45; book 1957) — the concise, technical version of the anti-historicist argument.
- *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) — the most accessible collection of his essays, including his account of Einstein versus Marx, Freud, and Adler.
- *Objective Knowledge* (1972) — his later evolutionary epistemology and theory of “World 3,” the world of objective contents of thought.

## Contributions

Popper changed the default question in philosophy of science. Before him, the positivist picture asked how theories are verified; after him, even opponents had to talk about testability, severity of tests, and demarcation. He gave everyone a usable slogan with real content: state what would show you are wrong. In politics, he supplied post-war liberalism with an epistemological foundation: fallibilism became an argument for free criticism, pluralism, and reversible reform.

## Impact and legacy

Popper’s influence on methodology taught to scientists was worldwide, even where practitioners simplified him into naive falsificationism. Lakatos and Feyerabend, both formed in his LSE seminar, built major rival accounts in rebellion against him — a legacy of productive disagreement. His student George Soros named the Open Society Foundations after Popper’s book, carrying the phrase into global philanthropy. Debates about pre-registration, replication, and specifying predictions in advance still echo his insistence that a test must be able to fail.

## Criticisms and debates

Historians, following Kuhn, object that Popper describes how science *ought* to behave, not how it does: real scientists hold on to theories through anomalies, and progress would stall if they did not. Philosophers press the Duhem–Quine problem and ask whether corroboration smuggles induction back in — why prefer a well-tested theory if past success gives no reason at all? Scholars of Plato and Marx also contest his readings in *The Open Society*, arguing he flattened complex texts; Popper revised some passages while standing by the main charge. These remain live debates, not settled verdicts.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Popper did not say a single failed experiment instantly refutes a theory in practice. Auxiliary hypotheses are always involved; his demand was not to rescue a theory ad hoc.
- Corroboration is not verification, confirmation of truth, or a probability. It is only a record of tests survived so far.
- Falsifiability is a criterion for being *scientific*, not for being meaningful, true, or worth discussing.
- The open society is not a claim that democracies are wise. It is a claim about error-correction: criticism and peaceful removal of rulers.

## What to read first

1. *Conjectures and Refutations*, chapter 1 — Popper’s own story of Einstein, Marx, Freud, and Adler; the best entry point.
2. Selections from *The Open Society and Its Enemies* — especially on historicism and piecemeal reform.
3. *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, in excerpts — attempt the full text only after the essays; it is dense and technical.
