# Mary Midgley

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1919–2018
- **Place:** Born and raised in London, England; long career at Newcastle upon Tyne
- **School/tradition:** Moral philosophy and philosophy of science; the wartime Oxford circle of Murdoch, Foot and Anscombe; critic of reductionism and scientism
- **Known for:** *Beast and Man* (1978), an ethology-informed account of human nature, the image of philosophy as "plumbing", and a lifelong campaign against "nothing-but" reductionism

**Responds to:** [Anscombe & Foot](anscombe-and-foot.md), [Iris Murdoch](iris-murdoch.md), and the sociobiologists and popular reductionists she argued with · **Answered by:** Later critics of scientism and philosophers of biology who took up her case against reductive explanation

## Biography

Mary Midgley (born Mary Scrutton) was born in London in 1919 and read classics ("Greats") at Somerville College, Oxford, from 1938, graduating in 1942. Her Oxford years put her in the cohort now known as the **Wartime Quartet**: with Iris Murdoch and Philippa Foot (both also at Somerville) and Elizabeth Anscombe (at St Hugh's), she belonged to the group of friends who, while most of the university's men were away at war, worked out a shared dissatisfaction with the thin, behavior-centered moral philosophy of the day — the story recovered in Mac Cumhaill and Wiseman's *Metaphysical Animals* (2022). Wartime work of national importance followed — civil service posts and school teaching; she later described the delay it imposed on her generation as, philosophically, an unexpected gift of time to think.

Midgley taught philosophy at the University of Reading from 1949, moved to the University of Newcastle upon Tyne in 1962, and stayed there until her retirement in 1980. She married Geoffrey Midgley, himself a philosopher, in 1950, and raised three sons. Her own first book came remarkably late: *Beast and Man* appeared in 1978, when she was fifty-nine. It made her, in her sixties and seventies, one of Britain's most widely read philosophers — a public moralist whose Gifford Lectures (published as *Science as Salvation*, 1992) and a dozen further books addressed readers well outside the academy. She died in Newcastle in 2018, aged ninety-nine, with her last book, *What Is Philosophy For?*, appearing the year she died.

## Major theories and concepts

### Human nature, taken seriously

*Beast and Man: The Roots of Human Nature* (1978) argues that philosophy had backed itself into a false choice about human nature: either deny that humans have one (the blank-slate and purely cultural pictures she associated with existentialism and behaviorism) or crude-model it on the more sensational animal behaviors. Drawing on ethology — the comparative study of animal behavior associated with Lorenz and Tinbergen, whose weaknesses she herself criticized — Midgley proposed that humans, like other social species, come equipped with a structured repertoire of needs, emotions and tendencies: for companionship, territory, hierarchy and its limits, play, curiosity, aggression and its restraint. These dispositions are not a hydraulic fate; culture works *on* them and can frustrate or deform them. But moral and political theory that ignores them is built on sand. Humans are not angels, not blank paper, and not merely clever wolves — and comparisons with animals inform precisely because they are partial, run in both directions, and discipline our self-flattery as much as our self-contempt.

### Against "nothing-buttery": reductionism and scientism

Midgley's most famous target is what she called **nothing-buttery**: the move that explains something real away by announcing that it is "nothing but" something simpler — thought nothing but brain chemistry, morality nothing but gene strategy, persons nothing but illusions put up by their selfish replicators. Her objection is methodological before it is moral. Explanations at one level do not abolish the phenomena at another; a map of the drainage does not replace the geography. Behind nothing-buttery she diagnosed **scientism**: the quasi-religious faith that one successful method must expand until it explains everything, including the values by which we judge it — a faith she treated, in *Science as Salvation* (1992) and *Evolution as a Religion* (1985), as a modern myth, complete with eschatology (the Singularity's ancestors) and a promise of rescue from the human condition. Science itself, she insisted, she admired; it is the imperial expansion of science into a total worldview that she opposed, in *Are You an Illusion?* (2014) against claims that neuroscience has dissolved the self.

### Philosophical plumbing

Midgley's best-known image of her own discipline comes from her later work (developed in *Utopias, Dolphins and Computers*, 1996): philosophy is a kind of **plumbing**. Our concepts are the pipework through which thought flows, mostly invisible and taken for granted — until something goes badly wrong, when the blockage shows up as confusion, superstition or bad policy upstream. The philosopher's job is not to build palaces of theory above ground but to get down, unglamorously, and find where the pipes have clogged or cross-connected: where, for example, a picture of humans as either beasts or angels keeps jamming moral thought. The metaphor is a deliberate rebuke to both system-building grandiosity and the idea that conceptual work is dispensable ornament.

### Earth, animals and the later work

From *Animals and Why They Matter* (1983) onward, Midgley argued that moral consideration does not stop at the species boundary, grounding it in the same ethological attention to what creatures are actually like. She was drawn to James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis — examined sympathetically but not uncritically in *Science and Poetry* (2001) — as a corrective imaginative frame: a scientific image that, unlike the selfish gene, pictures life as woven into a shared system. Late books (*The Solitary Self*, 2010, on Darwin and the selfish-gene picture; *What Is Philosophy For?*, 2018) returned to her constants: the interdependence of creatures, the plurality of legitimate explanations, and skepticism about any single key to everything.

## Key works

- *Beast and Man: The Roots of Human Nature* (1978) — the founding statement: human nature via ethology, against blank-slate and beast-machine pictures alike.
- *Heart and Mind: The Varieties of Moral Experience* (1981) — reason and emotion as partners, not rivals.
- *Animals and Why They Matter* (1983) — the moral status of animals, grounded in comparative attention.
- *Wickedness: A Philosophical Essay* (1984) — evil as privation and failure of understanding, against Manichaean pictures.
- *Evolution as a Religion* (1985) and *Science as Salvation* (1992) — the scientism critique; the latter from her Gifford Lectures.
- *The Ethical Primate* (1994) — a further, primatology-informed defense of moral naturalism.
- *Science and Poetry* (2001) — imagination, Gaia, and the plurality of our world-pictures.
- *Are You an Illusion?* (2014) — the self defended against neuroscientific dissolution.
- *What Is Philosophy For?* (2018) — her final, short summing-up.

## Contributions

Midgley restored human nature as a respectable topic for moral philosophy at a moment when both blank-slate culturalism and crude biological determinism were squeezing it out, and she showed how comparative animal study could inform ethics without reducing it — a middle path later work in moral naturalism and animal ethics repeatedly retraces. She was also among the earliest and sharpest philosophical critics of scientism as a cultural phenomenon, naming confusions (the selfish gene read as moral psychology, neuroscience read as the abolition of persons) that have only become more common. Her plumbing image gave the discipline a durable account of its own unglamorous usefulness.

## Impact and legacy

Through books aimed at general readers, Midgley became a public presence unusual for an academic philosopher: a guest on radio debates about science and religion, and a standing invitation to journalists needing a dissenting voice on reductionist claims. Within the academy her reputation has risen with the recovery of the Wartime Quartet, which returned her to the history of post-war ethics alongside [Murdoch](iris-murdoch.md), Foot and Anscombe. Philosophers of biology and critics of the "one true method" picture of science cite her as a predecessor; her defense of conceptual plurality anticipates later work on explanatory pluralism, even by writers who reach it independently.

## Criticisms and debates

**The Dawkins exchange.** Provoked by a sympathetic article on *The Selfish Gene* in the journal *Philosophy*, Midgley replied there with "Gene-Juggling" (1979), arguing that Dawkins's rhetoric slid from a technical claim about replicators into a psychological and moral thesis: his central point, she wrote, was that "the emotional nature of man is exclusively self-interested", though "genes cannot be selfish or unselfish, any more than atoms can be jealous". Dawkins answered at length ("In Defence of Selfish Genes", 1981) that she had misunderstood both the metaphor and the technical argument, and had attacked claims the book never made; Midgley returned in 1983 with "Selfish Genes and Social Darwinism", apologizing for her first article's impatient tone while maintaining that her basic objections stood. The dispute is still cited on both sides: Dawkins's defenders hold that she attacked a straw position about psychological egoism that the gene's-eye view never asserted; hers reply that her target was the public moral use of the picture, not only the genetics. The episode remains a standard case study in how scientific metaphors migrate into moral discourse.

More generally, critics have questioned how much normative weight ethology can bear — whether Midgley's species-repertoire is determinate enough to guide ethics, or is itself a selective picture. Her sympathy for Gaia drew charges of softness toward speculative science from some of the same quarters that praised her hardness toward sociobiology. Her defenders answer that she consistently applied one standard — ask whether an image illuminates or replaces the phenomena — to friend and foe alike.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Midgley was not anti-science. She admired science and opposed scientism — its expansion into a total worldview; confusing the two inverts her position.
- *Beast and Man* does not say morality is "in our genes" or that animal behavior justifies human behavior. Her ethology constrains and informs moral reflection; it does not dictate it.
- The Dawkins articles were not a quarrel about whether evolution is true. It was about what a famous metaphor licenses readers to conclude about motivation and morals.
- She was not a system-builder manqué. The plumbing image is a considered claim about what philosophy mostly is, not false modesty.

## What to read first

1. *Beast and Man* (1978), especially the opening chapters — the human-nature argument in full, and still her most substantial book.
2. *Are You an Illusion?* (2014) — short, late and combative: the self against its neuroscientific dissolvers.
3. *What Is Philosophy For?* (2018) — a brief final statement, plumbing included, that works as an introduction to her whole outlook.
