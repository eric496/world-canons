# G. E. Moore

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** George Edward Moore, 1873–1958.
- **Place:** Born in Upper Norwood, London; educated at Dulwich College and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he spent most of his career and edited the journal *Mind* for over two decades.
- **School/tradition:** Co-founder, with Russell, of analytic philosophy; a central figure of Cambridge moral philosophy.
- **Known for:** *Principia Ethica* (1903) and the "naturalistic fallacy"; the open-question argument against defining "good"; and the plain-speaking defense of common sense — "Here is one hand" — against philosophical skepticism and idealism.

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Berkeley](../03-early-modern/george-berkeley.md) · **Answered by:** [Russell](bertrand-russell.md)

## Biography

Moore was born on 4 November 1873 into a prosperous, devoutly Baptist family; his father was a doctor who had given up practice. At Dulwich College he was educated as a classical scholar, and he went up to Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1892 intending to continue classics. The decisive event was meeting Bertrand Russell, who persuaded him to take the Moral Sciences Tripos; Moore graduated with firsts in both subjects and won a Trinity fellowship in 1898 with a dissertation on Kant's ethics.

With Russell, Moore led the Cambridge revolt against the British Idealism of Bradley and McTaggart — the doctrine that reality is one indivisible Absolute and ordinary beliefs about separate things are appearance. Moore's 1903 paper "The Refutation of Idealism" attacked the idealist slogan *esse est percipi* (to be is to be perceived) by distinguishing the act of awareness from its object: consciousness, he argued, is "transparent" — when we try to attend to it we find only what we are conscious *of*, so nothing in experience supports the claim that objects depend on being experienced. The same year brought *Principia Ethica*, written when he was not yet thirty, which redirected English-speaking ethics for a generation.

Moore's manner mattered as much as his arguments. He wrote slowly, in plain prose, revising obsessively, and his conversation — persistent, puzzled, demanding exact statements of what a sentence meant — became a Cambridge institution. Wittgenstein attended his lectures and later called him "absolutely a child" in some respects while ranking his influence highly ("Moore's lectures were the stimulus"). Moore became professor of philosophy at Cambridge in 1925, edited *Mind* from 1921 to 1947, and delivered the lectures that became *Some Main Problems of Philosophy* and his later papers on perception and certainty.

Outside the university he was a quiet celebrity of Bloomsbury: Lytton Strachey, Virginia Woolf, and John Maynard Keynes had sat in his undergraduate circle (the Cambridge Apostles), and Keynes's memoir "My Early Beliefs" records how Moore's ethics — its religion of personal affection and aesthetic contemplation, as the young Bloomsburyites selectively received it — shaped their lives. Moore himself was famously unworldly; he married Dorothy Ely in 1916, raised two sons, and lived a settled domestic life in Cambridge, where he died on 24 October 1958. His papers "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) and "Proof of an External World" (1939) became the point of departure for Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*.

## Major theories and concepts

### The naturalistic fallacy and indefinable good

*Principia Ethica* opens with a claim about method. Ethics asks "What is good?" and philosophers, Moore argues, have systematically answered the wrong question, identifying good with some other property — pleasure (hedonism), evolutionary fitness (Spencer), the object of desire, a metaphysical reality. To identify good with *any* natural (or metaphysical) property is the **naturalistic fallacy**: good is a simple, indefinable property, like *yellow*. You cannot define yellow to someone who cannot see it; you can only point. Likewise "good" names a simple, non-natural quality that propositions about conduct may have. Everything else in ethics — what things *are* good, what we *ought* to do — must be argued from intuitions about this quality, not smuggled in by definitions. Tension: later philosophers asked whether a property can be both simple and detectable, and whether Moore had confused *defining* a term with *analyzing* the property it names — a confusion Ramsey and Frankena pressed hard — yet the demand "don't define away the normative" remains a fixed point, restated by every anti-reductionist since.

### The open-question argument

Moore's weapon is a test. Take any proposed definition — good = pleasure, good = what we desire to desire. Now ask: "This is pleasant, but is it *good*?" If the definition were correct, the question would be closed, like "this is a bachelor, but is he unmarried?" It is not closed: the question stays intelligible and substantive for every candidate property. Therefore, Moore concludes, good is identical with none of them. The argument, if sound, refutes naturalism in one stroke. It also generalizes uneasily: the same test seems to cast doubt on true analyses ("this is water, but is it H₂O?" can sound open to the ignorant) — the paradox of analysis. The modern verdict is mixed: the argument may trade on confusing our *concepts* with the *properties* themselves, but it permanently raised the standard for definitional claims in ethics.

### Common sense and the defense against skepticism

Moore's other campaign was against skepticism about the external world. In "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) he lists things he claims to know with certainty: that he has a body, born years ago and existing continuously on or near the earth; that other people exist; that the earth existed long before his birth. These **common-sense certainties**, he argues, are more certain than any premise that could be marshalled against them — the skeptic's argument must start somewhere less secure than what it attacks, so rational method keeps the certainties and rejects the premises. "Proof of an External World" (1939) stages it as theater: holding up his hands — "Here is one hand, and here is another; therefore at least two external things exist." The proof is formally valid and its premise something he claims to *know*; the skeptic demands a proof of the premise not assuming what is in question, and Moore replies that the demand misunderstands proof — a proof need not proceed from premises *more* certain than its conclusion. Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* recast the certainties as the "hinge" propositions of a practice rather than items of knowledge; the debate those papers opened still frames epistemology's treatment of skepticism.

### Ideal utilitarianism and the method of isolation

Moore's positive ethics is often missed behind his critique. He asks which states of affairs have the most intrinsic value, using the **method of absolute isolation**: imagine a thing existing entirely alone, and judge whether its existence would be good. A beautiful world no one sees is, he judges, somewhat good — but a world with conscious appreciation of beauty is far better; personal affection and aesthetic experience are the greatest goods we know. His theory of right action is consequentialist — the right act produces the most good overall — hence "ideal utilitarianism." Yet rankings of wholes cannot be computed from their parts: his doctrine of **organic unities** holds that a whole's value may bear no regular relation to the sum of its parts' values. Bloomsbury read this as a licence to value love and art above convention; Moore, more cautiously, as a constraint on all ethical theory-building.

## Key works

- *Principia Ethica* (1903) — The naturalistic fallacy, the open-question argument, and ideal utilitarianism; the most influential work of English ethics of its generation.
- "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903) — The short paper that helped break British Idealism's hold by distinguishing awareness from its object.
- *Ethics* (1912) — A short Home University Library volume restating his consequentialism and free-will views; the gentlest entry point.
- "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) — The list of certainties and the methodological reply to skepticism.
- "Proof of an External World" (1939) — "Here is one hand": the most famous two paragraphs in twentieth-century epistemology.
- *Some Main Problems of Philosophy* (1953) — Lectures from 1910–11 on perception and sense-data, published late with Moore's own doubts attached.

## Contributions

Moore gave analytic philosophy two of its founding gestures: ethics cleared of definitional sleight-of-hand, and metaphysics recalled to the tribunal of common sense. The naturalistic fallacy named a perennial temptation — dissolving normative questions into factual ones — and every later distinction between analysis and reduction descends from his arguments. His insistence that philosophers state exactly what they mean, and distinguish the proposition asserted from the words used, became the working discipline of the analytic movement. He also modelled an intellectual ethic: slow, honest, ready to say "I don't know what that means" — a style transmitted through his editorship of *Mind* and generations of Cambridge supervision.

## Impact and legacy

The aftershocks ran in several directions. Bloomsbury turned *Principia Ethica*'s final chapter into a secular gospel of love and beauty (Keynes's memoir, including his later partial recantation, is the key witness). Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* is unthinkable without the common-sense papers. In ethics, Moore's intuitionism fed Ross and Prichard, his consequentialism anticipated later act-utilitarianisms, and the open-question argument set the problem — how can moral terms be naturalistically analyzed? — that metaethics still works on. In epistemology, "Mooreanism" is now a named research program: perceptual knowledge, it holds, is more secure than skeptical premises. Even logic keeps his name, in "Moore's paradox": the absurdity of asserting "p, but I don't believe that p," though the conjunction might be true.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Frankena's charge (1939):** the "naturalistic fallacy" may itself be a fallacy — definitional naturalists need not claim that "good" *means* a natural property, only that goodness *is* one; Moore's test may beg the question against them.
- **The paradox of analysis:** the open-question test appears to invalidate all informative analyses, including correct ones in science; whether a refined version survives is unsettled.
- **Did the hand proof prove anything?** Critics from the start held that Moore merely *assumed* what the skeptic questions; defenders reply that demanding more begs the question in the skeptic's favor. Wittgenstein's hinge reinterpretation is the most influential middle path; Barry Stroud's verdict — that Moore never engaged the skeptic's actual worry — keeps the quarrel open.
- **Intuition as a method:** Moore's rankings of goods rest on intuition, and critics ask whose intuitions, checked how. Defenders note he treats intuitions as fallible evidence, not oracles — but the worry seeded the later anti-intuitionist reaction in ethics.
- **Bloomsbury's Moore vs Moore's Moore:** Keynes conceded the disciples ignored the consequentialist chapters on conduct and kept only the religion of states of mind — a reception debate about whether *Principia Ethica* licensed the aestheticism practiced in its name.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The naturalistic fallacy is not the is–ought gap under another name, nor the appeal-to-nature fallacy of popular usage; it is a thesis about defining "good."
- Moore did not think common sense is infallible about everything; his certainties are a specific, modest list — he denied knowing many things his opponents claimed for philosophy.
- "Here is one hand" was not naïveté; it was a deliberate methodological challenge about which propositions an argument may legitimately take for granted.
- Moore was not a hedonist or a simple utilitarian; his good includes friendship and aesthetic experience, and wholes have values their parts don't predict (organic unities).

## What to read first

1. *Principia Ethica*, chapter I — the fallacy and the open question, in Moore's own plain prose.
2. "Proof of an External World" and "A Defence of Common Sense" — fifteen pages that set up a century of epistemology.
3. *Ethics* (1912), chapters I–III — his positive view, briefly.
4. Keynes's "My Early Beliefs" (1938) — for what Moore meant to a generation, told by its most brilliant member.
5. Then Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* — the deepest reply Moore ever received.
