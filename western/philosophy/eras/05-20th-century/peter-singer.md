# Peter Singer

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Peter Singer, born 1946.
- **Place:** Born in Melbourne, Australia; educated in Melbourne and at Oxford; taught in Australia and the United States, most prominently at Princeton (from 1999).
- **School/tradition:** Utilitarian ethics — for most of his career a preference utilitarian, later defending a hedonistic utilitarianism.
- **Known for:** The drowning-child argument for a demanding duty to aid the global poor, *Animal Liberation* (1975) and the modern animal rights movement, and as a founding influence on effective altruism — and for controversies that have followed several of his conclusions about the value of life.

**Responds to:** [Sidgwick](../04-19th-century/henry-sidgwick.md), [Bernard Williams](bernard-williams.md) · **Answered by:** a generation of applied ethicists and effective altruists — debate ongoing

## Biography

Peter Albert David Singer was born on 6 July 1946 in Melbourne, Australia. His parents were Austrian Jews who had fled Vienna after the Anschluss; three of his four grandparents died in the Holocaust. Singer has said that this family history — in particular, learning how ordinary people had stood by — lies behind his lifelong insistence that "I was only following my own concerns" is no excuse when one can prevent suffering at little cost.

He studied at the University of Melbourne, first law and then philosophy, and went to Oxford for the BPhil, studying under R. M. Hare, from whom he took the universalizing machinery of his early utilitarianism. A brief period as a vegetarian-curious graduate student — a lunch conversation about why a fellow student avoided meat — preceded his turn to animal ethics. After teaching at Oxford, NYU, and universities in Australia (La Trobe and Monash, where he co-founded a center for human bioethics), he moved in 1999 to Princeton as Ira W. DeCamp Professor of Bioethics in the University Center for Human Values, a chair he held for over two decades.

Two papers and a book made his name before he was thirty. "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) argued that affluent people are obliged to give far more to relieve famine than almost anyone does. *Animal Liberation* (1975) supplied the modern animal movement with its philosophical manifesto. *Practical Ethics* (1979; third edition 2011) applied his utilitarianism across abortion, euthanasia, the environment, and global poverty, and repeatedly provoked public protest — lectures in Germany were disrupted or cancelled in 1989 and later years over his views on severely disabled infants, and his Princeton appointment itself drew protests from disability advocates. Singer responded, characteristically, by restating the arguments. Later books — *The Life You Can Save* (2009) and *The Most Good You Can Do* (2015) — carried his aid argument into the effective altruism movement he helped to inspire. He remains the most widely read, and most argued-with, living moral philosopher.

## Major theories and concepts

### The drowning child and the duty to give

Singer's most famous argument begins with a shallow pond. If you walked past a small child drowning in it, you would surely wade in and save her, even at the cost of muddy clothes. Singer's principle generalizes this: **if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it.** Applied globally, the principle implies that spending on luxuries while children die of preventable disease and malnutrition is morally comparable to walking past the pond — the distance and the number of other bystanders, he argues, make no moral difference in themselves. The conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable: not that we should feel guilty about everything, but that the level of giving morality demands is far above conventional charity, closer to giving until further giving would cost something genuinely significant.

### Speciesism and the equal consideration of interests

*Animal Liberation* argues from a simple demand for consistency. **Speciesism** — a term coined by Richard Ryder and made famous by Singer — is prejudice in favor of one's own species, structurally parallel to racism and sexism: it gives less weight to similar interests solely because of who has them. Singer's alternative is **equal consideration of interests**: an interest (above all, the interest in not suffering) counts the same whoever feels it. Bentham's question about animals — "Can they suffer?" — becomes the criterion: sentience, the capacity to suffer and enjoy, is the defensible boundary of moral concern, not reason, language, or species membership. The practical targets are factory farming and much animal experimentation: if a practice inflicts severe suffering for trivial human gains (a preferred taste in food, a redundant test), equal consideration condemns it. Note what the argument does not claim: that all lives have equal value, or that animals should vote. It claims equal *consideration* of *comparable* interests — a distinction critics and supporters alike sometimes blur.

### Preference utilitarianism — and a later change of mind

For most of his career Singer was a **preference utilitarian**: the right act maximizes the satisfaction of preferences — what beings want, would want if informed, or have an interest in. The view handles animals (their preferences are simple but real) and grounds his positions on killing: painlessly killing a being with no preferences about its own future is a lesser wrong, on this view, than killing a self-conscious person whose plans and desires it thwarts — a distinction that drives his controversial conclusions about infants and some non-voluntary euthanasia. In *The Point of View of the Universe* (2014), written with Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek, Singer changed position, defending Sidgwick's classical **hedonistic utilitarianism** — what ultimately matters is pleasure and the absence of suffering — together with Sidgwick's claim that reason itself reveals the impartial point of view. The shift matters for reading him: cite early and late Singer differently.

### Effective altruism

Singer's aid argument asks not only *how much* to give but *where*. Charities differ in effectiveness by orders of magnitude; a donation's value is the good it actually does, which can increasingly be measured (bednets against malaria, deworming, direct cash transfers). *The Life You Can Save* (2009) — and the organization of the same name that Singer founded — turned this into a public program, and it converged with the **effective altruism** movement launched by Toby Ord and William MacAskill (Giving What We Can, 2009): use evidence and reasoning to do the most good, consider earning-to-give careers, and treat altruism as a problem in allocation, not just generosity. Singer is the movement's philosophical godfather, though its research agenda now extends well beyond his original famine-relief examples, to animal welfare, global health, and long-run risks.

## Key works

- "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) — The drowning-child argument in its original form, aimed at the Bengal famine relief effort.
- *Animal Liberation* (1975; updated editions since) — The founding text of the modern animal movement: speciesism, factory farming, experimentation.
- *Practical Ethics* (1979; 3rd ed. 2011) — Utilitarianism applied topic by topic; the best single map of his positions.
- *The Point of View of the Universe* (2014, with Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek) — The Sidgwickian turn to hedonistic utilitarianism and objective reasons.
- *The Life You Can Save* (2009) and *The Most Good You Can Do* (2015) — The aid argument and effective altruism for general readers.

## Contributions

Singer revived applied ethics as a serious philosophical enterprise: after him, ethics could not stay at the level of theory while ignoring famine, factory farms, and hospital wards. He gave the animal movement its argument (equal consideration), the aid debate its benchmark (the pond), and effective altruism its founding rationale. His insistence on following a consistent principle to an unwelcome conclusion — and publishing anyway — made him the clearest modern example of philosophy as public moral challenge.

## Impact and legacy

The practical footprint is unusually measurable. *Animal Liberation* galvanised a movement: activists including Henry Spira credited it directly, and it remains the standard citation in campaigns against factory farming. The Life You Can Save and Giving What We Can have redirected hundreds of millions of dollars toward highly effective charities, and effective altruism grew into a global research and philanthropy community. In the academy, his work is unavoidable: "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" is among the most taught papers in ethics, and critics of utilitarian demandingness define their positions against his.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Demandingness.** The most common objection: Singer's principle asks too much, leaving no room for personal projects, special obligations to family, or a life of one's own. Bernard Williams's "integrity" objection to utilitarianism is often pressed here. Singer replies that an objection showing morality is demanding does not show it is *wrong* — but how a life can be both fully moral by his standard and recognisably human remains genuinely disputed.
- **Disability and the value of life.** Singer's views on infanticide — that a severely disabled newborn's life may, in some circumstances, permissibly be ended, given the infant's lack of self-conscious preferences and the parents' interests — have drawn sustained protest from disability advocates, some of whom argue the reasoning devalues disabled lives generally; Singer responds that his criterion is capacities and preferences, not disability as such, and notes parents and doctors already make such decisions covertly. The dispute has at times shut down his lectures (Germany, 1989 and later), raising a secondary debate about protest versus platform. Presentations of this debate should state both the argument and the objection accurately, as both sides insist on being heard in full.
- **Personhood and killing.** Preference utilitarianism's distinction between merely sentient beings and self-conscious "persons" underwrites much of the controversy; critics argue it licenses too much, defenders that every theory needs some such distinction and Singer's is at least explicit.
- **Effective altruism's direction.** As the movement he inspired has shifted resources toward long-term and speculative risks, some critics (and some early supporters) debate whether this still follows from the original drowning-child reasoning or departs from it — a live argument conducted partly in Singer's name.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Singer says all animals are equal to humans."** He argues for equal consideration of comparable interests, not equal treatment or equal value of all lives; he explicitly denies the latter.
- **"Singer wants you to give away everything."** The principle demands giving until the cost becomes of comparable moral importance — a demanding standard, but a principled limit, not literal destitution.
- **"Speciesism is Singer's coinage."** The term is Richard Ryder's; Singer popularized it.
- **"Singer's utilitarianism never changed."** His move from preference to hedonistic utilitarianism (2014) is explicit and matters when citing positions on killing and value.

## What to read first

Read "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" first — five pages that contain the pond, the principle, and the challenge. Then chapters 1–2 of *Animal Liberation* for speciesism and equal consideration, and the global-poverty and euthanasia chapters of *Practical Ethics* to see the system applied and to encounter the controversial conclusions in their argued form rather than in summary. *The Life You Can Save* is the accessible route into the later, movement-facing work.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the drowning-child argument: state Singer's principle exactly, then show how he argues that distance and the presence of other bystanders make no moral difference when the pond goes global. Where does the principle stop demanding?
2. Press the demandingness objection, with Williams's integrity worry: a morality that absorbs every personal project leaves no recognisable life. Then give Singer's reply — that showing morality is demanding does not show it is false — and assess it.
3. Apply equal consideration of interests to a fresh case: painful tests on pigs to develop a new cosmetic. Which interests count, how are they weighed, and what would have to be true of the benefits for the tests to pass Singer's test?
4. Take the surgeon case: five dying patients, one healthy patient whose organs would save them. Singer's principle and Thomson's rights framework pull apart here. Show what each says and why, and identify the premise on which the disagreement turns.
