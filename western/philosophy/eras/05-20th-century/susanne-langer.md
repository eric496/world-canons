# Susanne Langer

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1895–1985
- **Place:** Born in New York City; studied and taught in New England (Radcliffe/Harvard, Connecticut College)
- **School/tradition:** Philosophy of symbolism; student of Whitehead; heir to Cassirer's philosophy of symbolic forms
- **Known for:** The distinction between discursive and presentational symbols, and the theory of art as the presentational form of human feeling (*Philosophy in a New Key*, 1942; *Feeling and Form*, 1953)

**Responds to:** [Whitehead](alfred-north-whitehead.md), Ernst Cassirer (no entry), and the logical positivists' narrow account of meaning · **Answered by:** Nelson Goodman and the aesthetics she reshaped, though her own name later slipped from the syllabi she had influenced

## Biography

Susanne Katherina Langer (née Knauth) was born in New York City in 1895 to German immigrant parents, and grew up in a household steeped in music — she played cello and piano seriously all her life, a fact her philosophy of art never lets you forget. She studied at Radcliffe College (then the women's coordinate of Harvard), taking her bachelor's degree in 1920 and her doctorate in 1926, and at Harvard she fell under the influence of Alfred North Whitehead, whose lectures and whose *Symbolism: Its Meaning and Effect* (1927) shaped her life's project; she would later dedicate *Philosophy in a New Key* to him as her "great Teacher and Friend". Her 1942 *Philosophy in a New Key* — written, unusually for a treatise, with a storyteller's fluency — became one of the best-selling philosophy books of its century and made her reputation in her late forties.

Teaching posts were scarce for women of her generation, and her career was correspondingly patchwork: tutoring and lecturing at Radcliffe, a period at the University of Delaware, visiting posts including Columbia and Northwestern, and finally a professorship at Connecticut College from 1954 to 1962. Thereafter, supported by a research grant, she worked independently at Old Lyme, Connecticut, on her three-volume magnum opus, *Mind: An Essay on Human Feeling* (1967–82), completed in her late eighties. She died in Old Lyme in 1985.

## Major theories and concepts

### Discursive and presentational symbols

The distinction at the heart of *Philosophy in a New Key* (1942) is between two kinds of symbolism. **Discursive** symbolism — language in the strict sense — strings units with fixed meanings into propositions, one element at a time, under rules of syntax; it is superb for statements and arguments, and hopeless at presenting anything whose elements matter simultaneously and as a whole. **Presentational** symbolism works the other way: an image, a rite, a piece of music presents its meaning all at once, through the relations of its parts, and cannot be translated into discursive form without residue — not because it is vague, but because its logic is different. Much that humans mean — the felt shape of experience, emotional life, the import of a dream or a ceremony — is presentational. The reigning philosophies of her day, she argued, recognized only the discursive as genuinely meaningful, and so declared most of human significance nonsense or mere emoting. Her project was to take presentational meaning seriously as *meaning*: articulate, structured, and knowable, on its own terms.

Behind the distinction sits a prior one, between **signs** and **symbols**. A sign announces its object — smoke signifies fire, a bell signifies dinner — and points away from itself. A symbol does not announce but *conceives*: it is an instrument of thought about its object, whether or not the object is present, or even real. Ritual, myth and dream are, on her reading, humanity's earliest symbolisms, not failed science; and the symbol-making impulse, rather than any single faculty, is what she calls the keynote of human mentality — the "new key" of her title, replacing reason narrowly conceived with symbolization as the basic activity to be understood.

### Art as the form of feeling

*Feeling and Form* (1953), subtitled "A Theory of Art Developed from Philosophy in a New Key", applies the distinction. A work of art is a presentational symbol of human feeling — not a report of the artist's emotions, and not a stimulus designed to produce feelings in the audience, but an *image* of feeling's forms: its patterns of growth and decay, tension and release, conflict and resolution, which music, painting, dance and literature each articulate in their medium's own way. Hence her famous claim that music is "the tonal analogue of emotive life". Art's importance is epistemic: it gives us knowledge by acquaintance of the inner life's morphology, the kind of knowledge discursive psychology names but cannot show. Each art creates its own primary illusion — music creates virtual time, painting virtual space, dance virtual power — and the history of each art is the exploration of its semblance.

### Mind as feeling

The late *Mind: An Essay on Human Feeling* (1967–82) attempts the naturalistic ground floor. Starting from biology rather than language, Langer treats feeling as the inward phase of organic process itself, intensifying with complexity, and reconstructs the emergence of mind — impulse, dream, symbol-making, culture — on that basis. The human mind's distinctiveness remains its symbolizing activity, but symbolism is now rooted in the physiology of feeling rather than floating above it. The work is vast, uneven and still being assessed; admirers see an ambitious process philosophy of mind in Whitehead's line, continuous with current embodied and enactive approaches to cognition, while critics find its biology speculative and its key notion of feeling stretched past the point of precision. Either way, its scale explains both its neglect and its current rediscovery.

## Key works

- *The Practice of Philosophy* (1930) and *An Introduction to Symbolic Logic* (1937) — the early work: a young Whiteheadian's textbooks, before the symbol theory found its subject.
- *Philosophy in a New Key: A Study in the Symbolism of Reason, Rite, and Art* (1942) — her breakthrough: signs versus symbols, discursive versus presentational forms, myth and rite taken seriously as meaning.
- *Feeling and Form: A Theory of Art* (1953) — the systematic aesthetics: art as presentational symbol of feeling; the primary illusions of the several arts.
- *Problems of Art* (1957) and *Philosophical Sketches* (1962) — lectures and essays refining the theory, including her response to critics.
- *Mind: An Essay on Human Feeling*, vols. I–III (1967, 1972, 1982) — the late naturalistic system, from organic feeling to human culture.
- Translation: Cassirer, *Language and Myth* (1946) — her Englishing of the philosopher whose "symbolic forms" her own work extends.

## Contributions

Langer gave Anglophone philosophy a worked-out alternative to the idea that all meaning is propositional. The discursive/presentational distinction let aesthetics discuss what artworks *do* cognitively without pretending they make disguised statements, and it fed directly into mid-century philosophy of art: Nelson Goodman's *Languages of Art* (1968), the other great symbol-theoretic aesthetics of the period, defines itself partly against her. Her insistence that feeling has *forms* — objective patterns that art can present and audiences can recognize — remains a standing challenge to purely expressionist and purely formalist theories alike. She also stands as a major interpreter of Cassirer and Whitehead to English readers.

## Impact and legacy

For two decades Langer was unavoidable in aesthetics and philosophy of culture; *Philosophy in a New Key* reached a general readership few academic books match. Then, unusually completely, she faded. Scholarly assessments of the eclipse — offered as explanations, not verdicts — usually combine several factors: the analytic mainstream's turn toward a philosophy of language in which her broad, system-building manner looked dated; her concentration in aesthetics just as aesthetics itself became a marginal subfield; the institutional precarity of a career spent at the edges of the research universities that set syllabi; and the sheer size and difficulty of the late *Mind*, which arrived as the profession's attention had moved elsewhere. Outside philosophy proper, her categories had a second career in arts education, where *Feeling and Form* long served as a theoretical warrant for treating the arts as ways of knowing rather than decoration — an influence that outlasted her standing in philosophy departments and complicates any simple story of disappearance. Recent decades have brought a measured revival within philosophy too: historians of analytic philosophy recovering the women of the period, aestheticians returning to presentational form, and philosophers of mind finding anticipations of embodied cognition in her feeling-based naturalism.

## Criticisms and debates

Critics pressed the central distinction from the start: is "presentational symbol" a genuine kind of meaning, or a metaphor grouping together things — pictures, music, ritual — that work quite differently? Goodman, while building on her, denied that artworks symbolize feeling in her sense at all, relocating picturing and expression within general symbol systems with their own rigor. Others objected that the analogy between musical form and emotional form is asserted more often than analyzed: resemblance between tensions and resolutions may be real but too generic to carry the knowledge claims she makes for art. Defenders reply that the charge assumes discursive articulation is the only test of content — the very assumption under dispute. The late work's biology divides readers between those who see prescient naturalism and those who see speculation uncontrolled by the science of its day.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Langer did not say art "expresses the artist's feelings". Artworks, for her, present the *forms* of feeling impersonally; the artist's biography is beside the point.
- Presentational does not mean ineffable or mystical. Her whole claim is that presentational symbols are articulate and structured — just not translatable into discourse without loss.
- She was not a logical positivist fellow-traveler. She took their question (what is meaning?) and answered it against them, widening meaning beyond the proposition.
- The eclipse was not a refutation. Her books went out of fashion faster than they were answered, which is why the current revival reads her as unfinished business rather than a closed case.

## What to read first

1. *Philosophy in a New Key* (1942), especially the chapters on sign and symbol and on music — the distinction and its most vivid application, in prose meant to be read.
2. *Feeling and Form* (1953), opening chapters — the theory of art as the presentational form of feeling, before the art-by-art detail.
3. *Philosophical Sketches* (1962) — shorter pieces that show the system responding to its critics; then *Mind*, vol. I, for the ambitious late naturalism.
