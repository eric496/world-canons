# Wittgenstein, Late

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Ludwig Wittgenstein, 1889–1951. The "late" Wittgenstein is the philosopher from his return to Cambridge in 1929 until his death, above all of the *Philosophical Investigations*.
- **Place:** Vienna-born; worked mainly in Cambridge, with periods in Ireland and wartime work in London and Newcastle.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy — but as its internal critic, turning it from logical construction toward ordinary language, practice, and forms of life.
- **Known for:** Meaning as use, language-games, family resemblance, and the private language argument — and for repudiating his own earlier *Tractatus*.

**Responds to:** [Wittgenstein (early)](wittgenstein-early.md), [Frege](gottlob-frege.md), [Russell](bertrand-russell.md) · **Answered by:** [Ryle](gilbert-ryle.md), [Anscombe & Foot](anscombe-and-foot.md), [Kuhn](thomas-kuhn.md)

## Biography

When Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge in 1929, he was already a legend: author of the *Tractatus* (1921/1922), who had declared philosophy finished and disappeared into village schoolrooms and designing a house in Vienna for his sister. He came back because the solution had stopped satisfying him. Conversations with Schlick and Waismann, Ramsey's criticisms, and difficulties such as color-exclusion — "this is red all over" and "this is green all over" exclude each other, though the *Tractatus* required elementary propositions to be independent — convinced him central parts of his early system were wrong.

Cambridge awarded him a PhD in 1929 on the strength of the *Tractatus* itself, examined by Russell and Moore, and a Trinity fellowship. What followed was not a second system, but two decades of dictations, notebooks, and revised typescripts. The *Blue Book* (1933–34) and *Brown Book* (1934–35) already contain, in embryo, language-games and meaning as use.

His teaching was legendary and demanding: lecturing without notes, thinking aloud, to small classes that included Elizabeth Anscombe, who would translate and edit his work, Rush Rhees, Norman Malcolm, and, for a period, Alan Turing. He distrusted professional academic philosophy and advised promising students to take up manual work instead. A famous, still-disputed anecdote from 1946 has him brandishing a poker during an argument with Karl Popper at the Moral Sciences Club — what exactly happened remains contested by witnesses, and is best told as a contested anecdote, not a settled fact.

During the Second World War he left Cambridge, unwilling to remain a professor while war was being fought. He worked as a porter at Guy's Hospital in London and then as a laboratory technician in Newcastle on wound-shock research. He had succeeded Moore as Professor of Philosophy in 1939, but resigned the chair in 1947 to concentrate on writing. He spent periods in Ireland — in Dublin and in a remote cottage at Rosro, County Galway — trying to finish the work that became the *Philosophical Investigations*. In 1949 he was diagnosed with prostate cancer. He died in Cambridge at the home of his doctor, Edward Bevan, on 29 April 1951. His reported last words, to Mrs Bevan, were: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life."

Part I of the *Investigations* was essentially ready by 1946 — Wittgenstein tried to publish it, then withdrew it. It appeared posthumously in 1953, edited and translated by Anscombe with Rhees. A vast *Nachlass* of some twenty thousand pages has since appeared in selections: *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* (1956), *Zettel* (1967), *On Certainty* (1969). The late Wittgenstein we read is therefore partly an editorial construction, and scholars debate how to weigh typescripts against notebooks never prepared for publication.

The early/late split is a scholarly framing, not Wittgenstein's own title. The break is real — he called his earlier view a grave mistake in the preface to the *Investigations* — but continuity is also real: both periods treat philosophy as a struggle against bewitchment by language and aim at clarity rather than new doctrines. Whether to emphasize rupture or continuity is a live debate. For learning, it helps to treat Wittgenstein I and II almost as two philosophers in argument with each other — remembering that the argument was internal, and lifelong.

## Major theories and concepts

### Meaning as use

The *Investigations* opens with a quotation from Augustine's *Confessions*: the picture that every word names an object, and that meaning is the object named. Wittgenstein treats this not as Augustine's personal error but as a seductive template behind much philosophy — including his own early picture theory.

Against it he offers, carefully hedged, his most famous slogan: "For a large class of cases of the employment of the word 'meaning' — though not for all — this word can be explained in this way: the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (§43). Note the qualifications, which popular summaries drop. He is not issuing a new essence of meaning. He is directing attention: do not ask first what hidden entity a word stands for; look at how it is actually employed — in ordering, describing, joking, calculating, praying.

His builders (§2) imagine a primitive language of calls — "block," "pillar," "slab," "beam" — between a builder and an assistant. The words work without naming inner objects in the Augustinian sense; their meaning is their role in the activity. Real languages are vastly richer, but the lesson scales: meaning lives in practice.

### Language-games

A *language-game* is a simplified pattern of language woven into activity: giving orders and obeying them, describing an object, making up a story, play-acting, guessing riddles, asking, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying (§23 lists many more). Wittgenstein calls language-games "objects of comparison" — models that illuminate by similarity and dissimilarity, not miniature essences of all language.

Two points matter. First, games are embedded in action; words are deeds. Second, there is no single feature common to all language-games, just as there is no single feature common to all games. That leads directly to the next concept.

### Family resemblance

In §§65–77 Wittgenstein considers games — board games, card games, ball games, ring-a-ring-a-roses — and challenges the reader to find one property present in all and only games. Winning and losing? Not in a child throwing a ball against a wall. Skill? Not in dice. Instead we find "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing," *family resemblances*, like the features that run through a family without any one appearing in every member.

The moral is anti-essentialist: the craving to state necessary and sufficient conditions for *knowledge* or *language* may be a craving for something not there. A concept can be usable with blurred edges; definitions are tools for purposes, not discoveries of hidden essences.

### Forms of life

Language-games are embedded in a *form of life* — the shared background of practices and reactions within which speaking makes sense. "To imagine a language means to imagine a form of life" (§19). Agreement in language (§§241–242) is not agreement in opinions but in judgments — shared responses that must be in place before truth and falsity can be discussed. If a lion could talk, we could not understand him, because his form of life is not ours. Scholars debate whether the term is mainly biological, mainly cultural, or both; Wittgenstein offers it as a reminder of where explanations run out: "What has to be accepted, the given, is — so one could say — forms of life."

### The private language argument

In §243 and following, Wittgenstein asks whether a language could refer only to the speaker's private sensations, so no one else could understand it. His test case (§258) is a diarist who writes "S" whenever a sensation occurs, with no public criterion for using "S" correctly next time. Where whatever seems right is right, we cannot talk about *right* at all: a rule needs a distinction between following it and merely seeming to.

His other image is the beetle in the box (§293): if everyone has a box with a "beetle" only they can see, the thing may change or be absent — it drops out as irrelevant to how the word is used. The argument's scope is disputed: private languages only, or also a Cartesian picture of mind? Kripke's influential, contested reading recasts it as a skeptical paradox about rule-following (§§185–242); whether Kripke's "Wittgenstein" is Wittgenstein is itself a standing debate.

### Philosophy as therapy

Philosophical problems arise "when language goes on holiday" (§38) — when words are lifted from their ordinary work and idled. The task is not to build theories but to assemble reminders of use, returning words "from their metaphysical to their everyday use" (§116), until the confusion dissolves and the problem disappears rather than being solved. "The philosopher treats a question; like an illness" (§255). Whether this quietism is liberation or abdication is one of the central arguments about the late work.

## Signature arguments

**The rule-following argument**

1. To follow a rule, such as continuing a number series, seems to require grasping what the rule demands in new, unencountered cases.
2. Whatever is offered to fix that demand — a formula, a mental picture, or a set of past examples — can itself be interpreted in more than one way, so that on some interpretation almost any next step can be made to accord with it.
3. An interpretation is only another sign, and it would in turn need a further interpretation to fix how it is to be applied.
Therefore, meaning and correct action cannot be fixed by interpretation alone; interpretations hang in the air unless they already rest on a practice of use.

Wittgenstein's resolution in *Philosophical Investigations* §§185–201 is that there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation but is shown in practice: in training, custom, and shared responses within a form of life. When I obey a rule, I do not choose at each step; I go on as I have been trained to go on. Meaning is therefore grounded in use within practices, not in mental items or in further interpretations stacked behind them.

**Objection.** Saul Kripke's influential reading turns this into a skeptical paradox about meaning, to be met by a "sceptical solution" in which correctness is fixed by agreement within a community. Many scholars object that this imports a skepticism Wittgenstein never endorsed, making him offer a theory of meaning where he meant only to dissolve a confused picture.

**Reply.** On the Wittgensteinian reply, the paradox arises only if we demand that every grasp of a rule must be an interpretation. Justification runs out at practice — the bedrock where, in his image elsewhere, my spade is turned — and describing that bedrock is not a concession that language fails. Whether Kripke's reading is faithful to Wittgenstein or a creative distortion of him remains an open scholarly dispute.

**The private language argument**

1. Suppose a language whose words refer to sensations that only the speaker can know, so that no one else could in principle understand it (*Philosophical Investigations* §243).
2. The speaker tries to fix a sign "S" by concentrating on a particular sensation and resolving to use "S" for that same sensation again.
3. With no public technique of use and no independent check, there is later no difference between using "S" rightly and merely seeming to oneself to use it rightly (§258).
Therefore, such a supposed language has no criterion of correct use, and a necessarily private language is impossible.

**Objection.** Surely I can name my own pains, and a solitary person such as a Robinson Crusoe seems able to keep a meaningful diary of his sensations. Memory, the objector adds, could itself supply the missing check, by comparing a present sensation with how the earlier one is remembered.

**Reply.** Wittgenstein replies that memory cannot check itself independently in this set-up: appealing only to another impression of memory is like buying several copies of the same newspaper to confirm a report (§265). The argument does not deny that we talk, in public language, about sensations that only we experience. It targets the picture of meaning as inner ostensive definition, in which a private mental pointing is supposed to do all the work on its own.

## Key works

- ***Philosophical Investigations*** (posthumous, 1953), edited by G. E. M. Anscombe and Rush Rhees, translated by Anscombe. Part I: 693 numbered remarks, essentially ready by 1946; Part II (now often published as *Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment*) is later material. The central late text.
- **The *Blue and Brown Books*** (published 1958) — Dictated preliminary studies from 1933–35; the most accessible bridge from the *Tractatus* to the *Investigations*.
- ***On Certainty*** (1969) — Notebooks from 1949–51 on Moore, doubt, and the hinges of belief; written in his final months.
- ***Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*** (1956, rev. 1978) — His sustained, controversial engagement with logic and mathematics as human practices.
- ***Zettel*** (1967) and ***Culture and Value*** (1980) — Clippings and remarks on art, religion, and culture; valuable, but editorial selections from the *Nachlass*.

## Contributions

1. **A practice-based turn in the philosophy of language.** After Wittgenstein, meaning could no longer be discussed responsibly without use, context, and activity. Ordinary language philosophy (Austin, Ryle) and much later pragmatism grow from this.
2. **Anti-essentialism as a method.** Family resemblance gave philosophy, and later psychology (prototype theory) and social science, a rigorous alternative to definition by essence.
3. **A new problem about rules and privacy.** The rule-following considerations and the private language argument set problems that philosophy of mind, linguistics, and cognitive science still work on.
4. **A model of philosophy as clarification.** Even philosophers who reject quietism define their explanatory ambitions against his therapeutic ideal.

## Impact and legacy

Inside philosophy, the *Investigations* redirected the analytic tradition. Austin's speech-act theory, Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949), Anscombe's *Intention*, and Strawson's work all bear its mark. Through Thomas Kuhn, who drew on family resemblance, its influence reached philosophy of science; through Stanley Cavell, literary studies; and the idea that concepts can cohere without essences traveled into psychology's prototype theory of categorization.

## Criticisms and debates

**Quietism and explanation.** Bertrand Russell disliked the late work intensely and said so; many have agreed that refusing theory leaves real questions — about truth, reference, and science — unanswered. Defenders answer that Wittgenstein attacks only a certain craving for theory, not explanation itself. The dispute remains open.

**Conservatism about ordinary language.** Critics in the tradition of Ernest Gellner's *Words and Things* (1959) charged ordinary language philosophy with sanctifying existing usage and evading substantive, including political, questions. Whether that charge fairly hits Wittgenstein himself is debated.

**Kripke's reading.** Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) made rule-following the center of the late work and proposed a "sceptical solution" grounded in community agreement. Many scholars (notably Baker and Hacker) argue this misrepresents Wittgenstein, importing a skepticism he aimed to dissolve. "Kripkenstein" is now a standard label for the possibly distinct philosopher of that book.

**Continuity vs. rupture.** Some scholars stress two Wittgensteins; others stress one lifelong project against linguistic bewitchment. The preface to the *Investigations*, which proposes publishing old and new thoughts together so the new can be seen "against the background of the old," supports holding both emphases at once.

**The *Nachlass* problem.** Because so much is posthumous and editorially arranged, appeals to "what Wittgenstein really thought" from notebooks are methodologically contested.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Meaning is use" is his new definition of meaning.** He explicitly limits it to a large class of cases, not all. It is a redirection of attention, not a replacement essence.
- **"Family resemblance means concepts have no boundaries."** Blurred boundaries are still boundaries for most purposes; a concept need not be sharply definable to be usable.
- **"The private language argument proves sensations do not exist."** It targets a picture of how sensation-words get meaning, not the reality of pain.
- **"He just describes ordinary language and forbids theory."** His examples are constructed comparisons, not dictionary reports, and the therapeutic aim is contested even by admirers.
- **"Late Wittgenstein refuted early Wittgenstein, so the *Tractatus* can be skipped."** He wanted the two published together. The later work is partly unintelligible without what it argues against — chiefly, itself.

## What to read first

1. ***Philosophical Investigations*, §§1–133, in short stretches** — start with Augustine (§1), the builders (§2), and the games discussion (§§65–77). Do not try to march through all 693 remarks at first.
2. **Norman Malcolm, *Ludwig Wittgenstein: A Memoir*** — a short, humane account of the man and his teaching.
3. **The *Blue Book*, opening sections** — the gentlest statement of the turn from essence to use.
4. **Then §§185–293** — rule-following and the private language argument, ideally with a guide such as Marie McGinn's *Wittgenstein and the Philosophical Investigations* or the [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/) article, because these sections attract strong rival readings.
5. **Finally, *On Certainty*, selections** — his last thoughts on doubt and hinge beliefs.

## Study questions

1. Work through the builders of §2 ("block," "pillar," "slab," "beam") twice: first as the *Tractatus* would analyze it — names standing for objects in a pictured arrangement — then as the *Investigations* redescribes it, meaning as a role in an activity. What must the assistant learn, on each account?
2. Reconstruct the private language argument using the diarist of §258: what goes wrong when "S" is fixed by private ostension alone, and why does the failure turn on the collapse of "seems right" into "is right"?
3. An objector replies that memory can check the diarist, and that a solitary Crusoe could keep a sensation diary. Give Wittgenstein's newspaper reply (§265), and judge whether it meets the Crusoe case.
4. Use the beetle in the box (§293) to explain why the private contents of each box drop out of sensation-talk. What picture of the mind is being rejected — and is pain itself being denied?
5. Apply family resemblance to a fresh case: competitive speedrunning of a video game, with no opponent and no winner beyond a fastest time. Decide whether it is a game, without appealing to any essence of games.
