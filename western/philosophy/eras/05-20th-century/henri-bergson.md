# Henri Bergson

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1859–1941.
- **Place:** Born in Paris, France; taught at the Collège de France and became the most famous living philosopher of his generation.
- **School/tradition:** French spiritualism and philosophy of life; a major influence on phenomenology, process thought, and Gilles Deleuze.
- **Known for:** Duration (*la durée*) — time as lived, indivisible flow rather than clock-time; intuition as a philosophical method; and the *élan vital* of *Creative Evolution*.

**Responds to:** the spatialised time of the physics and psychology he inherited — no single predecessor entry in this repo · **Answered by:** [Russell](bertrand-russell.md)

## Biography

Henri Bergson was born in Paris in 1859 to a Polish-Jewish father, a composer, and an Anglo-Irish Jewish mother. A brilliant student at the Lycée Condorcet and the École Normale Supérieure, he initially excelled in mathematics before turning to philosophy — a double formation that shaped everything he wrote, since his lifelong target was the misuse of mathematical and spatial habits of thought in domains where they do not belong.

His doctoral thesis, *Time and Free Will* (1889), introduced duration and made his reputation. After teaching posts in provincial lycées and Paris, he was appointed to the Collège de France in 1900. His lectures became a cultural phenomenon: society figures, artists, and students crowded the hall, and his books were read across Europe and America (William James was an enthusiastic admirer and correspondent). *Matter and Memory* (1896), *Laughter* (1900), and *Creative Evolution* (1907) made him arguably the most influential philosopher alive before the First World War.

During the war Bergson undertook diplomatic missions to Spain and the United States on behalf of France, and afterward he presided over the League of Nations' commission for intellectual cooperation — an early version of UNESCO. In 1927 he received the Nobel Prize in Literature. In 1922 his brief public exchange with Albert Einstein in Paris — and his book *Duration and Simultaneity* the same year — became a famous flashpoint between philosophy and physics. Retiring through ill health, he published his last major work, *The Two Sources of Morality and Religion*, in 1932.

As a Jew in occupied France, Bergson refused the exemptions offered to him by the Vichy regime and registered as a Jew in solidarity with those persecuted; he died in Paris in January 1941; according to the widely repeated account, he caught the chill that killed him while queuing in the cold to register. His posthumous reputation declined sharply as analytic philosophy and then existentialism dominated, before Gilles Deleuze's *Bergsonism* (1966) and a broader revival restored him as a central figure in twentieth-century thought.

## Major theories and concepts

### Duration (*la durée*)

Bergson's signature claim is that time, as we actually live it, is not what clocks measure. Clock-time treats time as a row of separate instants laid out side by side — a **spatialised** time, modelled on a line. Real duration is different: a continuous melting of states into one another, like the notes of a melody, where each moment carries the whole past within it and cannot be cut without changing what it is. The intellect, evolved for action on solid objects, habitually substitutes the spatial picture for the real flow, and philosophy inherits the confusion — pseudo-problems such as the traditional debate about free will dissolve, Bergson argued, once we see they arise from treating time as space. Tension: duration is meant as an objective feature of reality, not merely subjective experience, and critics have asked whether Bergson can have both without equivocation.

### Intuition versus intellect

Corresponding to the two kinds of knowledge of a thing: **intellect** analyses from outside, breaking things into parts and symbols useful for prediction and manufacture; **intuition** is a kind of sympathy by which one enters into the thing's own duration from within. Intuition is not a vague feeling but a disciplined method — the effort to coincide with what is unique and inexpressible in its object, then to express it in concepts cut to fit. Example: intellect gives you everything known about a city from maps and photographs; intuition is what you have after walking its streets as a resident. Tension: a method whose results resist ordinary statement risks being unfalsifiable, and Bertrand Russell pressed exactly this point.

### Memory and the cone

*Matter and Memory* argues that memory is not stored in the brain as photographs are stored in a box. Bergson distinguishes habit-memory (the body's learned routines) from pure memory (the unconscious survival of the whole personal past), picturing the past as an inverted cone whose point touches the present plane of action. The brain, on this view, is an organ of selection and action — a filter that admits only what is useful — not a storehouse. Brain damage, then, may block the *recall* of memories without destroying them. The claim anticipated later criticisms of naive storage models, though its stronger anti-materialist conclusions remain contested.

### The *élan vital* and creative evolution

In *Creative Evolution* (1907) Bergson argued that both mechanism (evolution as the unfolding of a pre-set plan) and crude chance-and-selection fail to capture life's inventiveness. He proposed the *élan vital*, an original impetus of life that diversifies as it meets resistance from matter — splitting, for instance, into the lines of plant torpor, animal instinct, and human intelligence. Evolution is genuinely creative: its outcomes are not contained in advance, any more than a work of art is contained in the artist's first idea. Example: the eye evolved independently in molluscs and vertebrates — for Bergson, a sign of a common impetus working through different materials, not of a blueprint. Tension: biologists objected that the *élan* explains nothing testable and names the mystery rather than solving it; Bergson replied that it was a heuristic image, not a mechanism, but the line between image and explanation in his text is genuinely blurry.

### The two sources of morality and religion

His late ethics distinguishes **closed morality** — the pressure society exerts to bind its members together, ultimately against outsiders — from **open morality**, the appeal of saints and moral heroes whose love extends to all humanity. Religion likewise has a static form (myth-making that protects society against the dissolving effects of intelligence) and a dynamic form (mysticism, which he treated as a genuine contact with the creative impetus). The book is both an ethics and a philosophy of history written between two world wars.

## Key works

- *Time and Free Will* (1889) — The thesis that introduces duration and reframes the free-will problem.
- *Matter and Memory* (1896) — Memory, perception, and the mind–body relation; the cone of memory.
- *Laughter* (1900) — Short, witty essay: the comic as "the mechanical encrusted on the living."
- *Creative Evolution* (1907) — His most famous book: the *élan vital* against mechanism and finalism.
- *Duration and Simultaneity* (1922) — His response to Einstein's relativity; see the debates below.
- *The Two Sources of Morality and Religion* (1932) — Closed and open morality; static and dynamic religion.

## Contributions

Bergson restored time — lived, irreversible, creative time — to the center of philosophy at the moment physics and psychology were both redefining it. He gave philosophy a worked-out critique of the spatialising intellect, a sophisticated defense of the reality of free agency, and one of the few genuinely original philosophies of biology. His account of memory and of the brain as filter rather than store remains a live reference point in philosophy of mind.

## Impact and legacy

Bergson's influence ran everywhere before 1914: into pragmatism (James), modernist literature (Proust's treatment of memory is routinely, if loosely, linked to him), theology, and early phenomenology — Merleau-Ponty and the young Sartre read him closely. His eclipse after mid-century was equally dramatic, as Russell's critique, logical positivism, and structuralism each treated him as the representative of an anti-scientific style. Deleuze's *Bergsonism* (1966) and later *Cinema* books rebuilt his reputation around duration, memory, and the virtual; process philosophers and some philosophers of physics now read him again as a serious interlocutor. His Nobel Prize (1927, in Literature) marks the era when a philosopher could be a public figure of the first rank.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The Einstein debate (1922).** At the Société française de philosophie in Paris, Bergson questioned Einstein's interpretation of simultaneity and time dilation; *Duration and Simultaneity* argued that the physicist's multiple times were "phantom" times and only one real time flows. Physicists judged that Bergson had misunderstood relativity — the twin paradox in particular — and the episode damaged his standing with scientists for decades. Recent scholarship (notably Jimena Canales's *The Physicist and the Philosopher*, 2015) has complicated the verdict: Bergson understood more of the mathematics than the caricature allows, his target was partly the *philosophical interpretation* of relativity rather than its predictions, and Einstein's own philosophical remarks in the exchange were not beyond criticism. The balanced position: Bergson lost the physics argument as it was then framed, but the exchange raised real questions about lived time that relativity does not by itself settle.
- **Russell's attack.** Russell's 1912 essay charged Bergson with trading on evocative metaphor and hostility to intellect. Bergsonians reply that Russell mistook an account of intellect's *limits* for contempt for it. The dispute still maps the analytic/continental divide.
- **The *élan vital*.** Vitalism was largely abandoned by biology; whether Bergson's version is a scientific hypothesis (and failed), a metaphysical image (and survives), or something in between is a standing debate.
- **Whether duration is psychology or metaphysics** — subjective flow or structure of reality — divides his interpreters, with Deleuze's reading (duration as ontological) currently dominant.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Bergson denied relativity."** He disputed its philosophical interpretation and the reality of the multiple times assigned to moving observers; he did not deny the experimental results. Even so, most physicists judge his positive account of the twin case mistaken — the careful position holds both.
- **"Intuition means gut feeling."** For Bergson intuition is the *result* of disciplined philosophical effort, the opposite of a first impression.
- **"The *élan vital* is a soul-force that explains evolution."** Bergson offered it as an image for life's inventive impetus, explicitly denying it was a mechanism or a plan.
- **"Duration is just subjective time."** The claim is about reality itself: the universe endures, not merely our experience of it.

## What to read first

Start with *Creative Evolution*, chapter 1, for the *élan* and the critique of mechanism, then *Time and Free Will*, chapter 2, for duration in its original setting. The short essay "An Introduction to Metaphysics" (1903) is the clearest statement of intuition as method. After that, *Matter and Memory*'s introduction and chapter 3, and *Laughter* for pleasure. Save *Duration and Simultaneity* until you can read it alongside an account of the Einstein exchange.
