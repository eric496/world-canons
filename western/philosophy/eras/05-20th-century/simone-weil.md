# Simone Weil

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1909–1943
- **Place:** Born in Paris, France; died in Ashford, England
- **School/tradition:** Independent — philosopher, political writer, and religious thinker at the crossings of Marxism, Platonism, and Christian mysticism
- **Known for:** The philosophy of attention and affliction, written from inside factory labor, war, and political exile

**Responds to:** [Plato](../01-ancient/plato.md), [Marx](../04-19th-century/karl-marx.md) · **Answered by:** [Iris Murdoch](iris-murdoch.md) and later philosophers of attention

## Biography

Weil was born in Paris in 1909 into a secular Jewish family; her elder brother André became one of the century's great mathematicians. A formidable student — Simone de Beauvoir remembered envying her heart that 'could beat right across the world' — she entered the École Normale Supérieure in 1928, studied under Alain (Émile Chartier), passed the agrégation in philosophy in 1931, and taught in provincial lycées while organizing workers' education and writing for revolutionary-syndicalist journals. Her politics were radical but never obedient: an early critic of Soviet communism after traveling in Germany in 1932 and seeing the left's failure before Hitler.

In 1934–35 she took leave from teaching and worked incognito on assembly lines — at Alsthom, then Renault — to know industrial labor from within; the *Factory Journal* records piece-rates, exhaustion, and humiliation in unflinching detail. In 1936 she went to Spain with an anarchist column, was scalded in a cooking accident, and left disillusioned by the violence on her own side. From 1937–38, religious experiences she had resisted — in Assisi, and hearing Gregorian chant at Solesmes while in pain — turned her thought toward God, though she refused baptism and remained deliberately outside the Church, a stance set out in her letters to Father Perrin (published as *Waiting for God*).

After the German occupation she escaped with her parents to New York in 1942, then insisted on reaching London to work for the Free French. There she wrote *The Need for Roots* as a program for postwar France, while demanding — unsuccessfully — to be sent on a mission into occupied territory. Eating, by most accounts, no more than she believed rations allowed the occupied French, she contracted tuberculosis and died at Ashford in August 1943, aged 34. Nearly everything she is known by appeared posthumously, edited from her notebooks by friends.

## Major theories and concepts

### Attention

Attention is Weil's master concept. It is not muscular effort or concentration by will, but a receptive, self-emptying waiting: suspending one's own thoughts and desires so that reality — or another person — can appear as it is. In her essay on school studies, the true purpose of study is training this attention, which is also the substance of both prayer and love of neighbor: attention, she wrote, is "the rarest and purest form of generosity." To attend to someone afflicted is to ask, "What are you going through?" — and to consent to hear the answer. Iris Murdoch later took the concept into moral philosophy.

### Affliction (*malheur*)

Affliction is not ordinary suffering. *Malheur* is suffering that uproots a life: physical pain, social degradation, and contempt combined, crushing the sufferer toward thinghood — the condition of the factory hand reduced to a function, the slave, the war victim. Affliction poses philosophy's hardest problem: those most destroyed by it are least able to speak or be heard. Her analysis insists that understanding oppression requires attending to this extreme, not only to wages and laws.

### Gravity and grace

*Gravity and Grace* (assembled posthumously from her notebooks) structures the moral world with a physics metaphor. **Gravity** is the natural law of the soul: self-interest, force, prestige, the filling of every void with compensation. **Grace** is the sole exception — the supernatural counter-movement of self-emptying, which cannot be manufactured by the will but only consented to. Hence **decreation**: not self-destruction, but undoing the insistent "I" so that what is real can pass through. Force, analyzed in her famous 1940 essay on the *Iliad*, turns both victim and wielder into things; the poem's greatness is its equal, lucid love for both sides.

### Uprootedness and roots

*The Need for Roots* (written 1943) diagnoses modern deracination — by conquest, money, education, and factory work — and argues a human being needs roots: real participation in a community that keeps "treasures of the past" and a sense of the future. Obligations, she argues, are prior to rights: the needs of the soul (order, liberty, obedience, responsibility, honor, rootedness, among others) should ground political reconstruction.

## Key works

(Almost all published posthumously, edited from manuscripts and notebooks.)

- *Reflections Concerning the Causes of Liberty and Social Oppression* (written 1934; published 1955) — her great political essay on why revolutionary regimes reproduce oppression.
- "The Iliad, or The Poem of Force" (1940–41) — the classic essay on force and the *Iliad*.
- *Gravity and Grace* (1947) — notebook fragments on attention, gravity, decreation; the usual entry point.
- *The Need for Roots* (1949) — her political testament for postwar France.
- *Waiting for God* (1950) — letters and essays on attention, affliction, and her position outside the Church.
- *Factory Journal* (in *La Condition ouvrière*, 1951) — the diary of her factory year.
- *Notebooks* (1951–56) — the vast *Cahiers*, published in three volumes, from which the posthumous books were drawn.

## Contributions

Weil fused first-person experience of labor with political theory in a way almost no philosopher has matched — she did not write about factory work, she submitted to it and analyzed what it does to a soul. She gave moral philosophy the concept of attention in its modern form, gave political thought a penetrating category in uprootedness, and modelled a religious thinking that refuses institutional belonging and easy consolation alike. Her account of force remains a standard reference in just-war and violence debates.

## Impact and legacy

Albert Camus, who edited her works for Gallimard, called her "the only great spirit of our times." T. S. Eliot praised her genius in an introduction to *The Need for Roots*. Iris Murdoch built part of *The Sovereignty of Good* on Weilian attention; the concept now runs through ethics, education, and even debates about the "attention economy," usually with the direction of value reversed in ways Weil would have resisted. Her political writings feed communitarian and post-liberal thought across the spectrum, and her work has drawn sustained attention from theologians — she is one of the very few modern thinkers read seriously by both secular philosophers and the Church she stayed outside.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Her death and self-starvation:** whether her refusal of food was political solidarity, ascetic theology, an eating disorder, or some entanglement of these remains genuinely contested among biographers; the coroner's verdict (suicide "while the balance of her mind was disturbed") settles nothing interpretively.
- **Judaism and Christianity:** her harsh writings on ancient Israel and Judaism, alongside her refusal of baptism, make her relation to her own Jewishness one of the most debated areas in Weil scholarship.
- **Practicality:** critics charge that decreation and attention offer no workable politics; defenders answer that *The Need for Roots* is nothing but practical — the debate turns on what philosophy is for.
- **The corpus problem:** because the famous books are posthumous constructions from notebooks, scholars debate how far "Weil's system" is an artefact of her editors.

## Misreadings to avoid

- She was not a cloistered mystic indifferent to politics: political writing came first and continued to the end of her life.
- Attention is not "mindfulness" or productivity technique; it involves renouncing the self's claims, not optimizing them.
- Her sympathy for Christianity did not make her a Christian apologist — she stayed unbaptised deliberately and criticized the Church's uses of force at length.

## What to read first

Start with *Gravity and Grace* (the chapters on attention and affliction) and "The Iliad, or The Poem of Force." Then *Waiting for God* for the religious thought in her own voice, and *The Need for Roots* for the politics. The *Factory Journal* gives the experiential ground of all of it.
