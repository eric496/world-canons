# John Rawls

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1921–2002
- **Place:** United States; American philosopher at Harvard
- **School/tradition:** Kantian and social-contract traditions
- **Known for:** *A Theory of Justice* (1971) and its account of “justice as fairness” — principles chosen behind a veil of ignorance, including equal basic liberties and the difference principle

**Responds to:** [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md), [Locke](../03-early-modern/john-locke.md) · **Answered by:** [MacIntyre](alasdair-macintyre.md), [Nussbaum](martha-nussbaum.md)

## Biography

John Bordley Rawls was born in Baltimore, Maryland, in 1921, the second of five sons in a comfortable, politically engaged family. His childhood was marked by losses biographers treat as formative: two younger brothers died of infections contracted, in each case, from Rawls himself — one of diphtheria, one of pneumonia — leaving him with a lasting awareness of how arbitrary fortune is. That no one deserves the accidents of birth that shape a life sits at the center of his philosophy.

Rawls studied at Princeton, graduating in 1943, and served as an infantry soldier in the Pacific during the Second World War, and in the occupation of Japan, where he saw the aftermath of Hiroshima. He had entered the war considering the ministry; he left it having lost his orthodox faith, partly in response to a chaplain’s sermon suggesting God had protected some soldiers and not others, and to knowledge of the Holocaust. He returned to Princeton for a doctorate in moral philosophy (1950), held posts at Princeton, Cornell, MIT, and briefly Oxford, and joined Harvard in 1962, where he remained until retirement.

Rawls published slowly and revised obsessively. Papers through the 1950s and 1960s — notably “Justice as Fairness” (1958) — built toward *A Theory of Justice* (1971), published after roughly two decades of work. Its scale and timing mattered: Anglophone political philosophy had largely retreated from systematic normative theory, and Rawls, writing through the civil rights movement and the Vietnam War, produced a comprehensive liberal theory of the just society. The rebirth of normative political philosophy is routinely dated to this book.

By temperament Rawls was modest, courteous in dispute, and reluctant as a public figure. His later work responded to critics and to a problem his theory raised — how a just society remains stable when citizens hold irreconcilable religious and philosophical views — producing *Political Liberalism* (1993) and *The Law of Peoples* (1999). A stroke in 1995 limited his final years; he died in Lexington, Massachusetts, in 2002.

## Major theories and concepts

### The original position and the veil of ignorance

Rawls revives the social contract tradition of Locke, Rousseau, and Kant, but transforms it. The contract is not a historical event and not a bargain among people who know their bargaining power. It is a thought experiment — a *device of representation* — called the *original position*.

Imagine representatives choosing the basic principles that will govern their society’s main institutions — its constitution, property system, and economy — behind a *veil of ignorance*. They do not know their class, wealth, race, sex, natural talents, health, religion, or conception of the good life. They do know general facts: how societies and economies work, that resources are moderately scarce, that people have differing life plans, and that they themselves must live with whatever principles they choose, whoever they turn out to be.

The veil does the moral work. Because no one can tailor principles to favor a position they might not occupy, the choice is forced to be impartial. Principles chosen under these conditions, Rawls argues, are fair — hence “justice as fairness.” The device models an idea most readers already hold: that advantages of birth are morally arbitrary and should not by themselves determine a person’s life prospects or political standing.

### The two principles of justice

Rawls argues the parties in the original position would choose two principles, in a strict *lexical* order — the first must be fully satisfied before the second counts.

*First principle:* each person has an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties — including liberty of conscience, freedom of speech and association, political liberties, and the rights of the rule of law — compatible with the same scheme for all.

*Second principle:* social and economic inequalities must satisfy two conditions. They must be attached to positions open to all under conditions of *fair equality of opportunity*; and they must be to the *greatest benefit of the least advantaged* members of society — the *difference principle*.

The ordering matters. Basic liberties cannot be traded away for economic gains, however large; fair opportunity takes priority over the difference principle, which in turn governs the inequalities that remain. Rawls’s reasoning for the difference principle runs through risk and reciprocity: behind the veil, not knowing whether you will be talented or untalented, healthy or disabled, you would not gamble your whole life on average welfare; you would protect the worst position you might occupy. And once the veil lifts, inequalities can be justified to those who benefit least only if the arrangement works to their advantage too — if the talented earn more in a system that also raises the floor, rather than one that merely enriches the top.

A common beginner’s example helps. Two tax-and-enterprise regimes are on offer. In one, incentives produce a larger economy in which the worst-paid are better off than under strict equality. In the other, incomes are exactly equal but lower for everyone, including the least advantaged. Rawls’s principles can permit — indeed prefer — the first: inequality is allowed precisely when, and only when, it benefits those at the bottom. What the difference principle forbids is inequality justified only by gains to the already advantaged, or by total output that leaves the worst off behind.

### Reflective equilibrium

How do we know the original position is set up correctly, or that its output is right? Rawls’s method is *reflective equilibrium*. We start with our *considered judgments* — convictions about justice in which we have confidence, such as that slavery and religious persecution are unjust — and with candidate principles, and adjust each in light of the other until they cohere. If a principle condemns a judgment we cannot give up, we revise the principle; if a principle we trust exposes a judgment as a prejudice of our upbringing, we revise the judgment. There is no foundation outside this process of mutual adjustment — no self-evident axioms from which justice is deduced. Critics ask whether coherence merely systematizes the prejudices we started with; Rawls’s reply is that wide reflective equilibrium, which also tests our judgments against rival theories and background facts, is the best justification moral and political philosophy can honestly offer.

### The argument against utilitarianism

Rawls’s chief rival is utilitarianism: maximize total or average welfare, even at serious cost to individuals. Rawls objects that utilitarianism “does not take seriously the distinction between persons.” What is rational for one person — accepting a present pain for a greater future gain within a single life — is transferred to society, as if losses to some were automatically compensated by gains to others. But there is no single social person who experiences the total. Behind the veil, Rawls argues, no one would accept principles that might license sacrificing their basic liberties for a larger sum of welfare for strangers. Justice as fairness is built instead on the inviolability of each person and on reciprocity among free and equal citizens.

### Political liberalism, overlapping consensus, and public reason

Rawls’s later work confronts a problem his theory itself exposed. A just society will contain a permanent plurality of reasonable but incompatible religious, philosophical, and moral doctrines — what he calls the fact of *reasonable pluralism*. How can such a society be stable for the right reasons, rather than by mere truce or force?

His answer in *Political Liberalism* (1993) is to make the political conception *freestanding*: justice as fairness, restated as a political rather than comprehensive doctrine, can be the object of an *overlapping consensus*, endorsed by citizens from within their own different worldviews — as a Kantian on grounds of autonomy, a religious believer on grounds of the dignity of persons, a utilitarian on grounds of its good effects. When citizens and officials justify coercive laws to one another on constitutional essentials, they should offer *public reasons* — reasons drawn from the shared political conception, not solely from their own comprehensive doctrine. In *The Law of Peoples* (1999) Rawls extended the approach internationally, arguing liberal peoples owe toleration to decent non-liberal peoples and a duty of assistance to burdened societies, but not a global version of the difference principle.

## Signature arguments

**The original-position argument**

1. Principles of justice are fair if they would be chosen by parties choosing under fair conditions, in which no one is advantaged or disadvantaged by morally arbitrary facts about themselves.
2. The original position models those conditions with a veil of ignorance: the parties know general facts about society and human psychology, but not their own place in society, class, talents, health, or conception of the good (*A Theory of Justice* §§3–4, 24).
3. Behind the veil, no one can tailor principles to their own advantage, because no one knows which person in the society they will turn out to be.
Therefore, the parties would choose equal basic liberties for all, and would arrange social and economic inequalities only to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged — Rawls's two principles of justice.

The reasoning for the second principle is *maximin* reasoning under deep uncertainty: with no usable probabilities, grave possible outcomes, and a choice that is one-shot and irrevocable, the parties protect the worst position they might occupy rather than gambling on the average.

**Objection.** John Harsanyi argued that the parties' extreme risk-aversion is smuggled in rather than derived: rational parties, he held, would maximize expected utility and choose average utilitarianism instead. A related objection is that abstracting away everything distinctive about persons may abstract away too much to yield determinate results at all.

**Reply.** Rawls replies that this choice situation is special: the principles govern a whole life, cannot be renegotiated, and must satisfy the strains of commitment — you must be able to honor them even if you turn out worst off. Where the possible losses are that grave and probabilities cannot responsibly be assigned, guaranteeing a decent minimum is, he argues, the rational stance. Whether maximin or expected-utility reasoning is right here remains a live debate.

**The argument against utilitarianism: the separateness of persons**

1. Utilitarianism extends to society the principle of choice for one person: within a single life, it is rational to accept a loss now for a greater gain later, because the same person both loses and gains.
2. Utilitarian aggregation applies the same balancing across a society, summing gains and losses that fall on different people.
3. But there is no social super-person who experiences the total: one person's loss is not automatically compensated by another person's gain, because they are not the same subject.
Therefore, utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons, and a just society may not sacrifice one person's basic rights for a greater total of welfare.

**Objection.** Consequentialists reply that separateness is a slogan, not an argument: each person's suffering matters equally, and only aggregation counts every person's good impartially. Side-constraints on aggregation, they add, can themselves license terrible outcomes, as when refusing to infringe one right allows far greater harms to many others.

**Reply.** Rawls's point is about the structure of justification: principles must be justifiable in terms each person can accept, and in the original position no party would gamble basic liberties for aggregate gains they might never share. Modern contractualism develops the same thought as justifiability to each person, rather than justification by appeal to the total. The dispute with consequentialism is continuing, and should be read as such.

## Key works

- *A Theory of Justice* (1971; revised ed. 1999) — the masterwork: original position, two principles, and the systematic alternative to utilitarianism.
- *Political Liberalism* (1993) — stability under reasonable pluralism: overlapping consensus and public reason.
- *The Law of Peoples* (1999) — the extension to international justice, human rights, and toleration of decent peoples.
- *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001) — Rawls’s own shorter, clearer restatement of the whole theory; the best starting text.
- *Lectures on the History of Moral Philosophy* (2000) and *Lectures on the History of Political Philosophy* (2007) — posthumous teaching, showing the tradition Rawls worked within.

## Contributions

Rawls restored systematic normative political philosophy to the center of Anglophone philosophy after decades in which it had been marginal. He gave liberal egalitarianism its most influential modern argument: a rigorous case that combines inviolable basic liberties with fair opportunity and a principled limit on economic inequality, without appeal to utility or to natural rights as unexplained givens. His tools — the original position, the veil of ignorance, reflective equilibrium — became standard equipment, used even by those who reject his conclusions. And by shifting attention to the *basic structure* of society — the institutions that distribute rights, opportunities, and prospects — he set the questions subsequent theories of justice have had to answer.

## Impact and legacy

The responses effectively constitute modern political philosophy. Robert Nozick’s libertarian *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) was written as a direct reply, arguing that patterned principles like Rawls’s require continual interference with liberty. Communitarians such as Michael Sandel and Alasdair MacIntyre questioned the unencumbered self they saw behind the veil. Feminist critics, above all Susan Moller Okin, asked why the family — a primary site of inequality for women — was largely exempted from Rawlsian scrutiny. G. A. Cohen pressed Rawls from the egalitarian left, arguing the difference principle is too permissive toward incentives for the talented, and Amartya Sen shifted the debate from resources toward *capabilities* — what people are actually able to do and be. Beyond the academy, Rawlsian language entered law, public policy, and welfare economics: priority for the least advantaged, fair equality of opportunity, and maximin reasoning under uncertainty are now familiar far outside philosophy departments.

## Criticisms and debates

The debates remain live and should be read as debates, not verdicts. Libertarians hold that the difference principle violates rights of self-ownership and acquisition. Egalitarian critics hold the opposite — that it concedes too much, letting the talented hold society to ransom with incentive demands. Political theorists debate whether public reason illegitimately excludes religious citizens’ real reasons, or fairly protects equal citizenship; Rawls softened the requirement in later essays. Others ask whether the original position smuggles in its conclusions — whether parties reasoning as Rawls describes them are already committed individualists — and whether reflective equilibrium can validate principles that merely polish inherited prejudice. Rawls’s exclusion of global distributive equality, and his treatment of disability and of non-human animals, also remain contested. That so many incompatible criticisms are pressed with equal confidence is itself evidence of the theory’s centrality: the field argues *with* Rawls even when arguing against him.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The veil of ignorance is a device for choosing principles, not a policy proposal to ignore real differences between people in everyday life; the parties behind it use full general knowledge about society.
- The difference principle does not demand equal incomes. It *permits* inequalities under two strict conditions, including greatest benefit to the least advantaged.
- Rawls is not a utilitarian with extra safeguards. Priority of liberty over welfare gains is a structural rejection of maximizing trade-offs.
- The original position is not a claim that any such agreement historically happened, nor a negotiation in which the strong bargain from advantage — the veil removes exactly that.

## What to read first

1. *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001) — do not start with *A Theory of Justice* cover to cover; the Restatement is Rawls’s own shorter, clearer version.
2. *A Theory of Justice*, §§1–4, 11–13, and 24 — the original position and two principles in the classic text, once the Restatement has given you the map.
3. Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, selections — the great rival; reading Rawls against Nozick is the fastest way to see what is at stake.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the route from the original position to the two principles: what does the veil of ignorance exclude and permit, and how does maximin reasoning under uncertainty — rather than expected-utility calculation — deliver the difference principle?
2. State Harsanyi's objection that Rawls smuggles in extreme risk-aversion, and Rawls's reply from the strains of commitment: principles govern a whole life and cannot be renegotiated. Which side better fits a choice that is one-shot and irrevocable?
3. Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example: fans voluntarily pay a star player, producing inequality from an equal start. Reconstruct his objection that patterned principles require continual interference with liberty, and Rawls's reply that the principles govern the basic structure's background conditions, not each transaction.
4. Contrast Rawls with Singer: Singer's pond principle binds individuals directly; Rawls's principles bind institutions. Why, then, does Rawls's theory by itself not tell you what to give to charity — gap, or deliberate division of labor?
5. Apply the two principles, in lexical order: a city funds elite sports facilities by cutting library hours in its poorest districts, citing rising total welfare. Which principle is engaged first, does the difference principle permit this, and what change would make it permissible?
