# Paul Ricoeur

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1913–2005
- **Place:** France; philosopher at Strasbourg, the Sorbonne, Nanterre and the University of Chicago
- **School/tradition:** Hermeneutic phenomenology
- **Known for:** The hermeneutics of symbols and the "conflict of interpretations", *Time and Narrative* (1983–85), narrative identity and the *idem*/*ipse* distinction, *Memory, History, Forgetting* (2000)

**Responds to:** [Husserl](edmund-husserl.md), [Gadamer](hans-georg-gadamer.md), [Levinas](emmanuel-levinas.md) · **Answered by:** the historians, theologians and theorists of narrative who took up narrative identity — his influence runs through disciplines more than through a single successor

## Biography

Paul Ricoeur was born in Valence, France, in 1913, into a devout Protestant family — a religious minority in Catholic France, and a fact that shaped his lifelong, carefully bounded engagement with biblical texts. His mother died within months of his birth and his father was killed in the Second Battle of Champagne in 1915; the war orphan (his schooling paid by the state) was raised by his paternal grandparents and an aunt in Rennes. He studied philosophy at Rennes and then at the Sorbonne, where Gabriel Marcel's seminars mattered to him, qualified as a teacher in 1935 — the year he married Simone Lejas — and taught in provincial lycées.

Mobilised in 1939, Ricoeur was captured in 1940 and spent five years in prisoner-of-war camps in Pomerania. The captivity was, improbably, an education: among fellow prisoners including the philosopher Mikel Dufrenne, the camp ran courses rigorous enough to be recognized for degrees, and Ricoeur read Jaspers deeply and produced a translation of Husserl's *Ideas I*, written in the margins of a copy concealed from his guards. Released in 1945, he taught at the Protestant Collège Cévenol at Chambon-sur-Lignon, then in 1948 took the chair in the history of philosophy at Strasbourg, succeeding Jean Hyppolite. His doctorate (1950) paired the Husserl translation with *Freedom and Nature*, the first volume of a philosophy of the will.

He moved to the Sorbonne in 1956, and in 1965 to the new university at Nanterre, whose creation he had championed. As dean of letters in the aftermath of May 1968 he tried to mediate between students and administration, was rewarded with vilification from both sides, and resigned in 1970. Three years at Louvain followed; meanwhile, in 1970, he had succeeded Paul Tillich as John Nuveen Professor at the University of Chicago, teaching there part of each year until 1992 while keeping his Paris seminar — a double life that made him a two-way bridge between French and Anglophone philosophy. He died at Châtenay-Malabry, outside Paris, in 2005.

## Major theories and concepts

### The hermeneutics of symbols

Ricoeur's route out of phenomenology's first person runs through **symbols**: expressions with a double meaning, in which a literal sense points beyond itself to a second sense available only through the first — his examples are the religious symbols of stain, sin and guilt analyzed in *The Symbolism of Evil* (1960). Symbols, in his famous formula, "give rise to thought": because their meaning is opaque and surplus-laden, they demand interpretation rather than introspection. The self, therefore, cannot be known by a Cartesian short circuit of immediate consciousness; it is reached only by the **long detour** through the signs, texts, myths and institutions in which human life has deposited its meaning. Hermeneutics — the theory of that interpretation — becomes the graft onto phenomenology that defines his method.

### The conflict of interpretations and the masters of suspicion

Interpretation is not one method but a battlefield. In *Freud and Philosophy* (1965) and *The Conflict of Interpretations* (1969), Ricoeur sets two great styles against each other: a hermeneutics of **recollection**, which listens to symbols to restore and recover meaning (the phenomenology of religion is his example), and a hermeneutics of **suspicion**, practiced by his "three masters of suspicion" — Marx, Nietzsche and Freud — for whom consciousness is largely false consciousness, and interpretation means demystification. His reading of Freud is itself a small masterpiece of the genre: psychoanalysis as an "archaeology" of the subject, tracing desire back to its archaic objects, which must be balanced by a "teleology" that looks forward to the figures of spirit. Ricoeur refuses to referee the conflict into a draw; the point is that a mature self-understanding must pass through suspicion rather than around it, emerging — his phrase is a *second naïveté* — able to hear the symbol again on the far side of critique.

### The capable human

Running through the philosophy of the will is an anthropology of **fallibility** and capability. In *Fallible Man* (1960) the human being is characterized by a constitutional disproportion — finite perspective, infinite demand; mortal body, rational ambition — which makes fault possible without making it necessary. Ricoeur's later work gathers the positive side into the figure of the **capable human being**: I can speak, I can act, I can tell my story, I can hold myself imputable for my acts, I can promise and remember. Attestation — the fragile confidence "I can" — replaces both Cartesian certainty and sheer suspicion as the epistemic register appropriate to selfhood.

### Time and Narrative: threefold mimesis

*Time and Narrative* (1983–85) opens with an aporia bequeathed by Augustine's *Confessions*: time is intimately known and yet inexplicable — what is it, if past and future do not exist and the present has no extent? Ricoeur's answer is not a theory but a practice: **narrative**. Drawing *mythos* (emplotment) from Aristotle's *Poetics*, he argues that telling stories mediates between lived time and cosmic time. The mediation has three moments — **threefold mimesis**. *Mimesis₁* is prefiguration: the practical world as already symbolically structured before any telling. *Mimesis₂* is configuration: emplotment itself, which draws heterogeneous events, agents and accidents into a coherent, followable story, producing what he calls a concordant discordance. *Mimesis₃* is refiguration: the intersection of the world of the text with the world of the reader, where narrative reshapes the reader's own temporality. Fiction and history, often opposed, are shown to borrow each other's devices throughout.

### Oneself as Another: idem, ipse, and narrative identity

*Oneself as Another* (1990), from his Gifford Lectures, gives the detour its ethical destination. Ricoeur distinguishes two senses of identity. **Idem** is sameness: numerical identity, and the permanence of character — the sedimented dispositions by which we recognize someone as the same. **Ipse** is selfhood: the identity displayed in keeping one's word, where a person remains faithful across time not by not changing but by holding themselves answerable — the promise maintained despite altered character. **Narrative identity** mediates between the two: the story a life tells, revised as it is retold, is how a person answers the question "who?" rather than "what?" On this basis he builds his "little ethics": the aim of "the good life **with and for others, in just institutions**" — self-esteem completed by *solicitude* for the other and extended, against [Levinas](emmanuel-levinas.md)'s asymmetry, to the reciprocal relations of friendship, and beyond them to the anonymous others of just institutions.

### Memory, history, forgetting

His last major work, *Memory, History, Forgetting* (2000), works in three registers: a phenomenology of memory (distinguishing remembering from imagining, and memory's claim to faithfulness from certainty), an epistemology of history (the historian's archive, document and explanation, and the way historical narrative "stands for" an absent past), and a hermeneutics of forgetting — blocked, manipulated and obligated forgetting, against which he sets amnesty as an institutional question and forgiveness as an eschatological horizon: a "difficult forgiveness" that neither commands forgetting nor pretends the irreparable can be undone. Written against both the abuse of commemoration and enforced amnesia in post-war Europe, it is his most politically pointed book.

## Key works

- *Freedom and Nature: The Voluntary and the Involuntary* (1950) — phenomenology of the will: decision, movement, consent.
- *The Symbolism of Evil* (1960) — myths and symbols of fault; "the symbol gives rise to thought."
- *Freud and Philosophy: An Essay on Interpretation* (1965) — psychoanalysis as hermeneutics; the masters of suspicion.
- *The Conflict of Interpretations* (1969) — essays staging hermeneutics against structuralism and psychoanalysis.
- *The Rule of Metaphor* (1975) — metaphor as semantic innovation; his celebrated exchange with Derrida's "White Mythology".
- *Time and Narrative*, 3 vols. (1983–85) — Augustine and Aristotle, emplotment, threefold mimesis.
- *Oneself as Another* (1990) — idem/ipse, narrative identity, and the little ethics of the good life with and for others.
- *Memory, History, Forgetting* (2000) — memory, historiography, and the problem of forgetting and forgiveness.

## Contributions

Ricoeur performed a graft that should not have worked and did: he joined Husserlian phenomenology to hermeneutics, then opened the result to structuralism, psychoanalysis, biblical scholarship, historiography and — unusually for a French philosopher of his generation — analytic philosophy, engaging Austin, Searle, Strawson and von Wright as serious interlocutors. The concepts that traveled furthest are narrative identity, taken up across literary studies, theology, psychology and medicine; the idem/ipse distinction, now standard equipment in discussions of personal identity that want an alternative to purely psychological or bodily criteria; and his models of memory and historical representation, which historians and memory theorists still argue with by name.

## Impact and legacy

His legacy is dispersed because his work is used where selves, stories and records are studied: narrative medicine and psychotherapy, historiography and memory studies, legal theory (imputation), theology and biblical hermeneutics, and the ethics of commemoration in post-conflict societies. In France he long stood slightly apart from the dominant fashions — too conciliatory for the structuralists, too philosophical for the theologians — while in the Anglophone world he was often the most teachable of the great French post-war thinkers. [Gadamer](hans-georg-gadamer.md), his nearest analogue in German hermeneutics, debated him productively for decades over whether interpretation needs method and critique or is dissolved into the event of understanding itself; the Gadamer–Ricoeur exchange remains the clearest map of hermeneutics' internal choices.

## Criticisms and debates

Conciliation is both his signature and the standing charge against him. Critics — Derrida most consequentially, over metaphor — have held that Ricoeur's mediations domesticate genuine breaks: that he always finds a middle path (archaeology *and* teleology, suspicion *and* restoration) where his opponents see forced choices. Derrida's "White Mythology" (1971) argued that Ricoeur's account of metaphor remains inside the metaphysics it analyses; Ricoeur replied in *The Rule of Metaphor*, and the dispute is still assigned as the cleanest case of hermeneutics versus deconstruction. Historians have been divided over *Memory, History, Forgetting*: some welcomed a philosopher's respect for their craft, others thought the phenomenology of memory claims more than the evidence bears. Theological readers dispute his strict separation of philosophical argument from biblical faith — a boundary he policed in his own writing while writing extensively on the Bible in another register. And ipse-identity has its critics, who doubt that promise-keeping can bear the weight of selfhood set against character and body.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Hermeneutics is not the claim that texts mean whatever a reader likes. Ricoeur's whole machinery — the autonomy of the text, validation, the conflict of interpretations — exists to discipline interpretation, not license it.
- Narrative identity does not say the self is a fiction we invent. Idem (character, body, sameness) anchors the story; narrative *mediates* identity, it does not fabricate it.
- He was not a theologian in disguise. A lifelong Protestant who wrote seriously on biblical texts, Ricoeur kept philosophical argument methodologically independent of faith, and policed that line even when his readers didn't.
- The "conflict of interpretations" is not indecision. Passing through suspicion to a second naïveté is a determinate position, not a shrug between Freud and faith.

## What to read first

1. *Oneself as Another* (1990), especially the fifth and sixth studies — narrative identity in its mature form, demanding but self-contained.
2. *Freud and Philosophy* (1965), Book I — the masters of suspicion and the problem of interpretation stated at its sharpest; or the short essay "Existence and Hermeneutics" in *The Conflict of Interpretations* for the whole method in miniature.
3. *Time and Narrative*, vol. 1, part I — Augustine's aporia and the threefold mimesis; the plot of the whole trilogy is visible from here.
