# Simone de Beauvoir

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1908–1986
- **Place:** Paris, France
- **School/tradition:** Existential phenomenologist, novelist, and founder of modern feminist philosophy
- **Known for:** *The Second Sex* (1949) and its claim that “one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman,” and for *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947)

**Responds to:** [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md) · **Answered by:** Betty Friedan, Kate Millett and Judith Butler's generation of feminist theorists — outside this repo's entries

## Biography

Beauvoir was born in Paris in 1908 into a bourgeois family whose fortunes declined sharply after the First World War, shaping her lifelong suspicion of bourgeois marriage and security. Dutiful and devout as a schoolgirl, she lost her Catholic faith in adolescence and decided to become a writer who would live by her mind.

She studied philosophy at the Sorbonne and, in 1929, passed the *agrégation* in philosophy at twenty-one — the youngest person to do so at the time — placing second to Jean-Paul Sartre, whom she met while preparing for the examination. Their partnership lasted until Sartre’s death in 1980: a pact of intellectual and personal “essential love,” deliberately without marriage, cohabitation for most of its course, or exclusivity, with each committed to telling the other everything and to putting work first. Later publication of their letters and her memoirs prompted controversy about the human cost of that openness — notably relationships both had with younger women in Beauvoir’s teaching years, some begun when the women were her students, and one affair, with Bianca Bienenfeld (later Lamblin), a former student of Beauvoir's, whose own memoir gave a sharply different account of being drawn in and discarded. Beauvoir also lost a teaching licence in 1943 after a parent’s complaint concerning one such relationship. A factual biography has to record this: the arrangement celebrated as radical honesty is also, in the documentary record, a source of lasting hurt to third parties, and how to weigh that against the work is a live question in Beauvoir scholarship, not a settled verdict.

The partnership also raises the direction-of-influence debate, presented here as a debate. The older picture — Sartre the originating philosopher, Beauvoir applying his system — has been substantially reversed by scholars working from drafts, diaries, and letters (notably Margaret Simons and, in another register, Kate and Edward Fullbrook). They argue that key existential ideas, including aspects of the ethics and the analysis of the Other, were developed by Beauvoir in works such as her 1943 novel *She Came to Stay* — composed in 1938–41, before *Being and Nothingness* appeared, though both books were published in 1943 — and that the two developed their thought in genuine dialogue. Other scholars accept mutual influence while cautioning against simply inverting the hierarchy and crediting everything to Beauvoir. The current consensus supports substantial, two-way influence and rejects the “disciple” picture; the precise priority for individual concepts remains contested.

Through the 1930s Beauvoir taught in Marseille, Rouen, and Paris and wrote fiction slowly. The war years brought the breakthrough: *She Came to Stay* (1943), the essay *Pyrrhus and Cinéas* (1944), and co-founding with Sartre of *Les Temps modernes* in 1945. *The Ethics of Ambiguity* followed in 1947, and *The Second Sex* in 1949 — an immediate scandal, praised, attacked from right and left, and placed by the Vatican on its Index of Forbidden Books. Her novel *The Mandarins* won the Prix Goncourt in 1954. Her relationship with the American writer Nelson Algren, begun in 1947, was passionate but ultimately incompatible with her Paris life.

In later decades she became an activist feminist: she signed the 1971 Manifesto of the 343, in which women declared they had had abortions and demanded legalization. *The Coming of Age* (1970) turned her method onto old age. Her memoirs — beginning with *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* (1958) — are major works in their own right. She died in Paris in 1986 and is buried beside Sartre in Montparnasse.

## Major theories and concepts

### “One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman”

The opening sentence of Volume II of *The Second Sex* is the most quoted line in feminist philosophy, and it is a thesis, not a slogan. Beauvoir distinguishes the biological facts of sex — which she does not deny — from *femininity*: the meanings, behaviors, dress, ambitions permitted and forbidden, and sense of self that a society attaches to a female body. No single cause produces “woman” as she is encountered. She is made across a lifetime: girls are dressed, corrected, praised, and warned differently; toys, stories, religious myths, schooling, the division of domestic labor, marriage law, and the economics of dependence all form a situation in which certain futures open and others close.

Her existential framing gives the claim its bite. A human being is freedom situated in a body and a world; the problem is not the female body itself but a civilization that trains girls to experience themselves as relative beings — defined by their relation to men rather than by projects of their own. Hence her interest in each life stage, from childhood to ageing. In each chapter the method is the same: assemble biology, psychoanalysis, history, economics, literature, and lived testimony, test each partial explanation, and show what it misses about experience as actually lived.

Interpreters debate how far the claim goes. One influential reading, developed later by Judith Butler, takes Beauvoir to open the door to the sex/gender distinction and even beyond it: if being a woman is becoming, the categories themselves are historical. Others stress that Beauvoir herself retained a firmer view of biological sex than Butler’s reading suggests. The debate is genuine; what is not in doubt is that Beauvoir relocated the question from essence to formation.

### Woman as Other: Subject and immanence/transcendence

Beauvoir takes from Hegel the drama of two consciousnesses, each seeking recognition, and recasts it socially. In the patriarchal order she analyses, man occupies both terms at once in a way woman cannot: he is the *Subject*, the default human, the One — and woman is constituted as the *Other*, the inessential counterpart against whom he defines himself. “He is the Subject, he is the Absolute — she is the Other.” Unlike Hegel’s slave, she argues, women have been dispersed among men, divided by class and race, lacking the shared institutions and work through which an oppressed group typically forges solidarity — one reason the Other-position has proved so durable, and one she states as historical analysis, not destiny.

The ethical vocabulary is *transcendence* versus *immanence*. Transcendence is the reach beyond the given: projects, creation, risk. Immanence is existence confined to repetition and maintenance. Beauvoir’s charge is that women have systematically been confined to immanence — housework that undoes itself daily, beauty labor, emotional maintenance — while transcendence is reserved to men, and women are then blamed for the narrowness the confinement produces. She is equally sharp about *complicity*: surrendering freedom can be tempting, since freedom is risky and immanence is safe. Her double refusal — structural analysis without treating women as pure passive victims — is one of the book’s most argued-over strengths.

### The ethics of ambiguity

Sartre had promised an ethics at the end of *Being and Nothingness* and never delivered one. Beauvoir did, in *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), and it is arguably the most complete moral theory produced by French existentialism. Her premise is that the human condition is genuinely *ambiguous*: we are both freedom and object, mind and body, self for ourselves and thing in others’ eyes — and any doctrine that denies one pole (pure determinism, pure spiritual freedom, absolute rules handed down from God or History) is an evasion.

From ambiguity she derives an ethic without absolutes but not without demands. To will oneself free, she argues, is incoherent unless one also wills the freedom of others, because my projects need a world of other free people to give them meaning. Oppression is therefore the central moral evil: it treats freedoms as things. The book works through failure modes — the “serious man” who hides his freedom inside an absolute cause, the nihilist, the adventurer — and faces hard cases, including when opposing oppression may require violence, without pretending a rule can decide in advance. Whether her derivation of obligation from freedom itself succeeds remains its central scholarly debate.

### Situated freedom and the body

Underlying everything is Beauvoir’s version of existential freedom: never abstract, always *situated*. The body is not a machine the self drives; it is our grasp on the world, experienced differently when the world treats it as an object of the gaze or a reproductive destiny — an insight she later extended in *The Coming of Age*, arguing that societies likewise *make* “the aged” by withdrawing projects from them. Her insistence that freedom is real but unequal in its conditions marks her clearest advance on the more individualist early Sartre.

## Key works

- *She Came to Stay* (1943) — Novel of a love triangle as a philosophical drama of consciousness and the Other; composed before *Being and Nothingness* (both published 1943) and central to the influence debate.
- *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947) — Her short, systematic ethics: freedom, oppression, and responsibility without absolute rules. The best entry point.
- *The Second Sex* (1949, two volumes) — The founding work of modern feminism: biology, history, myth, and lived experience, from childhood to old age. Use the 2010 Borde and Malovany-Chevallier English translation; the 1953 translation cut and distorted substantial material.
- *The Mandarins* (1954) — Prix Goncourt-winning novel of post-war Paris intellectuals; the Sartre–Beauvoir circle in lightly fictionalised form.
- *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* (1958) — First volume of her memoirs; formation of a mind, and a classic of the genre in itself.
- *The Coming of Age* (1970) — *The Second Sex* method applied to old age: biology, history, and lived experience of a status societies impose.

## Contributions

Beauvoir created philosophical feminism as a systematic discipline: before *The Second Sex*, no work had combined phenomenology, history, economics, and testimony to argue that femininity is socially made and to map the making across an entire life. She also supplied existentialism with the ethics it had promised and lacked, and with a situated account of freedom more adequate to oppression than the early Sartrean version. She showed that lived experience — housework, ageing, being looked at — is not decoration on philosophy but evidence for it.

## Impact and legacy

*The Second Sex* ignited second-wave feminism: Betty Friedan’s *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) drew directly on it, and Kate Millett and a generation of feminist theorists worked from its framework. Judith Butler’s theory of gender developed its “becoming” thesis in a more radical direction. The scholarly recovery since the 1980s — new translations, editions of her early writings — has re-established her as a major philosopher in her own right rather than an adjunct to Sartre, now the standard view in reference works.

## Criticisms and debates

Critics from several directions have pressed *The Second Sex*. Some argue Beauvoir judges women’s lives by a masculine standard of transcendence, devaluing care and domestic work rather than the confinement to them — a point care ethicists developed, and one she partly anticipates by targeting compulsion, not the activities themselves. Her chapters on biology and psychoanalysis are dated in places, and her treatment of motherhood is a perennial flashpoint. Postcolonial and Black feminist critics argue that “women” in the book too often means bourgeois European women. And the biographical controversies over the couple’s relationships with younger women continue to be weighed, very differently by different scholars, against her philosophy of liberation.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Beauvoir was not Sartre’s disciple applying his ideas to women. The influence was mutual, and on some questions hers is the earlier or stronger formulation.
- “Becomes a woman” does not deny biology. It denies that biology dictates the meanings and limits built on it.
- She did not claim women are simply passive victims; complicity under constraint is one of her central, uncomfortable themes.
- *The Second Sex* is not only about gender in the abstract: its core demand is economic and practical — independent work and material conditions that make transcendence possible.

## What to read first

1. *The Ethics of Ambiguity* — short, clear, and arguably the most accessible entry into French existentialism as a whole.
2. *The Second Sex*: the Introduction and the opening of Volume II, where the famous sentence appears, in the 2010 translation.
3. Then Volume I’s chapters on myth and history, or Volume II on childhood and the married woman, according to interest.
4. *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* alongside, for the formation behind the philosophy — read, as all memoirs should be, as an account rather than a transcript.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct how "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" applies Sartre's "existence precedes essence" — and where Beauvoir departs from him: why does she insist that becoming happens inside a situation that trains girls toward immanence rather than transcendence?
2. In *The Second Sex*'s Introduction, Beauvoir asks why woman is the Other if human existence is freedom for everyone. Reconstruct her answer: what makes the Other-position so durable for women in particular, and what role does complicity — the temptation to surrender freedom — play in it?
3. Reconstruct the derivation in *The Ethics of Ambiguity*: how does willing my own freedom commit me to willing the freedom of others? Identify the step critics most question.
4. Care ethicists object that Beauvoir judges women's lives by a masculine standard of transcendence, devaluing care itself. State the objection and her strongest reply: that her target is confinement and compulsion, not the activities.
5. Apply her method to a fresh case: parental leave exists on paper, but taking it quietly ends promotion prospects. Map the structures of immanence, locate any complicity, and say what willing others' freedom would concretely require here.
