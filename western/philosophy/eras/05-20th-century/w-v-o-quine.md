# W. V. O. Quine

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1908–2000
- **Place:** Born in Akron, Ohio, USA; spent his career at Harvard
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy; logical empiricism's heir and internal critic
- **Known for:** "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and the naturalization of epistemology — pulling philosophy down into science

**Responds to:** [Vienna Circle](vienna-circle-logical-positivism.md), [Whitehead](alfred-north-whitehead.md) · **Answered by:** post-Quinean analytic philosophy, from Kripke onward — outside this repo's entries

## Biography

Willard Van Orman Quine was born in Akron, Ohio, in 1908. A mathematics and philosophy undergraduate at Oberlin, he arrived at Harvard for a PhD in mathematical logic, finishing in 1932 under Alfred North Whitehead's supervision in just two years. A traveling fellowship took him to Europe: he met Rudolf Carnap in Prague and sat in on the Vienna Circle — the logical positivists whose program he admired, absorbed, and eventually subverted from within.

Back at Harvard he spent the rest of his career (interrupted by wartime service as a naval intelligence officer, deciphering German U-boat communications). Early work was in formal logic — *A System of Logistic* (1934), *Mathematical Logic* (1940), the set theory NF. The essays that made his philosophical reputation came from the 1940s–50s: "On What There Is" (1948) and "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951). *Word and Object* (1960) became his most famous book. He traveled compulsively — he prided himself on visiting well over a hundred countries — and worked nearly to the end, dying on Christmas Day 2000.

## Major theories and concepts

### The two dogmas

**"Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951)** attacks two assumptions of the empiricist tradition. The first is the **analytic/synthetic distinction**: the belief that some truths hold purely by meaning ("no bachelor is married") and others by fact. Quine argues every attempt to define "analytic" — by synonymy, definition, interchangeability, semantic rules — moves in a small circle of equally unclear notions. The second dogma is **reductionism**: the idea that each meaningful statement separately faces experience. Against it, Quine (following Duhem) argues for **confirmation holism**: "our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body." Knowledge is a "web of belief," touching experience only at the edges; when recalcitrant experience strikes, *any* statement can be held true if we adjust elsewhere in the web — even, in principle, logic or mathematics, though they sit near the protected center.

### Naturalized epistemology

If there is no first philosophy prior to science, epistemology cannot be an a priori tribunal judging science from outside. In "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) Quine proposes it become a chapter of psychology: study, scientifically, how meager sensory input produces torrential theoretical output. Normative questions about justification are not so much answered as replaced by the descriptive science of how we actually form beliefs.

### Indeterminacy of translation

*Word and Object* imagines a field linguist translating a wholly unknown language. A native says "gavagai" as a rabbit passes. Does *gavagai* mean *rabbit*, *undetached rabbit part*, *rabbit-stage*, or *rabbithood manifesting itself*? Quine argues no behavioral evidence, however complete, can decide between translation manuals that fit all dispositions to speech behavior yet conflict with each other. Meaning, on his austere behaviorist-cum-physicalist basis, simply does not contain the facts that would settle the question. This is the **indeterminacy of translation**, and its domestic cousin is the **inscrutability of reference**.

### Ontological relativity and ontological commitment

What a theory says there is depends on the background language into which we translate it — **ontological relativity**. But within a theory, commitment is disciplined: "to be is to be the value of a bound variable." A theory is committed to exactly those entities its quantifiers must range over when regimented in first-order logic. This is why Quine accepts physical objects and (grudgingly, because mathematics needs them) sets, while rejecting meanings, propositions, and possible-but-nonexistent objects: entities posited only because grammar misleads us.

## Key works

- "On What There Is" (1948) — the famous essay on ontological commitment; Plato's beard and the Pegasus problem.
- "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) — the most influential philosophy paper of the century's second half.
- *From a Logical Point of View* (1953) — essay collection containing both papers above.
- *Word and Object* (1960) — translation, reference, and ontology systematically developed.
- *Ontological Relativity and Other Essays* (1969) — includes "Epistemology Naturalized."
- *The Roots of Reference* (1974) — how reference could grow from pre-linguistic behavior.
- *Pursuit of Truth* (1990) — his late, compact summing-up.

## Contributions

Quine demolished the framework within which positivist philosophy of science had operated, and rebuilt philosophy's relation to science on naturalistic terms: philosophy is continuous with science, differing in generality, not in kind or authority. His holism reshaped philosophy of language and of science; his regimentation program set the terms for decades of metaphysics (the "Quinean" mainstream in analytic ontology is named for him); and his behaviorist strictures pushed semantics toward the use-and-evidence questions Davidson and others then worked with.

## Impact and legacy

Few philosophers have set an agenda so completely. Naturalized epistemology became a research industry; holism is the background assumption of much philosophy of science; and the analytic/synthetic debate never recovered its pre-1951 innocence, even among those who defend a version of the distinction. Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972) pushed back toward essences and necessities Quine distrusted, defining the post-Quinean battlefield. His influence extends to linguistics (via the critique of Chomsky, and Chomsky's replies), cognitive science, and the naturalistic turn in ethics and philosophy of mind.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Defending analyticity:** Grice and Strawson (1956) argued Quine conflates the absence of a behaviorally respectable definition with the absence of a real distinction — synonymy-talk is orderly even if unreduced. The debate continues in work on conceptual role and two-dimensional semantics.
- **Meaning skepticism vs. overreach:** Chomsky objected that indeterminacy trades on behaviorist premises linguistics itself had refuted; defenders of meaning reply that translation in practice is determinate enough, and ask whether Quine's thesis has the radical consequences advertised.
- **Naturalism's normativity:** if epistemology is descriptive psychology, what happens to *ought* questions — how we *should* form beliefs? Jaegwon Kim pressed that Quine discards the normative core that made it epistemology at all.
- **Holism's excesses:** moderate holists (including the later Quine) concede that units of confirmation are usually smaller than "the whole of science"; how large, and for what, remains contested.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Anything can be held true come what may" is not a licence for irrationalism: Quine stresses the web's edges are constrained, and revising the center is maximally costly and rarely rational.
- Indeterminacy of translation is not the claim that translators are often unsure, or that communication is impossible; it is a thesis about whether *facts of meaning* exist beyond all behavioral facts.
- Quine was not a crude verificationist — he was dismantling the positivists' verificationism from inside.

## What to read first

"On What There Is" and "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (both in *From a Logical Point of View*) give you the essential Quine in two sittings. Then chapters 1–2 of *Word and Object* for gavagai, and *Pursuit of Truth* for the mature, moderate statement.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Quine's circularity charge: trace how attempts to define "analytic" through synonymy, definition, and interchangeability presuppose the very notion they were meant to explain. What would a non-circular definition have to supply?
2. A well-confirmed theory yields a failed prediction. Using the web of belief, identify three places where revision could fall — an auxiliary hypothesis, an observation report, a law near the center — and explain why experience alone cannot dictate the choice.
3. Grice and Strawson reply that synonymy-talk is perfectly orderly even if it resists behavioral definition. State their objection, and assess whether it answers Quine or changes the subject.
4. An informant says "pelu" whenever rain begins. Construct two incompatible translations of "pelu" that fit all observed behavior, and explain why, for Quine, no further behavioral evidence could decide between them.
5. Kim objects that naturalized epistemology discards the normative question — how we *should* form beliefs — that made it epistemology at all. State the objection and Quine's best available reply.
