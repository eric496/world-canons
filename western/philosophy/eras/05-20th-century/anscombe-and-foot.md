# Anscombe & Foot

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** G. E. M. (Elizabeth) Anscombe (1919–2001) and Philippa Foot (1920–2010)
- **Place:** Britain
- **School/tradition:** Analytic tradition, shaped by Aristotle, Aquinas, and Wittgenstein
- **Known for:** Reviving virtue ethics: Anscombe for *Intention* (1957) and “Modern Moral Philosophy” (1958), Foot for natural goodness and the trolley problem

**Responds to:** [Wittgenstein (late)](wittgenstein-late.md), [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Sidgwick](../04-19th-century/henry-sidgwick.md) · **Answered by:** [MacIntyre](alasdair-macintyre.md), [Thomson](judith-jarvis-thomson.md), [Murdoch](iris-murdoch.md), [Midgley](mary-midgley.md)

## Biography

Gertrude Elizabeth Margaret Anscombe was born in Limerick, Ireland, in 1919 and educated at Oxford, where she converted to Catholicism as an undergraduate. She became one of Wittgenstein’s most important students, and later his translator and a literary executor; her translation of *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) shaped how the English-speaking world read him. She taught at Oxford and then, from 1970, as professor at Cambridge. She was a formidable controversialist: she publicly opposed Oxford’s honorary degree for President Harry Truman, arguing that dropping atomic bombs on cities was murder. Her monograph *Intention* (1957) was called by Donald Davidson the most important treatment of action since Aristotle.

Philippa Foot — born Philippa Ruth Bosanquet in 1920 — was largely educated at home by governesses, yet won a place at Oxford, graduating with first-class honors in 1942. She taught at Oxford for most of her career, later moving to the University of California, Los Angeles. Where Anscombe was polemical, Foot was patient, returning to the same problems for decades and changing her mind in public when arguments required it: she began close to the non-cognitivism of Ayer and Hare, then spent decades arguing against them.

The two were allies in a culture dominated by emotivism and prescriptivism — theories treating moral judgments as expressions of attitude or universal commands. Both thought that picture had hollowed ethics out. Their remedy was to return to Aristotle, and for Anscombe also Aquinas, and rebuild moral philosophy on action, character, and human nature.

## Major theories and concepts

### Anscombe: the critique in “Modern Moral Philosophy”

Anscombe’s 1958 paper makes three claims. First, moral philosophy should be laid aside until we have an adequate philosophy of psychology — an account of action, intention, wanting, and pleasure. Second, moral *obligation*, moral *duty*, and the special moral “ought” are survivals of a divine-law conception of ethics. Detached from a lawgiver, they retain mesmeric force but lose coherent content — like “criminal” surviving where criminal law has been abolished. Third, the main English theories since Sidgwick are all forms of what she named here *consequentialism* — right action fixed by consequences — which she held corrupts judgment by making it possible, in principle, to justify any horror if the sums come out right.

Her proposal was to drop the thin moral “ought” for thicker Aristotelian terms: *unjust*, *untruthful*, *cowardly*. Those connect to the virtues and to what humans need to flourish — *eudaimonia* — rather than to free-floating obligation. The paper is routinely credited with restarting modern virtue ethics.

### Anscombe: intention and action under a description

*Intention* (1957) asks what makes an action intentional. Anscombe’s answer centers on the question “Why?” An action is intentional when a certain sense of that question applies — when the agent can give reasons showing the point of what they are doing, rather than merely citing a cause (“My elbow was jogged” refuses the relevant sense).

Her famous example: a man works a pump. Under one description he is moving his arm; under another, replenishing the house water supply; under another, poisoning the inhabitants — the source is poisoned and he knows it. One movement, several actions *under different descriptions*, intentional under those the agent knows and intends. Intention is therefore not an extra inner mental event accompanying the movement. It is a feature of the action’s whole structure, knowable by the agent without observation — you normally know you are writing without watching your hand. The account reshaped philosophy of action, feeding into Davidson and into debates about responsibility and the law.

### Foot: natural goodness

Foot’s mature book, *Natural Goodness* (2001), argues that moral evaluation is of a piece with evaluation of any living thing. We judge a root *defective* if it fails to take nourishment, an owl *defective* if it cannot see — not from preference, but relative to the species’ life form and what its members need to survive and reproduce. Foot calls this *natural normativity*.

Humans are unusual because our characteristic operation involves practical reason. But the *form* of evaluation, Foot argues, is the same: courage, temperance, and justice are virtues because they are traits humans characteristically need to live well; a person who cannot recognize another’s need as a reason shows a defect of practical rationality as real as the owl’s defective sight. If this works, moral goodness is objective and natural — grounded in facts about the human life form — without needing a divine lawgiver (Anscombe’s problem).

### Foot: the trolley problem and double effect

Foot’s most famous export began in “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect” (1967). Double effect distinguishes harms intended as a means from harms merely foreseen as a side effect. To test it, Foot imagined a runaway tram driver who must choose: continue and kill five workmen ahead, or steer onto a siding and kill one. Most judge steering permissible, though one dies by the driver’s active choice. Comparing cases where killing one to save five seems plainly wrong, Foot argued the real work is done also by the distinction between negative duties (not to harm) and positive duties (to aid), which are not symmetrical.

Judith Jarvis Thomson later coined the name “trolley problem” and devised the bystander and footbridge variants — pushing one man off a bridge to stop the trolley — that made it famous. The vast literature, and later applications to self-driving cars, descend from Foot’s paper.

## Key works

- Anscombe, *Intention* (1957) — the founding text of modern philosophy of action; difficult, rewarding, best read with commentary.
- Anscombe, “Modern Moral Philosophy” (1958) — roughly nineteen pages, polemical and readable; the manifesto of revived virtue ethics.
- Foot, “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect” (1967) — the paper that introduced the trolley case.
- Foot, *Virtues and Vices* (1978) — essays collecting her developing case against non-cognitivism and for the virtues.
- Foot, *Natural Goodness* (2001) — her final system: moral evaluation as natural evaluation of a life form.

## Contributions

Anscombe coined “consequentialism” as a term of criticism, supplied the dominant modern framework for intention, and named the incoherence she saw in modern moral language. Foot showed how an ethics of human nature could be stated in a rigorously analytic idiom, and inadvertently created modern ethics’ most discussed thought experiment. Jointly, they restarted talk of character, virtue, and the good life after decades in which ethics had shrunk to rules, consequences, and expressions of attitude.

## Impact and legacy

MacIntyre’s *After Virtue* (1981) builds explicitly on Anscombe’s diagnosis. Rosalind Hursthouse developed virtue ethics along Foot’s naturalist lines; McDowell and Foot debated how much nature can explain alone. Foot’s *Natural Goodness* (2001) is now treated as the mature statement of the revival Anscombe’s 1958 manifesto announced, and natural normativity remains a live research program in metaethics. *Intention*, through Davidson, set the agenda for philosophy of action. The trolley problem escaped philosophy into psychology, law, popular culture, and debates about autonomous vehicles.

## Criticisms and debates

Natural goodness is contested. Critics ask whether life-form facts can generate genuine *reasons* for someone who asks why they should care about flourishing as a human at all; Foot’s reply — that the question has the same shape as asking why an owl should hunt — persuades some, not others. Others note human social life varies across cultures far more than owl life, making the relevant facts thinner and more disputed. Anscombe’s history is also debated: scholars of Kant dispute whether the moral “ought” is as law-dependent as she claimed, and consequentialists reply that her charge ignores rule-based versions of their theory. Her Catholic convictions informed her positions on war and abortion; how far the arguments stand independently of them is a standing scholarly question.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Anscombe did not argue that morality itself is impossible without God. She argued that one *specific* concept — law-like moral obligation — lacks sense without a law conception of ethics; Aristotelian virtue talk, she thought, works without it.
- Foot’s naturalism is not “whatever is natural is good.” Defect is judged against a life form’s characteristic life, and Foot explicitly allows that natural traits, such as aggression, can be defects in humans.
- Foot did not invent the footbridge case, and the trolley problem was not originally a puzzle about self-driving cars. Both the variants and the applications came later, chiefly through Thomson.
- Neither was simply nostalgic. Both argued with their opponents’ analytic tools, and Foot changed her mind over a career.

## What to read first

1. Anscombe, “Modern Moral Philosophy” (1958) — short, forceful, and the natural doorway into both thinkers.
2. Foot, “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect” (1967) — read the original trolley case before any of its descendants.
3. Foot, *Natural Goodness*, opening chapters — then *Intention*, with a commentary.
