# Vienna Circle / Logical Positivism

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** A movement, not a person — active in Vienna from the mid-1920s until its dispersal in the late 1930s; its influence runs through the rest of the twentieth century.
- **Place:** Vienna, Austria; with a parallel group in Berlin and, after emigration, centers in Britain and the United States.
- **School/tradition:** Analytic philosophy and philosophy of science; the Circle's doctrine is called logical positivism, or logical empiricism.
- **Known for:** The verification principle — meaningful statements must be analytic or empirically verifiable — and the program of a unified science clarified by logic.

**Responds to:** [Russell](bertrand-russell.md), [Wittgenstein (early)](wittgenstein-early.md) · **Answered by:** [Popper](karl-popper.md), [Quine](w-v-o-quine.md), [Kuhn](thomas-kuhn.md)

## Biography

The Vienna Circle grew out of discussions Moritz Schlick (1882–1936) began after his appointment in 1922 to a Vienna chair once held by Ernst Mach. From about 1924 a group met on Thursday evenings around Schlick: Rudolf Carnap (1891–1970), its leading theorist; mathematician Hans Hahn; sociologist Otto Neurath, its organizer; Herbert Feigl; Friedrich Waismann; and others, including Kurt Gödel as an occasional participant. A parallel Berlin group gathered around Hans Reichenbach. In "Red Vienna," several members — Neurath above all — saw the scientific world-conception as part of a broader project of public enlightenment.

Two inheritances converged: empiricism modernized by science (Mach, relativity, Frege and Russell's logic), and Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* (1921/1922), read line by line in 1926–27 for its line between science and metaphysical nonsense. Wittgenstein kept his distance — he was not an empiricist or a member — and the Circle's Wittgenstein was partly its own construction.

In 1929 the group announced itself with the manifesto *The Scientific Conception of the World* (mainly Carnap, Hahn, and Neurath): a unified science, clarified by logical analysis. Expansion followed — *Erkenntnis*, unity-of-science congresses, and Ayer, whose *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) carried positivism into English.

The end came from politics. In 1936 Schlick was murdered on the steps of the University of Vienna by a deranged former student. After the Anschluss of 1938 the group — many of whose members were Jewish, socialist, or both — dissolved. Its members emigrated, above all to the United States (Carnap, Feigl, Reichenbach, Philipp Frank), where logical empiricism dominated philosophy of science for a generation.

## Major theories and concepts

### The verification principle

The Circle's signature doctrine: a statement is *cognitively meaningful* only if it is either *analytic* — true by logic and definitions alone — or *empirically verifiable* in principle: some possible experience must count for or against it. A statement meeting neither condition is not false but literally meaningless, a pseudo-statement.

The target is traditional metaphysics — claims about the Absolute or Being as such — together with theology. Carnap's "Elimination of Metaphysics" (1932) applied the test to Heidegger, arguing that "the Nothing nothings" violates logical syntax while mimicking meaningful speech. Verifiability is *in principle*, not current practice: statements about the far side of the Moon (then unobserved) pass, because we know what would settle them.

Strong versus weak versions were debated. Strong verification excludes science's universal laws, which no finite observations conclusively verify. Ayer's weak version required only that experience be *relevant* to truth or falsity — a loosening critics charged with letting almost everything back in.

### The analytic/synthetic distinction and the rejection of the synthetic a priori

Kant had held that some truths are *synthetic a priori*: informative about the world yet knowable independently of experience. The Circle denied any such category exists. Every meaningful truth is either analytic and a priori (true by logic, saying nothing factual) or synthetic and a posteriori (factual, answerable to experience). Relativity was their Exhibit A: geometry, Kant's paradigm, had turned out partly empirical. This tidy two-box scheme is exactly what Quine attacked in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951).

### Philosophy as logical clarification; the unity of science

Positivists demoted rather than abolished philosophy: it is not a science delivering facts about a higher realm, but an *activity* — the logical clarification of the language of science. Its program was *unified science*: Neurath's encyclopedia project and the physicalist thesis that scientific statements can be translated into a common physical language. A famous internal dispute concerned *protocol sentences*, the observation statements on which testing rests. Neurath denied any incorrigible foundations — sailors rebuilding their ship at sea, never able to start from dry dock. Schlick sought firm affirmations tied directly to experience. The Circle was never a monolith.

### The emotive theory of ethics

If ethical sentences are neither analytic nor verifiable, what are they doing? Ayer's answer (chapter 6) was *emotivism*: "Murder is wrong" does not state a fact; it expresses disapproval and aims to arouse it. Charles Stevenson developed a subtler version. Whether emotivism captures moral disagreement — we seem to *contradict* each other, not merely cheer against each other — became a standard objection and a starting point for modern metaethics.

### The self-refutation problem — and the positivist replies

The classic objection: the principle is itself neither analytic nor verifiable, so by its own standard it is meaningless. "Self-refuting, end of story" is too quick; the dialectic should be reported rather than a knockout declared.

The positivists replied that the principle is not a factual assertion but a *proposal* — a recommended definition of "meaningful" for science, judged by fruitfulness, not verification. On Carnap's principle of tolerance, we choose linguistic frameworks. Critics responded that a mere proposal loses polemical force: the metaphysician can simply decline it. Defenders answered that proposals can still be rationally motivated. The exchange is the start of an argument about philosophical criteria, not a one-line refutation.

Other tensions accumulated: universal laws, weak verification, Karl Popper's rival *falsifiability* — a criterion not of meaning but of *demarcation* between science and non-science, offered by a Viennese contemporary who later claimed to have "killed" logical positivism, a claim historians treat as partly self-promotion — and finally Quine and Kuhn.

## Key works

- **Carnap, *The Logical Structure of the World* (1928)** — Construction of all concepts from elementary experiences using Russellian logic.
- ***The Scientific Conception of the World*** (1929), by Hahn, Neurath, and Carnap — The manifesto; the best short statement of aims and the unity-of-science program.
- **Carnap, "The Elimination of Metaphysics"** (1932) — The verificationist attack on Heidegger; the clearest exhibition of the method.
- **Schlick, "Positivism and Realism"** (1932/33) — The founder's calmer statement, useful against caricatures from Carnap alone.
- **Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic*** (1936) — Chapters 1 and 6: elimination of metaphysics and emotivism. Read as a manifesto, not settled doctrine.

## Contributions

1. **A criterion-driven philosophy of meaning.** The demand — say what would count for or against your claim — permanently raised analytic philosophy's standard of clarity.
2. **Logic applied to science.** Carnap and Reichenbach founded modern philosophy of science as a technical discipline.
3. **The linguistic turn, institutionalized.** Journals and congresses made language and logic philosophy's common currency across two continents.
4. **Metaethics as a field.** Emotivism, even as a target, set the questions metaethics still organizes itself around.

## Impact and legacy

In America, the émigrés trained the generation that made philosophy of science a profession; in Britain, Ayer defined positivism for students. Its self-image — philosophy continuous with science — became analytic philosophy's default. Its critics were its heirs: Popper, Quine, and Kuhn (1962) each defined themselves against a legacy they had absorbed.

## Criticisms and debates

**The verification principle's troubles** are detailed above; most historians judge no version achieved exactly the intended cut between science and metaphysics.

**Quine and Kuhn.** Quine removed the principle's scaffolding; Kuhn challenged its picture of cumulative science. Whether these refute positivism or develop Neurath's own holism is debated — Neurath's boat anticipates Quine, who quoted it.

**Historical reassessment.** Recent scholarship stresses diversity: Neurath, Schlick, and Carnap do not reduce to Ayer's slogans.

**Politics.** Frankfurt School critics charged positivism with technocratic blindness; defenders point to Neurath's housing work.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"The positivists proved metaphysics is false."** Their claim was stronger: metaphysical sentences are *meaningless*, lacking cognitive content.
- **"The principle refutes itself, so there is nothing to learn."** The objection opens a genuine debate about proposals and conventions.
- **"Popper was a member who improved the principle."** Popper was never a member; falsifiability was a criterion of scientific status, not meaning.
- **"The Circle was just Ayer's book."** Ayer popularized one strand; Schlick, Carnap, and Neurath disagreed on foundations and politics.
- **"Wittgenstein founded the Circle."** He inspired it and resisted its reading of him. Inspiration is not membership.
- **"Positivism died, so it failed."** Its project collapsed, but it set the agenda its successors still work within.

## What to read first

1. **Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic*, chapters 1 and 6** — elimination of metaphysics and emotivism, read as a 1936 manifesto, not current doctrine.
2. **The 1929 manifesto** — short, programmatic, and surprisingly political; the Circle in its own voice.
3. **Carnap, "Elimination of Metaphysics" (1932)** — the method applied to a real target.
4. **The [Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/) article on logical empiricism** — for the self-refutation debate and the Quine/Kuhn aftermath, with the care the slogans lack.

## Study questions

1. Classify each sentence by the verification principle, stating your reasoning: (a) "All metals expand when heated"; (b) "The Absolute unfolds itself in history"; (c) "Cruelty is wrong." For any sentence you judge cognitively meaningless, say what Ayer thinks it is doing instead.
2. Reconstruct the positivist argument that there is no synthetic a priori, using geometry after relativity as the key exhibit. What exactly is the two-box scheme Kant is accused of overfilling?
3. State the self-refutation objection, the positivist reply that the principle is a proposal rather than an assertion, and the critic's rejoinder that a mere proposal can simply be declined. Where does this leave the attack on metaphysics?
4. Quine's "Two Dogmas" attacks the analytic/synthetic distinction the Circle relied on. Explain why removing that distinction threatens the verification principle from inside empiricism, rather than merely refuting Kant.
5. Why do universal scientific laws embarrass strong verification, and what does Ayer's weak version concede in order to save them?
