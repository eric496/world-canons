# Martin Heidegger

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance
- **Dates:** 1889–1976
- **Place:** Born in Meßkirch, Germany, and based mainly at Freiburg
- **School/tradition:** Phenomenological and hermeneutic tradition; reopened the question of Being
- **Known for:** *Being and Time* (1927), the analysis of human existence as *Dasein*, and a later, influential critique of modern technology

**Responds to:** [Husserl](edmund-husserl.md), [Nietzsche](../04-19th-century/friedrich-nietzsche.md) · **Answered by:** [Levinas](emmanuel-levinas.md), [Gadamer](hans-georg-gadamer.md), [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md), [Jaspers](karl-jaspers.md), [Strauss](leo-strauss.md)

## Biography

Heidegger came from a modest Catholic family in rural southwestern Germany; his father was a cooper and church sexton. A church scholarship made his education possible. He entered a Jesuit novitiate in 1909 but left within weeks for health reasons, then studied theology and later philosophy at Freiburg, writing his habilitation on a grammatical treatise then attributed to Duns Scotus (the *Grammatica Speculativa*, now credited to Thomas of Erfurt). In 1917 he married Elfride Petri, a Lutheran. By 1919 he had broken publicly with what he called “the system of Catholicism” — though not, he insisted, with Christianity as such.

At Freiburg he became Edmund Husserl’s assistant, and his lecture courses of the early 1920s acquired a legendary reputation among students. Hannah Arendt, Hans-Georg Gadamer, Karl Löwith, and Herbert Marcuse all passed through them. Arendt and Heidegger began a love affair in 1925, when she was his eighteen-year-old student; it was kept secret, resumed in changed form after the war, and became public knowledge only decades later through their correspondence. In 1923 Heidegger took a chair at Marburg, and there wrote his masterpiece at speed, under pressure to publish for promotion. *Being and Time* appeared in 1927, dedicated to Husserl. In 1928 he returned to Freiburg as Husserl’s successor.

Then comes the period no honest biography can soften. Heidegger joined the Nazi Party on 1 May 1933, days after becoming Rector of Freiburg University. As rector he delivered a notorious address aligning the university with the new regime’s aims, implemented aspects of Nazi policy within the university, and in some documented cases denounced or failed to protect colleagues and students. He resigned the rectorate in April 1934 after clashes with the ministry and party officials, but he remained a party member until 1945 and never afterwards made a public apology or a full public reckoning. The 1966 *Der Spiegel* interview, published posthumously at his insistence, offered self-justification that many readers found evasive.

After the war the French occupation authorities banned him from teaching; the ban lasted until 1951, with emeritus status restored as part of his reinstatement. Arendt, remarkably, interceded on his behalf and renewed contact. He rebuilt an audience through lectures such as those on technology, spent much of his later life at his hut at Todtnauberg in the Black Forest, and died in 1976. Publication of his private *Black Notebooks* in 2014, containing antisemitic passages from the 1930s and 1940s, reopened — with new documentary force — the question of how deeply Nazism and antisemitism were woven into his thought, not only his conduct. That debate is presented below as a debate; it is not settled.

## Major theories and concepts

### The question of Being and Dasein

Heidegger’s starting claim is historiographical and philosophical at once: Western philosophy since Plato has forgotten to ask what it means for something *to be* — the question of Being (*Sein*). Philosophers catalogue beings — things, people, gods, numbers — while treating “being” itself as too obvious to examine.

His method is indirect. Start with the one being for whom being is always an issue: ourselves. He calls human existence *Dasein*, literally “being-there.” The choice of a neutral word matters. “Man,” “subject,” and “consciousness” all smuggle in theories — a rational animal, a mind set over against a world. *Being and Time* instead analyses the structures of everyday existence itself: we always find ourselves already in a meaningful world, involved with things and other people, understanding ourselves through possibilities, and moving towards a future that will end. This analysis is called existential, and its categories — *existentials* — are meant to describe how Dasein exists, not properties a thing happens to have.

### Being-in-the-world, thrownness, and care

Dasein is not a detached mind that must first prove an external world exists. It is always already *being-in-the-world*: absorbed in a workshop, a kitchen, a commute, where things show up first not as neutral objects but as usable or obstructive — a hammer as good for hammering, not as an arrangement of wood and metal contemplated from outside. Heidegger calls this ready-to-hand mode *Zuhandenheit*, contrasting it with the present-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*) stance of theory, which arises when involvement breaks down — the hammer snaps and suddenly we stare at it as a mere thing.

We did not choose the world we wake into. We are *thrown* (*Geworfenheit*) into a language, family, century, and body, with a past already underway. Yet thrownness is only half the structure: Dasein also *projects* itself into possibilities — the career, the relationship, the person it is trying to become. Heidegger gathers thrownness, projection, and everyday absorption into a single structure: *care* (*Sorge*). To be Dasein is for things to matter, one way or another. Even indifference is, for him, a deficient mode of caring, not its absence.

### Das Man and inauthenticity

Mostly, we do not choose from a clear-eyed view of our own possibilities. We do what “one” does. Heidegger calls this anonymous pressure *das Man* — “the They.” One dresses like this, one has opinions about that, one keeps busy. The They is not a conspiracy or a particular crowd; it is a structural feature of social existence, and no one escapes it entirely. It relieves us of responsibility: if everyone does it, no one in particular has decided.

Existence lived wholly under the They, fleeing its own finitude into distraction and conventional interpretation, is *inauthentic* (*uneigentlich*). The translation misleads if heard as “fake.” Inauthenticity, for Heidegger, is our default and in a sense necessary mode — we learn language, work, and norms socially. The problem is not participating in a shared world; it is losing oneself in it so completely that one’s own possibilities are never owned as one’s own.

### Being-toward-death and authenticity

What can individuate Dasein — pull it out of the They — is confronting mortality in a specific way. Death, in Heidegger’s analysis, is my *ownmost* possibility: no one can die my death for me. It is *non-relational* for the same reason, and *not to be outstripped*: every other project ends there. It is also indefinite in its “when,” and always possible.

Ordinarily the They tranquillizes this: “one dies, eventually, but not yet, and not me in particular.” *Being-toward-death* (*Sein zum Tode*) reverses the evasion. If I understand my existence as finite through and through — if I *anticipate* death rather than merely expecting it as a future event — my other possibilities change character. They become visibly mine, limited, and urgent. This owning of finitude, joined with taking over one’s thrown situation rather than disowning it, is *authenticity* (*Eigentlichkeit*). A related experience is the call of conscience and *resoluteness*: not a dramatic self-invention, but a readiness to act in one’s concrete situation without the alibi that the They decided.

Internal tensions are real. Critics ask whether an account built on *my* death can do justice to other people as more than background, and whether authenticity has any moral content at all — a resolute person, on the bare analysis, could resolutely choose something terrible. Heidegger’s defenders argue the analysis is formally about owning choices, not a moral code, and that later work supplies more on others, history, and ethics. The gap remains one of the most discussed in the literature.

### The later Heidegger: language and technology

After a “turn” (*Kehre*) much debated by scholars, the later Heidegger shifts emphasis from Dasein’s analysis to the history of Being itself — how different epochs disclose beings in different ways. Language becomes central: in the “Letter on Humanism” (1947) he calls language “the house of Being,” and rejects Sartre’s humanist existentialism as still trapped in metaphysics.

The best-known late essay, “The Question Concerning Technology” (lectured 1953, published 1954), argues that the essence of modern technology is not machines. It is a way of revealing the world he calls *enframing* (*Gestell*): nature and even people show up as *standing-reserve* (*Bestand*) — a river as hydroelectric capacity, a forest as timber inventory, workers as human resources — stock to be ordered and optimized. The danger is not any single device but that this one way of revealing crowds out all others, including our sense of ourselves. His counter-words — *poiesis*, bringing-forth, and later *Gelassenheit*, releasement — point to a more receptive relation to things, though critics find the practical conclusions thin.

## Key works

- *Being and Time* (1927) — The major work: Dasein, being-in-the-world, das Man, death, and temporality. Published unfinished relative to its announced plan; Division Three and the projected destruction of the history of ontology never appeared in that form.
- *Introduction to Metaphysics* (lectures 1935; published 1953) — A sweeping reading of Greek thought and modern decline; also politically notorious for retaining, in the 1953 text, a reference to the “inner truth and greatness” of the National Socialist movement, with Heidegger’s later gloss.
- “Letter on Humanism” (1947) — A reply to Sartre, redefining his project against existential humanism; source of “language is the house of Being.”
- “The Question Concerning Technology” (1954) — The enframing essay; the most taught of the late works and highly influential outside philosophy.
- *Black Notebooks* (published from 2014) — Private notebooks, 1931–1970s, whose antisemitic passages intensified the debate about philosophy and politics in his case. Essential evidence for scholars; not a starting point for beginners.

## Contributions

Heidegger made the question of Being a live problem again and gave phenomenology a historical, practical turn it lacked in Husserl: existence is not primarily knowing but involved, temporal, finite being-in-a-world. Concepts such as being-in-the-world, authenticity, and enframing migrated far beyond his school. His readings of Aristotle, Kant, Hölderlin, and Nietzsche, whatever their scholarly liberties, permanently changed how those figures are taught.

## Impact and legacy

Within philosophy his influence runs through Sartre and Beauvoir, Merleau-Ponty, Arendt, Gadamer’s hermeneutics, Levinas (largely as a critic), Derrida, and Foucault. Beyond it, his ideas shaped existential psychotherapy, theology (Rudolf Bultmann), literary studies, and contemporary philosophy of technology and environmental thought, where enframing is routinely used to discuss extraction and data. Few twentieth-century philosophers are as unavoidable — or as contested.

## Criticisms and debates

The political debate dominates and divides scholars. One line, associated with critics such as Emmanuel Faye, holds that Heidegger’s Nazism and antisemitism are integral to the philosophy and should change how — or whether — it is taught. Others, including many working phenomenologists, argue that central analyses in *Being and Time* can be assessed and used independently, while not excusing the man; still others locate the contamination in specific later texts and categories rather than the whole corpus. The *Black Notebooks* strengthened the integral-contamination case factually; they did not produce consensus. What is no longer respectable is ignoring the record.

Philosophical criticisms are independent and substantial: Levinas charged that Heidegger subordinates the ethical relation to the Other to ontology; Carnap used Heidegger’s prose as a paradigm of meaninglessness; Adorno attacked the “jargon of authenticity”; and many readers find the later etymological method suggestive rather than demonstrative.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Authenticity is not “be yourself” self-help. It is owning finitude and thrownness — closer to dropping alibis than to self-expression.
- *Das Man* is not a sneer at ordinary people; it names a structure everyone, including the philosopher, inhabits.
- Enframing is not a complaint that machines are bad. A handcrafted, pre-industrial outlook can still, on his terms, miss the point, and technology’s danger lies in a mode of revealing.
- Do not treat the Nazi period as a brief youthful error: he was 43 in 1933, rector, a party member until 1945, and silent afterwards. Equally, do not assume the political verdict settles every philosophical question — that inference is itself one of the contested moves.

## What to read first

1. Start with essays in *Basic Writings*: “The Question Concerning Technology” and the “Letter on Humanism,” with the editor’s introductions.
2. Then *Being and Time*, Division I, guided — on being-in-the-world and das Man — using a companion such as Simon Critchley’s lectures or Michael Inwood’s *A Very Short Introduction* beforehand.
3. Then Division II on death, conscience, and temporality.
4. Read a sober account of 1933–45 alongside, not afterwards — for example from a reputable biography or encyclopedia entry — so the philosophy and the record are held together from the start.

## Study questions

1. A carpenter's hammer breaks mid-task. Reconstruct, in Heidegger's terms, what changes in how the hammer shows up, and why he treats the present-at-hand stance of detached theory as derivative from ready-to-hand involvement rather than primary.
2. Death is called Dasein's ownmost, non-relational, unsurpassable possibility. Explain each term, and show how anticipating death differs from merely expecting that one will die someday — and what the They does to tranquillize it.
3. Critics object that authenticity carries no moral content: one could resolutely own a terrible choice. State the objection, the defender's reply that the analysis is formal rather than a moral code, and judge whether the reply is enough.
4. Contrast being-in-the-world with the Cartesian picture of a subject that must first prove an external world exists. Which familiar philosophical problem does Heidegger claim simply fails to arise on his account, and why?
5. Apply enframing to a fresh case: a river administered solely as hydroelectric capacity, its valley's other meanings treated as obstacles. What, for Heidegger, is the danger — and why is it not a complaint about machinery?
