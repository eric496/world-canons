# Iris Murdoch

*Era: 20th Century to Present (1900 – ) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1919–1999
- **Place:** Born in Dublin, Ireland; lived and worked in England, at Oxford
- **School/tradition:** Moral realism; Platonism; the wartime Oxford circle of Anscombe, Foot and Midgley
- **Known for:** Attention and "unselfing", *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970), the Good as a real, magnetic object of moral vision

**Responds to:** [Anscombe & Foot](anscombe-and-foot.md), [Simone Weil](simone-weil.md), [Sartre](jean-paul-sartre.md) · **Answered by:** [Martha Nussbaum](martha-nussbaum.md), and the moral-perception literature she made possible

## Biography

Jean Iris Murdoch was born in Dublin in 1919 to Anglo-Irish parents — her father a civil servant from County Down, her mother a Dubliner who had trained as a singer — and grew up in London. She read classics ("Greats") at Somerville College, Oxford, from 1938, graduating with first-class honors in 1942. Her Oxford years placed her in a remarkable cohort: with Elizabeth Anscombe, Philippa Foot and Mary Midgley (Murdoch, Foot and Midgley at Somerville, Anscombe at St Hugh's), she formed the circle now known as the **Wartime Quartet**. With most male students and many tutors away at war, taught in part by refugee scholars, women and conscientious objectors, the four friends developed a shared conviction that the dominant moral philosophy of the day — Ayer's emotivism, on which moral utterances merely express feeling — was hopeless against the realities the war was revealing, and that ethics needed metaphysics, human nature and a serious account of inner life. Their story, long neglected, has been recovered in Clare Mac Cumhaill and Rachael Wiseman's *Metaphysical Animals* (2022).

Murdoch spent the war in the civil service (the Treasury, 1942–44) and then with the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (1944–46), working with displaced people in Belgium and Austria. In Brussels in 1945 she met Sartre, whose writing electrified her; her *Sartre: Romantic Rationalist* (1953) was the first book on Sartre published in English. After a research fellowship at Newnham College, Cambridge (1947–48), she became a tutorial fellow at St Anne's College, Oxford, in 1948 and taught philosophy there until 1963, resigning to write full-time. She married John Bayley, an Oxford scholar of English literature, in 1956.

Alongside the philosophy she wrote twenty-six novels, beginning with *Under the Net* (1954); *The Sea, the Sea* won the Booker Prize in 1978. The novels are not illustrations of the philosophy but they share its obsessions: contingency, illusion, the difficulty of seeing other people as real. Her philosophical essays appeared across four decades, gathered around *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970); the late summa, *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* (1992), grew from her Gifford Lectures of 1982. Diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease in the mid-1990s, she died in Oxford in 1999; Bayley's memoir *Elegy for Iris* (1998) made her decline a matter of public record while she still lived.

## Major theories and concepts

### Attention and unselfing

From Simone Weil, Murdoch took the concept of **attention**: a just and loving gaze directed outward, which discovers reality rather than projecting fantasy onto it. Her famous example is internal and unglamorous. A mother-in-law, M, finds her daughter-in-law D vulgar and tiresome; M behaves impeccably throughout, but inwardly she works — she attends, she looks again, she corrects the picture her own jealousy and snobbery had painted, until she sees D as she is: not vulgar but spontaneous, not tiresome but gay. Nothing in M's outward behavior need change; the moral work is entirely in the quality of her vision. Against philosophies for which morality exists only in overt acts and choices, Murdoch insists that this continuous inner labor *is* the moral life, happening "every moment" and not only at points of decision.

The enemy is the ego: "the fat relentless ego," as she calls it, which spins self-consoling fantasy and blocks perception. **Unselfing** — the progressive liberation of attention from ego — is her name for moral progress. It can be trained by anything that genuinely shows us something other than ourselves: nature, mathematics, learning a language, great art, and above all other people. Her most-quoted sentence, from "The Sublime and the Good" (1959), defines the goal: "Love is the extremely difficult realisation that something other than oneself is real."

### The Good as a magnetic object

The title essay of *The Sovereignty of Good* and its companions argue that the Good is real — not a projection of choice or feeling, but a single, perfect, transcendent object of attention, functioning in moral life as Plato's Form of the Good and the sun in the cave allegory function in his: as the condition of seeing anything else truly. Drawing an analogy with the traditional idea of God while dispensing with a personal deity (Murdoch described her position as a kind of Platonism that could survive the death of God), she treats Good as a **magnetic** center: attention is drawn toward it in degrees, and virtue is the name for a life increasingly oriented by it. Moral realism follows: differences between people are often differences not of values chosen but of *reality perceived* — one person simply sees more, and more truly, than another.

### Moral vision against behaviorism and voluntarism

Murdoch's polemical target is the picture of the moral agent she found dominant in both analytic ethics and existentialism — what she called the "Kantian-existentialist" man: an isolated, rational will confronting a neutral world of facts, free precisely at the moments when it leaps into overt choice. On that picture (she names Hare's prescriptivism and Hampshire, and Sartre as its romantic version), the inner life drops out as morally irrelevant because it cannot be cashed in behavior, and moral concepts are reduced to recommendations attached to descriptions anyone could share.

Her alternative: human beings are unified creatures who *see*, and their vision is already moral. Concepts are not neutral labels plus an optional evaluation; the concepts we have determine what we notice at all. Hence her insistence, against the fact/value machinery of her contemporaries, that moral philosophy needs a richer psychology and a metaphysics — a picture of human beings as self-deceiving, fantasy-prone creatures for whom becoming truthful is the central task. Freedom, correspondingly, is not uncaused choosing but the slow emancipation of vision from the ego.

### Art and the novel as moral forms

Great art matters to Murdoch because it is attention practiced in public: the good novel defeats fantasy by presenting characters who are genuinely other — opaque, contingent, not arranged for the reader's consolation. Her own fiction is the working-out of this conviction rather than its advertisement, and she resisted having the novels read as philosophy in code. Criticism, hers included, should judge art by the quality of attention it embodies and elicits — art, on her account, is among the most educational of all human activities, not a diversion from the moral life but one of its schools.

## Key works

- *Sartre: Romantic Rationalist* (1953) — the first English book on Sartre; formative statement of her quarrel with existentialist freedom.
- "The Sublime and the Good" (1959) and "Against Dryness" (1961) — the early essays: love as attention to the real; a manifesto for the novel.
- *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970) — three lectures ("The Idea of Perfection", "On 'God' and 'Good'", "The Sovereignty of Good over Other Concepts") forming her central philosophical statement.
- *The Fire and the Sun: Why Plato Banished the Artists* (1977) — her negotiation with Plato, her master and opponent.
- *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* (1992) — the late, capacious summa, from the Gifford Lectures; duties, axioms, art, religion and the Good.
- Novels (context, not the philosophy's center): *Under the Net* (1954), *The Black Prince* (1973), *The Sea, the Sea* (1978).

## Contributions

Murdoch helped reopen moral philosophy to the inner life. Writing when the field's official methods defined morality in terms of overt choice and public criteria, she made concepts like attention, vision and fantasy philosophically respectable again, and so prepared ground shared with the virtue-ethics revival led by her friends Anscombe and Foot — though her Platonist moral realism is her own, not a version of theirs. Her insistence that moral differences are often differences of perception underlies the later literature on moral perception and moral particularism, and John McDowell's work on value and secondary qualities develops in a space she cleared. She also modelled a way for philosophy and literature to inform each other without either dissolving into the other.

## Impact and legacy

For decades Murdoch the philosopher was eclipsed by Murdoch the novelist, and by the biographical industry around her life. That balance has shifted: the recovery of the Wartime Quartet has restored her to the center of the story of post-war British ethics, and *The Sovereignty of Good* is now a standard text. [Nussbaum](martha-nussbaum.md) took up her picture of moral attention in work on love and literary perception (while pressing her on its Platonic metaphysics); Cora Diamond and others in the "moral perception" tradition work in her wake. Within religious thought, her God-less devotion to the Good made her a key figure for theologians exploring faith after the death of God, even though she declined the label of Christian thinker.

## Criticisms and debates

The Good itself is the contested point. Critics ask whether it does explanatory work or merely names Murdoch's conviction that morality is objective: is it a metaphysical posit, a regulative ideal, or a piece of disciplined metaphor? She welcomed some of this vagueness as honesty about the limits of theory, which her critics count as evasion. Her account is also charged with neglecting action-guidance: attention tells you to look better, but the agent mid-dilemma wants to know what follows, and Murdoch's distrust of rules and principles can leave her view quietist. Whether she belongs inside virtue ethics at all is debated — her center of gravity is perception and metaphysics rather than character or flourishing. Finally, readers divide over the relation between the lucid essays and the sprawling, sometimes oracular *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals*, and over how much of the fiction the philosophy can legitimately claim.

## Misreadings to avoid

- The novels are not the philosophy in disguise, and the philosophy is not a key for decoding them. Murdoch kept the enterprises distinct even where they share themes.
- Murdoch was not a Christian apologist. She took religion's moral seriousness seriously while denying a personal God; her Good is Plato's, not the Church's.
- Attention is not a mindfulness technique or a tip for better decisions. It is a metaphysical claim about what morality mostly consists in.
- She did not deny that choices and actions matter; she denied that they exhaust the moral life, which is mostly the prior work of seeing.

## What to read first

1. *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970) — under a hundred pages and the whole position: begin with "The Idea of Perfection" and the mother-in-law example.
2. "The Sublime and the Good" (1959) — short, quotable, and the clearest statement of love as attention to reality.
3. *Under the Net* (1954) — the most philosophical of the early novels, read as fiction first; then *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* selectively for the mature system.
