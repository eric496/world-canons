## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1138 – 1204 (born 1135 on some traditional datings; 1138 is the more common scholarly dating)
- **Place:** Born in Córdoba, al-Andalus; exiled across North Africa; settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt. Wrote the *Guide* in Judeo-Arabic, his legal works in Hebrew.
- **School/tradition:** Jewish philosophy and law — a rabbinic jurist, physician, and Aristotelian philosopher in the Arabic tradition of al-Fārābī and Avicenna.
- **Known for:** The *Guide of the Perplexed*, negative theology, and the *Mishneh Torah*, the great code of Jewish law.

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Avicenna](avicenna.md) · **Answered by:** [Spinoza](../03-early-modern/baruch-spinoza.md)

## Biography

Moses ben Maimon — Maimonides in Greek/Latin dress, Rambam (Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon) in Hebrew — was born in Córdoba in 1138 (the traditional dating 1135 still appears; 1138 is now the more common scholarly date) into a family of judges and scholars; his father, Maimon, was a rabbinic judge who gave him his first education in Bible, Talmud, mathematics, and astronomy. When Moses was about ten, the Almohads conquered Córdoba and abolished the protected-but-subordinate status of Jews and Christians, imposing conversion, exile, or death. The family spent about a decade wandering southern Spain, then crossed to Fez in Morocco — itself Almohad — living outwardly precariously (the details, including later accusations that the family lived for a time as outward converts, remain genuinely contested by historians). Around 1165 they left for good: a storm-tossed voyage to Palestine, a brief stay, then settlement in Egypt, in Fustat (Old Cairo). The move proved decisive. In Egypt, Maimonides' brother David, who had supported the family as a gem merchant, drowned in the Indian Ocean around 1177 with the family's fortune. Forbidden to profit from Torah teaching, Maimonides turned to medicine, rising to become physician to the vizier al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil and then to the sultan's court — in his famous letter to his translator Samuel ibn Tibbon he describes days consumed by court attendance in Cairo, then consultations until nightfall, leaving the Sabbath for study and communal duties. Yet in those same years he headed the Jewish community of Egypt, issued legal rulings across the Mediterranean world, wrote the *Mishneh Torah* (c. 1170–80), the first comprehensive code of all Jewish law, and completed in 1190 the *Guide of the Perplexed*, addressed to his student Joseph ibn Aknin. He died in Fustat in 1204; by tradition his body was carried to Tiberias for burial. Grief was public across communities: in Fustat Jews and Muslims alike mourned.

## Major theories and concepts

### Negative theology

The *Guide*'s most famous doctrine: we cannot say what God **is**, only what God is **not**. Positive attributes predicated of God in the same sense as of creatures ("wise," "powerful," "existing") would introduce multiplicity into God's absolute unity and simplicity — a wise-part and a power-part — and would compare God with creatures on a shared scale. Even "one" and "exists" are, strictly, equivocal when said of God. What remains legitimate: negations, which do not describe God but progressively strip away imperfections ("God is not weak," "not multiple," "not a body," "not ignorant") and thereby genuinely increase knowledge of what God is not; and "attributes of action," which describe the pattern of God's effects in the world (merciful in outcomes, as rain is "merciful" to fields) without describing his essence. Scripture's anthropomorphisms — "the hand of God," "God was angry" — must therefore be read figuratively; much of *Guide* Part I is a dictionary dissolving literal readings of such terms. The internal tension is obvious and was felt immediately: if negations pile up, do we know *anything* of God, or does negative theology collapse into agnosticism? Maimonides embraces a severe conclusion — the most perfect praise of God is silence, citing "Silence is praise to You" (Psalm 65) — while insisting negations do guide the mind, each one excluding an error and marking intellectual progress.

### Creation versus eternity: reason at its limit

Aristotle's arguments made the world's eternity look demonstrated; Scripture teaches creation. Maimonides' cool verdict (*Guide* II): **neither** eternity **nor** temporal creation is demonstrable by reason — Aristotle himself, he argues, knew his arguments were probable, not demonstrative. Where demonstration fails, Maimonides accepts creation on scriptural grounds, with a frank decision-theoretic twist: Aristotelian necessity (an eternal world produced by God's nature) would undermine miracles, the Law, and divine will, whereas nothing in demonstrated science forbids creation; nor does Scripture have to be read literally where demonstration *does* exist — had eternity been proven, he says, he would have reinterpreted the creation verses as he reinterpreted the anthropomorphisms. Aquinas took over precisely this "undemonstrable either way" verdict: the world's beginning is held by faith, not proof.

### Providence, evil, and the purpose of the Law

Providence, for Maimonides, tracks intellectual perfection, not tribal membership: divine providence over individuals is proportional to their actualized intellect — a view that distances him from comforting orthodoxy and scandalized some readers. Evil is analyzed as privation in the Aristotelian–Neoplatonic manner: most human evils, in his famous threefold division (*Guide* III.12), come from matter and embodiment, from other people's violence, and — the largest share — from our own choices (overindulgence, greed, competition). The Book of Job is read accordingly: Job's comforters voice the standard theories of suffering; the right answer is that our categories of providence do not map onto God's. The Law, then, is rational legislation with comprehensible aims: every commandment has a purpose directed at the welfare of the soul (true beliefs, moral character) and of the body politic (order, justice, restraint of violence) — sacrifices themselves are explained as a divine pedagogy weaning Israel from idolatrous worship habits. Law as moral and political education: this reading shaped all later Jewish rationalism and was studied by the Latins.

### The esotericism problem

The *Guide* is framed as a letter to a student "perplexed" between philosophy and religion, and its introduction announces deliberate contradictions — Maimonides lists seven causes of contradiction in books, the seventh being the necessity, in difficult subjects, of saying in one place what strictness requires unsaying in another. He writes in parables (*mashal*) whose surface and depth diverge, warns the unqualified reader away, and structures the book so its teaching must be assembled. Leo Strauss made this esotericism the center of twentieth-century Maimonides scholarship ("the literary character of the Guide"), reading him as a philosopher concealing radical views from the multitude; other scholars (and most historians of Jewish law) resist Straussian readings as over-systematic, pointing to the *Mishneh Torah*'s open rationalism. The debate is live and shapes every page of contemporary commentary.

## Key works

- **Guide of the Perplexed** (*Dalālat al-Ḥāʾirīn*, completed c. 1190) — three parts: I, language about God and negative theology; II, creation, prophecy, angels; III, providence, evil, and the reasons for the commandments. Translated into Hebrew in his lifetime (Samuel ibn Tibbon, in correspondence with the author), into Latin in the thirteenth century.
- **Mishneh Torah** (c. 1170–80) — fourteen books codifying the whole of Jewish law in lucid Mishnaic Hebrew, requiring no recourse to the Talmud's debates. Its first book, the *Book of Knowledge*, opens with philosophical foundations — God's existence and unity, ethics, study — a manifesto that law begins in metaphysics.
- **Commentary on the Mishnah** (c. 1168) — written in his twenties; its introduction to the tractate *Sanhedrin* contains the famous **Thirteen Principles** of Jewish faith.
- **Eight Chapters** (*Shemonah Perakim*) — the introduction to tractate *Avot* in that commentary: his classic short work on ethics and moral psychology — the health of the soul, virtue as a mean, and free will.
- **Epistles** — including the *Letter to Yemen* (c. 1172), pastoral-political counsel to a persecuted community.

## Contributions

Maimonides gave Judaism both its most complete legal code and its most rigorous philosophical theology — deliberately in that order of public visibility. Philosophically, he produced the most uncompromising negative theology in any of the three monotheisms, a model analysis of the creation/eternity question as a case study in reason's limits, and a naturalistic-yet-reverent theory of prophecy (a perfection of intellect and imagination, with Moses unique). His synthesis of al-Fārābī's political philosophy with rabbinic law set the terms for Jewish thought ever after.

## Impact and legacy

The Latin scholastics knew him as "Rabbi Moyses": Aquinas cites him on creation (adopting the undemonstrable verdict), on the divine names, and on providence; Scotus engages his doctrine of attributes. Within Judaism the reception was stormier — the Maimonidean controversy of the 1230s saw bans proposed against philosophy books in Montpellier and his works denounced to (and burned with the help of) the Dominican Inquisition's apparatus against heresy — yet the *Mishneh Torah* became canonical law while the *Guide* inspired centuries of commentary and, later, fierce opposition from kabbalists and anti-rationalists. Spinoza is his great critic-heir: formed by the *Guide* and breaking with its scriptural hermeneutics. The modern revival — Strauss, and after him a large scholarly industry — made the esotericism debate a fixture of political philosophy too.

## Criticisms and debates

Contemporaries objected that providence proportioned to intellect abandons ordinary believers; that negative theology empties prayer and Scripture of content; and that his natural history of the commandments (sacrifices as anti-idolatry pedagogy) demystifies revelation. Medieval bans and counter-bans show how contested he was inside Judaism. Modern scholarly debates: the depth of his esotericism (Straussian vs. moderate readings), the sincerity/compatibility of the Code's dogmatic Thirteen Principles with the *Guide*'s austere theology, and the contested biography of the Fez years.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That the *Guide* is a systematic theology textbook: it is a deliberately layered letter to one perplexed student, with contradictions the author flags in advance.
- That negative theology means God is unknowable in every sense and talk of God is meaningless: negations and attributes of action do real, ordered work.
- That Maimonides was only a philosopher: to most Jews across history he is first the codifier of the law, "the Great Eagle," and the philosophy is embedded in that vocation.
- That he "proved" creation: he explicitly held it indemonstrable, accepting it on Scripture plus the costs of the alternative.
