# John Scotus Eriugena

*Era: Medieval Philosophy (c. 400 – c. 1400) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 800 – c. 877 (dates are conventional)
- **Place:** Born in Ireland ("Scotus Eriugena" means roughly "the Irishman, born in Ireland"); worked in Francia, at the court of Charles the Bald
- **School/tradition:** Latin Christian Neoplatonism; the outstanding thinker of the Carolingian Renaissance
- **Known for:** The *Periphyseon*, which divides all of "nature" — God and creation together — into four species, and for bringing Greek negative (apophatic) theology into the Latin West

**Responds to:** Pseudo-Dionysius and the Greek Christian Platonists he translated, who have no entry in this collection · **Answered by:** later Latin readers of his Dionysius translation, with no direct successor in this collection

## Biography

Almost nothing is known of Eriugena's early life in Ireland, though his superb Greek — vanishingly rare in the ninth-century West — suggests an unusually good training. By about 845 he was at the court of the West Frankish king Charles the Bald, probably as head of the palace school, where he taught grammar and dialectic (the arts of language and argument basic to the medieval curriculum).

His court career brought him into controversy early. In 851 the archbishop Hincmar of Reims commissioned him to refute the monk Gottschalk, who taught a strict doctrine of double predestination (that God predestines some people to damnation as well as some to salvation). Eriugena's treatise *On Divine Predestination* argued, using dialectical reasoning rather than appeals to authority, that evil and sin have no real being and that God, being one simple good, cannot predestine anyone to evil. The argument scandalized his contemporaries: councils at Valence (855) and Langres (859) condemned the work, and one critic sneered at it as *pultes Scotorum* — "Irish porridge."

Eriugena's lasting importance rests on his second role at court: translator. Charles commissioned him to translate the works of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, a mysterious late-antique author (writing around 500, under the borrowed name of St Paul's Athenian convert) whose writings on the divine names, the celestial hierarchy, and the ascent beyond affirmations into darkness became fundamental to Western mysticism. Eriugena's Latin version (c. 860–862) replaced an earlier, nearly unreadable one, and he went on to translate Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa as well — importing a whole tradition of Greek Christian Platonism into Latin thought.

His masterpiece, the *Periphyseon* ("On Nature," also known by its Latin title *De Divisione Naturae*, "On the Division of Nature"), was written c. 862–866 as a dialogue between a teacher and a pupil. Eriugena disappears from the record after Charles's death in 877. Transmission was troubled: the *Periphyseon* was formally condemned in 1225, when Pope Honorius III ordered copies burned because the heretical Amalricians had drawn pantheist-sounding conclusions from it. Enough manuscripts survived — often anonymously — for it to be recognized today as the most ambitious philosophical system between Augustine and Aquinas.

## Major theories and concepts

### The fourfold division of nature

Eriugena begins with a deliberately vast definition: "nature" (*natura*) is the totality of all things that are and all things that are not — including God and including nothingness. He then divides this totality by two distinctions: whether a thing creates, and whether it is created. The result is four species:

1. **Nature that creates and is not created** — God as source and origin of all things.
2. **Nature that is created and creates** — the "primordial causes," the eternal archetypes or reasons of all things, created by God in his Word (the divine Wisdom) and themselves causally producing the world below.
3. **Nature that is created and does not create** — the world of things in place and time: bodies, souls, the whole spatiotemporal creation, which are effects and not (in the primary sense) causes.
4. **Nature that neither creates nor is created** — God again, now as the end and goal of all things, to which everything returns.

The elegance of the scheme is that God appears twice, as beginning and end, while reality forms a single movement: a *procession* (going-forth, *exitus*) of all things from God through the primordial causes into the temporal world, and a *return* (*reditus*) of all things back into God. The second species is the hinge: the causes are created (so God alone is uncreated) yet genuinely creative (so the world flows from God through intelligible intermediaries rather than being manufactured directly, item by item).

The tension was felt by Eriugena's later readers: if God and creatures together form one "nature," is God a genus containing creatures — and is this pantheism? His defenders reply that the division is a tool for contemplation, not a literal classification, and that God remains beyond every category, including being itself.

### Negative (apophatic) theology in the Latin West

Following Pseudo-Dionysius, Eriugena holds that God cannot be described by any affirmation drawn from creatures. We say God is good, wise, being itself — but each affirmation must then be denied, because God is not good in the way creatures are good, nor a being among beings. More precisely, affirmations are true only "superessentially": God is not *goodness* but *more-than-goodness*, not *being* but *beyond being*. Eriugena pushes this to striking extremes. God can be called "nothing" (*nihil*) — not from lack, but from excellence: he is no *thing*, none of the items that can be defined. Pressed hard, Eriugena even says that God does not know himself as a "what," since self-definition would limit him — a claim that startled medieval and modern readers alike and has to be read within his rigorous logic of negation: every "what" belongs to the created order of definable things.

### Theophanies and the primordial causes

How, then, does an unknowable God create or reveal anything? Eriugena's answer is the doctrine of *theophanies* — divine appearances or manifestations. Everything that exists is, in some measure, a theophany: a showing-forth of the hidden God in a form proportioned to the minds that receive it. The primordial causes are the highest theophanies, formed in the Word; from them proceed the genera and species of the visible world. Creation is thus not an event that happened once, externally, but God's continuous self-manifestation: "God becomes all in all things," as Eriugena repeatedly puts it, while remaining in himself utterly beyond them.

### The return and universal restoration

The cosmic procession is mirrored by a universal return, worked out in Book V. All things return to their causes, nature is progressively unified — body into soul, soul into intellect — and human nature is restored in the resurrection, a doctrine developed from Gregory of Nyssa and Maximus. Drawing on the Greek tradition of *apokatastasis* (universal restoration), Eriugena holds that even punishment is medicinal and bounded: evil, having no substance of its own, cannot survive the return. Scholars note he is not always consistent about how much individual differentiation survives in the final deified state, in which humanity participates in God by grace while God remains transcendent in himself.

## Key works

- **On Divine Predestination** (*De divina praedestinatione*, 851) — the condemned early treatise arguing that God cannot predestine evil, since evil is nothing; a manifesto for the authority of dialectic.
- **Translations of Pseudo-Dionysius** (c. 860–862) — the *Celestial Hierarchy*, *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*, *Divine Names*, and *Mystical Theology*, with Eriugena's own commentary; the channel through which Dionysian mysticism reached the Latin Middle Ages. Followed by translations of Maximus the Confessor's *Ambigua* and Gregory of Nyssa's *On the Making of Man*.
- **Periphyseon (On the Division of Nature)** (c. 862–866) — the masterwork in five books: the fourfold division, the primordial causes, the creation of the world and humanity, and the universal return. One of the most original speculative systems of the early Middle Ages.

## Contributions

Eriugena single-handedly changed what Latin philosophy had to work with. His Dionysius translation defined the vocabulary of Western mystical theology for six centuries, and his versions of Maximus and Gregory transmitted Greek patristic thought otherwise almost unknown in the West. Philosophically, he produced the first great systematic metaphysics of the medieval West — two centuries before Anselm — built on a single unified scheme rather than on commentary. He also modelled a striking confidence in reason, insisting that true authority and right reason can never conflict, since both flow from the same divine wisdom.

## Impact and legacy

The influence ran underground but wide. Eriugena's Dionysius was the standard text commented on by Hugh of St Victor and read by Aquinas, who cites Dionysius constantly (through Eriugena's Latin). Honorius Augustodunensis's epitome, the *Clavis Physicae*, carried his cosmology into the twelfth century, and echoes appear in later medieval Neoplatonism and the Rhineland mystics. The 1225 condemnation reflects how live a threat — or resource — his ideas remained. He is now recognized as the greatest philosopher of the Carolingian age and a key bridge between late-antique Platonism and the high Middle Ages.

## Criticisms and debates

- **Pantheism or not?** The fourfold division, the claim that God "becomes" all things, and the identification of God's being with the being of creatures in the return have led some readers (and the 1225 condemnation) to charge Eriugena with pantheism. Most modern scholars defend him: God's transcendence is protected by the negative theology — God in himself is beyond being and is no thing, so the "one nature" is a contemplative framework, not a claim that God and world are one substance. The debate remains a live one in the scholarship.
- **Universal salvation.** His Origenist-tinged account of the return raises the question whether all rational creatures are ultimately saved, including the devil. Eriugena's texts are genuinely difficult here, and interpreters disagree over how far he went — a debate entangled with his earlier condemnation over predestination.
- **Reason versus authority.** The councils that condemned the predestination treatise objected as much to his method — settling doctrine by dialectic — as to his conclusions. Whether Eriugena subordinated Scripture to philosophy, or rather understood the two as harmonious expressions of one truth, is still discussed.

## Misreadings to avoid

- He is not a straightforward pantheist: his God is "nothing" precisely because he exceeds every being, and the creator/creature distinction is preserved within the scheme.
- "Scotus" here is not John Duns Scotus (four centuries later); *Scotus* was a common label for an Irishman, and "Eriugena" ("Ireland-born") was added to distinguish him.
- The fourfold division is not a classification of ordinary objects into four boxes; the second and fourth species especially are aspects of one divine movement, not separate things.

## What to read first

Start with *Periphyseon* Book I, where the fourfold division is introduced and the negative theology is explained through the categories — it is the clearest door into the system. Then take Book II on the primordial causes, and finally Book V's account of the return. The modern edition and translation by I. P. Sheldon-Williams and J. J. O'Meara (in the Classics of Western Spirituality volume *Periphyseon: On the Division of Nature*) is the standard entry point; pair it with the [*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*](https://plato.stanford.edu/) article for orientation on the debates.
