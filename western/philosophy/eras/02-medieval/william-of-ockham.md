# William of Ockham

*Era: Medieval Philosophy (c. 400 – c. 1400) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 1287 – 1347
- **Place:** Born at Ockham, Surrey; studied at Oxford; spent his later life at the Franciscan convent in Munich under imperial protection.
- **School/tradition:** Latin Christian; Franciscan friar, the "Venerable Inceptor" (*Venerabilis Inceptor* — he completed the requirements for the theology degree but never "incepted" as master, hence the title).
- **Known for:** Nominalism, Ockham's Razor, a powerful theory of logic and mental language, and the separation of faith from demonstration.

**Responds to:** [Duns Scotus](john-duns-scotus.md), [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md) · **Answered by:** the *via moderna* that followed him, with no direct successor in this collection

## Biography

William of Ockham was born around 1287 in the village of Ockham in Surrey and entered the Franciscan Order as a boy. He was educated at the Franciscan house in London and then at Oxford, where he lectured on Peter Lombard's *Sentences* around 1318–19 (his commentary, the *Ordinatio*, is the vehicle of his early system) and completed the requirements for the master's degree — but never took it up, probably because the queue of Franciscan candidates was regulated and politics intervened. His troubles began when his teaching was denounced: John Lutterell, a former Oxford chancellor, extracted dozens of propositions from his writings and carried them to the papal court at Avignon. Summoned there in 1324, Ockham spent four years while a commission examined him (in the end he was never formally condemned). Meanwhile a larger explosion was building: Pope John XXII had condemned the Franciscan doctrine of absolute apostolic poverty — the claim that Christ and the apostles owned nothing, individually or in common — as heretical, an existential threat to the order's identity. In 1328 Ockham, having studied the papal bulls, concluded the pope himself had fallen into heresy; with the Franciscan minister general Michael of Cesena and others, he fled Avignon by night and boat to the court of the pope's enemy, Emperor Louis of Bavaria, in Munich. Excommunicated, he spent his remaining nineteen years writing political philosophy against papal claims of unlimited power (*plenitudo potestatis*): the *Short Discourse on Tyrannical Government*, the vast *Dialogue*, and works on the rights of emperors, subjects, and the church. He died in Munich in 1347; the cause is not recorded — and the traditional guess of plague is unlikely, since the Black Death reached Germany only in 1349. The speculative system — logic, ontology, theology — all predates the flight: two careers in one life.

## Major theories and concepts

### Nominalism: only individuals exist

Ockham's ontology is radical parsimony: everything that exists is individual — this human, this red thing, this act of thinking. There are no universal *things*, neither Platonic forms nor Scotus's common natures, neither outside nor inside individuals. What, then, are universal terms ("human," "redness")? They are **signs**: spoken and written words, and beneath them, **mental terms** in a language of thought. A universal is a sign by its very nature "predicable of many" — it *stands for* (*supponit*) many individuals without being a thing shared among them. "Human" signifies each human directly; its universality is a property of the sign's function, not of an entity. Against Scotus, Ockham presses a dilemma: if the common nature were a real thing in Socrates distinct from Socrates, puzzles multiply (it would be both one and many, both in heaven-bound Socrates and damned Plato) — and in any case God could not make the nature exist without its singularity, so nothing explanatory is gained. Knowledge and science are untouched: science works on signs and propositions, whose truth is grounded in the individuals they stand for.

### The Razor — and what it actually says

The famous slogan "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" (*entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*) is **nowhere verbatim in Ockham's texts** — a later coinage summarizing his constant working principle ("it is futile to do with more what can be done with fewer," in one of his actual formulations). Crucially, as the [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/) emphasizes, the Razor is a rule about **positing**, not a detector of non-existence: it never licenses *denying* that entities exist; at most it justifies *refraining from positing* them without compelling reason — experience, self-evidence, or (for the theologian) Scripture, in Ockham's own formulation of evidence. Its real work in his system is as a tiebreaker within explanatory theory-choice, powered by independent arguments: he rejects universals not because the Razor dislikes them but because concrete dilemmas show they do no explanatory work signs cannot do. And the same parsimony prunes Aristotle's ten categories drastically: only **substance** and **quality** name absolute things; quantity, relation, place, time, and the rest are not extra entities but ways substances and qualities are — "length" is the substance itself extended, "similarity" is two qualities themselves, not a third thing relating them. Much of his physics follows: motion is not a distinct entity but the moving body itself successively in places.

### Logic, supposition, and mental language

The *Summa of Logic* (c. 1323) rebuilds logic around **supposition theory** — a precise account of what a term stands for in a given proposition (the thing itself, the word, or the concept), which dissolves classic puzzles about universals, future contingents, and theological language by showing which reading makes a sentence true or false. Underlying it is the **mental language** hypothesis: beneath spoken and written languages (conventional, variable) lies a universal language of thought whose terms — concepts — are natural signs caused by the things themselves. This makes Ockham's work a bridge to modern philosophy of language: truth, consequence, and meaning are analyzed at the level of mental propositions, and spoken language inherits its logic from thought.

### Intuitive cognition

Ockham's epistemology rests on a distinction he takes from Scotus (see his entry) and makes load-bearing. **Intuitive cognition** is the cognition by virtue of which we judge a thing to exist when it does exist, and not to exist when it does not — the direct awareness of a present singular thing. **Abstractive cognition** leaves existence aside: it is how we think about what a thing is, whether or not it is there. Because only individuals exist, all knowledge of the world begins with intuitive cognition of singulars; concepts and the mental language above are formed from it and answerable to it. It is also Ockham's answer to skepticism: evident knowledge of contingent existence is possible precisely because intuitive cognition is caused by the existing thing itself. Where Scotus uses the distinction mainly within a richer metaphysics of natures, Ockham strips it down to singulars and their direct cognition — the sharpest point of his break with his Franciscan predecessor.

### Faith, reason, and divine power

Ockham separates the domains more sharply than Aquinas: central Christian doctrines — Trinity, Incarnation, the soul's immortality strictly construed — are articles of faith, **not demonstrable** by natural reason; theology is not a science in Aristotle's strict sense. Within philosophy he is correspondingly austere: he allows at most a proof of *a* first conserving cause, and argues God's unity and infinity cannot be strictly demonstrated. Behind this lies his distinction between God's **absolute power** (*potentia absoluta* — everything God *could* do, short of contradiction, considered in itself) and God's **ordained power** (*potentia ordinata* — what God has in fact chosen to establish). Taken one way (the way scholars now generally prefer), this is a distinction between two perspectives on one power, not two powers: its effect is to stress the radical **contingency** of the created order — this world, these natures, these moral arrangements are as they are because God freely chose them, so nature must be investigated empirically rather than deduced from necessities. How strongly this fed early modern empiricism and science is a genuine scholarly debate, not a settled lineage.

### The Munich political works

The poverty controversy radicalized into political theory. Defending the Franciscan thesis that Christ and the apostles held no property, and that the friars' "use without ownership" is coherent, Ockham developed careful analyses of rights (*ius utendi*, rights of use), property, and legitimate government; against papal *plenitudo potestatis* he argued the pope's power is limited by divine and natural law, that a heretical pope can be judged and resisted, and that imperial authority does not derive from the papacy. These works became early fuel for conciliarism, resistance theory, and later rights discourse — the line to Locke is indirect but real, through late scholastic and conciliar channels.

## Key works

- **Summa of Logic** (c. 1323) — the complete rebuild of logic: terms, supposition, propositions, consequences. Part I is the accessible core.
- **Ordinatio** (from his *Sentences* lectures, 1317–19; the author-revised Book I) — nominalism and the parsimony arguments in theological setting.
- **Quodlibetal Questions** (c. 1322–25) — open-floor disputations on anything asked ("quodlibet" = "whatever you please"); lively, wide-ranging.
- **Short Discourse on Tyrannical Government** and the **Dialogue** (1330s–40s) — the Munich political writings against papal plenitude of power.

## Contributions

Ockham produced the most thoroughgoing nominalist system of the Middle Ages, dissolved pseudo-entities across ontology with a single methodological discipline, brought medieval logic to its peak in supposition theory and the mental-language hypothesis, and — in a second, involuntary career — wrote foundational texts of political resistance and rights theory. After him, the scholastic synthesis could not be reassembled in its old form; textbooks conventionally end the medieval era with his generation.

## Impact and legacy

His logic and nominalism drove the *via moderna* that dominated late medieval universities (Buridan in Paris, the Oxford Calculators nearby); his theology shaped late scholastic debates on grace and justification that form part of the Reformation's background (Luther was educated in the via moderna and called himself of Ockham's school). The Razor, detached from its author, became science's favorite methodological slogan — usually in the slogan form he never wrote. Whether nominalism's contingency metaphysics materially caused modern empirical science remains contested among historians; that early modern philosophy inherited his questions — individuals, signs, contingency, the limits of demonstration — is not.

## Criticisms and debates

Scotists and Thomists objected that without common natures, science's universality and the likeness among things become brute or verbal; that the formal distinction he mocks does work his signs cannot. His category-pruning faces the converse charge: relations and quantities do explanatory work (in physics especially) that mere "ways of being" may not recover. Theologians worried that potentia absoluta makes God arbitrary and the world unintelligible — a charge his defenders answer by distinguishing the two "powers" as one power viewed two ways. Textual note: like Scotus, his *Ordinatio* reached posterity in layered states; modern critical editions (the St. Bonaventure *Opera*) undergird current scholarship.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That the Razor proves simpler theories true: it is a rule for not positing without reason, not a truth-machine, and the slogan is not his wording.
- That nominalists denied universal *thought* or meaning: signs are richly meaningful; what is denied is universal *things*.
- That Ockham was a skeptic or proto-atheist: he was a Franciscan theologian for whom faith's claims stand firmer *because* reason is not overclaimed for them.
- That he and Scotus were allies against Aquinas: fellow Franciscans, they were each other's sharpest opponents on universals and distinctions.
