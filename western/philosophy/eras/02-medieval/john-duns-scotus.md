## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 1266 – 1308
- **Place:** Born at Duns, Scotland; studied and taught at Oxford and Paris; died at Cologne.
- **School/tradition:** Latin Christian; Franciscan friar, the "Subtle Doctor" (*Doctor Subtilis*) — head of the Scotist school, scholasticism's second great system after Aquinas.
- **Known for:** Univocity of being, haecceity ("thisness"), the formal distinction, and the primacy of the will.

**Responds to:** [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md), [Avicenna](avicenna.md) · **Answered by:** [William of Ockham](william-of-ockham.md)

## Biography

John Duns Scotus was born around 1266 in Duns, Berwickshire, Scotland ("Duns Scotus" means roughly "John of Duns, the Scot"). He joined the Franciscan Order, probably as a boy, and was ordained priest in 1291. His education ran through the Franciscan studia to Oxford, where by the late 1290s he was lecturing on Peter Lombard's *Sentences* — the standard theology textbook — producing the early *Lectura* (lectures as delivered) and beginning the *Ordinatio*, his own revised, unfinished edition of those lectures. In 1302 he went to Paris, then the capital of theology, but in 1303 he was exiled with other foreign Franciscans for refusing to side with King Philip IV against Pope Boniface VIII in their quarrel; he returned when the political weather changed, completed his Paris lectures (preserved in student reports, the *Reportatio*), and became regent master at Paris in 1305. In 1307 he was transferred to the Franciscan house at Cologne — the order moved its star teachers — and died there in November 1308, aged about forty-two. He left no polished *Summa*: his masterwork is a lecture commentary he was still revising, full of interventions marked "up to here" and passages left rough, assembled posthumously by editors. The density and precision of the argument — distinctions layered on distinctions in service of exactness — earned him the title *Doctor Subtilis* and a reputation for difficulty that is entirely deserved and entirely worth pushing through with help. Late works include the *Treatise on First Principle* (*De Primo Principio*, c. 1307–08), his most self-contained piece of writing.

## Major theories and concepts

### Univocity of being

Scotus's most consequential break with Aquinas. Aquinas held that terms like "being," "good," and "wise" apply to God and creatures **analogically** — related but different senses. Scotus objects: for any theological inference to be valid, and for us to have any *certain* concept of God at all, there must be a concept of being that is **univocal** — one and the same concept — as applied to God and creatures, infinite and finite. His test: a concept is univocal if affirming and denying it of the same subject yields a contradiction, and if it can serve as the middle term of a syllogism without equivocation. If "being" meant something wholly different in "God is a being" and "Socrates is a being," every argument in natural theology would commit the fallacy of equivocation, and the pagan philosophers who (Scotus notes) were certain God exists while doubting whether he is finite or infinite, created or uncreated, must have had one neutral concept under dispute. Univocity does *not* mean God and creatures are beings in the same *way*: being is contracted to God by the intrinsic mode of **infinity** (an intrinsic mode is a degree or manner internal to the concept itself, which modifies it without changing what the concept is), to creatures by finitude — the concept is common, the modes are not. Infinity, not simplicity, becomes the master divine attribute: God is *infinite being*. Many historians call this the hinge between medieval and modern metaphysics: being becomes a single neutral field that God and world both occupy — a precondition, arguably, for modern ontology, and the point where Heidegger and Deleuze later claimed him.

### Common nature, haecceity, and individuation

Scotus is a realist about universals, but a fine-grained one, adapting Avicenna. Humanity in Socrates and Plato is a **common nature**: really the same kind of thing in both, with a unity "less than numerical" — not a Platonic separate entity, and not a mere word either; it exists in individuals, contracted to each. But then what makes Socrates *this* one, numerically distinct from Plato though sharing the nature? Not matter (Aquinas's answer, which Scotus finds insufficient — matter is itself common in kind), and not quantity or any qualitative property. The individuating principle is a non-qualitative, positive entity: **haecceity** (*haecceitas*, "thisness") — the ultimate reality of the individual, contracting the common nature to *this*. Contrast *quiddity* ("whatness": what a thing is, shareable) with haecceity (the thisness that is incommunicable). Example: two perfectly identical billiard balls, or identical twins — no list of qualities distinguishes them, yet they are two; haecceity names that irreducible individuality, and it gives the individual a metaphysical dignity that later traditions (and poets like Gerard Manley Hopkins, who read Scotus) celebrated.

### The formal distinction

Scotus's signature tool: between the common nature and its haecceity — and likewise between God's attributes (wisdom and goodness in God), and between the divine persons' properties — there holds a **formal distinction** (*distinctio formalis*): not a real distinction (the items are not separable things; you cannot have the nature without a thisness, nor divine wisdom without divine goodness) and not a merely mental distinction (it is not invented by our thinking; it obtains in the thing itself, *a parte rei*). The items are formally non-identical — the definition of one does not include the other — while being really inseparable. This middle-grade distinction lets Scotus say how the Trinity and the attributes can be genuinely distinct in content yet one in being, and how metaphysics can track real structure finer than thinghood. Critics from Ockham onward charged that it is a distinction without a difference — a pseudo-entity multiplied to patch problems; defenders reply that collapsing it flattens real differences (wisdom and goodness do not *mean* the same in God even if inseparable). It remains the most debated of his devices.

### Intuitive and abstractive cognition

Scotus divides cognition of objects into two kinds. **Intuitive cognition** is cognition of a thing as present and existing — seeing *this* white wall, here and now. **Abstractive cognition** considers what a thing is while prescinding from whether it exists or is present — thinking about whiteness, or about a unicorn. The distinction does heavy lifting. Only intuitive cognition puts us in contact with existence itself, so it alone grounds certain knowledge that a contingent thing *is*: everything we know about what exists starts there. It also underwrites his account of contingency — the intellect can consider an object abstractively, in its possibilities, while the will acts on what is intuitively present — and it sets the terms Ockham inherits and sharpens (see his entry).

### Will, freedom, and morality

With Aquinas, Scotus contrasts the intellect as a "natural" power (determined to its object, like fire to heating) and the **will** as the only genuinely *rational*, self-determining power: nothing other than the will is the total cause of volition — the intellect presents objects, but the will freely determines itself, and at the very moment it wills A it retains the real power to will not-A (synchronic contingency — the power to do otherwise at the very moment of acting, not merely before or after — a modal innovation underpinning his modal metaphysics). In ethics, the will has two inclinations: the *affectio commodi* (toward one's own advantage and happiness) and the *affectio iustitiae* (toward justice, loving things for their own sake) — freedom consists in the second's ability to moderate the first. Morally, Scotus is a qualified **voluntarist**: many moral norms (especially the second table of the Ten Commandments) depend on God's free willing and could have been otherwise, because God is bound by no standard outside himself; but the first principles — God is to be loved, nothing else is to be worshipped — are necessary truths God could not contradict, since they concern God's own goodness. So morality is neither arbitrary divine command nor a fully independent order: it is necessarily anchored in God's nature and contingently extended by God's will. His modal proof of God in the *Treatise on First Principle* builds from possible production to an actually existing, unproducible first efficient cause, shown also to be final end and most eminent — and finally *infinite*, completing the univocity framework.

## Key works

- **Ordinatio** (c. 1300–07, unfinished) — his revised Oxford lectures on the *Sentences*; the main repository of the mature system. Read in selections, with a guide.
- **Lectura** — the Oxford lectures as delivered; often clearer, sometimes less developed.
- **Reportatio** — student reports of the Paris lectures; valuable where the *Ordinatio* is incomplete.
- **Treatise on First Principle** (*De Primo Principio*, c. 1307–08) — the modal proof of an infinite first principle; short, self-contained, his best entry point.
- **Questions on the Metaphysics** — incisive treatment of being and univocity.

## Contributions

Scotus re-founded metaphysics on univocal being and modal possibility, gave the individual metaphysical standing through haecceity, forged a new grade of distinction, and built the most sophisticated medieval account of free will — a parallel system to Thomism, point-by-point different (analogy vs univocity, intellect vs will, matter vs haecceity as individuator). The Scotist school institutionalized the system; Franciscan theology ran on it for centuries.

## Impact and legacy

Scotism became one of the two great scholastic systems (Thomism its rival), taught in universities into the seventeenth century and kept alive in the Franciscan order; Suárez and through him early modern metaphysics absorbed Scotist elements wholesale (univocity and modal theory reach Descartes and Leibniz partly by this channel). Modern metaphysics' possible-worlds idiom has deep Scotist roots (his synchronic contingency). Deleuze celebrated univocity as philosophy's great emancipation; Heidegger's early career began with a habilitation on (what he took to be) Scotus; Hopkins turned haecceity into poetry of "inscape." In Catholic theology he is also remembered for his defense of the Immaculate Conception — a position long contested, dogmatised in 1854; he was beatified in 1993.

## Criticisms and debates

Ockham attacked the formal distinction and common natures as unnecessary multiplications of entities — the razor was aimed substantially at Scotus. Thomists then and now hold that univocity flattens the creator–creature difference and seeds modern "onto-theology." Scholars debate how voluntarist Scotus really is (the necessary first principles bound the divine will significantly), whether the formal distinction is coherent, and — a textual problem — what exactly he wrote, given the *Ordinatio*'s unfinished, editorially layered state (the Vatican critical edition, begun in 1950, is still the foundation of serious work).

## Misreadings to avoid

- That Scotus is "Aquinas but more complicated": he disagreed with Aquinas on univocity vs analogy, will vs intellect, and individuation — hence rival schools, not a footnote.
- That univocity makes God one being among others: the concept is common, but the mode — infinity — is not; God is not in a genus with creatures.
- That "voluntarism" means morality is God's arbitrary whim: the first principles are necessary; Scotus's contingency applies within that anchor.
- That haecceity is a hidden quality or label: it is precisely non-qualitative — the thisness no description captures.
