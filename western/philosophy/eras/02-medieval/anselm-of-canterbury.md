# Anselm of Canterbury

*Era: Medieval Philosophy (c. 400 – c. 1400) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1033 – 1109
- **Place:** Born in Aosta (Piedmont, Italy); monk and abbot of Bec in Normandy; Archbishop of Canterbury from 1093.
- **School/tradition:** Latin Christian; Benedictine monk in the Augustinian tradition; often called the founder of scholasticism.
- **Known for:** The ontological argument for God's existence and the satisfaction theory of the atonement.

**Responds to:** [Augustine](augustine-of-hippo.md) · **Answered by:** [Descartes](../03-early-modern/rene-descartes.md), [Kant](../03-early-modern/immanuel-kant.md)

## Biography

Anselm was born in 1033 in Aosta, in the Italian Alps, to a Lombard noble family. His pious mother drew him toward religion; his worldly father opposed a monastic vocation, and after a period of youthful wandering — studying in Burgundy and France and living, by his own later account, carelessly — Anselm arrived at the abbey of Bec in Normandy, drawn by the fame of its prior, Lanfranc, the leading scholar-teacher of the day. He entered the monastery in 1060, succeeded Lanfranc as prior and then as abbot (1078), and made Bec a center of learning. It was in these monastic years that he wrote his philosophical works, as meditations for his monks: the *Monologion* (1076), an attempt to prove God's existence and attributes by reason alone without quoting Scripture, and the *Proslogion* (1077–78), which reduced that whole investigation to a single argument he had been seeking — one consideration, he says, that by itself suffices.

When Lanfranc, by then Archbishop of Canterbury, died, Anselm was drafted as his successor in 1093 — he claimed, with evident sincerity, against his will. His archiepiscopate was consumed by conflict with two kings, William Rufus and Henry I, over the rights of the church: royal control of appointments (investiture), church revenues, and obedience to the pope. Twice Anselm went into exile (1097–1100, 1103–1106), traveling to Rome and writing, in the first exile, his *Cur Deus Homo* ("Why God Became Man"). He returned, reached a settlement on investiture, and died at Canterbury in 1109. His works were preserved by his monks and his biographer Eadmer; he was canonized in 1494 and named a Doctor of the Church in 1720.

## Major theories and concepts

### The ontological argument

The *Proslogion* is a prayer, and the argument unfolds inside it. Anselm addresses God: we believe you are "something than which nothing greater can be conceived" (*aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit*). Even the fool of the Psalms, who says in his heart "there is no God," understands this phrase when he hears it — so that-than-which-nothing-greater exists at least in the understanding. But suppose it existed *only* in the understanding. Then we could conceive of something greater: the same being existing in reality as well. That is a contradiction — the greatest conceivable being would not be the greatest conceivable. Therefore it exists in reality too (chapter 2). Chapter 3 adds the modal twist: a being that cannot be conceived *not* to exist is greater than one that can be conceived not to exist; so the greatest conceivable being exists necessarily — it cannot even be thought away. Chapter 4 then explains how the fool can say "there is no God" at all: one can utter the words without genuinely conceiving the thing.

The argument's frame matters: "faith seeking understanding" (*fides quaerens intellectum*, the *Proslogion*'s original title). Anselm is not a missionary trying to force assent on unbelievers by logic; he is a believer asking to understand what he already believes, and reasoning from within the concept of God that faith supplies. Internal tensions: does "existing in reality" function like a greatness-making property? Is the concept itself coherent? These became the whole later debate.

### Gaunilo's reply and the perfect island

Within Anselm's lifetime, the monk Gaunilo of Marmoutiers answered *In Behalf of the Fool*: parody the reasoning with a "lost island" more excellent than all other lands — if we can conceive it, by Anselm's logic it must exist, or a greater (existing) island could be conceived; but plainly it need not exist, so the argument form must be flawed. Anselm appended a reply, and insisted the two texts always be copied together. His counter: the argument works only for that-than-which-nothing-greater, whose non-existence is inconceivable; an island, however excellent, is the kind of thing that can always be conceived not to exist and can always be conceived greater — no contradiction arises. Whether the reply succeeds — whether God is uniquely positioned so that the argument applies only there — remains the crux of modern discussion.

### Satisfaction theory of the atonement

*Cur Deus Homo* asks, by reasoned dialogue (Anselm's student Boso voices the objections), why the Incarnation and death of Christ were necessary rather than arbitrary. The feudal-flavoured model: sin is failing to render God the honor due to him; the offense is measured by the dignity of the one offended, so humanity's debt is infinite, and mere repentance does not restore the order of justice — satisfaction must be made, or punishment follow. No mere human can make proportionate satisfaction for an infinite debt; God owes nothing and can give freely. Only a God-man — fully God, fully human — can both owe (as man) and pay (as God). The Incarnation is thus shown to be rationally "fitting," indeed necessary given God's purposes. Tensions: does this bind God to a justice above himself? Anselm answers that God's honor is God's own order and God cannot act against himself — but critics from the twelfth century (Abelard emphasized instead the cross as a demonstration of love awakening love in us) to today debate whether the model makes God look vindictive or the necessity genuine.

### Method: faith seeking understanding, reason alone

Anselm's methodological novelty: in the *Monologion* he undertakes to establish the central doctrines "by reason alone" (*sola ratione*), bracketing scriptural authority — not to replace faith but to display its intelligibility. This disciplined separation of the argument from appeals to authority, pursued inside a monastic culture of prayer, is why Anselm is credited with founding scholasticism, the university tradition of the next centuries: theology done by question, objection, and rational resolution.

## Signature arguments

**The ontological argument (*Proslogion* II)**

1. God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived — a being even the fool who denies God understands when he hears the phrase.
2. That than which nothing greater can be conceived therefore exists at least in the understanding.
3. To exist in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than to exist in the understanding alone.
4. Suppose it existed only in the understanding: then a greater could be conceived, namely the same being also existing in reality.
Therefore, that than which nothing greater can be conceived cannot exist only in the understanding; God exists in reality.

**Objection.** Gaunilo's lost island parodies the form: take an island more excellent than all other lands, existing in the understanding; by the same reasoning it must exist in reality, or a greater, really existing island could be conceived — yet plainly it need not exist. If the reasoning proves too much, something in its form must be wrong.

**Reply.** Anselm replies that the cases are not parallel. An island, however excellent, is the kind of thing that can always be conceived to be greater than any island so far conceived, and can without contradiction be conceived not to exist at all, so the reasoning never gets a grip on it. His argument is claimed to work uniquely for that than which nothing greater can be conceived, whose non-existence is supposed to be inconceivable. Whether that uniqueness claim succeeds, or simply asserts what the parody disputes, remains the crux of the debate.

**The necessary-existence argument (*Proslogion* III)**

1. A being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than a being that can be conceived not to exist.
2. Suppose that than which nothing greater can be conceived could be conceived not to exist.
3. Then a greater could be conceived — one that cannot be conceived not to exist — which contradicts its being that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
Therefore, that than which nothing greater can be conceived so truly exists that it cannot even be conceived not to exist: God exists necessarily.

**Objection.** Kant's later objection is that existence is not a real predicate or perfection: the concept of a thing — his example is a hundred thalers — is not enlarged by adding that it exists, so no amount of analysis of a concept, however exalted, can settle whether anything answers to it. On this view, *Proslogion* II's crucial step, treating existing in reality as a greatness, is a mistake, and the same mistake carries into the modal version.

**Reply.** The standard reply on Anselm's behalf is that the point at issue is not bare existence but *necessary* existence: Anselm is not adding a property to a list, but arguing that a being whose non-existence is inconceivable cannot be treated as one more contingent item whose concept leaves reality open. Defenders add that Kant's thalers are contingent things, where the reply concedes his point. Scholars debate whether this answers Kant — a critic may grant the concept of necessary existence while denying that anything instantiates it, or that the concept is even coherent — so the exchange is best presented as an open dispute, not a settled verdict either way.

## Key works

- **Monologion** (c. 1076) — a "meditation" proving God's existence and attributes by reason alone; the long way round that the *Proslogion* sought to replace with one argument.
- **Proslogion** (1077–78) — chapters 2–4 contain the ontological argument; traditionally printed together with Gaunilo's critique and Anselm's reply.
- **On Truth**, **On Free Will**, **On the Fall of the Devil** (c. 1080s) — dialogues clarifying truth as "rectitude," freedom as the power to keep rectitude for its own sake.
- **Cur Deus Homo** (c. 1094–98) — the satisfaction theory, worked out in exile; his most influential theological work in the Middle Ages.

## In their own words

> Hence, even the fool is convinced that something exists in the understanding, at least, than which nothing greater can be conceived. For, when he hears of this, he understands it. And whatever is understood, exists in the understanding. And assuredly that, than which nothing greater can be conceived, cannot exist in the understanding alone. For, suppose it exists in the understanding alone: then it can be conceived to exist in reality; which is greater. Therefore, if that, than which nothing greater can be conceived, exists in the understanding alone, the very being, than which nothing greater can be conceived, is one, than which a greater can be conceived. But obviously this is impossible. Hence, there is no doubt that there exists a being, than which nothing greater can be conceived, and it exists both in the understanding and in reality.

— Anselm, *Proslogion* II, trans. Sidney Norton Deane (1903; public domain)

Here is the ontological argument in its original form. Watch the engine: God is defined as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," and Anselm then argues that existing in reality is greater than existing only in the mind — so a God who existed only in the mind would not be the greatest conceivable being, a contradiction. The "fool" is the atheist of Psalm 14, whom Anselm traps using nothing but the fool's own understanding of the word "God."

## Contributions

Anselm produced the single most discussed argument in the history of philosophy — a two-page proof that has occupied Aquinas, Descartes, Leibniz, Kant, and Plantinga — and a theory of the atonement that structured Western soteriology for centuries. Methodologically, he pioneered arguing "by reason alone" within theology and the tightly dialogic, objection-driven style that scholasticism institutionalized.

## Impact and legacy

Aquinas rejected the ontological argument (we do not know God's essence, so his existence is not self-evident *to us*) while respecting it; Descartes revived a version, building existence into the idea of a supremely perfect being; Leibniz tried to repair it by proving the concept of God possible; Kant's famous objection — existence is not a real predicate, so a thing's concept is unchanged whether or not it exists (a hundred real thalers contain not a coin more than a hundred possible ones) — was long treated as fatal. In the twentieth century, Hartshorne, Malcolm, and Plantinga revived the argument in modal form: if a maximally great being is *possibly* instantiated, then (in standard modal logic) it exists necessarily — shifting the debate onto the possibility premise. The satisfaction theory shaped Catholic and later Protestant accounts of the cross (the Reformers adapted it into penal substitution). Anselm is also the source of the classic definitions later thinkers work with: God as that-than-which-nothing-greater, freedom as power for rectitude.

## Criticisms and debates

Beyond Gaunilo and Kant: does the argument illicitly move from concept to reality? Is "greater" well-defined across the mind/reality boundary? Plantinga himself concedes his modal version is rationally acceptable rather than coercive. The satisfaction theory has been criticized since Abelard for foregrounding honor and debt over love, and contemporary theologians debate whether its feudal imagery distorts its deeper claim about the costliness of forgiveness. Scholars also debate how to read the *Proslogion* — proof, prayer, or a prayer that is a proof — with consequences for what would count as refuting it.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That the ontological argument is a cheap word-trick, "defining God into existence": Anselm's logic turns on *necessary* existence and the unique status of the greatest conceivable; serious opponents have engaged it for 900 years.
- That the perfect-island parody simply refutes it: Anselm's reply — islands can always be conceived not to exist and always exceeded; the argument is claimed to work only where non-existence is inconceivable — is part of the text and must be answered too.
- That Anselm wrote as an evangelist to atheists: the *Proslogion* is addressed to God, by a believer seeking understanding.

1. *Proslogion* chapters 1–4, together with Gaunilo's *In Behalf of the Fool* and Anselm's reply — the exchange, a few pages total, is the real lesson (Davies & Evans, *Anselm: The Major Works*, Oxford).
2. *Cur Deus Homo* Book I, selected chapters, for the satisfaction theory's logic.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct *Proslogion* II premise by premise: God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived; that being exists in the understanding; existing in reality is greater. Identify exactly which step a critic should attack, and why.
2. State Gaunilo's lost-island parody at full strength, then Anselm's reply — an island can always be conceived greater and can be conceived not to exist, unlike that than which nothing greater can be conceived. Does the reply prove the argument's unique application, or merely assert it?
3. Compare chapter 3's necessary-existence version with chapter 2's: what extra claim does it add, and does recasting greatness as necessary existence escape Kant's objection that existence is not a real predicate?
4. Apply the satisfaction theory to a restorative-justice scheme that cancels a wrongdoer's debt without payment: what, for Anselm, remains unsatisfied, and how would Abelard's love-centered alternative answer?
