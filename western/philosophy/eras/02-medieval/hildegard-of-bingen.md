## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1098 – 1179
- **Place:** Bermersheim in the Rhineland, Germany; head of the women's community at Disibodenberg, then founder and abbess of Rupertsberg near Bingen
- **School/tradition:** Benedictine monasticism; visionary theology, natural philosophy, and medicine in the monastic tradition
- **Known for:** The visionary trilogy beginning with *Scivias*, her concept of *viriditas* ("greening power"), among the largest surviving bodies of medieval monophonic song attributed to one composer, and practicing natural philosophy and medicine as a twelfth-century abbess

**Responds to:** [Plato](../01-ancient/plato.md) · **Answered by:** no direct successor in this collection — her influence was regional in her lifetime and her wider revival came much later

## Biography

Hildegard was born in 1098 at Bermersheim, the tenth child of a noble Rhineland family — a fact her family read religiously: as a "tithe" child she was offered to the Church, and at about the age of eight she became an oblate — a child dedicated to religious life; she later followed Jutta of Sponheim, a noblewoman living as an anchoress walled into a cell attached to the church of the Benedictine monastery of Disibodenberg, joining her anchorhold there (from 1112, when Hildegard was about fourteen). There Hildegard learned Latin, the psalms, and the monastic round of prayer; by her own account she received only a modest formal education, without training in the dialectical techniques of the schools — a limitation she later turned into a credential: her knowledge, she insisted, came not from human teaching but from vision.

The visions began in early childhood. "In the third year of my age," she wrote, "I saw so great a light that my soul trembled." For most of her life she kept them private, sharing them only with Jutta and with the monk Volmar, who became her lifelong secretary. In 1141, at the age of forty-two, she experienced a decisive vision — a fiery light that, she says, poured into her brain and instructed her to write down what she saw and heard. When Jutta died in 1136, Hildegard had already been elected head of the small community of nuns at Disibodenberg; now, encouraged by Volmar and emboldened by her command vision, she began the work that became *Scivias*.

Writing as a woman — and as one formally barred from the priesthood, preaching office, and university teaching — required authorization, and Hildegard obtained it at the highest level. Her abbot hesitated; her correspondent Bernard of Clairvaux, the most influential churchman in Europe, interceded; and at the Synod of Trier in 1147–48, Pope Eugenius III had extracts of *Scivias* read aloud and approved her writing. This papal endorsement was the licence on which her entire public career rested.

In about 1150 she took the bolder step of founding her own monastery at Rupertsberg, near Bingen, moving her nuns against the strong opposition of the Disibodenberg monks; a second foundation at Eibingen followed in 1165. From Rupertsberg she conducted an enormous correspondence with popes, emperors (including Frederick Barbarossa, whom she did not hesitate to rebuke), and abbots; undertook four preaching tours through the Rhineland and beyond — public preaching by a woman was almost unheard of — and wrote continuously: theology, natural philosophy, medicine, music, and even an invented language. She died at Rupertsberg on 17 September 1179. Although local veneration was immediate, formal recognition took centuries: she was canonized and named a Doctor of the Church only in 2012, by Benedict XVI.

## Major theories and concepts

### Visionary knowledge and the "living light"

Hildegard's epistemology — her account of how she knows — is foundational to everything else. She describes seeing her visions not with the outer eye or in dreams but "in my soul," awake, in what she calls "the shade of the living light" (*umbra viventis lucis*): a constant luminous presence within which more intense visions occasionally appear. This matters in two ways. First, it gives her a source of knowledge bypassing the all-male institutions of learning: her authority is charismatic and divine, not academic, and she foregrounds her status as an unlearned "poor little female figure" (*paupercula feminea forma*) precisely because it certifies that the teaching is God's, not hers. Second, her visions are not formless ecstasies: each is a structured, symbolic tableau — a cosmic egg, a tower of the virtues — which an accompanying voice then interprets allegorically. Vision supplies the image; interpretation supplies the doctrine. Scholars debate how the process worked in practice (Volmar and later secretaries plainly helped shape the Latin), but the claim itself shaped how a medieval woman could do theology at all.

### Viriditas — the greening power

Hildegard's most characteristic natural-philosophical concept is *viriditas*, literally "greenness" or "greening power": the living, moist, generative vitality that God pours into creation and that flows through plants, animals, and human souls alike. A plant that loses its *viriditas* withers; a soul that loses it dries into sin and despair; the earth itself, she warns, can be wounded by human misuse and lose its greening freshness. The concept bridges her medicine, her theology, and her ethics: health — physical and spiritual — is participation in this divinely sustained vitality, and healing means restoring it. It is not a scientific theory in the modern sense, but a unifying principle that makes the natural and spiritual orders continuous with each other, which is exactly how Hildegard thought they were.

### Cosmos and microcosm

In *Scivias* and especially in her last visionary work, the *Book of Divine Works*, Hildegard presents a cosmology in which the universe — pictured first as an egg, later as a great wheel containing a human figure with outstretched arms — and the human being correspond part for part. The elements (fire, air, water, earth) that compose the cosmos are the same elements whose balance composes the human body; the winds that move the heavens have counterparts in the humors that move the body. This microcosm–macrocosm scheme was standard medieval inheritance (from Plato's *Timaeus* through the medical tradition), but Hildegard works it with unusual thoroughness, making the human being the center and summary of creation — wounded by the Fall, which she pictures as a literal disordering of the elements, and restored through the Incarnation.

### Natural philosophy and medicine

Hildegard's *Physica* (Book of Simple Medicine) catalogues plants, trees, animals, stones, and metals with their properties and medical uses, while *Causae et Curae* (Causes and Cures) explains disease through the interaction of the humors, the elements, and the patient's constitution, framed by the fall of Adam. What distinguishes her from a mere compiler is the amount of local, practical, observational material from the Rhineland — remedies, fishing and farming lore, the properties of regional plants and stones — recorded alongside inherited lore. Her medicine remains humoral and theological in framework (illness is never merely mechanical), but it represents empirical monastic medicine: knowledge gathered by a community that actually treated the sick.

## Key works

- **Scivias** (1141–1151) — "Know the Ways [of the Lord]": twenty-six visions in three books covering creation and fall, redemption, and the virtues and the Church; the work that made her famous, famous also for its extraordinary illuminations.
- **Symphonia armonie celestium revelationum** ("Symphony of the Harmony of Celestial Revelations," compiled c. 1150s) — some seventy-seven chants for the liturgy, among the largest surviving bodies of medieval monophonic song attributed to one composer, plus the *Ordo Virtutum*, a sung morality play of the Virtues contending for a soul — the earliest surviving morality play.
- **Physica** (*Liber simplicis medicinae*, c. 1151–1158) — nine books on the healing properties of the natural world.
- **Causae et Curae** (*Liber compositae medicinae*, c. 1151–1158) — the causes and cures of diseases, integrating cosmology, humoral theory, and practical remedies. (The transmitted text may be a compilation from her materials assembled by others; its authorship has been questioned.)
- **Liber Vitae Meritorum** (Book of Life's Merits, 1158–1163) — a visionary dialogue of virtues and vices on moral choice and its consequences.
- **Liber Divinorum Operum** (Book of Divine Works, 1163–1173/74) — her final, most ambitious visionary work: cosmology, the human being as microcosm, history, and last things.
- **Letters (*Epistolarium*)** — correspondence with popes, emperors, and abbots, including the rebukes to Barbarossa; some 300 letters survive.
- **Lingua ignota** — an invented language of roughly a thousand words with its own alphabet, the earliest known constructed language attributed to a named author; its purpose is still debated.

## Contributions

Hildegard expanded the range of who could be a public intellectual in the twelfth century: she is the first medieval woman to produce a large, authorized body of theological writing in her own name, and she did it by developing a new legitimation strategy — visionary authority — that turned her exclusion from the schools into proof of divine origin. In natural philosophy she left the most substantial body of medical and natural writing by a medieval woman and an unusually observational record of regional natural knowledge. In music she composed on a scale no contemporary matched among named composers. Her concept of *viriditas* gave the tradition a durable image of creation as living, interconnected, and morally charged.

## Impact and legacy

Within the Middle Ages her influence was real but regional: her prophecies were consulted, her medical works circulated in the Rhineland, and her reputation as a prophet ("the Sibyl of the Rhine") grew after her death. The revival came in stages — Romantic interest in her music and visions, twentieth-century recordings that made her chants globally famous, feminist scholarship that recovered her as a thinker rather than merely a mystic, and growing attention to her natural writings in histories of science and environmental thought. Her proclamation as a Doctor of the Church in 2012 — one of only a handful of women so honored — formalized her standing, and she is now routinely included in histories of medieval philosophy that once omitted women entirely.

## Criticisms and debates

- **How did visionary authority work?** Historians debate the mechanics of Hildegard's licence to speak. One reading stresses strategy: the rhetoric of female weakness (*paupercula feminea forma*) was a calculated humility topos that disarmed opposition. Another stresses sincerity and institutional reality: papal approval at Trier was a genuine theological judgment, and Hildegard's own sense of compulsion by the vision was real. Most scholarship now holds the two together — an authentic religious experience articulated through the rhetorical forms available to her.
- **Were the visions migraines?** A famous modern suggestion (popularized by Oliver Sacks) holds that the scintillating lights in Hildegard's illuminations record migraine auras. Physicians and historians remain divided: some features match, but critics note the visions' structured doctrinal content fits poorly with a purely neurological reduction. The debate says as much about modern categories as about Hildegard.
- **Philosopher, theologian, or mystic?** Older histories excluded her because her works are visionary rather than argumentative in form. The counter-argument — now widely accepted — is that her cosmology, epistemology of vision, and natural philosophy are philosophy in a monastic key, comparable to the allegorical and contemplative modes used by respected male contemporaries.

## Misreadings to avoid

- She was not an isolated, uneducated visionary: she ran two monasteries, managed estates and lawsuits, advised rulers, and built her works over decades with trained secretaries.
- *Viriditas* is not modern environmentalism in advance; it is a theological principle about divine vitality in creation — suggestive for ecological thought today, but rooted in twelfth-century cosmology and humoral medicine.
- Her medicine was not modern herbalism either: remedies sit inside a humoral and theological framework, and some attributions in the printed editions were shaped by later editors.

## What to read first

Begin with *Scivias* Book I, visions 1–3 — the call vision and the creation visions show her method at its clearest — best in the Classics of Western Spirituality translation (*Scivias*, trans. Columba Hart and Jane Bishop), using the illuminations alongside the text. Then read selections from the *Physica* (Book I, on plants) to see the natural philosopher at work, and listen to any chant from the *Symphonia* alongside its text: Hildegard insisted the music itself was part of the revelation. The [*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*](https://plato.stanford.edu/) entry gives a compact scholarly orientation to the debates.
