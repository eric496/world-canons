## At a glance

- **Dates:** 354 – 430 CE
- **Place:** Born in Thagaste (Numidia, modern Algeria); educated in Carthage and Rome; taught rhetoric in Milan; bishop of Hippo Regius from 396 until his death.
- **School/tradition:** Latin Christian, writing in the Roman North African church as the Western Empire collapsed around him.
- **Known for:** Original sin and grace, the divided will, the two cities, and a philosophy of time and memory that still anchors modern discussions.

**Responds to:** [Plato](../01-ancient/plato.md), [Plotinus](../01-ancient/plotinus.md) · **Answered by:** [Anselm](anselm-of-canterbury.md), [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md)

## Biography

Augustine was born in 354 in Thagaste, a small town in Roman North Africa, to a pagan father (Patricius, who converted late) and a devout Christian mother, Monica, whose persistence runs through his story. He was a brilliant, ambitious student who disliked the beatings of the local school and loved Latin literature — Virgil above all. Sent to Carthage at seventeen to study rhetoric, the standard passport to a career, he took a concubine (a long-term partner he could not legally marry because of class), fathered a son, Adeodatus, and fell in with the Manichaeans, a dualist sect that promised rational answers about evil: the world, they said, is a battleground between a good God of light and an evil power of darkness, so God is not responsible for evil and neither, quite, are we. Augustine stayed a Manichaean "hearer" for about nine years.

The sect's intellectual promises crumbled. Astronomy's predictions outclassed Manichaean cosmology; the famous Manichaean bishop Faustus, when he finally arrived, proved eloquent but shallow. Augustine crossed to Italy, taught rhetoric in Rome, and in 384 won the prestigious municipal chair of rhetoric in Milan — effectively the imperial court's official speechwriter. There he heard Bishop Ambrose preach, and Ambrose's allegorical reading of Scripture removed his intellectual blockage: the Bible was not the crude text he had assumed. Simultaneously, friends introduced him to "the books of the Platonists" — Plotinus and Porphyry in Latin translation — which gave him a non-materialist picture of God and the soul, and a way to think of evil not as a substance but as a turning away. What the Platonists could not give him was humility and a healed will: he knew the good and could not do it. His conversion, narrated in Book 8 of the *Confessions* through the famous garden scene ("take up and read," *tolle lege*), joined intellect to will through grace. He was baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387, alongside Adeodatus.

Monica died on the journey home; Adeodatus died soon after. Augustine returned to Thagaste, sold his property, and formed a small monastic community. In 391, visiting Hippo, he was famously seized by the congregation and ordained a priest against his plans; in 396 he became bishop of Hippo, a post he held for 34 years of preaching, judging disputes, administering charity, and writing at astonishing volume — sermons, letters, biblical commentaries, and the great controversial works against the Donatists (a North African schismatic church), the Manichaeans, and finally Pelagius, a British monk who taught that humans can choose the good by unaided free will — a claim Augustine came to see as destroying the need for grace. His last years were shadowed by the Vandal invasion of North Africa; he died in 430 with Hippo under siege, the penitential psalms posted on his wall. His library and writings were preserved and shipped out, which is why we have so much of him: about five million words survive.

## Major theories and concepts

### Evil as privation

If God made everything and everything God made is good, where does evil come from? Augustine's answer, reached after escaping Manichaean dualism: evil is not a thing at all. It is a *privation* — a lack, corruption, or disorder in something that is itself good, as blindness is not a substance but the absence of sight in an eye that should see. Every nature, insofar as it exists, is good; evil names the ways natures fall short of what they should be. An evil will, likewise, has no efficient cause — it is a *deficient* movement, a turning from higher goods to lower ones. This preserves both God's goodness (he authors no evil substance) and creaturely responsibility (the will's turning is its own). The tension: critics from his day to ours ask whether "privation" adequately captures the felt positivity of cruelty and pain, or merely redescribes it.

### Original sin, grace, and the divided will

Augustine's mature doctrine: Adam's Fall damaged human nature itself, and the damage is inherited — every person is born with a will biased toward disordered loves, a condition he called *concupiscence*. The *Confessions* dramatize this in his own story: he famously prayed "give me chastity and continence, but not yet," and describes a will split against itself, able to command his body but not his own wanting. After the Fall, free will (*liberum arbitrium*) remains — we choose what we do — but it is not *free* in the deeper sense (*libertas*): without grace, we reliably choose lesser goods and cannot heal ourselves. Salvation therefore depends on God's grace operating before and within our choices (the anti-Pelagian writings push this toward predestination: God elects whom he will save). Virtue, positively stated, is *rightly ordered love* (*caritas*): loving God supremely, other things in proportion. The two great drives, ordered love and disordered love, generate the two cities. Internal tensions are real and were debated immediately: how is inherited guilt just? How do grace and responsibility cohere? The Western church spent a millennium arguing inside Augustine's framework.

### The two cities

The sack of Rome in 410 by Alaric's Visigoths prompted pagans to blame Christianity for abandoning the old gods. The *City of God* (22 books, written over 13 years) answers by reinterpreting all of history. Two cities, Babylon and Jerusalem, earthly and heavenly, are formed by two loves: "the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self." The cities are not identical to any visible institution — not Rome, and not even the empirical church, which contains citizens of both. They are intermingled until the final judgment. The earthly city seeks earthly peace and domination (Rome's glory, on Augustine's acidic reading, was driven by lust for praise and power); the City of God is on pilgrimage, using earthly peace but not mistaking it for the final good. This was the first philosophy of history in the West: history has a plot, direction, and meaning, but no earthly regime is the kingdom.

### Time, memory, and eternity

Book 11 of the *Confessions* asks: what is time? The past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no duration — if the present were always present, it would be eternity, not time. Yet we measure time. Augustine's solution relocates time in the mind: time is a *distension of the soul* (*distentio animi*). The present has three aspects: the present of past things (memory), the present of present things (attention), the present of future things (expectation). When I recite a psalm, expectation drains through attention into memory; we measure that passage in the soul. God, by contrast, is eternal — not everlasting through time but outside it, seeing all times in an unchanging present. This makes Augustine a founding figure both for phenomenology of time-consciousness and for the doctrine of divine eternity later refined by Boethius.

### Inner teacher and illumination

In epistemology Augustine held that the mind judges sensible things by standards (of truth, number, beauty) it did not derive from the senses; truth is "above" the mind, and Christ as inner teacher illuminates the mind so it can see intelligible truth — a Christianised version of Platonic recollection. His early dialogue *On Free Choice of the Will* also contains an argument from self-awareness: "if I am deceived, I exist" (*si fallor, sum*) — a striking anticipation of Descartes' *cogito*, deployed against skepticism a millennium earlier.

## Key works

- **Confessions** (c. 397–400) — spiritual autobiography through Book 9; Books 10–11 pivot to memory and time. The philosophical core of the Western autobiographical tradition.
- **City of God** (413–426) — 22 books: refutation of pagan charges, then the two-cities philosophy of history. Read selectively.
- **On the Trinity** (c. 399–419) — his most speculative work; psychological analogies of the Trinity (memory, understanding, will) shape later Western theology.
- **On Free Choice of the Will** (begun c. 388, completed c. 395) — early dialogue on evil and free will; contains the *si fallor, sum*.
- **Anti-Pelagian writings** (c. 412–430) — *On Nature and Grace*, *On Grace and Free Will*, *On the Predestination of the Saints*: the hard edge of the grace doctrine.

## In their own words

> Great art Thou, O Lord, and greatly to be praised; great is Thy power, and Thy wisdom infinite. Thee would man praise; man, but a particle of Thy creation; man, that bears about him his mortality, the witness of his sin, the witness that Thou resistest the proud: yet would man praise Thee; he, but a particle of Thy creation. Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.

— Augustine, *Confessions* I.1, trans. E. B. Pusey (1838; public domain)

The *Confessions* opens as a prayer, not an argument — Augustine is talking to God, and the reader overhears. The famous last sentence states the book's whole plot in advance: human beings are made for God, so every substitute satisfaction leaves the heart "restless." Everything that follows — the pear theft, the Manichees, Monica's tears — is Augustine testing that claim against his own life.

## Contributions

Augustine essentially created the agenda of Western theology and much of Western philosophy: the problems of evil, will, grace, time, memory, selfhood, and history are posed in his terms for the next thousand years. He fused the biblical narrative with Neoplatonic metaphysics into a single intellectual system. He invented (or decisively shaped) the genre of autobiographical self-examination, gave the Latin West its standard account of original sin, and supplied the conceptual tools — privation, ordered love, the threefold present — that medieval thinkers inherited as common property.

## Impact and legacy

Nearly every later medieval figure in this era cites him as the master authority: Boethius, Anselm, Abelard, Aquinas (who quotes him constantly while quietly revising him), Scotus, and Ockham all work in his long shadow. The Reformation was, in part, a fight over his estate: Luther (an Augustinian friar) and Calvin leaned on the anti-Pelagian Augustine; the Council of Trent tried to hold his grace doctrine without his predestination. His influence runs beyond theology — Descartes' cogito, phenomenology's analysis of time, and modern theories of memory all have Augustinian sources. The Eastern Orthodox church, which never adopted his doctrine of original sin in the same juridical form, regards him with more reserve — a reminder that "Augustine" means somewhat different things East and West.

## Criticisms and debates

Pelagius contested the grace doctrine in Augustine's own lifetime, charging that it made moral effort pointless and God unjust; the semi-Pelagian controversy continued after his death, and the Second Council of Orange (529) moderated his hardest predestinarian edges. Modern scholars debate how "Augustinian" the later Augustine is versus the young Platonising convert; whether his account of evil as privation succeeds; and how much his doctrine of sex and concupiscence distorted Western attitudes to the body. Augustine systematized a distinctively Western reading of Paul on original sin — he did not invent it unopposed, and the East took a different path.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That Augustine was a world-denying pessimist: he affirmed creation's goodness against the Manichaeans; disordered love, not the world, is the problem.
- That the *Confessions* is mere memoir: Books 10–13 are technical philosophy (memory, time, creation) and the whole work is a theological argument in narrative form.
- That "original sin" means he thought babies are monsters: the doctrine concerns a wounded condition of the will, though its implications for unbaptised infants troubled even him.
- That he despised reason: he held that faith and understanding interpenetrate ("believe so that you may understand"), and his works are intensely argumentative.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Augustine's account of time in *Confessions* XI in five sentences: the past and future do not exist, the present has no duration, yet we measure time. How does time as a *distentio animi* — present memory, attention, and expectation — solve that puzzle?
2. State the problem of evil as a triad — God is omnipotent, God is wholly good, evil exists — then show how evil as privation and the will's deficient turning answer it. What does privation arguably fail to explain about the felt positivity of cruelty and pain?
3. Press Pelagius's objection: if the will is wounded by inherited sin, why are we responsible, and why is moral effort not pointless? Build Augustine's best reply from grace and *libertas*, then assess it.
4. Compare Manichaean dualism with privation: what does each preserve about God's goodness, and what does each sacrifice about creaturely responsibility?
5. Apply ordered love to a modern institution torn between earthly peace and a higher good: which loves are disordered, by Augustine's measure?
