## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1126 – 1198
- **Place:** Born in Córdoba, al-Andalus (Muslim Spain); worked in Seville, Córdoba, and Marrakech. Wrote in Arabic.
- **School/tradition:** Islamic philosophy (*falsafa*) and Islamic law (Mālikī jurisprudence); chief qāḍī (judge) of Córdoba.
- **Known for:** The great commentaries on Aristotle — in Latin he is simply "the Commentator" — and the doctrine of the unity of the intellect.

**Responds to:** [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md), [Avicenna](avicenna.md) · **Answered by:** [Thomas Aquinas](thomas-aquinas.md)

## Biography

Ibn Rushd was born in Córdoba in 1126 into a distinguished family of Mālikī jurists: his grandfather had been chief judge and imam of the Great Mosque, and his father a judge too. Averroes was trained accordingly — Qur'an, law, theology (*kalām*) — and also in medicine, mathematics, astronomy, and philosophy, studying Avicenna's works while quietly finding them unsatisfying on Aristotle. His career fused the two worlds: he served as judge in Seville (1169) and Córdoba, and as court physician to the Almohad caliph Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf. A famous report describes the caliph, behind a curtain of court protocol, asking him what the philosophers say about the heavens — eternal or created? — and Averroes, after initial fear, discussed it freely; the caliph then commissioned accessible summaries of Aristotle, the seed of the commentary project that consumed the rest of his life. Under the next caliph, al-Manṣūr, court politics turned: in about 1195 Averroes was disgraced — charged, officially, with heresy — exiled to Lucena near Córdoba, and his philosophical books were burned, part of a caliphal bid for support from conservative jurists before a military campaign. He was rehabilitated and recalled to Marrakech shortly before his death in 1198. His body was later returned to Córdoba.

The bitter irony of reception followed: Averroes was largely neglected in the later Islamic world, where Avicenna's system dominated, and many of his works — especially the great Long Commentaries — survive only in Hebrew and Latin translation, the Arabic originals lost. His immortality was in someone else's tradition.

## Major theories and concepts

### The commentary project: recovering "the real Aristotle"

Averroes judged that Avicenna had blended Aristotle with Neoplatonic emanationism until the master's own views were obscured. His remedy was the most ambitious commentary enterprise of the Middle Ages: on nearly the whole Aristotelian corpus he wrote, in many cases, three tiers — **short commentaries** (paraphrastic summaries), **middle commentaries** (systematic paraphrase-explanations), and **long commentaries** (line-by-line exegesis quoting each lemma of Aristotle and unpacking it), produced mostly in the 1170s–80s. His aim was philological and philosophical at once: separate Aristotle's demonstrations from later accretions, and let the arguments be assessed at full strength. When thirteenth-century Paris read Aristotle, it read him through Averroes' lens — which is why Aquinas cites him, respectfully and relentlessly, as "the Commentator," even while fighting his conclusions. The tension inside the project: Averroes also argues *with* Aristotle's commentators (Alexander, Themistius) and sometimes silently corrects Aristotle himself, so "the real Aristotle" is partly an Averroist construction.

### The harmony of religion and philosophy; against "double truth"

The *Decisive Treatise* (c. 1179–80) is a legal opinion by a chief judge: does Islamic law permit, forbid, or *command* philosophy? Averroes rules that for those qualified, it commands it — the Law itself summons reflection on creation, and philosophy is simply reflection perfected by demonstration. Hence Scripture and demonstrated truth cannot finally conflict, since "truth does not oppose truth but accords with it." Where a literal scriptural passage clashes with a demonstrated conclusion, the passage must be interpreted allegorically (*taʾwīl*) — a task reserved to demonstrative scholars, because preaching allegory to the general public destroys their faith without replacing it with knowledge. People divide into rhetorical, dialectical, and demonstrative classes, and each must be addressed in its own register. This is emphatically **not** the doctrine of "double truth" (a proposition true in philosophy and false in theology, or vice versa): that hostile label was pinned on his Latin followers, the so-called Latin Averroists around Siger of Brabant, and Averroes' whole treatise exists to deny that truth can split.

### The unity of the material intellect

In the *Long Commentary on the De Anima* (especially Book III), Averroes argued that Aristotle's "material" (possible) intellect — the capacity that receives intelligible forms — cannot be a power of each individual body. His reasoning: if the intellect were individuated by bodies, the forms it received would be individuated too, and universal knowledge (of *humanity as such*, not just this human) would be impossible; a receptive capacity must be wholly unmixed with matter to receive all forms. So the material intellect is a single, separate, eternal substance for the whole human species; the agent intellect is likewise separate; individuals contribute their own sense-images (the "intentions" of imagination) which the separate intellects work upon — rather as one sun illuminates many eyes. Individual people think, on this account, by conjoining with the one intellect through their images, but no individual intellect survives death: personal immortality is excluded (Averroes held religious language about the afterlife to be symbolic truth for the non-philosophical majority, binding as law). Latin Christendom heard this as a catastrophe: Aquinas devoted a whole treatise, *On the Unity of the Intellect* (1270), to refuting it — in it he calls Averroes "not so much a Peripatetic as a perverter of Peripatetic philosophy" — and it figured centrally in the Paris condemnations of 1270 and 1277.

### Incoherence of the Incoherence: defending natural causation

Al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers* had attacked the philosophers on twenty points, most radically denying necessary causal connection: fire does not burn cotton, on the occasionalist view; God creates the burning *at* the contact, not *by* it. In the *Incoherence of the Incoherence* (c. 1180), Averroes defends real, necessary natural causation: deny that things have natures acting according to what they are, and you dissolve knowledge, language, and rational action ("intelligence is nothing but the perception of things with their causes"). He likewise defends the eternity of the world as Aristotle argued it, against Ashʿarite theology. The book is a bracing point-by-point duel and a foundation document for later debates about causality and miracles.

### Astronomy and the critique of Ptolemy

Less famous but genuinely scientific: Averroes rejected Ptolemaic epicycles and eccentrics as physically impossible fictions, demanding an astronomy of concentric spheres consistent with Aristotelian physics. His program failed to produce a working model, but it set the agenda that drove Iberian and later European astronomers toward reform — an interesting case of philosophical purity obstructing and inspiring science at once.

## Key works

- **Long Commentaries** on the *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *De Caelo*, and especially *De Anima* (mostly 1180s–90s) — the line-by-line exegeses through which Latin Europe read Aristotle; the *De Anima* commentary contains the unity-of-intellect doctrine.
- **Decisive Treatise** (c. 1179–80) — the legal ruling that philosophy is obligatory for the qualified and that Scripture and demonstration harmonize.
- **Incoherence of the Incoherence** (c. 1180) — defense of philosophy and natural causation against al-Ghazālī.
- **The Distinguished Jurist's Primer** (*Bidāyat al-Mujtahid*) — a comparative-law classic still read in Mālikī jurisprudence; a reminder that he was a working chief judge, not only a philosopher.

## Contributions

Averroes created the medieval Aristotle commentary as a rigorous discipline, recalibrated the tradition away from Avicenna's Neoplatonised reading, and posed — in the unity of the intellect and the harmony treatise — the questions about mind, immortality, and faith/reason that Latin scholasticism organized itself around answering. His insistence that truth is one, with public and philosophical registers of discourse, remains a landmark in the philosophy of religion. His commentary on Plato's *Republic* — his principal work of political philosophy, whose Arabic original is lost and which survives in Hebrew — extends his range well beyond commentary on Aristotle.

## Impact and legacy

In Paris and the other universities, "the Commentator" was cited as routinely as "the Philosopher" (Aristotle himself): Aquinas learned Aristotle from him and defined positions against him; Scotus engaged him; a self-conscious Latin Averroist movement (Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia) drew the condemnations of 1270/77 that shaped scholastic boundaries. Jewish philosophy preserved and translated him (the Hebrew Averroes tradition runs through Gersonides and beyond), and Maimonides recommended his commentaries to his translator. In the Islamic world his star eclipsed after his death — Avicenna won the East — but modern Arab and Muslim thought, and a famous Borges story, reclaimed him as a symbol of rationalism.

## Criticisms and debates

The unity-of-intellect doctrine is the eternal flashpoint: Aquinas argued it makes *this man thinks* inexplicable and destroys responsibility and immortality; modern scholars debate whether Averroes changed his mind across his career (early/middle commentaries are more Avicennan) and how the conjunction theory is even supposed to work. The "double truth" question persists as a labeling debate — the consensus now is that it misdescribes Averroes himself, fitting some Latin followers better. His harmony thesis is pressed from both sides: theologians found it subordinated Scripture to philosophy; philosophers note the elitism of reserving interpretation to a demonstrative class. Historians also reconsider his disgrace — theology, court faction, and caliphal politics are all in play.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That Averroes taught "double truth": he taught the opposite — one truth, two registers of expression, and allegorical interpretation where needed.
- That he was a marginal heretic in his own society: he was chief judge of Córdoba and court physician; the disgrace came late and was reversed.
- That his commentaries are passive summaries: they argue, correct, and systematize — the Latin Aristotle is partly his construction.
- That he single-handedly "saved" Aristotle for Europe: the Arabic tradition and the translators of Toledo and Sicily share the story; his distinction is the commentary's authority.

## What to read first

1. *Decisive Treatise* (short and lucid; Charles Butterworth's translation) — a judge ruling that philosophy is legally required.
2. A chunk of the *Long Commentary on the De Anima*, Book III, alongside the Aristotle passage it glosses, for the unity of intellect in the original.
