## At a glance

- **Dates:** 1079 – 1142
- **Place:** Born at Le Pallet, Brittany; taught in and around Paris; died at the priory of St Marcel, Chalon-sur-Saône.
- **School/tradition:** Latin Christian; the great dialectician of the twelfth-century schools, before the universities proper.
- **Known for:** His solution to the problem of universals, the *Sic et Non* method, intention-based ethics — and the love affair with Héloïse.

**Responds to:** [Boethius](boethius.md), [Aristotle](../01-ancient/aristotle.md) · **Answered by:** the scholastic quaestio tradition carried forward through Peter Lombard's *Sentences*, with no single successor in this collection

## Biography

Peter Abelard was born in 1079 at Le Pallet near Nantes, the eldest son of a knight, Berengar. He renounced his inheritance and military career for study — he speaks of exchanging the camp of Mars for the battles of disputation — and became a wandering student of logic, the prestige discipline of the day, studying under Roscelin of Compiègne, a leading "nominalist." By about 1100 he was in Paris, studying under William of Champeaux, master of the cathedral school of Notre Dame and the era's leading realist on universals. Abelard attacked William's doctrine in public disputation so effectively — forcing him to modify it, by Abelard's own gleeful account in ways that destroyed it — that students flocked to Abelard's rival lectures at Melun and Corbeil, then back in Paris itself. He became the most famous teacher in Europe, master at Notre Dame, his lectures drawing thousands (the numbers are rhetorical, the fame real).

At the height of his career, about 1115–16, he took on a private pupil: Héloïse d'Argenteuil, the brilliant niece of Canon Fulbert (her exact age is uncertain — traditionally about seventeen), already famed for her learning. Tutoring became a love affair; she became pregnant; they married secretly (she argued, with sophisticated reasoning reported in her letters, against marriage as a threat to his career and to philosophy itself). Fulbert publicised the marriage Abelard wanted kept quiet; Abelard sent Héloïse to the convent of Argenteuil for safety; Fulbert, believing Abelard was discarding her, hired men who broke into his room at night and castrated him. The scandal ended his teaching career in its old form. Abelard became a monk at St Denis; Héloïse took the veil at Argenteuil. His intellectual life continued in storms: his treatise on the Trinity was condemned and burned at Soissons in 1121 (he was made to recite the creed and throw his own book into the fire); he quarrelled with his monks (pointing out, correctly and tactlessly, that their patron saint Dionysius could not be the Dionysius of Athens); he founded the oratory of the Paraclete in the wilderness near Troyes, where students literally built huts to hear him, and later gave the Paraclete to Héloïse and her expelled nuns, becoming their spiritual director. The letters exchanged between Abelard and Héloïse — his autobiographical *Historia Calamitatum* ("Story of My Calamities," c. 1132) and their subsequent correspondence — are among the great documents of medieval literature, though scholars continue to debate details of the letters' transmission and authenticity. In 1140–41 Bernard of Clairvaux, the era's most powerful churchman, campaigned against Abelard's theology; at the Council of Sens (1141) Abelard, who had expected a disputation, instead faced a pre-arranged condemnation of propositions from his works, engineered by Bernard, and the pope confirmed it. He withdrew to Cluny under the protection of Peter the Venerable, was reconciled in part, taught quietly, and died in 1142.

## Major theories and concepts

### Universals: from vocalism to sermones

The problem, as Boethius had posed it: when we say "Socrates is human" and "Plato is human," is there one thing, *humanity*, present in both? **Realists** (William of Champeaux) said the same essence exists wholly in each individual — Abelard's refutation: then contraries (rational/irrational in man/ass) and identity problems follow (the essence of Socrates would be wherever Plato is). **Vocalists** (Roscelin, in the crude report) said a universal is a mere puff of voice (*flatus vocis*) — a sound. Abelard's mature position (in the *Logica Ingredientibus* and its Glosses on Porphyry, c. 1119–20): a universal is a ***sermo*** — a word not as physical sound but as a meaningful sign, instituted to signify. The word "human" is predicable of many because it was imposed from a **common cause of imposition**: humans agree in being human — but that agreement, the *status* of being a man, is **not a thing** (*res*). The universal term signifies individuals through a confused common conception the mind forms; the concept is real as a mental act, the resemblance is real, but no universal *entity* exists. This is why the label "conceptualism" is attached to him — though the label is later, and scholars debate whether his *status* doctrine or his theory of mental conception does the real work. Either way it is the most sophisticated early medieval position: it saves the objectivity of classification (not arbitrary puff) without multiplying entities.

### Sic et Non: contradiction as method

The *Sic et Non* ("Yes and No," c. 1120–21) assembles 158 theological questions — was God able to do otherwise? is lying ever permitted? — each furnished with apparently contradictory quotations from Scripture and the Fathers, ranged pro and contra, with no resolutions given. Its prologue, however, supplies the resolution *rules*: check whether a word is used in different senses; whether a passage is authentic or corrupt; whether an author retracted or spoke under dispensation; attend to context and to the difference between command, counsel, and permission. This is not skepticism: it is training in **dialectic** — disagreement among authorities is reframed from an embarrassment into the engine of inquiry, "for by doubting we come to inquiry, and by inquiring we perceive the truth" (a sentiment the prologue endorses from Augustine and Aristotle). The *Sic et Non* is the founding document of the scholastic *quaestio*, and Peter Lombard's *Sentences* systematized its approach into the textbook on which Aquinas, Scotus, and Ockham all later commented.

### Intention ethics

Abelard's *Ethics* (*Scito te ipsum*, "Know Thyself," c. 1138–39) relocates sin. Neither the desire nor the outward act is sin in itself: people who crucified Christ believing they did right did not sin *in that act's intention*; those who act against conscience — consenting to what they believe God forbids — sin even if the act is objectively good or the belief mistaken. Sin is **consent**: the will's contempt for God, knowingly choosing what one believes to be against God's will. Merit and guilt track intention and conscience, not outcomes or external conformity. This inward turn — radical for its day — makes morality a matter of the will's orientation, anticipates Kantian emphases on motive in some respects (while remaining a theology of sin, not autonomous ethics), and provoked Bernard's charge that Abelard emptied moral and sacramental realism. Tensions: if sincere bad conscience binds absolutely, how do error and responsibility interact? Abelard bites part of the bullet — acting against conscience is always sin — while holding that a mistaken conscience's acts may still be "good" in another respect only when the error is invincible, a knot later scholastics worked to untie. The *Collationes* carry the same ethics outward into natural law: Abelard's Philosopher, who follows no revelation but only the law written by reason, can attain genuine virtue and the highest good — letting Abelard test Christian morality against a Jewish interlocutor and a purely rational one.

### Logic and language

Beyond universals, Abelard's logical writings develop an influential semantics: distinctions between what terms *signify* and what they *denominate*, treatments of consequence and of the copula, and a theory of propositions' *dicta* (what a proposition says — neither a thing nor a mere sentence). Historians of logic regard him as the most original logician between Boethius and the thirteenth century.

## Key works

- **Historia Calamitatum** (c. 1132) — autobiography in letter form: the career, the affair, the castration, the controversies. Short and gripping; the entry point.
- **Sic et Non** (c. 1120–21) — 158 questions with contradictory authorities and a methodological prologue.
- **Logica Ingredientibus / Glosses on Porphyry** (c. 1119–20) — the universals theory in his commentary on the *Isagoge*.
- **Ethica (Scito te ipsum)** (c. 1138–39, unfinished) — intention ethics.
- **Collationes (Dialogue Between a Philosopher, a Jew, and a Christian)** (c. 1125–35; dating debated) — an interreligious dialogue on the highest good, and Abelard's fullest statement of his natural-law ethics.
- **Letters of Abelard and Héloïse** — the correspondence following the *Historia*, including Héloïse's formidable letters of argument and grief.

## Contributions

Abelard turned conflict among authorities into a method — the dialectical machinery on which scholasticism ran — gave the early schools their most sophisticated account of universals, and moved ethics inward to intention and consent. He also modelled the public intellectual as celebrity teacher, for better and worse: the Paris schools' star system starts with him.

## Impact and legacy

Peter Lombard built the *Sentences* on the *Sic et Non*'s approach; the quaestio format became the university's standard technology. His universals position seeded the via moderna's debates. The ethical emphasis on intention fed into later scholastic moral theology and canon law's treatment of culpability. The Héloïse correspondence kept his name alive outside the schools — Rousseau, Pope's "Eloisa to Abelard," and modern novels all retell it — sometimes at the expense of the logician, whom scholars since the nineteenth century have worked to restore to view.

## Criticisms and debates

Bernard's campaign (and the condemnations at Soissons and Sens) charged Abelard's Trinitarian theology with heresy and his method with arrogance — a clash between monastic theology and the new dialectical schools as much as a doctrinal dispute. Modern debates: whether "conceptualism" accurately names his position; the authenticity and editing history of the Letters (a long controversy; the current mainstream accepts them, with some dissenting voices); and how much of the *Historia*'s self-portrait — including the famous refutation of William — is literary self-fashioning.

## Misreadings to avoid

- That *Sic et Non* shows Abelard revelling in contradiction or doubting the faith: the prologue's rules resolve contradictions; the point is training in inquiry.
- That he was a modern free-thinker martyred by dogma: he was a believing theologian whose methods and some conclusions were condemned, and who sought vindication within the church.
- That the Héloïse story is merely scandal: her letters are intellectually serious interventions on marriage, intention, and religious life, and she became an abbess of stature.

## What to read first

1. *Historia Calamitatum* — short, vivid autobiography.
2. *Sic et Non*, the Prologue plus two or three questions, to see the method.
3. Universals excerpts in an anthology (Spade, *Five Texts on Medieval Problem of Universals*) to compare his position against the realists and later nominalists.
