## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 470 – 399 BCE (conventional dates)
- **Place:** Athens, Greece
- **School/tradition:** No school and no writings; originator of the Socratic method, teacher of Plato
- **Known for:** Elenchus, professed ignorance, the unexamined life, his trial

## Biography

Everything about Socrates must start with a warning: he wrote nothing. No treatise, no letter, no fragment in his own hand survives. Everything we know comes from other people — chiefly Plato, Xenophon, Aristophanes, and, at one remove, Aristotle — who disagreed about him. Reconstructing the historical man from these portraits is the famous **Socratic problem**, discussed below. The outline that follows is the broad scholarly consensus, but its details are less secure than for almost any other major philosopher.

Socrates was born c. 470 BCE in Athens, son of Sophroniscus, a stonemason or sculptor, and Phaenarete, a midwife. He later played on his mother's profession: in Plato's *Theaetetus* he calls himself a midwife of ideas, helping others give birth to thoughts and testing whether they are genuine or wind-eggs. Tradition says he practiced his father's trade early; statues on the Acropolis were attributed to him, though this cannot be verified.

He was not wealthy or beautiful by Athenian standards: snub-nosed, bulging-eyed, often barefoot in the same cloak. He married Xanthippe, later proverbial (probably unfairly, on thin evidence) for a sharp tongue, and had three sons.

Athens in his lifetime passed from golden age to catastrophe: the Peloponnesian War against Sparta (431–404 BCE), plague, and imperial fall. Socrates served with distinction as a hoplite — a heavy infantryman — at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis. Plato stresses his endurance and courage: at Potidaea he saved the wounded Alcibiades, at Delium his orderly retreat, alongside Laches, was noted for its courage, and once he stood motionless in thought from dawn to dawn. He was no recluse but a citizen-soldier.

He showed civic courage too. Under the democracy, on the Council, he alone opposed the illegal collective trial of the generals after Arginusae (406 BCE). Under the Thirty Tyrants (404 BCE) he refused to arrest an innocent man, Leon of Salamis, risking his life. Reported by Plato and Xenophon, these episodes complicate the charge that he was anti-democratic: he defied both regimes when they acted unjustly.

His public activity was conversation. In the agora and gymnasia he questioned generals about courage, priests about piety, and politicians about wisdom — usually showing they could not define what they claimed to know. He took no fees, unlike the Sophists, traveling professional teachers of rhetoric whom he criticized. His circle included Alcibiades, Critias, and Plato — associations that later damaged him, since Alcibiades betrayed Athens and Critias led the Thirty.

A turning point, as he tells it in Plato's *Apology*, was the Delphic oracle's answer to his friend Chaerephon: no one was wiser than Socrates. Puzzled, Socrates tested politicians, poets, and craftsmen, concluding his wisdom consisted in knowing he did not know, while they thought they knew and did not. This made him, in his word, a gadfly stinging the lazy horse of Athens awake — and made enemies.

In 399 BCE, aged about seventy, he was tried by Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon: not recognizing the city's gods, introducing new divinities, and corrupting the young. The "new divinity" likely meant his *daimonion*, an inner sign warning him against actions but never commanding. A jury of 500 or 501 convicted him narrowly. Asked to propose an alternative penalty, he provocatively suggested public maintenance as a benefactor, then a modest fine. The jury chose death. He refused escape (the *Crito*), spent a final day on the soul's immortality (the *Phaedo*), and drank hemlock calmly among friends — philosophy's founding martyrdom.

## Major theories and concepts

Attributing "theories" to Socrates is itself part of the Socratic problem. The positions below are those of Plato's early dialogues, which most scholars regard as closest to the historical Socrates.

### The elenchus — the Socratic method

The *elenchus* is Socrates' method of cross-examination. Socrates asks "What is X?" — courage, piety, justice. The interlocutor proposes a definition. Socrates, professing ignorance, asks further questions the interlocutor accepts, and shows they contradict the definition. The dialogue ends in *aporia* — perplexity, an impasse.

Take the *Euthyphro*. Euthyphro, prosecuting his father for murder, claims piety is what the gods love. Socrates poses the dilemma bearing the dialogue's name: is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because loved? If the first, "loved by gods" is no definition; if the second, piety is arbitrary whim. Euthyphro leaves, his definition in ruins.

The elenchus is not mere debating technique. Its aim is therapeutic: false confidence about the most important things is a dangerous condition of the soul, and aporia is inquiry's honest beginning. Its tension is plain: the method is destructive. It tests definitions superbly but rarely produces a surviving one — almost every early dialogue ends without one. Whether Socrates had constructive doctrines behind the questioning, or aporia *was* the doctrine, divides interpreters.

### Socratic ignorance

"I know that I do not know" — the popular formula — is a paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation, but it fairly summarizes the *Apology*'s position. Socrates disavows knowledge of the most important matters: what virtue is, what the good life is, what happens after death. Fear of death, he argues, is a case in point: it pretends to know that death is an evil, when for all we know it may be a blessing — a dreamless sleep or a migration of the soul.

This is not humility as a pose, nor skepticism that all claims are equal. Socrates holds some moral beliefs with striking firmness — that it is better to suffer injustice than to do it, that one must never return wrong for wrong — while denying that he can define or fully justify them. Scholars call this his disavowal of knowledge and debate its exact scope: does he claim no knowledge at all, or no *expert, definitional* knowledge, while allowing himself true belief and certainty on some moral points? The texts support both readings in places, which is why the debate persists.

### Virtue is knowledge — Socratic intellectualism

Socrates' most distinctive doctrine is that virtue is knowledge. If you truly know what is good, you will do it; no one does wrong willingly. Wrongdoing is ignorance — a miscalculation of what is genuinely good for oneself. In the *Protagoras* he models this on measurement: weakness of will is like misjudging sizes at a distance; knowing real values would save us.

Hence there is, strictly, no *akrasia* — no knowing the better and doing the worse. What looks like akrasia is ignorance at the moment of action. Aristotle objected that this "plainly contradicts the phenomena": people obviously act against what they know. Modern readers tend to agree, while finding Socrates' challenge bracing — how complete is your "knowledge" that something is bad for you if it has no grip on your action?

### Care of the soul

Against Athenian priorities — wealth, reputation, success — Socrates insists the soul (*psychē*) comes first. In the *Apology* he urges everyone "not to care for your body or your wealth more than, or even as much as, for the perfection of your soul." Excellence, not possessions, determines whether life goes well; virtue makes wealth good, not the reverse.

This is his deepest legacy: philosophy as care of the self, a daily practice of examination, not an academic specialism. Its slogan comes near the *Apology*'s end: "the unexamined life is not worth living." Note the context — he is explaining why he would not accept exile or silence instead of death. Examination is the condition of a worthwhile life, worth dying rather than abandoning.

### The daimonion

Socrates reported a divine sign — a voice, first heard in childhood — that intervened to oppose actions he was about to take, but never urged him forward. It stopped him entering politics, he says, which probably saved his life. At his trial its silence during his defense was, he argued, evidence that death was no evil. Interpreters ancient and modern have read the daimonion as conscience, as religious experience, as a literary device of Plato's, or as evidence of some psychological peculiarity. It resists tidy explanation and was literally a capital issue: it grounded the charge of introducing new divinities.

## Key works

Socrates wrote nothing. The "works" are portraits of him:

- **Plato, *Apology*** — Socrates' defense speech as Plato reconstructs it. Our best source for his mission, the oracle story, and the unexamined life. Plato was present at the trial.
- **Plato, *Crito*** — Socrates refuses escape, arguing he must obey the laws that reared him. The founding text on political obligation.
- **Plato, early dialogues** (*Euthyphro*, *Laches*, *Charmides*, *Lysis*, *Protagoras*, *Gorgias*) — The elenchus at work on piety, courage, temperance, friendship, and virtue. Probably closest to the historical Socrates.
- **Plato, *Phaedo*** — The death scene and arguments for immortality. Sublime literature; its doctrines (Forms, recollection) are generally judged Plato's, not Socrates'.
- **Xenophon, *Memorabilia* and *Apology*** — A more conventional, practical Socrates: respectable adviser on estate management, self-control, and duty. Less brilliant than Plato's, and a crucial corrective — or a duller distortion; scholars argue both ways.
- **Aristophanes, *The Clouds*** (423 BCE) — A contemporary comic caricature: Socrates suspended in a basket, running a "thinkery," teaching students to make the worse argument the stronger. Hostile and distorted, but performed while Socrates lived, and evidence of his public image.
- **Aristotle's references** (4th century BCE) — Brief, external testimony. Aristotle credits Socrates with inductive arguments and universal definitions, and criticizes his intellectualism — valuable precisely because Aristotle had no motive to invent a Socrates.

## Contributions

Socrates turns Western philosophy toward ethics. The Presocratics asked what the world is made of; Socrates asked how one should live, making definition, consistency, and self-knowledge the tests of an answer. Cicero said he "called philosophy down from the heavens" into cities and homes — an exaggeration, but a fair mark of the shift.

He contributes methods more than doctrines: the elenchus, the demand for definitions, the exposure of expert pretension, and philosophy as lived practice ending, if necessary, in death rather than recantation. Through Plato he founds the Academy tradition; through Antisthenes he influences the Cynics, and through them Stoicism.

## Impact and legacy

Socrates became the archetype of the philosopher: a questioner, martyr, and saint of reason. Cynics, Stoics, and Skeptics each claimed him. Early Christian writers saw parallels with Jesus' death and sometimes treated him as a Christian before Christ. The Enlightenment made him an emblem of free inquiry against authority. The "Socratic method" survives, diluted, in law schools and critical-thinking courses; Kierkegaard modelled his authorship on Socratic midwifery. Every age gets the Socrates it deserves — democrat, dissident, saint, ironist, therapist — itself a symptom of the Socratic problem.

## Criticisms and debates

**The Socratic problem dominates.** Our sources conflict: Aristophanes' buffoon, Xenophon's conventional moralist, and Plato's ironic metaphysician cannot all be the whole man. Trusting Plato's early dialogues over the middle ones is a judgment call, not a proof. Gregory Vlastos argued for a sharp break between the early Socrates and Plato's later mouthpiece; others see more continuity.

**Was the elenchus sincere?** Socrates claims to know nothing, yet demolishes every opponent. His irony may be a teaching device, a weapon, or genuine. Alcibiades, in the *Symposium*, complains his professed ignorance is a performance concealing power; the complaint has never quite been answered.

**Politics.** He criticized selection by lot and taught Critias and Alcibiades; a restored democracy had reason to fear him. Yet he fought for the democracy and defied the Thirty at personal risk. I. F. Stone's *The Trial of Socrates* (1988) prosecuted him as an enemy of open society; most classicists reply Stone overstates the case. The trial remains debatable: legally regular, morally troubling, politically explicable — all at once.

**Akrasia.** Aristotle's objection stands. Defenders reply Socrates means full, present knowledge, not verbal assent — but whether that saves the doctrine or empties it ("real knowledge always motivates, by definition") is still argued.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Socrates wrote dialogues."** He wrote nothing. Every Socratic text is by someone else.
- **"I know that I do not know" is a quotation.** It is a fair paraphrase of the *Apology*, not a verbatim line.
- **"Socrates was executed for corrupting youth in the modern sense."** The charges were religious and political as much as moral, in a city traumatised by war, plague, and oligarchic coups led by his former associates.
- **"The Socrates of the *Republic* is the historical Socrates."** The Forms, philosopher-kings, and recollection are Plato's mature doctrines placed in his teacher's mouth.
- **"Socrates was a sceptic who believed nothing."** He held firm moral convictions — never return wrong for wrong; better to suffer injustice than commit it — while disavowing definitional knowledge.

## What to read first

1. **Plato, *Apology*** — first and essential: the trial, the mission, the unexamined life.
2. **Plato, *Crito*** — why he would not escape; short and moving.
3. **Plato, *Euthyphro*** — the elenchus and the dilemma, in a dozen pages.
4. **Xenophon, *Memorabilia* Book I** — a contrasting portrait, to feel the Socratic problem directly.
5. **Aristophanes, *The Clouds*** (selections) — the hostile caricature Athens laughed at in 423 BCE.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the Euthyphro Dilemma: piety is either loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved. Show why the first horn fails as a definition and the second makes piety arbitrary, leaving Euthyphro in *aporia*.
2. Aristotle objects that Socratic intellectualism "plainly contradicts the phenomena": people know the better and do the worse. Construct Socrates' reply — that such "knowledge" was not full, present knowledge — and judge whether it saves the doctrine or empties it.
3. Compare Socrates with Aristotle on how virtue is acquired: knowledge grasped through examination versus character formed by habit and *phronēsis*. What case of wrongdoing does each account handle better?
4. Apply the Euthyphro Dilemma to a modern authority: is an action right because a law, manager, or AI system approves it, or approved because it is right? Work through both horns for your chosen case.
