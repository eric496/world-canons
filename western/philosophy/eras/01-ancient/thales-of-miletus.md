# Thales of Miletus

*Era: Ancient Philosophy (c. 600 BCE – c. 270 CE) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 624 – c. 546 BCE (conventional; no dates for him are secure)
- **Place:** Miletus, a wealthy Greek trading city in Ionia, on the coast of what is now Turkey
- **School/tradition:** The first of the Milesians, the Ionian thinkers who opened the Presocratic period
- **Known for:** Proposing that everything's *archē* — its originating principle and underlying stuff — is water, and so explaining the world by nature rather than by gods

**Responds to:** Homeric and Hesiodic myth, Ionian natural inquiry · **Answered by:** [Pythagoras](pythagoras.md), [Heraclitus](heraclitus-of-ephesus.md)

## Biography

Almost everything said about Thales' life needs a warning label. He left nothing that survives, and he may have written nothing at all. What we have comes through *doxography* — the ancient practice of compiling summaries of earlier thinkers' opinions — and through writers who lived centuries after him, above all Aristotle (4th century BCE), Herodotus (5th century BCE), and Diogenes Laertius (probably 3rd century CE), who mixed fact, inference, and anecdote. Separating man from legend is not a preliminary chore here; it is most of the work.

The plausible core is this. Thales lived in Miletus around 600 BCE, when it was one of the richest ports in the Greek world, trading with Egypt, Phoenicia, and the Black Sea, and in close contact with Babylonian and Egyptian learning. That setting matters: Miletus was cosmopolitan, relatively free of a powerful priesthood, and full of practical knowledge — navigation, surveying, calendars — imported from abroad. Later tradition made Thales one of the Seven Sages of Greece and credited him with political advice, including urging the Ionian cities to federate against Persia. Whether he gave it is unverifiable, but it fits a figure remembered as a public man, not a recluse.

Two famous stories illustrate the problem. Herodotus says Thales predicted a solar eclipse that stopped a battle between the Lydians and the Medes, traditionally dated to 585 BCE. Babylonian astronomers knew cycles that made a *possible* eclipse year predictable, so the story has a plausible core — but predicting a total eclipse visible at a particular place was beyond anyone then, and many scholars think luck or later embroidery did the rest. The second story, told by Aristotle, is frankly a parable: mocked as an impractical stargazer, Thales supposedly rented all the olive presses cheaply before a bumper harvest, then charged what he liked — proving, Aristotle says, that philosophers could be rich if they cared to be. Plato's companion tale has him falling into a well while stargazing. Both anecdotes tell us how later Greeks *thought* about the first philosopher; neither can be trusted as biography.

His death, c. 546 BCE, is equally obscure. What is firm is his succession: Anaximander, a younger Milesian, is said to have been his pupil or associate, and Anaximenes followed. Through them, his questions became a research program. His work "survives" only as attributed doctrines filtered through Aristotle — who himself signals uncertainty, reconstructing Thales' reasons with phrases like "perhaps he got the idea from seeing that…".

## Major theories and concepts

### Water as archē

An *archē* (plural *archai*) is a beginning, source, or ruling principle — the thing from which everything comes and which explains what everything fundamentally is. Aristotle reports that Thales made water the archē of all things. The claim can be read two ways, and scholars debate which Thales meant: as a *material* claim (everything is, deep down, a form of water) or as an *origin* claim (everything arose from water, as things arise from a seed). It may not have distinguished the two.

Aristotle, guessing at the reasoning, offers plausible observations: nourishment is moist; seeds are wet; water takes any shape and is essential to all life; Thales is also reported to have said the earth floats on water like a log, which would explain earthquakes as rocking rather than a god's anger. Each reason appeals to ordinary experience, not revelation. The tension is obvious, and his successors felt it: how does one uniform, wet stuff become fire, stone, and bone? Thales left no surviving answer, and that gap is what Anaximander's more abstract principle was designed to fill.

### A naturalistic method

Arguably more important than water is *how* the claim works. Earthquakes, magnets, and growth are explained by natural processes, not by Poseidon shaking the earth. Yet Thales also reportedly said a magnet has a soul because it moves iron, and that "all things are full of gods" — complicating any picture of him as a modern atheist scientist: his nature may still have been alive and divine, but it behaved regularly and could be reasoned about. The contribution is the *style* of explanation — public, arguable, answerable to observation.

### Geometry and astronomy

Later writers credited Thales with several theorems: that a circle is bisected by its diameter, that base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, that vertical angles are equal, that a triangle in a semicircle is right-angled, and a method for measuring ships at sea or pyramids by their shadows. Egyptian and Babylonian practitioners knew many of these as practical rules. The Greek tradition's real claim is that he began turning rules of thumb into *demonstrated* propositions. No proof survives, and the attributions are centuries late. Treat the list as a plausible core wrapped in legend: he probably imported Near Eastern mathematical knowledge; which theorems, if any, he proved is unknowable.

## Key works

- **None survive, and probably none were written.** No title reliably attributed to Thales has come down to us. A *Nautical Astronomy* was attached to his name in antiquity, but even ancient sources doubted it was his. Everything attributed to him comes through Aristotle, Herodotus, Plato, and the later doxographical tradition.

## Contributions

- **The archē question itself.** Asking "what single natural principle underlies the variety of things?" set the agenda for a century of Presocratic thought, even for those who answered it differently.
- **Natural explanation as a public activity.** Causes were to be found in the world, stated openly, and criticized by others — a break, in method if not always in content, with mythic accounts in which events happen because a god wills them.
- **A bridge for Eastern knowledge.** Whether or not he proved theorems, the tradition correctly remembers Miletus, and Thales with it, as a conduit through which Babylonian astronomy and Egyptian geometry entered Greek thought and were transformed there.

## Impact and legacy

Thales' direct influence ran through Anaximander and Anaximenes, whose rival archai — the indefinite, air — show his question being argued with rather than repeated. Aristotle placed him at the head of the first history of philosophy, as founder of the search for material causes, and that placement stuck: for over two thousand years he has served as the symbolic starting point of Western philosophy and science. The honor is partly a construction — non-Greek traditions asked cosmological questions too — but it marks something real: in Miletus we first see, in the Greek record, explanation offered as argument, open to replacement by a better argument.

## Criticisms and debates

Aristotle himself found a single material cause insufficient, since it cannot explain change or form. Modern debates are mostly about evidence. Did Thales hold the water doctrine as Aristotle reports, or did Aristotle systematize a vaguer claim? Did he write anything? Could he have predicted the 585 BCE eclipse? Scholars also debate how sharp the "break with myth" was: a thinker who says all things are full of gods has not left religion behind. None of these can be settled from new evidence; they turn on how much trust to place in Aristotle.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Thales the modern scientist."** He offered no experiments, no mathematics of nature that survives, and possibly religious-sounding claims about souls and gods. His importance is naturalism of approach, not modern method.
- **"Water was a silly guess."** Given moisture's role in life, growth, weather, and the geography of a port city, it was a reasonable, experience-based choice — and the lasting move was positing one principle at all.
- **Taking the anecdotes as fact.** The eclipse, the olive presses, and the well are evidence about his later reputation first, and about his life only cautiously, if at all.
- **Assuming he wrote a book we lost yesterday.** Nothing survives because, most likely, there was little or nothing written to survive.

## What to read first

1. A secondary chapter on the Milesians first — the [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/) or [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/) entry on the Presocratics — to learn what is evidence and what is reconstruction.
2. Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Book 1 (983b–984a), the earliest substantial report on Thales, read while noticing Aristotle's own agenda.
3. Herodotus, *Histories* 1.74–75 and 1.170, for the eclipse and political stories in their earliest surviving form.
4. Then Anaximander: seeing what his successor changed is the quickest way to understand what Thales started.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Thales' case for water as *archē* from the evidence Aristotle reports — moisture in nourishment and seeds, water's shapeless adaptability, the earth floating on water — and state whether the conclusion is a material claim, an origin claim, or both.
2. Aristotle objects that one material cause cannot explain change or form. Press this against water: how does a uniform wet stuff become fire or bone? What reply is open to Thales, and does it quietly change what *archē* means?
3. Compare Thales with Heraclitus: is the basic principle a stuff things are made of, or a pattern according to which they change? What does each account explain better?
4. Apply Thales' naturalistic method to a flood explained by a community as a god's anger. What would count as a Thales-style explanation, what evidence could settle between the two accounts, and does his "all things are full of gods" weaken the contrast?
