# Plotinus

*Era: Ancient Philosophy (c. 600 BCE – c. 270 CE) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 204/5–270 CE
- **Place:** Egypt-born; taught in Rome from 244 CE
- **School/tradition:** Neoplatonism — a modern name; Plotinus saw himself as Plato's interpreter
- **Known for:** Reality emanating from the ineffable One through Intellect and Soul, and philosophy as the soul's return to its source.

**Responds to:** [Plato](plato.md), [Aristotle](aristotle.md) · **Answered by:** [Augustine](../02-medieval/augustine-of-hippo.md), [Eriugena](../02-medieval/john-scotus-eriugena.md), [Nicholas of Cusa](../02-medieval/nicholas-of-cusa.md)

## Biography

Almost everything we know comes from the *Life of Plotinus* by his student Porphyry, prefixed to his edition of the works. Porphyry says Plotinus was ashamed of being in a body, refused to sit for a portrait or discuss his birthplace and parents, and gave his birth date only in a form letting us calculate c. 204/5 CE, probably in Egypt.

At twenty-eight, dissatisfied with Alexandria's famous teachers, he was taken to hear Ammonius Saccas — who left no writings and about whom little is known — and reportedly exclaimed, "This is the man I was looking for." He stayed eleven years. In 243 he joined the emperor Gordian III's expedition against Persia, hoping to study Persian and Indian philosophy; when Gordian was murdered, Plotinus escaped to Antioch, then Rome.

In Rome from 244 he taught for over twenty-five years, at first writing nothing, conducting seminars as open discussion of Plato and Aristotle. Only after ten years did he begin the treatises that became the *Enneads*. His circle included senators, physicians, and women students such as Gemina; the emperor Gallienus admired him and, Porphyry says, nearly granted him a city in Campania to refound as "Platonopolis," run on Plato's *Laws* — court rivals blocked it.

Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–c. 305 CE), his most important student, arrived in 263, and Plotinus entrusted him with editing the writings. His health failed; in 268 he retired to Campania, where he died in 270. His reported last words urged bringing "the god in us" back to "the god in the All."

Porphyry arranged the 54 treatises written between 253 and 270 not chronologically but systematically, into six sets of nine — *ennead* means "group of nine" — moving from ethics and physics (I–III) through Soul (IV) and Intellect (V) to the One (VI). That arrangement shapes how every later reader meets the system.

## Major theories and concepts

### The One and the hypostases

Plotinus organizes reality into three *hypostases* — fundamental levels, literally "things standing under." First is the One, also called the Good: absolutely simple, with no parts or internal distinction — not even between being and thinking. It is "beyond being" and strictly indescribable; we can say what it is not more easily than what it is. Second is Intellect (*Nous*): where Plato's Forms exist not as inert blueprints but as living thoughts in a mind where being and thinking are identical. Third is Soul (*Psychē*): Intellect's image turned outward, which reasons step by step, moves through time, and animates the material world. Below Soul lies matter, the last and faintest level, barely real.

*Tension:* if the One is beyond description, how can Plotinus write treatises calling it "the Good" and cause of all? He accepts the paradox: language points and negates rather than describes, and the One is known finally by union, not argument.

### Emanation

How does multiplicity come from absolute unity without dividing it? Plotinus's answer is emanation: reality overflows from the One as light radiates from the sun — necessarily, eternally, without the source losing or deciding anything. The One does not create at a moment or need the world; what is perfect naturally produces an image of itself. Intellect emanates by turning back toward the One in contemplation; Soul emanates from Intellect likewise; the sensible world from Soul. Sunlight does not choose to shine or grow poorer by shining; each level produces the next as a by-product of contemplation. *Tension:* a necessary, eternal process makes the world unavoidable — critics asked whether this leaves room for freedom in the source, and Christians later had to transform the doctrine into free creation.

### The soul, descent, and return (henōsis)

Individual souls are portions of Soul, partly descended into bodies. Plotinus says part of the soul never descends but remains in the intelligible world, so return is recovering awareness of what we already are. Embodiment is both a fall, driven by restless self-assertion, and a natural administration of the lower world; he holds both views in uneasy balance.

The way back runs through purifying virtue, philosophy, and contemplation of Beauty and the Forms, culminating — rarely, beyond discursive thought — in *henōsis*, union with the One, where knower and known cease to be distinct. Plotinus closes the *Enneads* with "the flight of the alone to the Alone" (VI.9). Porphyry reports Plotinus achieved union four times while they were together — a claim we can neither verify nor dismiss, since the philosophy is built to make it intelligible.

### Evil as privation

Evil is not a substance or rival power. It is privation — distance from the Good, deficiency, formlessness — ultimately identified with matter, which as the last emanation has least reality and goodness. Moral evil is the soul turning outward and identifying with the body. This answered the Gnostics, whom Plotinus attacked (II.9) for despising the cosmos: as Soul's product, the sensible world is beautiful and ordered, however faintly. *Tension:* if matter comes, however distantly, from the One, does the Good ultimately cause evil? Plotinus argues production must end somewhere, and its limit is privation — an answer Augustine later adapted and contested.

### Beauty and contemplation

In *Ennead* I.6 Plotinus argues beauty is not symmetry of parts: a single color can be beautiful, and a living face outshines a symmetrical corpse. Beauty is presence of Form, recognized because the soul is akin to Form. Contemplation (*theōria*) is not passive: things produce *by* contemplating — nature contemplates silently, and production is its overflow. Contemplation, not action, is the universe's most fundamental activity.

## Key works

All Plotinus wrote is in the *Enneads*: treatises of 253–270 CE ordered thematically by Porphyry.

- **Ennead I.6, *On Beauty* (c. 253–263).** The first treatise written, placed as a gentle entry: beauty, the soul's kinship with Form, and ascent to Beauty itself.
- **Ennead V.1, *On the Three Primary Hypostases* (c. 253–263).** The clearest map of One, Intellect, and Soul — the system's best summary.
- **Ennead II.9, *Against the Gnostics* (c. 263–268).** A defense of the cosmos against world-despising rivals; Plotinus arguing, not just contemplating.
- **Ennead VI.9, *On the Good or the One* (c. 253–263).** Placed last as the destination: the One's transcendence and union with it.
- **Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus* (c. 301 CE).** Indispensable preface: biography and publication history.

## Contributions

Plotinus fused Plato, Aristotle, and Stoic materials into a single graded metaphysics centered on a first principle more radically transcendent than Plato's Form of the Good or Aristotle's God — a principle beyond being itself. He gave philosophy a rigorous account of mystical union as its goal, a privation theory of evil, and a model of production by contemplation.

## Impact and legacy

Porphyry spread the system; Iamblichus and Proclus developed it into late-antique pagan theology. Its larger afterlife was in monotheisms it did not belong to: Augustine read Plotinus in Latin translation and took from him the privation theory of evil and the inward turn, reshaping Western Christianity. Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500) baptized Neoplatonic hierarchy and negative theology; Arabic transmission through the paraphrase known as the *Theology of Aristotle* structured medieval Islamic and Jewish metaphysics; Eriugena, Eckhart, and Cusanus carried its mystical line forward. The Renaissance Plato revival — Ficino translated the complete *Enneads* (1492) — ran on Plotinian rails.

## Criticisms and debates

Aristotelians resisted Forms as thoughts in an Intellect; Christians who used him most — Augustine included — rejected emanation's necessity in favor of free creation. Modern debates include: does the self survive *henōsis*, or is union dissolution? Is part of the soul really always undescended? Does "beyond being" collapse into incoherence or form a disciplined negative theology? And how far is the *Enneads'* system Plotinus's versus Porphyry's arrangement? The thin evidence for Ammonius, who wrote nothing, makes Plotinus's originality versus inheritance hard to apportion.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Mystical" does not mean anti-rational:** the *Enneads* are dense, argumentative metaphysics; union is the arguments' conclusion, not a substitute for them.
- **Emanation is not creation:** no moment, no decision — and no pantheism, since the One remains transcendent.
- **Matter is not an evil god:** evil has no substance; Plotinus defended the cosmos's beauty against the Gnostics.
- **Not a world-denying escapist:** descent serves the world's ordering, and the philosopher returns to teaching — as Plotinus did.

## What to read first

1. **Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus*** — short, vivid, explains how the *Enneads* were made.
2. **Ennead I.6, *On Beauty*, with a guide** — the most approachable treatise; use a modern translation alongside a secondary introduction.
3. **Ennead V.1** — the system in miniature, once I.6 is familiar.
4. **Ennead VI.9** — the summit, saved for last as Porphyry intended.
