## At a glance

- **Dates:** 384 – 322 BCE
- **Place:** Born in Stagira, Macedon; worked mainly in Athens
- **School/tradition:** Student of Plato; founder of the Lyceum and the Peripatetic school
- **Known for:** Hylomorphism, four causes, formal logic, virtue ethics

**Responds to:** [Plato](plato.md) · **Answered by:** [Plotinus](plotinus.md), [Averroes](../02-medieval/averroes.md), [Thomas Aquinas](../02-medieval/thomas-aquinas.md)

## Biography

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE in Stagira, in the Chalcidice peninsula under Macedonian influence. His father Nicomachus was physician to King Amyntas III of Macedon, grandfather of Alexander the Great. That court connection gave him early exposure to empirical inquiry, and later endangered him in Athens. Both parents died when he was young, and he was raised by a guardian, Proxenus.

At about seventeen, c. 367 BCE, he joined Plato's Academy in Athens and stayed twenty years, as student, then teacher. Ancient sources call him "the Mind" and "the Reader" — nicknames capturing his relentless study. His earliest writings were dialogues in Plato's style, now lost except for fragments. Intellectually he diverged: where Plato separated Forms from sensible things, Aristotle argued form exists *in* things. His mature philosophy is a sustained, respectful, critical conversation with Plato.

When Plato died in 347 BCE, leadership passed to Plato's nephew Speusippus, not Aristotle. Aristotle left Athens — historians debate whether from disappointment, anti-Macedonian feeling, or invitation — for Assos in Asia Minor, at the court of Hermias of Atarneus, whose niece Pythias he married. There and on Lesbos he did much of his marine biological research: observation, dissection, and classification of hundreds of species. After Pythias' death his partner was Herpyllis, mother of his son Nicomachus, after whom the *Nicomachean Ethics* is probably named.

In 343 BCE Philip II summoned him to tutor the thirteen-year-old Alexander. The tutorship lasted roughly three years; we know little of what was taught, though Aristotle prepared an annotated *Iliad* for him. In 335 BCE he returned to Athens and founded the Lyceum, in a grove sacred to Apollo Lyceus. His followers were called Peripatetics, supposedly from his walking (*peripatein*) while teaching. For twelve years the Lyceum rivalled the Academy, with a library, maps, and collections of constitutions and specimens — arguably Europe's first large-scale research institute.

Alexander's death in 323 BCE unleashed anti-Macedonian feeling. Charged with impiety, Aristotle withdrew to Chalcis — tradition says he remarked Athens should not "sin twice against philosophy," remembering Socrates. He died there in 322 BCE, aged sixty-two.

His writings divide sharply. The polished published dialogues are lost. What survives are treatises: lecture texts and research notes, edited by later hands, notably Andronicus of Rhodes in the 1st century BCE. Their compressed style is a school's voice, not a literary author's.

## Major theories and concepts

### Hylomorphism: matter and form

Aristotle's central metaphysical claim is that an ordinary substance — a statue, a tree, a person — is a compound of matter (*hylē*) and form (*morphē* or *eidos*). Hence *hylomorphism*. Matter is what something is made of; form is its structure, organization, and what makes it the kind of thing it is. Bronze is the matter of a statue; the shape of Hermes is its form. A living body is matter; the soul, for Aristotle, is its form — the organization that makes it alive.

This directly answers Plato. A Form of Man existing separately, Aristotle argues, explains nothing about actual men and cannot cause them. Form must be in the thing, studied by observing things. The tension: if form is inseparable from matter, what status do mathematical objects have, and can form be defined without reference to matter? Aristotle's answers shift across the *Metaphysics*, which is partly why that work remains contested.

### The four causes

To know a thing, Aristotle says, is to know its causes (*aitiai*) — better rendered "explanations" — and there are four kinds. The **material cause** is what it is made of (bronze). The **formal cause** is its form or essence (the statue's shape). The **efficient cause** is what brings it about (the sculptor). The **final cause** is its end or purpose, its *telos* (honoring the god).

A house illustrates all four: bricks and timber, the plan, the builder, and shelter. Final causes matter most in biology: an eye is *for* seeing, roots are *for* nourishment. This teleology — explaining by purposes — proved immensely fruitful for biology and immensely controversial for physics. Is the telos in nature itself, or a projection of human craftsmanship onto nature? Aristotle insists nature acts for ends without deliberating, as habit does; modern critics divide over whether this is deep insight or systematic anthropomorphism.

### Potentiality, actuality, and the Unmoved Mover

Change poses a puzzle inherited from Parmenides: how can something *become* what it is not? Aristotle answers with potentiality (*dynamis*) and actuality (*energeia*). An acorn is actually an acorn and potentially an oak; change is a potential becoming actual, not being springing from non-being. A builder who is not building has the capacity actually unexercised.

In *Physics* VIII and *Metaphysics* XII he argues that motion is eternal and requires an eternal cause: a first mover that moves without itself being moved — the Unmoved Mover. It moves as a final cause, as an object of desire moves a desirer, and its life is pure actuality: thought thinking itself. It is not a creator God and takes no interest in the world — a point later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theologians, who adopted much else from Aristotle, had to work hard to reconcile with their own doctrines.

### Logic and the syllogism

Aristotle invents formal logic. In the *Prior Analytics* he presents the syllogism: a deductive argument where a conclusion follows necessarily from premises, as in "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal." Crucially, he uses letters for terms — A, B, C — making logic about *forms* of argument valid for any content. He catalogues which syllogistic forms are valid and proves it. The works collected as the *Organon* ("instrument") — *Categories*, *On Interpretation*, the *Analytics*, *Topics*, *Sophistical Refutations* — governed logical teaching for over two thousand years. Immanuel Kant still thought logic had made no essential advance since Aristotle, a judgment overturned only in the late 19th century by Frege's very different mathematical logic.

### Eudaimonia and virtue

The *Nicomachean Ethics* asks what the highest human good is and answers: *eudaimonia*. The word is badly translated "happiness." It means flourishing — living well and doing well over a whole life, through activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. Happiness as a feeling is fleeting and passive; eudaimonia is achievement. Aristotle argues from function: as a flautist's good lies in playing well, a human's good lies in exercising our distinctive capacity, reason, excellently.

Virtues divide into virtues of thought (wisdom, understanding) and virtues of character (courage, temperance, generosity). Character virtue is acquired by habit — we become just by doing just acts — and consists in a mean between excess and deficiency, relative to the person and situation. Courage lies between recklessness and cowardice. But, as the source entry cautions, the mean is not mediocrity or a midpoint on a dial: it is the right amount, at the right time, toward the right people, for the right reason — and some acts, such as murder or adultery, admit no mean at all; they are simply bad.

Judging the right amount requires *phronēsis*, practical wisdom: the trained capacity to perceive what a concrete situation demands. It cannot be reduced to rules, which is why Aristotle says young people, lacking experience, cannot yet have it. The tension in the theory is *eudaimonia*'s dependence on fortune: Aristotle concedes that great misfortune — he gives Priam, king of Troy, who lost family and city in old age — can mar a life's flourishing, however virtuous the person. Virtue is necessary but perhaps not sufficient. Book X then crowns contemplation (*theōria*) as the highest activity, raising a scholarly puzzle about how this intellectualist peak fits the ethical virtues of the earlier books.

### Politics and the Poetics

Humans are by nature political animals (*zōa politika*): speech lets us debate justice, not merely signal pain, so the city (*polis*) exists not just for living but for living well. In the *Politics* Aristotle classifies constitutions, studies real ones — his school compiled 158 — and favors a broad middle-class polity as most stable. The work also contains his defenses of natural slavery and of women's inferiority, which the source entry rightly notes are rejected today; they contradict, many argue, his own principles about reason and flourishing.

The *Poetics* analyses tragedy: plot unified around an action, a protagonist falling through error (*hamartia*), and *catharsis* — a contested term, meaning purging, purification, or clarification of pity and fear. Its account of plot still structures writing manuals.

## Signature arguments

**The function argument (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7)**

1. For anything that has a function (*ergon*) — a flautist, a sculptor, an eye — its good lies in performing that function well.
2. If human beings as such have a function, it must be what is distinctive to them, not life as nutrition and growth, which plants share, nor perception, which other animals share.
3. What is distinctive is activity of the soul in accordance with reason — and, where several kinds of excellence are possible, in accordance with the best and most complete of them.
Therefore, the human good, *eudaimonia*, is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life: our characteristic activity, performed excellently.

**Objection.** Humans, critics object, do not obviously have a function at all: tools and organs have functions because they are made or used for something, but no maker assigns a purpose to a whole human life. And even granting that rational activity is distinctive of us, distinctiveness alone does not show it is our *good* — a capacity might be peculiar to humans without its exercise being what makes a life go well.

**Reply.** On Aristotle's behalf, his function is not a purpose imposed by a maker, as a knife's is imposed by a craftsman. It is internal to a life-form: the characteristic activity whose good performance just *is* that form of life going well, as seeing well is the eye's flourishing. Whether rationality can play this role depends on Aristotle's wider teleology of nature, and scholars still debate whether the move from "distinctive" to "good" is earned or assumed — but the argument is not the crude claim that humans were designed for a job.

**The unmoved mover (*Physics* VIII, *Metaphysics* XII)**

1. Motion — change in general — is eternal: it has no beginning and no end, since any supposed first change would already require a prior change in conditions.
2. Whatever is moved is moved by something else, and a series of movers each of which is itself only moved by another would leave the motion unexplained.
3. The series must therefore terminate in something that moves other things without itself being moved, as an object of desire moves a desirer without changing.
Therefore, a first, unmoved mover exists: pure actuality with no unrealised potentiality, moving the heavens as a final cause.

**Objection.** Why must the regress of movers stop at all — why not an eternal series with no first member? And why *one* mover? Aristotle's own astronomy assigns separate movers to the many celestial spheres, so his argument seems at most to establish many unmoved movers, not a single first principle.

**Reply.** The standard defense, developed carefully by later scholastic readers, turns on the kind of series at issue: in a *per se* ordered series, where each member moves only insofar as it is being moved here and now, an infinite regress would leave nothing with underived power to move, so the series cannot regress infinitely. Aristotle's prime mover is then first in order and dignity — the mover of the outermost heaven, on which the whole cosmic order depends — which is compatible with subordinate unmoved movers for the inner spheres. Whether this fully answers the objection, or instead imports later metaphysics into Aristotle, remains a matter of scholarly dispute.

## Key works

Dates are approximate; most treatises took shape during the Lyceum years, 335–322 BCE.

- **Organon** — The six logical works, including the *Categories* and *Prior Analytics*: the invention of formal logic and the theory of the syllogism.
- **Physics** — On nature, change, the four causes, infinity, place, time, and the argument for an Unmoved Mover.
- **Metaphysics** — A composite work (its name, "after the Physics," is an editor's label) on being, substance, form, and first principles. Difficult, unfinished in feel, foundational.
- **On the Soul** (*De Anima*) — The soul as form of the living body; perception, imagination, and intellect.
- **History of Animals**, **Parts of Animals**, **Generation of Animals** — The biological corpus: observation and classification on a scale unmatched until the early modern period.
- **Nicomachean Ethics** — Eudaimonia, virtue, the mean, practical wisdom, friendship, and contemplation. His most read work today.
- **Politics** — The city, constitutions, citizenship, slavery, household, and education; companion and corrective to Plato's *Republic*.
- **Rhetoric** — The art of persuasion analyzed through *ethos*, *pathos*, and *logos*.
- **Poetics** — Tragedy, plot, and catharsis; incomplete (the book on comedy is lost), yet foundational for literary theory.
- **Constitution of the Athenians** — A historical and institutional survey, attributed to Aristotle's school; recovered from an Egyptian papyrus in 1890 and first published in 1891.

Nearly all his published dialogues are lost, surviving only in fragments quoted by later authors — a major gap, since Cicero praised their "river of gold" style.

## In their own words

> Every art, and every science reduced to a teachable form, and in like manner every action and moral choice, aims, it is thought, at some good: for which reason a common and by no means a bad description of the Chief Good is, "that which all things aim at."

— Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.1, trans. D. P. Chase (1861; public domain)

These are the first lines of the *Ethics*, and the whole book unfolds from them. Aristotle starts not with a definition of the good life but with an observation about aiming: medicine aims at health, shipbuilding at a vessel, so human life as a whole presumably aims at something too. The key term is *end* (Greek *telos*) — the "that for the sake of which" everything else is done. His question is what that final end is, the one we want for its own sake and never merely as a means.

## Contributions

Aristotle is philosophy's great systematizer. He defined most sub-fields — logic, metaphysics, ethics, politics, poetics, rhetoric, philosophy of mind and science — and supplied much of their vocabulary: substance, category, essence, actuality, potentiality, cause. He founded formal logic, created empirical biology as a research program, and insisted, against Plato, that knowledge begins from attention to how things appear. His method of starting from *endoxa* — reputable opinions — and testing them remains a model procedure.

## Impact and legacy

No philosopher except Plato rivals his reach. Commentators carried his system through late antiquity; when his works were partly lost to the Latin West, Arabic philosophers — al-Fārābī, Avicenna, Averroes — preserved and transformed them, calling him simply "the Philosopher." Re-translated into Latin in the 12th–13th centuries, his corpus was integrated into Christian theology above all by Thomas Aquinas, making Aristotelian categories the framework of medieval universities. His physics was authoritative until Galileo and Newton dismantled it; Darwin praised Linnaeus and Cuvier as "mere schoolboys" beside Aristotle the biologist.

In the 20th century his ethics returned to the center: the virtue-ethics movement, from Elizabeth Anscombe and Philippa Foot to Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981), revives eudaimonia, habit, and practical wisdom against rule-based moral theories.

## Criticisms and debates

As science, much of Aristotle is wrong: spontaneous generation, geocentrism, heavier bodies falling faster. The deeper criticism, pressed since the Scientific Revolution, is that final causes in physics blocked mechanical explanation — though defenders reply teleology remains legitimate in biology, where function-talk survives Darwin in altered form.

His ethics attracts two objections. The mean can look empty unless phronēsis fills it in, and phronēsis resists codification. And his exclusions are not incidental: slavery and women's subordination are argued positions, and scholars debate whether they are detachable or expose a flaw in trusting reputable opinion. The relation of the *Nicomachean* to the parallel *Eudemian Ethics*, whether Book X's contemplative ideal contradicts the practical ethics, and whether the *Metaphysics* studies being in general or divine substance, all remain live debates.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Eudaimonia means feeling happy."** It is flourishing through excellent activity over a whole life, not a mood.
- **"The mean means moderation in everything."** It is the situationally right response, relative to the agent — and some acts have no mean.
- **"The Unmoved Mover is the Christian God."** It creates nothing, knows no particulars, and cares for nothing; later theologians adapted it heavily.
- **"Aristotle was an empiricist, Plato a rationalist, full stop."** Aristotle's forms, first principles grasped by *nous* (intellect), and teleology make him no simple empiricist; the textbook contrast is a rough map, not the territory.
- **"His books are finished books."** They are edited lecture materials; repetitions, abrupt transitions, and contradictions partly reflect that origin.

## What to read first

1. **Nicomachean Ethics** I–II — the function argument, eudaimonia, virtue and the mean; then X on contemplation and pleasure.
2. **Nicomachean Ethics** VI — on phronēsis, if you continue.
3. **Poetics** — short, sharp, and enjoyable whole.
4. **Politics** I and III — political animal, slavery (read critically), citizenship.
5. **Metaphysics** and **Physics** as guided reference, with commentary.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the function argument (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7): a thing's good lies in performing its *ergon* well; humanity's distinctive activity is reason; therefore *eudaimonia* is rational activity in accordance with virtue over a complete life. Mark each inferential step.
2. Object that humans, unlike knives or eyes, have no maker-assigned function, and that being distinctive does not make rational activity our *good*. Then give Aristotle's reply — function as internal to a life-form — and judge whether it earns the move.
3. Compare Aristotle with Plato on form: separate Forms versus form inseparable from matter. How does each answer Parmenides on change, and whose account better explains this particular horse, acorn, or statue?
4. Apply the doctrine of the mean to a manager deciding how bluntly to criticize an employee's failed project. Identify cowardice, recklessness, and courage relative to this agent and situation — and explain why the mean is not the midpoint.
