## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 570 – c. 495 BCE (conventional; ancient sources disagree wildly)
- **Place:** Born on Samos; emigrated to Croton in southern Italy, where he founded his community
- **School/tradition:** Founder of Pythagoreanism — part philosophical school, part religious brotherhood
- **Known for:** Teaching that reality is structured by number and harmony, and that the soul is immortal and transmigrates — though in his lifetime he was famous chiefly as a religious teacher on the fate of the soul

**Responds to:** [Thales of Miletus](thales-of-miletus.md) · **Answered by:** [Heraclitus](heraclitus-of-ephesus.md), [Empedocles](empedocles.md)

## Biography

If Thales is hard to see clearly, Pythagoras is harder. He wrote nothing, his followers treated his sayings as sacred and secret, and within a generation he was being credited with miracles. Later biographies — by Porphyry and Iamblichus, some eight centuries after his death — present a wonder-worker who charmed animals, was seen in two places at once, and had a golden thigh. Modern scholarship, following Walter Burkert's study of the tradition, strips those layers back to the earlier, more sober testimony of Xenophanes, Heraclitus, Empedocles, Plato, and Aristotle. What survives that filter is still remarkable, but different from the schoolbook mathematician.

Pythagoras was born on Samos, off the Ionian coast, around 570 BCE. Tradition made him a pupil of Thales and sent him traveling to Egypt and Babylon. Study trips were a standard ornament of sage-biographies, so the itinerary cannot be trusted in detail — but contact with Egyptian and Babylonian learning was possible for a well-connected Samian, and Pythagorean mathematics does have Near Eastern roots. Around 530 BCE he left Samos — later stories say fleeing the tyrant Polycrates — and settled in Croton in southern Italy.

There he founded the community that made him famous. It was not a school in the modern sense. Members held property in common, followed rules covering diet, dress, ritual, and silence, observed taboos (the puzzling ban on eating beans among them), and were bound by secrecy. Later tradition (Iamblichus) says recruits spent five years listening to the master from behind a curtain before being admitted to see him. The community was also political: Pythagoreans formed an aristocratic network across southern Italy and exercised civic power, which provoked a violent backlash — meeting houses were burned, members killed or scattered, and Pythagoras withdrew to Metapontum, where he died around 495 BCE. The movement survived him, and was still being developed by Philolaus and Archytas a century later.

That matters for everything below. "Pythagorean" doctrine in our sources is a blend: some may go back to the founder, much belongs to 5th-century followers, and Aristotle usually attributes theories to "the Pythagoreans" rather than to Pythagoras himself. Even the theorem bearing his name cannot be securely credited to him: Babylonian scribes knew the relation over a thousand years earlier, and no early source shows him proving it.

## Major theories and concepts

### The immortal, transmigrating soul

The earliest solid evidence concerns not mathematics but the soul. Xenophanes, a contemporary, satirised him for claiming to recognize a dead friend's soul in a beaten puppy — a joke that only works if Pythagoras was publicly known for teaching *metempsychosis*: the soul (*psychē*) is immortal and, after death, passes into another body, human or animal, in a cycle of rebirth. He was said to remember his own previous lives. The doctrine likely drew on Orphic religion — Greek cult centered on purity, initiation, and the soul's fate — and it explains the community's rules: if souls migrate between species, killing and eating animals risks killing kin, hence dietary restrictions and a purifying way of life. On this picture, philosophy is the care and liberation of the soul, pursued through purity, memory, music, and discipline. Plato's accounts of immortality and rebirth, in the *Phaedo* and the myth of Er in the *Republic*, descend substantially from this stream.

### Number and harmony: "all is number"

Aristotle reports that the Pythagoreans, impressed by numerical patterns in music, concluded that the principles of mathematics are the principles of all things — "all is number." The discovery behind it is concrete and probably genuine: consonant intervals correspond to simple whole-number ratios. Halve a vibrating string (2:1) and you get the octave; 3:2 gives the fifth; 4:3 the fourth. Here a *qualitative* phenomenon — harmony — turned out to be governed by exact *quantitative* structure. The Pythagoreans generalized boldly: if harmony is number, perhaps the heavens are too. They spoke of the *kosmos* — an ordered, beautiful arrangement, a word tradition credits Pythagoras with first applying to the universe — and of a "harmony of the spheres": planets at distances in musical proportion produce a cosmic music we do not hear because it has sounded in our ears since birth.

The tensions are instructive. Aristotle complained that they slid between treating numbers as *patterns* things imitate and as literal *stuff* things are made of. And their mathematics generated a crisis: the diagonal of a square is incommensurable with its side — no ratio of whole numbers expresses √2 — striking at the slogan that all is ratio. Legend turned this into a scandal, even a drowning of the man who revealed it; the drowning is fiction, but the problem was real.

### The way of life

Pythagoreanism was lived before it was theorized. The *akousmata* — terse oral sayings ("do not stir the fire with a knife," "do not eat beans") — governed daily conduct. Some look like primitive taboo, others were later given symbolic readings. Later sources distinguish the *mathēmatikoi*, devoted to learning, from the *akousmatikoi*, centered on sayings and rituals. Whether that split existed in his lifetime is debated, but it captures a real doubleness: a religion of the soul and a research program in mathematics grew from the same root.

## Key works

- **None.** Pythagoras wrote nothing — a gap later tradition filled with forgeries under his name.
- **The *akousmata* (oral sayings):** not a book, but the closest thing to founder-level teaching; preserved piecemeal in later authors.
- **Philolaus of Croton, fragments (5th century BCE):** the first Pythagorean book we know of, and the earliest source in which number cosmology appears in a follower's own words.
- **Testimony in Plato and Aristotle:** our most reliable guides to early Pythagorean doctrine, written within two centuries of Pythagoras' death.

## Contributions

- **Mathematics as a way of knowing reality.** Number became not just a counting tool but the key to nature's order — ancestor of every later claim that the universe is written in mathematics.
- **Connected demonstrative sciences.** Whatever the founder proved, his movement cultivated geometry, arithmetic, music theory, and astronomy as linked, proof-oriented inquiries.
- **The soul-centered philosophical life.** Alongside the Milesian study of nature, Pythagoras established a second model of philosophy: a disciplined communal life aimed at transforming the person, which shaped Plato and Western contemplative traditions.

## Impact and legacy

Plato is the great conduit. The *Timaeus* builds the cosmos on mathematical proportion; the *Phaedo* argues for an immortal soul shaped by Pythagorean ideas; his Academy prized mathematics. From Plato, Pythagorean themes passed into Neoplatonism, early Christian thought, and medieval education, where music and astronomy sat in the quadrivium beside arithmetic and geometry. Kepler, in the 17th century, revived the harmony of the spheres in his search for planetary laws, and found real laws while looking. The less happy legacy is numerology: the slide from "nature exhibits mathematical order" to magical number symbolism, which also begins here.

## Criticisms and debates

Heraclitus sneered that Pythagoras practiced inquiry "more than anyone" but made it mere polymathy — an early charge that the learning was borrowed. Aristotle's criticism is the serious one: Pythagoreans never explained *how* numbers constitute things, confusing model with material. Modern debates are dominated by the source problem. How much of "Pythagoreanism" is Pythagoras? Burkert argued the founder was primarily a charismatic religious teacher, the mathematics largely followers' work; others defend an earlier scientific Pythagoras. Honest accounts keep a visible line between the 6th-century man and the legend that swallowed him.

## Misreadings to avoid

- **"Pythagoras proved Pythagoras' theorem."** The relation was known in Babylon a millennium earlier; no secure evidence credits him with the theorem or a proof.
- **"Pythagoras was a mathematician who dabbled in religion."** For his contemporaries the priorities ran the other way: he was famous for the soul, rebirth, and the way of life.
- **Treating later biographies as history.** Porphyry and Iamblichus are precious evidence for what later ages *believed*, not reliable records of the 6th century.
- **"All is number" as modern physics.** It was a metaphysical and religious vision of cosmic order, not an empirical research method in the modern sense — even though it helped inspire one.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct the inference from musical ratios — octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3 — to "all is number." Which premise generalizes from harmony to the whole *kosmos*, and what observation is supposed to support that bold step?
2. Aristotle objects that Pythagoreans slide between numbers as patterns things imitate and numbers as stuff things are made of. Add the crisis of √2, inexpressible as a whole-number ratio. Can a Pythagorean reply to both without abandoning the slogan?
3. Compare Pythagoras with Thales: how does replacing a material *archē* with number change what explanation is — and what new problem about the relation between number and physical things does it create?
4. A streaming service reduces a song entirely to numbers and predicts listeners' responses from them. Apply Aristotle's distinction: has it shown the music *is* number, is *modelled by* number, or something else? Defend your verdict.
