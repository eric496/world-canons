# Protagoras and the Sophists

*Era: Ancient Philosophy (c. 600 BCE – c. 270 CE) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** Protagoras c. 490 – c. 420 BCE; Gorgias c. 483 – c. 375 BCE (conventional)
- **Place:** Protagoras from Abdera in Thrace; Gorgias from Leontini in Sicily; both traveled and taught across the Greek world, above all at Athens
- **School/tradition:** The Sophists — professional traveling teachers of rhetoric, argument, and political skill in the age of the Greek democracies
- **Known for:** The man-measure doctrine — "man is the measure of all things" — and the professionalization of rhetoric and higher education

**Responds to:** Eleatic logic, turned against itself in Gorgias' *On Non-Being* · **Answered by:** [Socrates](socrates.md), [Plato](plato.md), [Pyrrho & the Skeptics](pyrrho-of-elis-and-the-skeptics.md)

## Biography

The Sophists were not a school with a founder and a doctrine but a profession: itinerant experts who, from about the middle of the 5th century BCE, offered for fees the higher education the Greek city-states did not provide. Their timing was not accidental. In democratic Athens, advancement ran through the assembly and the lawcourts, where success belonged to whoever could speak persuasively; in a world of autonomous cities and shifting alliances, skill in argument was a marketable technology. The Sophists sold it, and charged handsomely — which their critics never let them forget.

**Protagoras of Abdera**, the most famous of them, was the first, Plato says, to call himself a Sophist and to teach openly for money. He visited Athens repeatedly over some forty years, moved in Pericles' circle, and was, by tradition, entrusted with drafting the laws for the panhellenic colony of Thurii in 444/3 BCE — a mark of real political standing. He wrote works *On the Gods*, *Truth* (also called the *Refutations*), and *Antilogiae* ("Opposing Arguments"), among many others; everything is lost except quotations. The dramatic tradition adds a prosecution for impiety — his book on the gods was said to have been burned in the agora and Protagoras himself to have drowned fleeing Athens by ship — but the story appears in late sources and conflicts with Plato, who shows him dying honored at about seventy; most scholars treat the trial as doubtful at best.

**Gorgias of Leontini** arrived at Athens in 427 BCE as an ambassador seeking aid against Syracuse, and stunned the city with his oratorical style — balanced antitheses, rhyming clause-endings, poetic diction imported into prose. He taught rhetoric across Greece, amassed great wealth, and was said to have lived past a hundred. Other major Sophists include **Prodicus** of Ceos, famous for fine distinctions between near-synonyms; **Hippias** of Elis, a flamboyant polymath; **Antiphon**, who probed the tension between law and nature; and **Thrasymachus**, whom Plato immortalised as the advocate of "justice is the advantage of the stronger."

## Major theories and concepts

### The man-measure doctrine

Protagoras' *Truth* opened with the most quoted sentence of the Sophistic movement: "Of all things the measure is man: of the things that are, that they are, and of the things that are not, that they are not" (fragment B1, preserved by Plato and Sextus Empiricus). Plato's *Theaetetus* develops it as a thoroughgoing relativism: as the same wind is cold to one person and not to another, so each perceiver's experience is true *for that perceiver*, and there is no standpoint outside perception from which to call one appearance simply false. Whether Protagoras intended the doctrine only for perception or for values and social questions too is debated. Plato also saddles him with a radical ontology — a secret doctrine of universal flux in which nothing *is* anything in itself, qualities arising only in encounters between perceiver and perceived. On the social reading, the doctrine underwrites democracy: if no one has privileged access to how things are, political judgment belongs to everyone, a conclusion Plato has Protagoras defend in the *Protagoras* through a myth in which Zeus distributes shame and justice to all humans alike — hence every citizen has a share of political virtue.

### Making the weaker argument the stronger, and opposite arguments

Protagoras was credited with the promise to "make the weaker argument stronger" — to take the apparently losing side of a case and win with it. Caricatured by Aristophanes and Aristotle as teaching people to make the worse cause defeat the better, the practice is better understood as the discipline of *antilogia*: for every argument there is an opposing argument, and the trained speaker constructs both. The anonymous *Dissoi Logoi* ("Double Arguments"), a surviving sophistic exercise text, works through paired speeches — good and bad, just and unjust are the same and are not the same. This two-sided technique is the ancestor both of the philosophical method of arguing in *utramque partem* and of modern adversarial legal training.

### Gorgias: non-being and the power of logos

Gorgias' *On Non-Being* (or *On Nature*) argues, with obvious Eleatic equipment turned against its owners, three theses: nothing exists; if anything existed, it could not be known; if it could be known, it could not be communicated. Whether it is a parody of Parmenides, a serious demolition of Eleatic logic, or a demonstration-piece showing that argument can prove anything, is still undecided. His *Encomium of Helen* performs the same power positively: Helen is blameless whether she acted by fate, force, love — or persuasion, since *logos* is "a powerful lord" that can stop fear, remove pain, and compel the soul; the persuaded mind is as much forced as the seized body. Gorgias thereby made persuasion itself a philosophical problem: speech is not a transparent medium for truth but a force, like a drug, acting on souls.

### Nomos and physis

Across sophistic writing runs the contrast between *nomos* (law, custom, convention) and *physis* (nature). Antiphon argues that the laws are agreements that often run against nature, and that a man does best to follow nature when unobserved. Others drew opposite morals — Hippias calls law a tyrant over nature, while Protagoras' myth treats civic convention as humanity's salvation. The debate is the first sustained European reflection on whether morality and institutions are natural or made, and it shapes Plato's *Republic* as its grand counter-argument.

## Key works

- Protagoras, ***Truth* / *Refutations*** and ***On the Gods*** (mid–late 5th century BCE) — the man-measure doctrine and a famous agnostic opening: "Concerning the gods I cannot know either that they exist or that they do not." Lost except for fragments and reports (Diels–Kranz 80).
- Protagoras, ***Antilogiae*** — model opposing arguments on standard questions. Lost.
- Gorgias, ***On Non-Being*** (mid–late 5th century BCE; ancient testimony dates it c. 444 BCE) — the threefold anti-Eleatic treatise; survives in two paraphrases, by Sextus Empiricus and in the pseudo-Aristotelian *On Melissus, Xenophanes, and Gorgias*.
- Gorgias, ***Encomium of Helen*** and ***Defense of Palamedes*** — surviving display speeches; the primary evidence for his theory of persuasion.
- Anonymous, ***Dissoi Logoi*** (c. 400 BCE) — a surviving workbook of two-sided arguments, the closest thing to a sophistic textbook we possess.

## Contributions

The Sophists created professional higher education in the West. Protagoras contributed to grammar — distinguishing the genders of nouns and the moods of sentences — and to the systematic study of argument; Gorgias effectively founded artistic prose and rhetoric as a theorized art. Collectively they shifted philosophy's center from the cosmos to the human being: language, education, law, and the justification of institutions became philosophical problems because they made them so. The *nomos/physis* debate, the analysis of persuasion, and the first relativist epistemology all begin here.

## Impact and legacy

Their influence runs through everything after them, largely through their enemies. Plato defined philosophy against the Sophists — paid teaching, rhetoric without knowledge, relativism — yet his dialogues preserve most of what we know of them. Aristotle codified rhetoric in reaction. Through Isocrates, the sophistic model of rhetorical education became the standard schooling of the Roman world and, via the liberal arts, of the European university. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries scholars — Hegel and Grote first, then Kerferd's *The Sophistic Movement* (1981) — rehabilitated them as an enlightenment: democratic educators and serious thinkers traduced by a hostile tradition.

## Criticisms and debates

Plato's charge-sheet: they sell the appearance of wisdom without knowledge, flatter audiences, make relativism self-refuting (the *peritrope* argument in the *Theaetetus*: if every belief is true, then the belief that Protagoras is wrong is true, so his doctrine refutes itself), and corrupt politics by teaching success without justice. The modern debates concern how much of this is fair. Does the man-measure doctrine really collapse into self-refutation, or can a sophisticated relativism — true-for-a-person, or the expert as "measure" by outcomes, as Protagoras suggests with the doctor and the teacher — survive it? Was *On Non-Being* parody or philosophy? And the largest question: whether "the Sophists" were ever a coherent movement at all, or a category invented by Plato and inherited uncritically ever since.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Sophist" did not originally mean a cheat: *sophistēs* meant an expert or sage; the sneer is Plato's legacy.
- Protagoras was not a skeptic who thought nothing could be known or taught: he charged fees precisely because he claimed to teach political excellence, and his myth defends democratic competence.
- Making the weaker argument stronger is a debating and forensic technique, not a confession of dishonesty — its modern descendants include moot courts and steelmanning.
- The impiety trial and book-burning of Protagoras are late, doubted traditions, not established fact.

## What to read first

Read Plato's *Protagoras* (for the man himself and the Great Speech) and the first part of the *Theaetetus* (for the man-measure doctrine and its refutation) — remembering that the host is also the prosecutor. Then read Gorgias' *Encomium of Helen* in translation, the surviving fragments of Protagoras (Diels–Kranz 80), and the *Dissoi Logoi*, before turning to Kerferd's *The Sophistic Movement* for the modern reassessment.
