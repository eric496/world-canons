## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 341–270 BCE
- **Place:** Born on Samos; taught in Mytilene and Lampsacus, then Athens
- **School/tradition:** Epicureanism, founder of the Garden in Athens
- **Known for:** A secular therapy for anxiety — pleasure rightly understood as freedom from pain and fear, grounded in an atomist physics with no divine intervention and nothing to fear in death.

**Responds to:** [Democritus](democritus-of-abdera.md) · **Answered by:** Lucretius and the later Epicurean tradition (no entry in this repo)

## Biography

Epicurus was born on the island of Samos in c. 341 BCE to Athenian parents. Tradition presents him as largely self-formed, though he encountered Democritean atomism, probably through Nausiphanes, and reacted against Plato's Academy and against popular religion. After teaching in Mytilene and Lampsacus, he bought a house and garden in Athens in c. 306 BCE and founded the community simply known as the Garden.

The Garden mattered as much as the doctrines. Unlike Plato's Academy or Aristotle's Lyceum, it admitted women and enslaved people as philosophical companions, and it was organized around friendship, shared simple meals, and withdrawal from competitive public life. Members memorized concise summaries and treated philosophy as a daily practice rather than a lecture subject. Epicurus lived the frugality he preached, suffered chronic ill health, and met death in c. 270 BCE with a famously composed letter claiming a happy final day despite severe pain.

He was extraordinarily prolific — about 300 works, almost all lost. What survives does so by two accidents. First, Diogenes Laertius (3rd century CE) copied three letters and a set of maxims into Book X of his *Lives*. Second, Lucretius (1st century BCE) turned Epicurean physics and ethics into the Latin poem *On the Nature of Things*, preserving the system in fuller detail. Charred papyri from Herculaneum have added fragments, but our picture still depends heavily on summaries made for insiders.

## Major theories and concepts

### Pleasure as the absence of pain: aponia and ataraxia

Epicurus is a hedonist — pleasure is the goal of life — but he redefines the term. The highest pleasure is not intense stimulation but a stable condition: *aponia*, no pain in the body, and *ataraxia*, no disturbance in the mind. A feast can produce a brief spike of pleasure followed by indigestion, expense, dependence, and anxiety; bread, water, and conversation with friends can restore the painless condition with almost no downside.

From this follows his classification of desires. Some are natural and necessary (food, shelter, friendship); some are natural but unnecessary (varied, luxurious food); some are neither natural nor necessary (fame, unlimited wealth, political power), resting on empty opinion. The practical rule is calculation: choose the course that yields the greatest net pleasure over a whole life, which often means enduring a small pain now or refusing a tempting pleasure now. The tension is obvious: is a life defined negatively, by what it avoids, rich enough? Epicurus replies that once pain and fear are removed, pleasure "does not increase, it only varies" — a serene life is complete, not empty.

### Atomism and the swerve (clinamen)

Following Democritus, Epicurus holds that reality consists of atoms — indivisible bodies of different shapes and sizes — moving in void. Everything, including the soul, is a temporary compound of atoms; compounds form, decay, and dissolve. This explains change without invoking purpose, design, or divine craftsmanship.

He adds one famous modification: atoms falling through the void occasionally swerve at no fixed time or place — the *clinamen*, or swerve. In Lucretius the swerve does two jobs. It lets atoms collide and form compounds instead of falling in parallel forever, and it breaks an unbroken chain of cause and effect, leaving room for voluntary action. The tension is that a random swerve seems a poor foundation for responsible agency: chance is not the same as freedom. Scholars still debate how central the free-will role was for Epicurus himself, as opposed to Lucretius and later readers — and the evidence itself needs flagging: the swerve is attested only in Lucretius and Cicero, not in any surviving text of Epicurus, and some scholars treat it as a later Epicurean development rather than the founder's own doctrine.

### The tetrapharmakos and the fear of the gods

Later Epicureans summarized the therapy as the *tetrapharmakos*, the "four-fold remedy": do not fear god; do not worry about death; good things are easy to get; terrible things are easy to endure.

Epicurus does not deny the gods. He argues they exist as blessed, imperishable beings, and precisely because they are perfectly blessed they cannot be troubled by managing the universe, rewarding worshippers, or punishing sinners. Storms and diseases are natural events with natural causes. The point is therapeutic: if the gods do not intervene, religious fear loses its object.

### Death is nothing to us

The second remedy is the most quoted argument in the school: "Death is nothing to us, since when we are, death has not come, and when death has come, we are not." Death is the dissolution of the atomic compound that makes sensation possible; where there is no sensation, there can be no suffering. The argument targets the fear of *being dead*, imagined as if one will be present to endure darkness or judgment. A modern objection — that death can still harm us by depriving us of future goods — is a live debate Epicurus' sensation-based premise does not by itself settle.

### Friendship, justice, and withdrawal

Friendship is the greatest security a life can have, and the Garden embodied it. Justice, by contrast, has no absolute existence: it is a mutual agreement neither to harm nor be harmed, valuable because it removes fear, and it changes when circumstances make an agreement useless. Politically this counsels caution: ambition breeds rivals and anxiety, so the sage will generally "live unnoticed." The tension is between prizing communal friendship and retreating from the wider city.

## Key works

- **Letter to Menoeceus** (date uncertain; preserved in Diogenes Laertius X) — The short ethics letter: pleasure, desire, death, and the gods. The best primary text to start with.
- **Letter to Herodotus** (preserved in Diogenes Laertius X) — Not the historian: a summary of physics — atoms, void, and perception — written for students who could not study the longer works. (The swerve, discussed above, appears in none of Epicurus' surviving writings.)
- **Letter to Pythocles** (preserved in Diogenes Laertius X) — On celestial and meteorological phenomena, offering multiple natural explanations rather than one mythic cause; its authorship in detail is debated.
- **Principal Doctrines** (40 short maxims, preserved in Diogenes Laertius X) — The authorized catechism of the school, meant to be memorized.
- **On Nature** (Epicurus' major work in 37 books; lost except for Herculaneum fragments) — The full system from which the letters are abridgements.
- **Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things*** (1st century BCE) — Not by Epicurus, but the fullest surviving exposition of his school, and a masterpiece in its own right.

## In their own words

> Accustom yourself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply awareness, and death is the privation of all awareness; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an unlimited time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality. […] Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not.

— Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* §124–125, trans. Robert Drew Hicks (1925; public domain)

This is the most famous piece of Epicurean therapy: the argument meant to remove the fear of death. Notice the logic is strictly materialist — good and evil exist only where there is sensation, and death is the end of sensation, so there is no subject left for death to harm. The payoff is not gloom but enjoyment: once the craving for immortality is gone, a finite life can be savoured as complete in itself.

## Contributions

Epicurus fused physics and ethics into a therapeutic system in which natural science exists chiefly to dissolve fear. He offered the ancient world's most fully secular, naturalistic answer to living without anxiety: no providence, no immortal soul, no post-mortem judgment — yet a positive account of happiness. He also modelled an unusually inclusive philosophical community and built an early form of hedonistic consequentialism, weighing actions by long-term effects on pain and pleasure.

## Impact and legacy

Epicurean communities lasted centuries across the Roman world; his doctrine was even carved on a portico in Oenoanda (2nd century CE). Early Christian writers attacked Epicurus as the archetypal atheist and voluptuary, fixing his distorted reputation. Lucretius' poem, recovered in 1417, fed Renaissance naturalism. In the 17th century Pierre Gassendi rehabilitated a Christianised atomism, influencing early modern science and, through Locke and later utilitarians, secular ethics. His classification of desires and death argument remain standard starting points today.

## Criticisms and debates

Ancient rivals, especially Stoics and Cicero, objected that pleasure is too base a goal, that a painless condition is not pleasure but a neutral state, and that withdrawal abandons civic duty. Plutarch argued it makes a rich life impossible by ruling out honor and public service.

Modern debates cluster around the swerve (physics, free will, or both?), whether "katastematic" (static) pleasures such as ataraxia can outrank active enjoyments, and whether the death argument confuses *experiencing* harm with *being harmed* by deprivation.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Epicurean" as a label for gourmet indulgence is almost the opposite of his teaching: he prized bread, water, cheese on feast days, friends, and philosophy.
- He did not preach "eat, drink, and be merry, for tomorrow we die." Because death ends sensation, prudence, not licence, follows.
- He was not an atheist in the simple modern sense: his gods exist, but are indifferent and irrelevant to us.
- Ataraxia is not apathy or numbness; it is active, grateful enjoyment undisturbed by false fears.

## What to read first

1. *Letter to Menoeceus* (about 10 pages) — the ethics in Epicurus' own summary.
2. *Principal Doctrines* — read slowly; each maxim compresses an argument.
3. Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things*, Books I–III — atoms, soul, and death at full length.
4. A modern introduction ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/), "Epicurus") for the swerve and pleasure debates.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct "death is nothing to us": good and evil require sensation; death is the dissolution of the atomic compound and so the privation of sensation; therefore no subject remains for death to harm. Identify the premise a critic should attack.
2. The deprivation objection replies that death harms by removing future goods, whether or not the loss is experienced. Epicurus' side can invoke Lucretius' symmetry argument — we do not dread the eternity before birth, so why dread the eternity after death? Evaluate both moves.
3. Compare Epicurus with Democritus: both build on atoms and void, but Epicurus adds the uncaused swerve. What problems — collisions, voluntary action — is it meant to solve, and why might randomness fail to deliver responsibility?
4. Apply Epicurus' classification of desires to a smartphone upgrade, a promotion campaign, or a luxury purchase: sort the desires involved as natural-and-necessary, natural-but-unnecessary, or neither, and calculate net pleasure over a whole life.
