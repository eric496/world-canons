## At a glance

- **Dates:** Seneca c. 4 BCE – 65 CE; Epictetus c. 50 – c. 135 CE; Marcus Aurelius 121 – 180 CE
- **Place:** The Roman Empire — Córdoba and Rome (Seneca), Hierapolis and Nicopolis (Epictetus), Rome and the Danube frontier (Marcus)
- **School/tradition:** Stoicism, late ("Roman") phase; heirs of Zeno of Citium, Cleanthes, and Chrysippus through the Greek and early Roman schools
- **Known for:** Turning Stoic doctrine into a practical discipline of life: the dichotomy of control, philosophy as daily training (*askēsis*), and counsel addressed to slaves and emperors alike

**Responds to:** [Zeno of Citium & the Stoics](zeno-of-citium-and-the-stoics.md) · **Answered by:** [Montaigne](../03-early-modern/michel-de-montaigne.md)

## Biography

The three great voices of Roman Stoicism span the whole social ladder of the Empire, and their lives test the philosophy in three different currencies: power, slavery, and supreme power.

**Seneca the Younger** was born in Córdoba, Spain, son of Seneca the Elder, a famous teacher of rhetoric. Brought to Rome, he rose as a senator and orator, was exiled to Corsica by the emperor Claudius (officially for adultery with the emperor's niece — politics at least as much as morals), and was recalled to become tutor, then chief adviser and speechwriter, to the young Nero. For years he was among the most powerful and richest men in the Empire, a position that sat ever more awkwardly with his preaching of poverty of spirit; after the Burrus partnership ended and Nero's reign curdled, Seneca tried repeatedly to retire and hand back his fortune. In 65 CE, implicated — probably falsely — in the Pisonian conspiracy to kill Nero, he was ordered to commit suicide, and died by opening his veins in imitation of Socrates, dictating to secretaries to the end, according to Tacitus' famous account.

**Epictetus** was born in Hierapolis in Phrygia and brought to Rome as a slave of Epaphroditus, a wealthy freedman who served as Nero's secretary. Tradition says his lameness came from his master breaking his leg — offered by Epictetus himself as a lesson: he calmly warned during the torture, "you will break it," and after, "did I not tell you?" Permitted to study under the Stoic Musonius Rufus, he was freed after Nero's death, taught in Rome until the emperor Domitian expelled the philosophers (c. 93 CE), and then founded a school at Nicopolis in Greece that drew students from across the Empire — including the young Arrian, whose lecture notes became the *Discourses*. He lived simply, adopted an orphaned child in old age, and left no writings of his own: everything we have is Arrian's record and abridgement.

**Marcus Aurelius**, the philosopher-emperor, was adopted into the imperial succession by Antoninus Pius and reigned from 161 to 180 CE. His reign was consumed by plague (the Antonine Plague, which may have killed his co-emperor Verus and finally Marcus himself) and by wars on the Danube frontier. On campaign he wrote in Greek, in private, the work we call the *Meditations*: not a treatise but a journal of self-exhortation never meant for any reader. He died on campaign in 180 CE, succeeded by his son Commodus — a choice his admirers have struggled to forgive.

## Major theories and concepts

### The dichotomy of control

Epictetus opens the *Enchiridion* ("Handbook") with the school's master distinction: "Some things are up to us and some are not up to us. Up to us are opinion, impulse, desire, aversion — in a word, our own doings. Not up to us are body, property, reputation, office — in a word, whatever is not our own doing." Freedom and misery alike follow from applying the distinction: misery is craving or fearing what is not up to us; freedom is wanting only what is — one's own judgments and choices (*prohairesis*). Seneca's version is temporal: the past is beyond recall, the future uncertain, and only the present is ours to use. Marcus applies the same cut to imperial scale: the emperor controls his response to events, not the events.

### Indifferents, and "preferred" indifferents

Health, wealth, reputation, and life itself are, strictly, **indifferent** (*adiaphora*) — neither good nor evil, since good and evil reside only in virtue and vice, in how things are used. But Stoics are not required to pretend all indifferents are equal: some are "preferred" (*proēgmena*) because they are according to nature and give virtue material to act on. Seneca's apologia for his own wealth rests exactly here: riches are an indifferent that a wise man may hold, provided they hold nothing of him — he admits, in *De Vita Beata* (in paraphrase), that he is not a wise man, only a man living as one who is becoming so should.

### Philosophy as training: the three disciplines and the exercises

For Epictetus, Stoicism divides into disciplines of desire, action, and assent: train what you want (only the controllable), how you act (with justice toward others), and what impressions you accept (test every appearance before assenting: "You are but an impression, and not at all what you appear to be"). Around these disciplines the Roman Stoics build a repertoire of practical exercises modern readers still use: **negative visualization** (*praemeditatio malorum*, rehearsing losses in advance so they cannot ambush you); **the view from above** (Marcus repeatedly zooms out to see human affairs from the perspective of the cosmos); **memento mori**; evening self-examination (Seneca reviews his whole day "when the lamp is taken away"); and the reserve clause — intending every action "fate permitting."

### Nature, the cosmopolis, and duty

The old Stoic physics survives as an ethical frame: the universe is a rational, providential order (*logos*), and the human good is "living according to nature" — one's own rational nature and the nature of the whole. Hence the **cosmopolis**: every rational being is a fellow-citizen of one cosmic city, which grounds both Marcus' injunctions against anger at wrongdoers ("what is bad for the swarm is bad for the bee") and Epictetus' role-ethics: we are each assigned parts — son, brother, citizen, slave, emperor — and excellence is playing one's part well. Seneca's *On Anger* adds the school's most developed applied topic: anger as temporary madness, a judgment to be corrected, never a tool of rule.

## Key works

- Seneca, ***Letters to Lucilius*** (c. 62–65 CE) — 124 letters of moral coaching to a friend: time, death, wealth, reading, self-examination. The most readable entrance into Roman Stoicism.
- Seneca, ***Essays (Dialogues)***, incl. *On the Shortness of Life*, *On Anger*, *On Providence*, *On the Happy Life* (c. 40s–60s CE) — treatises, mostly consolatory and paraenetic, addressed to family and friends.
- Seneca, ***Tragedies*** (dates uncertain) — violent dramas (Medea, Phaedra) that probe passion from inside; their relation to his philosophy is a standing puzzle.
- Epictetus, ***Discourses*** (recorded by Arrian, c. 108 CE) — four surviving books (of eight) of classroom teaching: freedom, impressions, training.
- Epictetus, ***Enchiridion*** (Arrian's abridgement) — fifty-three short chapters; the dichotomy of control in pocket form.
- Marcus Aurelius, ***Meditations*** (written 170s CE; Greek title, if any, unknown — "to himself") — twelve books of private self-address from the frontier wars.

## In their own words

> Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil. But I who have seen the nature of the good that it is beautiful, and of the bad that it is ugly, and the nature of him who does wrong, that it is akin to me, not only of the same blood or seed, but that it participates in the same intelligence and the same portion of the divinity, I can neither be injured by any of them, for no one can fix on me what is ugly, nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him.

— Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II.1, trans. George Long (1862; public domain)

Marcus opens Book II with a morning rehearsal: expect difficult people, and decide in advance how to meet them. The move to notice is the double step — first the diagnosis (they act from ignorance of good and evil), then the refusal of anger grounded in kinship: the wrongdoer shares the same reason and divinity. This is Stoicism as daily practice, written by an emperor to himself, not a treatise for the public.

## Contributions

The Roman Stoics transformed Stoicism from a technical system into a portable practice. The founders' logic and physics recede; what advances is moral psychology — the analysis of impressions, judgments, and passions as things we do and can retrain — and a literature of spiritual exercises unmatched in antiquity. Epictetus' dichotomy of control is arguably the most influential single distinction in the history of practical philosophy. Seneca effectively created the personal letter as a moral instrument, and gave Latin its classic vocabulary of time, conscience, and self-scrutiny.

## Impact and legacy

Seneca was read continuously: late antiquity even forged a correspondence between him and St Paul, earning him honorary near-Christian status ("Seneca noster" — "our Seneca" — to Tertullian), and Montaigne's essays are saturated with him. Epictetus shaped Enlightenment moral writing and, in the twentieth century, Admiral James Stockdale credited the *Enchiridion* with sustaining him through years as a prisoner of war in Vietnam. The founders of cognitive behavioral therapy — Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck — explicitly cite Epictetus' dictum that men are disturbed not by things but by their views of things as the ancestor of their method. Marcus' *Meditations*, unpublished in his lifetime and nearly lost, became the most widely read philosophical notebook in the world and the centerpiece of Stoicism's modern popular revival.

## Criticisms and debates

The ancient charge, revived ever since, is quietism: does a philosophy of accepting what is not up to us reconcile slaves to slavery and subjects to tyrants? Epictetus' freedom is entirely internal — a critic may ask whether the enslaved man who is "free in his mind" has been liberated or consoled. Seneca's life invites the opposite charge: the preacher of frugality who amassed a fortune at a tyrant's court is philosophy's classic case of hypocrisy — though his defenders, ancient and modern, note that he knew it, wrote about it, and tried to resign. Under Marcus, Christians were persecuted in the Empire; how far the philosopher-emperor personally bears responsibility remains debated among historians. Philosophically, critics press the tension between determinism and the supposedly "up to us" faculty of choice, and whether the extirpation of the passions is humanly possible — or desirable — at all.

## Misreadings to avoid

- "Stoic" in the modern sense (grim, emotionless endurance) is a caricature: the Roman Stoics allow rational joy, affection, and grief corrected, not abolished — Seneca weeps, then writes about it.
- The dichotomy of control is not passivity: it redirects effort wholly onto the part that is ours, including determined action in the world.
- Marcus did not write the *Meditations* as a book for readers: its repetitions and contradictions are a man arguing with himself, which is precisely its value.
- Preferred indifferents do not smuggle wealth back as a good: Seneca's fortune was a standing scandal to his contemporaries, not a licensed conclusion of his doctrine.

## What to read first

Start with Epictetus' *Enchiridion* (an evening's reading), then a selection of Seneca's *Letters* — 1 (on time), 47 (on slaves), 63 (on grief) — with *On the Shortness of Life*. Then Marcus' *Meditations* Book 2 and 4. Return to Epictetus' *Discourses* Book 1 for the full classroom voice once the pocket version has whetted your appetite.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Epictetus' dichotomy of control from the *Enchiridion*'s opening: opinion, impulse, desire, and aversion are up to us; body, property, reputation, and office are not. Show how misery is then diagnosed as craving or fearing what is not ours.
2. Press the quietism objection: if an enslaved person is "free" only in judgment, has Epictetus liberated or merely consoled them? Construct the Stoic reply using preferred indifferents and duty, then judge whether it answers the political charge.
3. Compare the Roman Stoics with Epicurus: withdrawal to the Garden versus playing one's assigned part — son, citizen, emperor — in the cosmopolis. Which better preserves friendship and security, and at what cost?
4. Apply the dichotomy to a rejected job application or cancelled flight: sort every element into up to you / not up to you, add the reserve clause ("fate permitting"), then state one criticism — for example, that it risks excusing preventable injustice.
