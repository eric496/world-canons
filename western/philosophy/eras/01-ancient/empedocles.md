# Empedocles of Acragas

*Era: Ancient Philosophy (c. 600 BCE – c. 270 CE) · [Era overview](README.md) · [Master index](../../index.md)*

## At a glance

- **Dates:** c. 494 – c. 434 BCE (conventional; ancient reports of his age conflict)
- **Place:** Acragas (modern Agrigento), a wealthy Greek city in Sicily
- **School/tradition:** Pluralist response to Parmenides; heir to both the Eleatic and Pythagorean traditions
- **Known for:** The four "roots" — earth, air, fire, water — eternally mixed and separated by two cosmic forces, Love and Strife

**Responds to:** [Parmenides](parmenides-of-elea.md), [Pythagoras](pythagoras.md) · **Answered by:** [Democritus](democritus-of-abdera.md)

## Biography

Empedocles was a citizen of Acragas, one of the richest Greek cities of the west, famous for its temples and its Olympic victories. Ancient sources portray a man of extraordinary range: poet, physician, political leader, religious prophet, and self-declared wonder-worker — sometimes in the same paragraph. The biographical tradition credits his grandfather with an Olympic horse-racing victory and his father Meton with helping to overthrow the tyrant Thrasydaeus, after which Empedocles himself became a champion of the democracy. He is said to have refused an offer of kingship, to have melted down his wealth into dowries for poor girls, and to have worn a purple robe, golden wreath, and bronze sandals while proclaiming — in verse that survives — "I go about among you an immortal god, no longer mortal." That self-presentation was deliberate: in a world where expertise and divine authority still overlapped, Empedocles claimed both.

Two works were attributed to him in antiquity, a poem *On Nature* and a religious poem, the *Purifications*; many modern scholars suspect they were one poem, or two parts of a single project, and the question remains open. In the *Purifications* he teaches that he is a fallen *daimon*, exiled from the gods for thirty thousand seasons for the sin of bloodshed and meat-eating, wandering through successive incarnations as boy, girl, bush, bird, and fish until purified. His death was correspondingly legendary: he was said to have leapt into the crater of Mount Etna so that, his body vanishing, he would be believed a god — a plan undone, the story goes, when the volcano threw back one of his bronze sandals. The tale was already doubted in antiquity (one source has him dying quietly in the Peloponnese) and is best read as hostile or playful legend rather than fact.

His work survives in fragments — around 150 passages quoted by later authors, chiefly Aristotle, Plutarch, and the commentators — plus one modern windfall: the Strasbourg Papyrus, identified in the 1990s, a first- or second-century CE copy of the poem that suggested the physical and religious material belonged to a single project and filled genuine gaps. In the Diels–Kranz edition his fragments are the B fragments of chapter 31.

## Major theories and concepts

### The four roots and the two forces

Parmenides had argued that nothing can come to be or perish, and that reality must be one and changeless. Empedocles accepts the first half and rejects the second. Nothing genuinely comes into being or is destroyed, he agrees — "there is no birth of any mortal thing, nor any end in destructive death" — but what we call birth and death is the mixing and unmixing of four eternal, unchanging stuffs he calls "roots" (*rhizomata*): earth, air, fire, and water. (Aristotle later renamed them "elements," and the four-element scheme Empedocles founded governed Western physics and medicine for two thousand years.) Each root is itself Parmenidean: ungenerated, imperishable, uniform. Plurality and change are then possible without anything arising from nothing — the roots only rearrange.

Rearrangement needs a cause, and Empedocles supplies two opposing powers. **Love** (*Philia*, also identified with the goddess Aphrodite) draws the roots together into unity; **Strife** (*Neikos*) drives them apart into separation. Neither is a "stuff" alongside the roots; they are the agents of mixture and division, and Empedocles speaks of them almost as rival gods.

### The cosmic cycle

The alternation of Love and Strife generates an eternal cosmic cycle. Under Love's complete rule, the roots fuse into a perfect, undifferentiated Sphere — the world as Parmenides described it, but only as one phase. Strife then gradually enters and separates the roots out into distinct masses; in the intermediate stages, with the roots partly mixed, worlds like ours exist, with mortal creatures in them. Strife reaches its maximum, Love returns, and the cycle repeats forever. Whether the cycle contains one world-formation or two per revolution is still disputed among scholars. The key point is that Empedocles answers Parmenides not by denying his logic but by restricting its scope: permanence belongs to the roots and the cycle, not to any arrangement of them.

### Perception, breathing, and living things

Empedocles explains perception by **effluences**: everything constantly sheds fine films of particles, which fit into pores in the sense organs; we perceive "like by like," fire in the eye seeing fire outside it. He applied the pore model even to breathing, arguing in a famous fragment (B100) — with a comparison to a girl playing with a water-clock (*clepsydra*) — that we breathe through pores in the skin as well as the nostrils. His zoogony, the generation of animals, is stranger: under rising Strife, separate limbs and organs wandered alone ("heads without necks, arms without shoulders"), then combined at random into creatures, many of them monsters — ox-headed men, double-faced beings — of which only the viable combinations survived. Aristotle quotes this with fascination and exasperation; modern readers inevitably compare it to natural selection, though for Empedocles the sorting is pure chance, not adaptation to environment.

### The fallen daimon

In the *Purifications* the physics becomes a religion. The soul is an exiled spirit polluted by bloodshed, doomed to wander through mortal forms for "thrice ten thousand seasons" until, purified, it rejoins the gods. Hence a moral law against killing and eating animals — they may house the soul of a relative — and a path of purification through which the best men rise through incarnations as prophets, poets, physicians, and leaders, "and thereafter arise as gods highest in honour." Empedocles places himself at the end of this ladder, which is why he addresses his fellow citizens as a god among mortals.

## Key works

- ***On Nature*** (mid 5th century BCE) — the physical poem, addressed to his pupil Pausanias: roots, Love and Strife, the cycle, perception, zoogony. Survives in fragments and the Strasbourg Papyrus.
- ***Purifications*** (mid 5th century BCE) — the religious poem: the fallen daimon, reincarnation, prohibitions on bloodshed. Whether it is a separate work is debated; fragments survive in quotation.

## Contributions

Empedocles invented the template of classical physics: a small set of permanent elements whose combinations explain all change. By making motion and plurality compatible with Parmenidean permanence, he set the program that the atomists and Plato would follow — every later pluralist system is a variation on his move. His four elements, mediated by Aristotle and Galenic medicine, structured natural science into the seventeenth century. He also gave Greek thought its most influential image of cosmic history as an eternal cycle driven by opposed principles of attraction and repulsion.

## Impact and legacy

Aristotle treated Empedocles as a major predecessor — criticizing him constantly, which is its own kind of honor — and adopted his four elements into the physics of the sublunary world. Plato's *Timaeus* builds on the four roots. Lucretius praised him in verse as almost divine, and the *Purifications* shaped Orphic and Pythagorean revival currents for centuries. The zoogony fragments made him a touchstone in nineteenth-century debates about evolution's Greek anticipations. The Strasbourg Papyrus made him, unusually for a Presocratic, a philosopher whose text is still growing.

## Criticisms and debates

Aristotle's standing objection is that Empedocles names Love and Strife as causes but uses them inconsistently, letting Love separate and Strife unite as the argument requires, and explaining nothing about *why* the cycle turns when it does. Modern debates include: whether the two poems are one; whether there are one or two zoogonies per cycle; whether Love and Strife are material stuffs or purely efficient forces; and how literally to take the religious teaching alongside the physics — as sincere doctrine, poetic persona, or the two being deliberately continuous.

## Misreadings to avoid

- Love and Strife are not simply "good" and "evil": both are necessary, and both can destroy.
- The four roots are not the claim that matter comes in exactly four flavours of stuff we would recognize — they are eternal, qualitatively distinct, unchanging beings in the Parmenidean sense.
- The Etna suicide is legend, not biography.
- His monster-sorting is not Darwinism: there is no heredity and no adaptation, only random combination and survival.

## What to read first

Read the longest fragments in a standard collection (the B fragments in Kirk, Raven & Schofield, or the Empedocles section of *Early Greek Philosophy*): start with B6 (the roots), B17 (the cycle), B100 (breathing), and the *Purifications* proem B112 ("I go about among you an immortal god"). Then the Strasbourg Papyrus material if your edition includes it.

## Study questions

1. Reconstruct Empedocles' answer to Parmenides: grant that nothing can come from nothing or perish utterly, then show how four eternal roots, mixed and separated by Love and Strife, make birth, death, and plurality possible without genuine generation.
2. Aristotle objects that Empedocles uses Love and Strife inconsistently — each sometimes doing the other's work — and never explains why the cosmic cycle turns when it does. How damaging is this, and what reply could Empedocles give?
3. Compare Empedocles with Parmenides on the Sphere: for Parmenides it is reality's permanent shape, for Empedocles only one phase under Love's complete rule. What does that demotion reveal about Empedocles' whole strategy?
4. Apply the roots model to burning a log or recycling a demolished building. Redescribe "destruction" and "creation" as mixing and separation, then identify one feature of the case the model illuminates and one it cannot explain.
