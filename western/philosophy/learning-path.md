# Learning Path — A Hybrid Spiral Through Western Philosophy

This is the route through the library. It replaces the old era-by-era march (Stages 0–5 in about a year) with a design based on how introductory curricula actually sequence this material (MIT OCW 24.01, Oxford Prelims, Yale's paired Ancient/Modern sequences) and on what the learning-science literature says actually sticks (Dunlosky et al. 2013 — see `sources.md`).

Read the time budget honestly before you start: **this is not a one-year plan.** Counted in full — desk study, the daily card queue, and the assigned listening and watching — it totals **≈454 hours**: either **64 study weeks at ≈7 hours a week all-in** (≈17 calendar months with buffer weeks), or **the same material at 4–5 hours a week stretched across roughly two to two-and-a-half years**. A trimmed 12-month version exists (§10) and costs ≈335 hours at a firm ~6.5–7 hours a week. That is not padding. It is what ~30 authors in primary text, read slowly enough to reconstruct their arguments, actually costs alongside a job — plus the review and media time that makes it stick. §1.5 shows the arithmetic; nothing in this plan hides time in a footnote.

## 1. How the plan works

### 1.1 A chronological spine, five threads on a spiral

**Spine:** you move forward in time, era by era, because later authors are literally replying to earlier ones. Hume is hard to understand without Locke and Descartes; Kant is an answer to Hume; Nietzsche presupposes Schopenhauer and Kant; Foucault presupposes Nietzsche. Chronology is not tidiness — it is what makes the arguments intelligible.

**Spiral:** at every era you revisit the same five problems, so each new author is also a new answer to a question you have already met:

1. **Knowledge** — What can we know? What defeats skepticism? Is induction justified?
2. **Mind** — What is a mind/self, and how does it relate to body and world?
3. **God** — Does God exist? What follows either way for meaning, morality, death?
4. **Ethics** — How should I live? Virtue, duty, consequences, freedom, authenticity?
5. **Politics** — How should we live together? Authority, justice, liberty, power?

These five are the working subset of the nine recurring questions mapped in [themes.md](themes.md) (which adds metaphysics, logic & language, aesthetics and science). Each stage below names its thread focus, and each stage ends with a short **spiral return** (30–45 minutes): re-answer one earlier thread question using the new era's authors. That return is not optional garnish, and it is what stops Stage 1 evaporating by Stage 3. Be precise about *why* it works, though. The meta-analytic evidence for interleaving as such (Brunmair & Richter 2019) is material-dependent: real for discriminating between similar categories, nonsignificant for expository texts, and reversed for word-level material (blocking wins there). Philosophy reading is expository text, so this plan does **not** interleave first-pass reading — new authors are read blocked, one at a time, exactly as the stages sequence them. What the spiral return actually is, and why it earns its time, is *spaced retrieval plus forced comparison*: re-answer an old question from memory, then set two similar positions on the same problem side by side and discriminate between them. Retrieval and comparison are the active ingredients; mixing for its own sake is not.

### 1.2 Two tiers: core and map-level

The library covers 112 figures. **You will not read 112 figures in depth, and the plan does not pretend otherwise.** University semesters don't either — MIT's covers about ten figures in primary text.

- **Core (~30 figures):** read in primary text, in the editions named below, slowly, with a written argument reconstruction for each. These are the load-bearing authors: skip one and later stages wobble.
- **Map-level (everyone else):** cover through their entry in this repo + the paired podcast episode (Adamson, or *Philosophize This!* where Adamson hasn't reached — §3 and each stage's *Listen & watch*) or Kenny section + **one checkpoint question** you can answer in your own words (≈30–45 minutes per figure). Map-level is a deliberate design choice, not a failure. It gives you the shape of the whole tradition — who argued with whom, about what — which is exactly what makes the core reading intelligible.

Core figures are marked ●, map-level figures ○, and map-level figures with an optional short primary excerpt worth doing if the stage is going well are marked ◐.

### 1.3 The three non-negotiables

1. **Never meet Kant, Hegel, Heidegger or late Wittgenstein before their named predecessors.** Kant after Hume. Hegel after Kant (and Fichte at map level). Heidegger after Husserl at map level. *Philosophical Investigations* after *Tractatus* excerpts and the positivists it argues against. Starting with the famous hard book is the single most common way self-taught learners quit.
2. **Every core author = one argument reconstruction in your own words, before any opinion.** Premises → conclusion, book closed, then one paragraph of critique — critique always second, never first.
3. **Do not study by rereading and highlighting.** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated rereading and highlighting *low utility*, summarizing alone likewise. The two techniques rated *high utility* — practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice — are built into the weekly rhythm below. If your review session is re-reading marked pages, you are doing the thing the evidence says doesn't work.

### 1.4 Weekly rhythm (4–5 hours at the desk, plus the queue and the media spine)

| Session | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Primary** | ~2 hrs | Primary text only, slow, with reconstruction notes. No lectures, no videos, no podcast in this slot — ever. |
| **B — Support** | ~1.5 hrs | Secondary support for this week's author (see §3 for timing), any media the stage marks FIT or REPLACE, finish the reconstruction, write the argument cards. |
| **C — Retrieval** | ~1 hr (weekend is fine) | The weekly checkpoint (3 prompts, book closed), the spiral return, and the spaced reconstruction revisits (§2.3). Session C is always about earlier material, never the current page. |
| **Daily queue** | ~10 min, ~6 days/week | Clear the Anki due queue (§2.2). Not a session — phone-on-a-walk counts. This is where the spacing actually happens, and it is not optional. |
| **Media spine** | ~1.5 hrs/week average | The stage's assigned podcast/lecture listening and watching (see §3 and each stage's *Listen & watch*). Mostly ears-on-walks time; it is counted in the budget (§1.5) even when your feet are moving. |

**Media rules — stated once, applied in every stage.** (1) Media is **preview or review around the primary text, never a substitute for it**: a voice before you read orients you; a voice after you read consolidates. If you can summarize a podcast's author but cannot reconstruct the argument from the page, that is failure mode 13 (§11), not study. (2) **Reconstruction before commentary** — the same rule the study guides use: your blank-page reconstruction (§2.3) precedes any interpretive media about that text, so the second voice argues with *your* reading instead of replacing it. (3) Session A stays media-free, always. (4) One spine voice per stage (Adamson, then *Philosophize This!*), supplements à la carte — running five podcasts per figure is system-building (§11), not study.

**Stage discipline:** you may not extend a stage by more than 2 weeks. If a gate isn't passed, use a buffer week (below) — do not quietly live in one era for six months because it's comfortable. Era tunnel vision is a failure mode, not a personality trait.

## 2. The retention system (a daily 10-minute queue + Session C, no teacher needed)

This system is part of the plan, not an accessory. It is what the checkpoints in §5–§9 assume you are doing.

1. **Argument cards, scheduled by FSRS — not by hand.** One Anki deck for the whole plan, cumulative across all stages. Front: a *do*-prompt — “Hume — induction: state the problem in 3 sentences.” Back: numbered premises + conclusion + **one objection + one reply**. About 5 self-made cards per core author (~150 total), plus the decks shipped in [`study/cards/`](study/cards/README.md) — the 40 argument cards and the per-guide checkpoint cards — imported author by author as you reach each figure, never as a day-one dump. A card that asks “when was Kant born?” is a wasted card; dates live in `index.md`. Set Anki up once, like this:
   - **Turn [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) on.** It is Anki's current scheduler (desktop, mobile, web) and the recommended one over the legacy SM-2 — but it is a *toggle* in Deck Options, so switch it on; do not assume it is already active.
   - **Desired retention: 0.90.** Do not raise it: above 0.90 the workload climbs very quickly, and above 0.97 it can become overwhelming.
   - **Learning and relearning steps: short — `10m` (or the default `1m 10m`), nothing of 1 day or more.** Under FSRS, day-plus steps are explicitly not recommended; FSRS sets the first multi-day interval itself.
   - **Buttons: press *Again* on any failed recall, always.** Pressing *Hard* for a failure makes FSRS record a successful-but-effortful recall and inflates every future interval.
   - **Optimize parameters once after ~300–400 reviews, then about monthly.** The optimizer needs a few hundred reviews of *your* history before it can fit you; do not copy someone else's parameters.
   - **Prompts attack each argument from more than one angle** — state it / object to it / what would its author say to X. And keep cards in their place: they *maintain the parts*. Retrieval practice demonstrably beats rereading — and even elaborative concept-mapping — on conceptual questions, not just facts (Karpicke & Blunt 2011; Butler 2010 on transfer), but for conceptual material the integrated test is the blank-page reconstruction (§2.3). Cards are subordinate to it, never a replacement for it.
   
   *What changed, and why:* an earlier version of this plan told you to hand-review each author's cards at +2 days, +1 week, +3 weeks, and stage end. That instruction is **retired for cards** — FSRS individualizes intervals, and hand-scheduling fights it. The +2d/+1wk/+3wk marks survive below, reassigned to reconstructions and checkpoints, where a human-scale rhythm is the right tool.
2. **The daily queue.** Clear Anki's due queue in **~10 minutes, ~6 days a week**, separate from Sessions A–C (a phone on a walk is the normal venue). Do the load arithmetic once so it never frightens you: at this plan's intake — a few new cards a week, and a hard cap of ~5 new cards/day you will rarely approach — the queue stays in single-digit-to-low-teens minutes. (For scale: a sustained 20 new cards/day is what produces ~200 reviews/day; that is a different hobby.) **Backlog rule: if the queue ever backs up, stop adding new cards until it clears.** Missed days happen; the fix is smaller intake, never a raised retention target.
3. **Blank-page reconstruction after every core text — on the successive-relearning rhythm.** 200–400 words, book closed, the argument rebuilt in your own words and in a clearer order if you can — explaining it to someone who hasn't read the passage, not paraphrasing sentence by sentence. Then, and only then, a second paragraph critiquing it. (This is Jim Pryor's standard undergraduate exercise, and his reading rule goes with it: read three times — skim for conclusion and structure, read carefully, then evaluate.) The scheduling principle is **successive relearning** (Rawson & Dunlosky): the active ingredient is *successful retrieval of the same material in multiple separate spaced sessions* — roughly, reconstruct each core argument successfully in **~3 separate sessions**. So re-reconstruct each core argument at **+2 days, +1 week, +3 weeks** after the first pass; Session C is where the later touches live. Two corollaries, both liberating: equal intervals perform about as well as expanding ones, so the marks are a rhythm you can keep, not a mechanism to fetishize; and **one clean successful recall per session is enough** — extra drilling crammed into a single session washes out once spaced relearning happens, so never marathon a reconstruction in one sitting.
4. **Weekly book-closed checkpoint.** In Session C, answer 3 prompts from your current stage's checkpoint list (§5–§9), aloud or in writing, book closed. Score honestly against four levels: *can state / can reconstruct / can compare / can object*. Only the last three count as passing. “I recognise it when I see it” is *can state* at best, and usually not even that. Checkpoints double as the later spaced touches for the stage's early authors (§2.3) — pick prompts that reach back, not just across.
5. **One gate essay per stage: 600–800 words.** A modest thesis, defended clearly. A strong argument for a weaker thesis beats a weak argument for a grand one. **No gate essay = no advancing to the next stage**, however much you have "read." Prompts are suggested at each stage; any question from that stage's checkpoints, argued properly, also works. (Kept at exactly this cadence on the evidence: writing about content aids learning of it, but modestly — Graham, Kiuhara & MacKay 2020 — which supports the gate as consolidation, not a heavier essay schedule that would crowd out primary reading.)
6. **Discussion substitutes, in order of value.** Solo study's weak point is that no one pushes back. Climb this ladder as needed: (a) record a 5-minute phone voice memo teaching the week's argument aloud — if you can't teach it, you don't have it yet; (b) steelman exchange — write the strongest objection to your gate essay *as the opposing author*, then reply; (c) post the gate essay or a reconstruction to a philosophy forum, reading group, or a scheduled AI-critique session with the instruction "find the weakest premise; do not summarise me back" — and treat anything it says as a discussion partner's claim to be verified against SEP or the primary text, never as authority; (d) Magee's interview series (*Men of Ideas*, [*The Great Philosophers*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Philosophers)) after each era block, as a way of hearing a second voice before you write.

## 3. Secondary support, the tools stack — and when to use them

Timing matters. Read a full SEP entry *before* a hard primary text and its interpretation hardens into prejudice: you will find in Nietzsche exactly the nihilist every introduction told you to find. The rule:

- **Before (20–40 min only):** orientation — who, when, what question, key terms. Stop there.
- **Alongside:** the companion paired with that week's reading.
- **After:** the interpretive and debate layer, once you have a reconstruction of your own.

## 4. Editions — buy by translator, not by cover

Cheap reprints of public-domain Victorian translations are the stealth failure mode of self-study: the book is "the same text" and quietly unreadable, or quietly wrong. Rules:

- **Translator + series must be named before you buy.** If the title page names no modern translator and editor, don't buy it.
- Default series: **Hackett** (cheap, student notes, standard on US syllabi) and **Cambridge Texts in the History of Philosophy** (translations commissioned for students, with introduction and glossary). Penguin Classics and Oxford World's Classics are good *when the translator is modern and named*.
- Per-author editions are given in each stage. The consolidated avoid-list:

> **Avoid / flagged:** Jowett's Plato (1871–92 — Victorian, flowery, often lacks Stephanus numbers in reprints); Allan Bloom's *Republic* except comparatively; Haldane & Ross for Descartes (1911 — renders *cogitatio* inconsistently and obscures the argument; the Cambridge edition exists to replace it); Selby-Bigge/Nidditch for Hume's *Enquiry* (superseded by Beauchamp's Clarendon edition); Kemp Smith (1929) or Meiklejohn for Kant's *Critique*, and any unlabelled [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/) *Critique*; Thomas Common's Nietzsche in cheap reprints; **Parshley's *Second Sex* (1953 — abridged by ~15% and mistranslates key terms; use Borde & Malovany-Chevallier, 2010/2011)**; the full *Summa Theologiae* in the old Dominican Fathers translation as a *first* read (it's a reference set, not a course text); Ross's Aristotle translations as a first read (accurate but terse and unhelped); the public-domain Marriott Machiavelli; Barnes's *Being and Nothingness* for technical terms without checking Richmond (2021). **Access note (Japan-realistic):** the core editions above are *owned*, on purpose — library lending windows (~14 days for Open Library) are shorter than this plan's per-author pace. Use Open Library as the first check for secondary and map-level books (with the post-*Hachette* caveat in §3.1: half a million in-copyright titles were removed), Chigasaki City Library for physical loans and reservations, and publisher/Kindle samples to check an edition before buying. The full tools stack is §3.1.

And one non-edition warning that belongs here: ***The Will to Power* is not a book by Nietzsche.** It is a posthumous editorial construction from his notebooks, assembled by his sister's circle. Read it, if at all, as notebooks — never cite it as his considered view.

## Stage 0 — Tools & Problems (3 weeks · 12 hrs desk · ≈17 hrs all-in)

**Purpose:** learn what an argument *is* before meeting 2,500 years of them, and meet all five threads in miniature so the spiral has hooks to hang on.

**Study order**

1. **One intro book, whole, and only one:** Thomas Nagel, *What Does It All Mean?* (1987, ~100 pp) — or Simon Blackburn, *Think* (1999), chs. 1–3 — or Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912). ● (as a tool, not a figure)
   Library orientation alongside: [Russell](eras/05-20th-century/bertrand-russell.md).
2. **The vocabulary of arguments:** valid vs. sound, premise vs. conclusion, *a priori*, necessary vs. contingent, charity (state an opponent's view so they'd accept your statement of it before you attack it). Jim Pryor's guidelines on reading and writing philosophy (free online, under an hour each) — read both now.
3. **A problem sampler, one per thread:** Knowledge — a Gettier-style case (justified true belief that isn't knowledge). Mind — Nagel's "what is it like to be a bat?" in miniature. God — the problem of evil as a triad. Ethics — the trolley problem and Nozick's experience machine. Politics — who should rule, and what would you accept behind a veil of ignorance? No reading list for these yet; the point is to have *your* first answer on record. You will re-answer all five in the capstone.
4. **Set up the system (§2 + §3.1):** Anki with FSRS on and the §2.1 settings (import nothing yet — decks enter per author); a plain Markdown folder, opened in Obsidian, for reconstructions and gate essays; a Zotero library with one collection per stage; a PhilPapers account with a reading list per stage. One-time cost ≈2 hrs, booked in §1.5. And the rule that Session A is primary-only.

**Listen & watch (all inside the 12 desk hours — this stage's media *replaces* orientation reading; it does not add to it):**
- **[BBC *A History of Ideas*](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b04bwydw)** (Radio 4, 2014–15, complete): pick 3–4 question-weeks matching the sampler above (≈4–5 hrs) as **preview**. Its question-first structure — *what makes us human? how can I know anything? how should we live together?* — is this stage's shape in audio. If BBC streaming of the old series is patchy, the collected audiobook edition is the reliable route.
- ***[CrashCourse Philosophy](http://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL8dPuuaLjXtNgK6MZucdYldNkMybYIHKR)*, subset only** (YouTube, free): episodes 1–3 plus the two "How to Argue" follow-ups, ~50 min in week 1. If time pinches, choose Talbot *or* the *History of Ideas* selection as your main orientation voice, not both at full length.
- **Wi-Phi intro videos**: the Critical Thinking series and the opening Epistemology videos, watch-before (~10 min each).
- **[MIT OCW 24.00](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/24-00-problems-of-philosophy-fall-2019/) *Problems of Philosophy* handouts** — Arguments, Skepticism, Personal Identity — as the sampler's structured reading (~30 min each). **These are PDFs, not videos**; OCW's philosophy courses in this plan post notes and handouts, not lectures, wherever the plan points to them.

**Priced option:** Duke's *Think Again I: How to Understand Arguments* (Coursera; Sinnott-Armstrong & Neta) is the one MOOC that directly trains this plan's core skill. (Edinburgh's *Introduction to Philosophy* is deliberately *not* assigned: its free audit was withdrawn in practice.)

**Warning, unchanged in substance:** do not "prepare" with ten *unassigned* hours of overview video; that is failure mode 1 (§11) arriving early. The list above is the whole Stage 0 media diet.

**Exit checkpoints — you can:**
- □ State the difference between validity and soundness, and construct a valid-but-unsound argument of your own.
- □ Reconstruct one short argument premise-by-premise (Nagel's bat point works) in ~200 words, book closed.
- □ Given a fresh paragraph of philosophy, mark its premises and its conclusion correctly.
- □ Define *valid, sound, a priori, necessary* without notes.

**Gate essay (600–800 words):** your current answer to one of the five sampler problems, argued — not surveyed. Keep it. The capstone grades your progress against it.

## Stage 2 — Medieval & Renaissance Bridge (8 weeks · 36 hrs desk · ≈62 hrs all-in · compressed)

**Do not skip this stage, and do not expand it.** Skip it and Descartes' God-proofs, the scholastic vocabulary in every early-modern text, and Locke/Kant on substance become unintelligible. Expand it and you'll still be in the 14th century in month ten. Eight weeks, held firmly.

**Threads in focus:** God (ontological vs. cosmological arguments; faith/reason) and Knowledge (what reason can prove unaided). **Spiral return:** the problem of evil, from Stage 0's triad to Augustine's answer.

- □ State the problem of evil as a triad and give one Augustinian response (evil as privation) — and say what it doesn't solve.

**Gate essay:** "What can reason prove about God unaided — everything, something, or nothing?" Anselm vs. Aquinas vs. one of the bridge figures.

**Known stall point:** Aquinas' form. Fix is above: excerpts in a modern student edition + Feser alongside, Q2 first. If you are reading the *Summa* cover-to-cover, you have left the plan.

## Stage 3 — Early Modern to Kant (16 weeks · 72 hrs desk · ≈111 hrs all-in · the load-bearing stage)

The longest stage, and deliberately so. **Do not compress Stage 3** (§10's trim cuts everywhere else first). Every later stage spends the capital earned here: Nietzsche assumes your Kant, analytic philosophy assumes your Hume, political philosophy assumes your contract theorists.

**Threads in focus:** Knowledge (rationalism/empiricism as *one conversation*, not two teams; induction), Mind (dualism and its discontents), Politics (the social contract — Hobbes first, then Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau). **Spiral returns:** skepticism, now with Descartes answering Montaigne's Pyrrhonism; the God thread, as the ontological and cosmological arguments get their early-modern rerun and Hume's audit.

**Exit checkpoints — the hardest gate; a repeat week is allowed and expected by many learners:**
- □ Reconstruct Descartes' cogito, and state why the "Cartesian circle" is charged against him.
- □ State Locke on primary/secondary qualities and on personal identity, and Berkeley's "master argument" reply to material substance.
- □ **State Hume's problem of induction and one reply** (Kant's, Popper's, or a Humean naturalist reply). This checkpoint is the center of the stage — if you can only pass one, pass this one.
- □ State Hume on causation as constant conjunction, and on miracles (the maxim, quoted or closely paraphrased).
- □ State Kant's categorical imperative in two formulations, apply both to a lying-promise case, and contrast the result with Mill (whom you'll meet properly next stage — the contrast is the spiral warming up).
- □ Explain *synthetic a priori*, and "thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind," in plain English, with Descartes and Hume as the foils Kant is answering.

**Gate essay:** "What, if anything, justifies our expectation that the future will resemble the past?" Hume's problem, and at least two replies, one of them Kant's.

## Stage 4 — Nineteenth Century: System, Revolt, Society (11 weeks · 48 hrs desk · ≈80 hrs all-in)

**Threads in focus:** Ethics and Politics fused (freedom, history, utility vs. duty revisited with real opponents), God as *critique* (religion explained, not just argued). **Spiral returns:** Kant's ethics, now facing Mill and Nietzsche; the contract tradition, now facing Marx and Tocqueville.

### 9.3 Capstone (final 2 weeks, inside Stage 5's 60 desk hours)

**The capstone essay: ~2,000 words.** Take **one** of Stage 0's five problems — knowledge, mind, God, ethics, politics — and answer it again, citing **at least 6 primary authors across at least 3 eras**, including at least one author you initially disagreed with, treated charitably. Mark it against three criteria: (1) reconstruction accuracy — would each author accept your statement of their view? (2) one genuine objection to your own position, considered, not waved at; (3) charity to opponents throughout.

Then put it next to your Stage 0 gate essay on the same problem. The distance between the two documents is the actual product of this plan; the reading was the method.

Submit it somewhere it can be answered: a forum, a reading group, a meetup, or the AI-critique protocol in §2.6(c) ("find the weakest premise; do not summarise me back" — then verify every factual claim it makes). An essay no other mind has pushed against is a draft, whatever its word count.

**Stage 5 exit checkpoints — you can:**
- □ Contrast Frege and Russell on definite descriptions, with one example sentence analyzed both ways.
- □ State the *Tractatus* picture theory and the *Investigations'* "meaning as use" reply, with the builders example or the beetle in the box.
- □ State Quine's attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction and why it threatens positivism from inside.
- □ State Sartre's "existence precedes essence" and Beauvoir's application *and* critique of it in the *Second Sex* introduction.
- □ Set up Rawls' original position, derive the two principles, state Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain objection, and state Singer's contrasting pond-principle argument.
- □ Capstone submitted to at least one external reader (human or §2.6(c) protocol).

## 10. Buffer weeks, the 12-month trim, and the comfortable version

**Buffer weeks (+8 in Calendar A, +~10–12 in Calendar B):** for illness, holidays, exactly two sanctioned academic uses — re-reading Hume's *Enquiry* or a second Kant pass — and, new in this revision, absorbing a stage's media spill (Stage 2's listening is the expected case). A buffer week spent starting a new author is not a buffer week; it's stage extension, and §1.4's 2-week extension cap applies.

**12-month trim (≈335 hrs all-in / 50 weeks at a firm ~6.5–7 hrs/week, no buffer).** If a year is a hard constraint, the trim still cuts desk study first (≈288 → ≈250 hrs), keeps the daily queue's *rate* untouched (fewer weeks simply means fewer queue hours, ≈50), and cuts media hardest, to its skeleton (≈35 hrs):
- Stage 2 desk → 24 hrs: Augustine + Anselm + Aquinas in primary text; *everyone* else in the stage, Machiavelli and Montaigne included, drops to map-level.
- *Republic* → Books I–II, VI–VII only (−~6 hrs from Stage 1).
- Stage 5B: Heidegger and Foucault → summary-only (map-level, no excerpt pass) (−~8 hrs).
- Media skeleton: Adamson Stage 1 selection halved (≈10 hrs); *Philosophize This!* reduced to the Kant, 19th-century-core and Rawls arcs (≈14 hrs); Sandel's *Justice* kept whole (11 hrs) — it is the trim's best media hour-for-hour buy. Everything else media is dropped, not deferred.
- **Do not trim Stage 3.** Not the Kant sequence, not Hume whole, not the *Meditations* in order. A 12-month plan that cuts Stage 3 produces a learner who has "done" philosophy and cannot follow an argument in Nietzsche or Rawls — the trim exists to protect the load-bearing stage, not to distribute pain evenly.
- And do not trim the queue to save the calendar: cutting review is how the whole 335 evaporates by month nine. If ~6.5–7 hrs/week is genuinely impossible, the honest floor is Calendar B's pacing applied to the trimmed content (~67 study weeks) — a 12-month, 5-hrs/week version of this plan does not exist, and this plan won't pretend otherwise.

**The comfortable version (Calendar B pacing, +40–60 hrs desk, plus priced media extensions à la carte):** add the whole *Phaedo*; *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII–X whole (friendship and contemplation — MIT's Ancient course assigns them, and they're the Ethics thread's ancient high point); the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* whole; an Avicenna/Averroes/Maimonides excerpt pass in Stage 2 (the compressed stage's acknowledged debt); a Plotinus excerpt pass (*Enneads* I.6, V.1) at the end of Stage 1; and a second, slower Hegel or Sartre pass. This is also the version where post-plan projects belong: Kuhn whole, Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* in selection, *Capital* I beyond ch. 1 with a companion. The media extensions menu, each priced once here and in its stage: Kagan's *Death* in full, +21 hrs ≈ +4–5 weeks on Stage 5 (the flagship — §9 *Listen & watch*); Gendler's PHIL 181 in full, +21 hrs, as its *alternative*, not its companion; Millican's *General Philosophy* in full, +~2 weeks on Stage 3; *Think Again I*, +~2 weeks on Stage 0; Sadler's full Kierkegaard lectures, +1 hr/week in Stage 4; PLSC 114 in full, +1 hr/week across Stages 1–4 — as an *alternative* to the Sandel thread, not an addition to it (do not run both full); Adamson upgrades — Stage 1's full run (+20 hrs over the curation), Stage 2's Latin-medieval core (+17 hrs over the curation). Taking *every* extension adds ~20 weeks; the recommended package is Sandel (already in the headline) + Kagan.

## 12. Do-not-start-here list (unchanged, and worth restating)
