# Common Misconceptions and Oversimplifications

Read this before the popular summaries — these are the errors the research flagged most often, with the correction for each.

> Repository rule: every entry below should become a **callout box** in the relevant philosopher file. Beginners remember slogans; the repo's job is to attach the correction to the slogan at the moment of first encounter.

1. **Stoicism ≠ suppressing emotion / "stiff upper lip."** Massimo Pigliucci: the common misconception is that "living like a Stoic is like living like Mr. Spock… stiff upper lip and suppressing emotions, both of which have nothing to do with actual Stoicism." Stoics distinguished destructive passions (*pathē*, based on false judgments) from healthy emotions (*eupatheiai*: joy, rational wish, caution) and cultivated the latter; *apatheia* means freedom from destructive passions, not apathy. Modern "pop-Stoicism" that sells emotional unavailability or mere productivity/grit — stripped of Stoic ethics, physics and concern for others — is a dilution to flag, not to reproduce.
2. **Epicureanism ≠ hedonistic indulgence.** An "epicure" in restaurant reviews means the opposite of Epicurus's teaching: the safest pleasures are modest, and overindulgence is *irrational* on Epicurus's own calculus because of its painful consequences. Friendship, not luxury, is his chief security.
3. **Nietzsche: three linked errors.** (a) "God is dead" is a *diagnosis of cultural unbelief and impending nihilism*, spoken by a madman who says he has come too early — not a cheerful atheist slogan. (b) The Übermensch is a *value-creating ideal*, not a racial/biological type; Nietzsche broke with Wagner in part over antisemitism, despised German nationalism, and called himself a "good European." His Nazi image was manufactured posthumously, substantially through his sister's editing and selective publication. (c) "Will to power" and "what doesn't kill me makes me stronger" are not self-help endorsements of cruelty or of all suffering — Nietzsche explicitly valued self-overcoming and creation, not domination for its own sake, and his texts are aphoristic and deliberately provocative, so single-quote summaries systematically distort him. Present the scholarly contest over his meaning rather than a settled caricature in either direction.
4. **Machiavelli ≠ "the ends justify the means."** He never wrote that sentence. *The Prince* argues a ruler must be prepared to act against conventional virtue *from necessity* to preserve the state, and even there praises founders and blames pointless cruelty (Agathocles' crimes, he says, cannot be called virtue). The *Discourses* — the longer, republican work — argue free republics with citizen virtue are the best regime. Reducing him to *The Prince* alone, chapters 15–18 alone, is the error.
5. **Plato's Forms are not "ideas in Plato's head," and the Cave is not "the Matrix with togas."** Forms are mind-independent realities on Plato's account — that is precisely what Aristotle attacks. The pop-culture Plato (a dreamy idealist who hated the real world) misses the mathematician, political theorist and relentless arguer of the dialogues — and many "Platonic doctrines" are spoken by characters in dialogues whose conclusions Plato may be testing.
6. **Socrates wrote nothing, and "Socratic" ≠ "Plato's character Socrates."** Any quote attributed to Socrates needs its source (Plato? Xenophon? Aristophanes' caricature?) named in the repo file.
7. **Aristotle's "mean" is not mediocrity or splitting the difference.** The mean is "relative to us," determined by practical wisdom in the situation — for a soldier, courage may lie far toward boldness. And *eudaimonia* is a lifetime's activity, not a mood.
8. **Medieval philosophy ≠ theology with no arguments, and it was not only Christian.** See §2: Avicenna, Averroes and Maimonides are not "transmitters" who merely couriered Aristotle — they are original philosophers *and* the Latin West's Aristotle arrived *through* their interpretations. Also avoid "Aquinas proved God, end of story": the Five Ways presuppose Aristotelian metaphysics (act/potency, causation) that must be presented with the arguments, or the proofs look like word-magic.
9. **Descartes' cogito is not "I think, therefore I am special / consciousness creates reality."** It is a minimal certainty claim found *while doubting*, and whether it is an inference or an intuition is a live scholarly question. Note the exact slogan does not appear in the *Meditations*.
10. **The rationalist/empiricist divide is a teaching map, not history itself.** [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-empiricism/) warns against it explicitly: Locke accepts intuition/deduction for God's existence; Descartes does extensive empirical science; Leibniz wrote a book-length reply to Locke. In the repo, tag philosophers "rationalist-leaning / empiricist-leaning *on the sources-of-knowledge question*" rather than as team members.
11. **Kant's thing-in-itself is not a hidden second world we could peek at with better instruments.** It is a limit-concept: things considered apart from the conditions under which we can experience them. And the categorical imperative is not "the Golden Rule" — Kant explicitly distinguishes his universal-law test from it.
12. **Hegel's dialectic is not a mechanical "thesis–antithesis–synthesis" machine** (see §4, Hegel entry), and "Hegel = apologist for the Prussian state" is as flattened as "Marx = Hegel + economics." Marx's own texts, later Marxisms, and regimes invoking Marx are three different subjects — keep the file structure separate.
13. **Utilitarianism is not "whatever maximises my happiness" or crude act-counting.** Mill's qualitative pleasures, rule-utilitarian variants, and Singer's impartial, demanding version each answer the standard objections differently; present at least one serious objection (rights/integrity — e.g. Williams, Rawls) in the same file.
14. **Existentialism is not a single doctrine and Sartre is not its pope for all members.** Kierkegaard (theist, pre-label), Heidegger (rejected the label for *Being and Time*), Camus (rejected it), Beauvoir (systematically corrected Sartre) differ on God, freedom and others. "Existence precedes essence" is Sartre's formula, not a shared creed.
15. **Analytic vs continental is not science vs nonsense (or clarity vs depth).** It is a historically produced divergence of canons, styles and institutions, with shared roots and growing overlap. A repo that sneers in either direction will mis-educate.
16. **Foucault does not say "everything is power, so nothing is true," and Derrida does not say "nothing exists outside language."** Both are caricatures addressed in their entries above. Genealogy and deconstruction are *reading and research methods* with specific steps — teach the steps.
17. **"God is dead," "the unexamined life," "I think therefore I am," "hell is other people," "the banality of evil"** — the repo should maintain a `quotes-in-context.md`: every famous line gets its work, chapter/section, speaker (often a character, not the author) and immediate context. Half of §8 is generated by decontextualised quotation.
18. **Montaigne ≠ a cheerful relativist, and "Que sçay-je?" is not a shrug.** The motto gets quoted as if the *Essays* license "who can say?" about everything, and passages like "Of Custom" are read as celebrating whatever a culture happens to do. In fact the question names a disciplined Pyrrhonian method — set argument against argument, suspend judgment where they balance, keep inquiring — aimed at dogmatic *knowledge-claims*, not at commitment itself. Montaigne holds firm loyalties (to tolerance, against cruelty, which he calls the vice he detests most) and treats custom as a powerful fact to reckon with, not a truth to celebrate. His skepticism also cuts against atheist certainty, which is why every party to the wars of religion tried to claim or condemn him.
19. **Montesquieu's separation of powers is not a rigid wall, and it is not most of his book.** Civics courses extract one chapter (XI.6) of a 31-book comparative study of law and teach it as his whole doctrine, often as a blueprint for hermetically sealed branches. Montesquieu's actual claim is that liberty — defined as security, the tranquillity of mind that comes from not fearing arbitrary power — requires that legislative, executive and judicial powers not be united in one person or body; his admired English model in fact mixes and overlaps them. Most of the *Spirit of the Laws* is about the "general spirit" of nations — climate, custom, religion, commerce — and the legislator's job of fitting laws to it, which is also why he is no preacher of sudden revolution or of modern democracy.
20. **Vico's ages do not turn in circles, and he is not a relativist.** The *corsi e ricorsi* get flattened into eternal recurrence — history going round forever, every age as good as every other. Vico's pattern spirals rather than repeats: language, religion and law transmit gains across collapses, so a return is never a rerun. He also ranks his ages: the fully human third age, with public law and reasoned speech, is a norm, and its late corruption — the "barbarism of reflection," cleverness severed from common sense — he ranks *below* primitive barbarism. And *verum factum* is a criterion of knowledge (we demonstrably know what we can grasp the making of), not the claim that truth is whatever we invent; fiction, which we also make, fails the test precisely because its maker knows it as arbitrary.
21. **Fichte's "I" is not your ego inventing the world.** "The I posits the Not-I" sounds like solipsism or fantasy, and Fichte gets filed as the man who thought his personality created reality. The absolute I of the *Wissenschaftslehre* is not Johann Fichte or any individual; it is the structure of self-consciousness as such, arrived at by deduction, not daydream. The system's machinery — the check (*Anstoß*), striving, limitation — exists precisely to explain why the world *resists* us: the Not-I is brutally real for the I, and a world that pushed back not at all would make action and selfhood impossible. Nor is he a mere stepping-stone to Hegel: recognition, the primacy of practice and the philosophy of striving are original contributions Hegel absorbed.
22. **Tocqueville neither celebrated democracy nor simply feared the mob.** He is quoted in both directions — prophet of American democracy, or aristocrat sneering at mob rule — because *Democracy in America* contains admiration and dread in both volumes. His position is that equality of conditions is inevitable and providential, not that it is good; the book is a warning manual for educating democracy against its own tendencies. His most original warning is not mob violence but "soft despotism": a mild, elected, comfortable tutelary power that infantilises citizens rather than terrorising them. He did not think resistance to democracy was possible or wise; his lifelong question was how liberty survives inside it.
23. **Sidgwick is not "Mill with footnotes," and esoteric morality is not his political program.** Because he reaches utilitarian conclusions in a dry, systematic style, Sidgwick is treated as a tidier Mill. In fact *The Methods of Ethics* is a proof attempt from self-evident axioms, and it ends where Mill never went: the dualism of practical reason, an admitted standoff between rational egoism and universal benevolence that Sidgwick could not honestly dissolve. The notorious "esoteric morality" — the possibility that utilitarianism might justify its own concealment — is a theorem he accepted reluctantly, presented as a cost of the theory to be weighed, not a recommendation for how to run society.
24. **Frege did not fail at everything, and his "senses" are not mental images.** Russell's paradox makes the headlines, so Frege is remembered as the man whose life's work collapsed in 1902. What collapsed was one principle (Basic Law V) in the logicist derivation of arithmetic. The predicate logic of the *Begriffsschrift* and the philosophy of language built on sense and reference succeeded completely and still structure both fields. The second error runs the other way: readers import psychology into "sense," as if a word's sense were a private mental picture. Frege's anti-psychologism is the core of his work — senses are objective, public and communicable, the same for everyone who grasps them.
25. **Moore's "here is one hand" is not table-thumping stubbornness, and the naturalistic fallacy is not the "appeal to nature."** The gesture sounds like a refusal to argue — a man waving his hand at skeptics. It is a deliberate methodological challenge: Moore held that we know many ordinary propositions more certainly than any premise a skeptical (or metaphysical) argument could offer against them, so an argument whose conclusion denies them indicts its own premises. His common-sense certainties are a specific, modest list, not infallibility about everything. Likewise the naturalistic fallacy is a thesis about *defining* "good" — that "good" names a simple, non-natural quality no natural property can be substituted for — not the is–ought gap and not the popular fallacy of assuming that what is natural is good.
26. **Bergson was not an anti-science mystic, and intuition is not a gut feeling.** The 1922 confrontation with Einstein left him branded an obscurantist poet who "denied relativity." He disputed relativity's philosophical interpretation — the reality of the multiple times assigned to moving observers — not its experimental results; even so, most physicists judge his positive account of the twin case mistaken, and the careful verdict keeps both halves. "Intuition," in his usage, is the *result* of disciplined philosophical effort, the opposite of a first impression, and the *élan vital* was offered as an image for life's inventive impetus, explicitly not a mechanism, soul-force or plan that explains evolution.
27. **Merleau-Ponty is not "Sartre-lite," and embodiment is not crude materialism.** Sharing cafés, journals and vocabulary with Sartre got him filed as a softer Sartre. Philosophically he rejected the for-itself/in-itself dualism at the heart of *Being and Nothingness* from the ground up, replacing radical freedom with situated, embodied freedom — the famous quarrel was philosophical before it was personal. Nor does the body-subject mean "the mind is really just the body": he criticized physiological reductionism as firmly as dualism, and his late "flesh" (*la chair*) is an ontological element, not anatomy. He was no irrationalist either — Gestalt psychology, neurology and linguistics were his lifelong partners in argument.
28. **Levinas's ethics is not "be kind to people you meet," and it is not an argument from the Holocaust.** The "face" gets read literally, as a doctrine of niceness toward whoever is in front of you. Levinas is making a structural claim about obligation: in the face of the Other, a command ("thou shalt not kill") is encountered that precedes my freedom and makes me responsible before I choose anything — responsibility before freedom, not instead of it. Nor is the philosophy derived from the Shoah as a premise: he presents the obligation as belonging to the human as such, treated the catastrophe as the historical exposure of ontology's limits, and resisted reducing his thought to his biography, terrible as that biography was.
29. **Gadamer's "prejudice" does not mean bigotry is fine.** Rehabilitating a word English hears as purely negative made *Truth and Method* sound like a licence for bias. Gadamer's *Vorurteile* are pre-judgments — the fore-understanding every interpreter brings, inherited from tradition — and his claim is that they are unavoidable and *enabling*: without them a text could not address us at all. They are not thereby excused. A prejudice proves itself enabling or blinding only by being risked in encounter with the text and revised where the text resists; that testing is the hermeneutic discipline. (The title is polemical in a second way, too: the book argues the humanities' most important truths are not produced by "method" at all.)
30. **MacIntyre is not nostalgic for the Middle Ages, and he refused the label "communitarian."** The closing line of *After Virtue* — that we await a new St Benedict — gets quoted as a restoration program, and he is shelved with the communitarians as a theorist of going back. The Benedict line closes a diagnosis, not a plan: the barbarians, he says, have already been governing us for some time, and the task is building local forms of resistance within modernity, not time travel. He rejected "communitarian" as a self-description outright. And where he is accused of relativism, his theory of traditions is designed as the *answer* to it — traditions in epistemological crisis can rationally defeat rivals. Whether the answer succeeds is the live debate, not the intention.
31. **Bernard Williams did not show that consequences don't matter, and moral luck does not abolish responsibility.** The Jim-and-the-Indians and Pedro cases get cited as a licence to ignore outcomes. Williams's target was narrower: one theory's *way* of counting consequences, in which the agent's own projects and integrity are made to vanish into an impersonal calculus — the rescuer who must first check that saving his wife is impartially permitted has "one thought too many." Moral luck, likewise, attacks the ideal of a morality sealed against luck; his lorry driver who faultlessly kills a child and rightly feels agent-regret is a study of responsibility's real texture, not its dissolution. And "internal reasons" are not whims: deliberation works on a motivational set that includes loyalties the agent cannot simply abandon.
32. **Parfit did not prove we don't exist, and the Repugnant Conclusion is not his recommendation.** Teletransportation headlines turned him into an identity nihilist who showed survival is an illusion. His claim is precise: personal identity is real, but it is *not what matters* in survival — what matters is Relation R (psychological connectedness and continuity), which can take a branching form identity cannot. Ordinary survival remains perfectly intact on his view. The Repugnant Conclusion — that a vast population with lives barely worth living could outrank a smaller flourishing one — he named because he found it repugnant: it is a problem he spent decades trying to escape, not a policy. The non-identity problem cuts against complacency too: that future people can't be harmed "person-affectingly" by our climate choices is, for Parfit, a puzzle showing our principles need revision.
33. **Thomson did not invent the trolley problem, and the violinist argument does not deny the fetus is a person.** Her name sits on the literature, so the whole apparatus is credited to her. The runaway tram is Philippa Foot's (1967); Thomson coined "the trolley problem," created the bystander and footbridge variants that made the cases decisive, and built the field — credit her with the right things. The violinist argument is routinely attacked for the opposite of its method: it *grants* the fetus personhood for argument's sake and argues that even a person's right to life does not include a right to use another's body uninvited. Finally, cite her with a date: her 2008 "Turning the Trolley" reverses her famous early verdict on the bystander case.
34. **Singer is not a cold calculator demanding you give away everything, and he does not say animals are equal to humans.** The caricature — an actuary of suffering indifferent to everything but the sums — misses the structure of the arguments. His claim is equal *consideration* of comparable interests, not equal treatment or equal value of lives, which he explicitly denies. The famous giving principle has a principled limit built in: give until further giving would cost something of *comparable moral importance* — a demanding standard, honestly stated, but not literal destitution. Two housekeeping points the caricature also garbles: "speciesism" is Richard Ryder's coinage, which Singer popularized, and his utilitarianism changed in print (preference to hedonistic, 2014), so cite him with a date.
35. **Pascal's Wager is not a proof of God's existence, and "the heart" is not a synonym for feelings.** The Wager is quoted as if Pascal thought a bet could settle theology. It is prudential reasoning offered precisely where he holds reason *cannot* settle the question — an argument that belief is not unreasonable and that the stakes make indifference the least rational option, addressed to the libertine who will not move on probabilities alone. Pascal also denies the argument manufactures faith: faith is grace's gift; the Wager at most removes obstacles. And when he writes that the heart has its reasons, "heart" names a cognitive faculty that apprehends first principles — space, time, number — on which reasoning itself depends. This is a working mathematician and experimental physicist, not an apostle of feeling over fact.
36. **Kuhn did not say "anything goes."** Pop usage turned "paradigm shift" into any upgrade or rebrand and *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* into a licence for relativism — the claim that science is just fashion between frameworks. "Anything goes" was Feyerabend's provocation, not Kuhn's position, and Kuhn spent later decades protesting the relativist reading. A paradigm is not a vibe or a worldview in the loose sense but a concrete exemplary achievement anchoring shared practice; incommensurability means frameworks lack a neutral common measure, not that their holders cannot understand each other at all or that all theories are equally good. Kuhn held that later paradigms are better instruments for puzzle-solving — progress is real, just not progress toward a final fixed truth.
37. **The Vienna Circle did not "prove metaphysics false," and Popper was not one of its members.** Ayer's brilliant popularization got mistaken for the whole movement, and the verification principle for a refutation. The Circle's claim was stronger and stranger than falsity: metaphysical sentences lack cognitive *meaning* altogether. Whether the principle refutes itself is a genuine, still-discussed problem about proposals and conventions, not a conversation-stopper. The Circle was also plural and quarrelsome — Schlick, Carnap and Neurath disagreed on foundations and politics — and Wittgenstein inspired it while resisting its reading of him. Popper, finally, was never a member: falsifiability was his rival criterion of *scientific status*, not of meaning, aimed partly against them.
38. **Anscombe did not argue morality is impossible without God, and Foot's naturalism is not "whatever is natural is good."** "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) is cited as a theist's proof that ethics needs a divine legislator. Anscombe's target was one specific concept — moral obligation in its law-like sense — which she argued is a survival from an earlier law conception of ethics and lacks sense without it; Aristotelian virtue talk, about what human beings need to flourish, she thought works perfectly well without a legislator, and she urged philosophers to return to it. Foot's *Natural Goodness* belongs to that return: natural does not mean automatically good, since defect is judged against a life form's characteristic life, and traits natural in the sense of typical — aggression, say — can be defects in humans. (Foot, not Thomson, created the original tram case in 1967; Thomson named it and built the variants.)

39. **Diderot was not "just the editor" of the *Encyclopédie*.** The editorship consumed two decades, but the philosophy is original work of the first rank — the materialism of the *Letter on the Blind* and *D'Alembert's Dream*, the aesthetics of the *Salons*, the dialogues — most of it written for the drawer because print was dangerous. Nor was he born an atheist materialist: the *Philosophical Thoughts* of 1746 are deist, and the materialism hardens across the 1740s. Flattening that development hides what makes the *Letter on the Blind* a turning point.

40. **Lessing's ring parable does not say all religions are equally true.** In *Nathan the Wise*, the genuine ring cannot be identified by inspection; each son is told to live as if his own were genuine and to prove it in conduct over generations. That is agnosticism about pedigree, not relativism about truth. The companion error concerns the "ugly broad ditch": Lessing does not argue that the historical reports are false — he grants they may be as true as historical reports get. His point is about types of truth: accidental truths of history cannot serve as proof of necessary truths of reason.

41. **Schleiermacher's "feeling" does not mean emotion, and his hermeneutics is not Bible study.** The feeling of absolute dependence is immediate self-consciousness — a structural feature of awareness, present as steadily in dry reflection as in religious ecstasy — not a warm glow; reading it as "religion is a feeling" repeats Hegel's caricature (the jibe that on this view a dog would be the best Christian). And while scripture is one application, the hermeneutics covers all speech and writing: misunderstanding, not understanding, is what happens as a matter of course, which is why interpretation needs an art at all.

42. **Schelling was not merely a bridge from Fichte to Hegel.** The textbook picture makes him a corridor between two greater men. His career spans sixty years, outlasts Hegel's by two decades, and ends in a "positive philosophy" aimed against the Hegelian completion. The related slogan — identity philosophy as "the night in which all cows are black" — is Hegel's polemic, not Schelling's doctrine: identity as a point of indifference is not the claim that everything is simply the same. And his *Naturphilosophie* was not anti-science; it engaged the physics, chemistry, and biology of its day closely, whatever its speculative excesses.

43. **Self-reliance is not selfishness, and Thoreau was not a hermit.** Emerson's doctrine is obedience to a moral intuition he believed universal; the nonconformity serves a law, not appetite — and it is not an ancestor of "do your own research." Thoreau's cabin stood on Emerson's land about a mile from the Concord he visited constantly; the *Walden* experiment was in living deliberately, not in hiding. A title caution belongs here too: the essay known as "Civil Disobedience" appeared as "Resistance to Civil Government"; the famous title is posthumous, and the mass movements that used it reinvented as much as they inherited.

44. **Whitehead was not Russell's assistant, and process theology is not Whitehead.** *Principia Mathematica* was a joint enterprise in which Whitehead was the senior collaborator; its projected fourth volume, on geometry, was his, and its abandonment marks his turn toward the philosophy of nature. The theology later built from his dipolar God by Hartshorne and Cobb is a reception of the work, not the work itself: Whitehead offered a metaphysical scheme in which God has a defined function and was markedly reticent about creeds. "Everything is connected" is likewise not the philosophy — the system is a precise categorial machinery of occasions and prehensions, not a slogan.

45. **Jaspers's Axial Age is a thesis, not a fact, and he is not a vaguer Heidegger.** The Axial Age — the claim that between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, China, India, Persia, and the West underwent a simultaneous spiritual breakthrough — is a fruitful, contested periodization proposed in 1949, to be used with its critics in view rather than recited as settled chronology. The comparison error runs parallel: Jaspers and Heidegger share sources and a vocabulary of existence, but communication and openness stand against resoluteness in Jaspers, and his boundary situations are not a self-improvement technique — they befall us; they cannot be scheduled.

46. **Strauss did not teach that every old book hides a secret doctrine, and "Straussian" is not a political platform.** Esotericism, on his account, belongs to specific conditions — persecution, and authors who signal the practice — and most of his readings turn on extreme attentiveness to argument and structure rather than on hidden codes. His students span the political spectrum; the school's unity is a method and a canon. Nor was he a nostalgic proposing a return to Athens: the return he proposed was to the *question* the classics posed. The claim that his teaching caused the Iraq War belongs to a contested scholarly and journalistic debate, not to the biographical record.

47. **Langer did not say art expresses the artist's feelings, and "presentational" does not mean ineffable.** For Langer, artworks present the *forms* of feeling — the morphology of sentience, its rises, tensions, and resolutions — impersonally; the artist's biography is beside the point, which is exactly what separates her view from the expression theories she opposed. Presentational symbols are articulate and structured; they are simply not translatable into discursive language without loss. Her later eclipse, finally, was not a refutation: the books went out of fashion faster than they were answered.

48. **Murdoch's "attention" is not a mindfulness technique.** It circulates now as a tip for better decisions or calmer living. In *The Sovereignty of Good* it is a metaphysical and moral claim: that morality consists mostly in the quality of our seeing — the just and loving attention that unselfs us — of which choices and actions are the downstream expression. Two neighboring errors: the novels are not the philosophy in disguise (she kept the enterprises distinct), and she was not a Christian apologist — her Good is Plato's, held without belief in a personal God.

49. **The capabilities approach is not a fancier GDP, and Nussbaum's version is not Sen's.** The ten central capabilities specify entitlements of justice — what each person must be able to do and be, up to a threshold. Collapsing them into a single index number for ranking nations is exactly what Nussbaum resists. The list, the threshold, and the Aristotelian grounding are hers; Sen declines all three, preferring to leave the content to public reasoning. Cite the approach with its author attached.

50. **Midgley was not anti-science, and *Beast and Man* does not say morality is "in our genes."** She admired science and opposed scientism — its expansion into a total worldview that claims other forms of understanding are nothing but defective science (her target was "nothing-buttery": the heart is *nothing but* a pump, the mind *nothing but* firing neurons). Her ethology constrains and informs moral reflection; it does not dictate it, and she never argued that animal behavior justifies human behavior. Her famous exchange with Dawkins was not about whether evolution is true but about what a metaphor — the "selfish gene" — licenses readers to conclude about motivation and morals.

51. **Pitkin did not define representation once and for all.** *The Concept of Representation* is routinely mined for a single-sentence definition to settle arguments. Her argument is the opposite: the concept has an internal structure — formalistic, descriptive, symbolic, and substantive views, each catching something real — and permanent tensions, above all the mandate–independence controversy, which she refuses to resolve for either side. Descriptive representation, in particular, is not her recommendation: resemblance is one partial view with real but limited force, not the goal of institutional design.

52. **Narrative identity does not mean the self is a fiction we invent.** Ricoeur's *idem* — the sameness of body, character, and disposition — anchors every life story in something not narrated at all; *ipse*, selfhood, is what narrative mediates, for instance in the keeping of a promise across change. Mediation is not fabrication. The companion error makes him an anything-goes relativist about texts: his whole machinery of textual autonomy, validation, and the conflict of interpretations exists to discipline interpretation — and the "conflict" is not indecision, since passing through suspicion to a second naïveté is a determinate position, not a shrug.
