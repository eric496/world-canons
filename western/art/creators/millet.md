## Biography

Jean-François Millet was born into a peasant farming family in the hamlet of Gruchy on the Normandy coast. He studied in Cherbourg and in Paris with Paul Delaroche, and supported himself for years with portraits and small pastoral pictures. In 1849, fleeing revolution and cholera in Paris, he settled in the village of Barbizon on the edge of the Forest of Fontainebleau, where he lived for the rest of his life among a group of landscape painters. The Sower (1850) and The Angelus (1857–59) made his name, and he was awarded the Legion of Honour in 1868.

Millet depicted the labour of French peasants, sowing, reaping, gleaning and digging, with monumental, almost biblical dignity, simplified forms and earthy colour. Conservative critics accused him of socialism, while others saw his work as deeply religious. The Angelus became one of the most famous paintings in France and later obsessed Salvador Dalí. Van Gogh revered Millet and made many copies after his compositions.

## Key artworks

### The Gleaners (1857)

[![The Gleaners](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1f/Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Millet_-_Gleaners_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg/1280px-Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Millet_-_Gleaners_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Millet_-_Gleaners_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Millet_-_Gleaners_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1857
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Gleaners%20Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois%20Millet)

Shown at the Salon of 1857, this painting shows three peasant women bending over the stubble of a harvested field to pick up the stray stalks of wheat left behind. Gleaning was a right granted to the poorest members of the rural community. In the bright background, a huge harvest is being loaded under the eye of an overseer on horseback. The women's heavy, monumental bodies and rhythmic poses give their back-breaking labour a timeless, almost religious dignity. Conservative critics saw it as a dangerous socialist reminder of rural poverty after the 1848 revolution. It is in the Musée d'Orsay.
