## Biography

Umberto Boccioni was born in Reggio Calabria and grew up in various parts of Italy. In Rome he studied with the Divisionist painter Giacomo Balla. In 1909 the poet Filippo Tommaso Marinetti published the Futurist Manifesto in Le Figaro, praising speed, machines, violence and modernity. The next year Boccioni co-signed the Manifesto of Futurist Painters and became the movement's leading artist, with paintings such as The City Rises (1910) and the series States of Mind. In 1912 he wrote a technical manifesto of Futurist sculpture. A fervent supporter of Italy's entry into the First World War, he volunteered in 1915 and died in 1916 after falling from a horse during cavalry training near Verona, aged 33.

Boccioni tried to show movement, energy and the fusion of objects with their surroundings, which he called 'dynamism' and 'lines of force'. He drew on Cubist fragmentation and Divisionist colour. His sculpture, in particular, broke down the boundary between figure and space. The Futurists' glorification of war and the movement's later links with Fascism complicate their legacy.

## Key artworks

### Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913)

[![Unique Forms of Continuity in Space](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/%27Unique_Forms_of_Continuity_in_Space%27%2C_1913_bronze_by_Umberto_Boccioni.jpg/1280px-%27Unique_Forms_of_Continuity_in_Space%27%2C_1913_bronze_by_Umberto_Boccioni.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%27Unique_Forms_of_Continuity_in_Space%27%2C_1913_bronze_by_Umberto_Boccioni.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%27Unique_Forms_of_Continuity_in_Space%27%2C_1913_bronze_by_Umberto_Boccioni.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1913
- **Type:** Bronze (cast after his death)
- **Where to see it:** Casts at MoMA, New York; Tate; Museo del Novecento, Milan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Unique%20Forms%20of%20Continuity%20in%20Space%20Umberto%20Boccioni)

Boccioni modelled this striding figure in plaster in 1913, his most famous sculpture and the key work of Futurism. It shows a human body in motion, its muscles stretched and swept back as if shaped by the speed and the air around it, so that the figure merges with its own movement. Boccioni wanted to show not a body at a single moment but the continuous flow of its motion. The plaster original is in São Paulo. The bronze casts in New York, London and Milan were made after his death, in 1931 and later. The sculpture appears on the reverse of the Italian 20-cent euro coin.
