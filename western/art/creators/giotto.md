## Biography

Giotto di Bondone was born around 1267 in the Mugello valley near Florence, probably at Colle di Vespignano. According to a famous legend told by Vasari, the painter Cimabue discovered him as a shepherd boy drawing his sheep on a rock and took him on as a pupil. Giotto worked across Italy: in Rome, in Assisi, where the fresco cycle of the life of St Francis in the Upper Church is traditionally but debatably attributed to him, in Padua, Naples and Milan. His greatest surviving work is the fresco cycle of the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua (around 1305). Late in life he was appointed chief architect of Florence Cathedral and designed its bell tower, still known as Giotto's Campanile. He died in Florence in 1337.

Giotto broke with the flat, stylised conventions of Byzantine painting. He gave his figures real weight and volume, placed them in believable spaces, and showed human emotion with a new directness and dignity, so that sacred stories became human dramas. Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio praised him in his own time, and Renaissance writers saw him as the artist who began the revival of painting. He is often called the father of Western painting.

### Lamentation (The Mourning of Christ) (c. 1305)

[![Lamentation (The Mourning of Christ)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/34/Compianto_sul_Cristo_morto.jpg/1280px-Compianto_sul_Cristo_morto.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Compianto_sul_Cristo_morto.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Compianto_sul_Cristo_morto.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1305
- **Type:** Fresco
- **Where to see it:** Scrovegni Chapel, Padua
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lamentation%20%28The%20Mourning%20of%20Christ%29%20Giotto%20di%20Bondone)

This scene belongs to the cycle of frescoes Giotto painted around 1305 for the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua. The chapel was commissioned by the banker Enrico Scrovegni, partly in atonement for his father's notorious usury. The dead Christ lies in his mother's arms at the lower left, and a bare rocky ridge runs diagonally down towards him, leading the eye to the focus of grief. Mourners crowd around, two of them seen from behind as anonymous solid masses, a daring new idea. Above, angels twist and wail in the sky. Each figure expresses sorrow differently, from John flinging his arms back to Mary Magdalene cradling Christ's feet. The scene has the force of real human drama, a decisive break from the stylised Byzantine tradition.

### Ognissanti Madonna (c. 1310)

[![Ognissanti Madonna](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/Giotto%2C_1267_Around-1337_-_Maest%C3%A0_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/1280px-Giotto%2C_1267_Around-1337_-_Maest%C3%A0_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Giotto%2C_1267_Around-1337_-_Maest%C3%A0_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Giotto%2C_1267_Around-1337_-_Maest%C3%A0_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1310
- **Type:** Tempera on panel
- **Where to see it:** Uffizi Gallery, Florence
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ognissanti%20Madonna%20Giotto%20di%20Bondone)

Painted around 1310 for the church of the Ognissanti (All Saints) in Florence, this altarpiece more than three metres tall shows the Virgin enthroned with the Child, surrounded by saints and angels. Compared with Cimabue's and Duccio's Madonnas, Giotto's Virgin has a weighty, three-dimensional body: her knees and breast press against her robes, and she sits firmly in a Gothic throne that recedes into space. Kneeling angels offer flowers, and the saints behind them overlap convincingly. It was a landmark in giving sacred figures physical presence.
