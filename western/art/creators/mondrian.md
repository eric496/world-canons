## Biography

Piet Mondriaan (later Mondrian) was born in Amersfoort to a strict Calvinist family and trained at the Amsterdam Academy, first painting landscapes in the tradition of the Hague School. He was drawn to Theosophy and its search for universal spiritual truths. Moving to Paris in 1911, he discovered Cubism, and his series of trees became increasingly abstract. Trapped in the Netherlands during the First World War, he co-founded the movement and magazine De Stijl ('The Style') with Theo van Doesburg in 1917. He split from van Doesburg in the 1920s, reportedly over the use of diagonals. He lived in Paris until 1938, then London, and from 1940 New York, where he died in 1944.

From around 1920 Mondrian developed what he called Neoplasticism: a pure, universal art reduced to straight horizontal and vertical lines, the three primary colours and the non-colours black, white and grey, in perfect asymmetrical balance. He believed this abstract harmony could point the way to a harmonious society. His style has had an enormous influence on architecture, graphic design, fashion and furniture design.

## Key artworks

### Broadway Boogie Woogie (1942–43)

[![Broadway Boogie Woogie](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/Piet_Mondrian%2C_1942_-_Broadway_Boogie_Woogie.jpg/1280px-Piet_Mondrian%2C_1942_-_Broadway_Boogie_Woogie.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Piet_Mondrian%2C_1942_-_Broadway_Boogie_Woogie.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Piet_Mondrian%2C_1942_-_Broadway_Boogie_Woogie.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1942–43
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Broadway%20Boogie%20Woogie%20Piet%20Mondrian)

Mondrian arrived in New York in 1940 and loved the city's energy and its boogie-woogie jazz. This painting of 1942–43, completed shortly before his death and bought by the Museum of Modern Art in 1943, abandons his usual black lines. Instead, a grid of yellow lines, broken by small squares of red, blue and grey, suggests the street grid of Manhattan, the flashing lights of Broadway, traffic and the syncopated rhythms of the music. A few larger blocks of colour float within the grid. His last, unfinished work, Victory Boogie Woogie, is in The Hague.
