# Roy Lichtenstein

**1923 – 1997** · American  
Born: New York · Died: New York  
Movements: [Pop Art](../movements/pop-art.md)  

> Lichtenstein enlarged comic-strip panels with hand-painted Ben-Day dots, parodying mass-media images and painterly heroics.

## Biography

Roy Lichtenstein was born in New York City and studied art at Ohio State University, interrupted by army service in Europe during the Second World War. He taught at universities in Ohio and New York, painting in Abstract Expressionist and other styles. In 1961, according to the story, his son challenged him to paint as well as a Mickey Mouse comic book, and he painted Look Mickey, based on a cartoon. His 1962 exhibition at Leo Castelli's gallery made him famous. He went on to parody art history and Abstract Expressionism itself in series such as Brushstrokes, Mirrors, Entablatures and Artist's Studio, and he made sculpture and public art. He died in New York in 1997.

Lichtenstein took images from comic strips and advertisements and repainted them on a huge scale with thick black outlines, flat primary colours and hand-painted Ben-Day dots imitating cheap commercial printing. His work raised questions about originality, mass media and taste. Some of the comic artists whose panels he adapted have argued that their contribution has been underappreciated.

## Key artworks

### Whaam! (1963)

> 🖼️ **[View it at Tate →](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/artworks/lichtenstein-whaam-t00897)**  
> <sub>Still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here.</sub>

- **Date:** 1963
- **Type:** Acrylic and oil on canvas (diptych)
- **Where to see it:** Tate Modern, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Whaam%21%20Roy%20Lichtenstein)

Painted in 1963, this large two-panel painting, about 4 metres wide, is based on a panel from a 1962 DC war comic, All-American Men of War. In the left panel, a fighter pilot fires a missile, with a speech balloon in the comic's style. In the right panel, an enemy plane explodes in a burst of red and yellow flame, with the giant onomatopoeic word 'WHAAM!'. Lichtenstein enlarged the comic image, simplified it and painted it with flat colours, black outlines and Ben-Day dots, giving a throwaway image the scale and presence of a history painting. Its acquisition by Tate in 1966 was controversial.
