## Biography

Agnolo di Cosimo, called Bronzino, was the pupil and adopted son of the eccentric Florentine painter Jacopo Pontormo. He became court painter to Cosimo I de' Medici, Duke of Florence, and his wife Eleonora of Toledo, decorating Eleonora's chapel in the Palazzo Vecchio and producing official portraits of the ducal family. He was also a witty poet and a founding member of Florence's Accademia del Disegno in 1563.

Bronzino's portraits are the epitome of Mannerist court style. His sitters are cool and aloof, flawlessly smooth as if made of porcelain, and dressed in sumptuous fabrics rendered with jewel-like precision. Their emotional reserve expresses the rank and self-control expected at court. His allegories are intricate intellectual puzzles that mix eroticism with moralising.

## Key artworks

### Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time (c. 1545)

[![Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/Angelo_Bronzino_-_Venus%2C_Cupid%2C_Folly_and_Time_-_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg/1280px-Angelo_Bronzino_-_Venus%2C_Cupid%2C_Folly_and_Time_-_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Angelo_Bronzino_-_Venus%2C_Cupid%2C_Folly_and_Time_-_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Angelo_Bronzino_-_Venus%2C_Cupid%2C_Folly_and_Time_-_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1545
- **Type:** Oil on panel
- **Where to see it:** National Gallery, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Venus%2C%20Cupid%2C%20Folly%20and%20Time%20Agnolo%20Bronzino)

Bronzino painted this allegory around 1545 as a diplomatic gift from Cosimo I de' Medici to King Francis I of France. Venus, holding the golden apple, is embraced and kissed by her son Cupid in a disturbingly erotic way. Around them crowd strange personifications. A laughing boy is about to throw roses, perhaps Folly or Pleasure. Behind him a sweet-faced girl offers honey but has a serpent's tail, perhaps Fraud. A figure tears its hair in anguish, interpreted as Jealousy or as the effects of syphilis. Above, bald Father Time and a mask-like figure struggle over a blue curtain, threatening to reveal or hide the scene. The cold polished surfaces and puzzling meaning are typical of Mannerist court art. It is in the National Gallery, London.
