## Biography

Hans Holbein the Younger was the son of the Augsburg painter Hans Holbein the Elder. He settled in Basel, where he painted portraits of the scholar Erasmus and designed book illustrations, including his famous woodcut series the Dance of Death. As the Reformation disrupted religious commissions, he went to England in 1526 with an introduction from Erasmus to Sir Thomas More. He returned for good in 1532 and became King's Painter to Henry VIII, recording the king, his court, his wives and his ministers until his death in London in 1543, probably of plague.

Holbein's portraits combine extraordinary realism, cool unflattering observation of each face, with the brilliant depiction of rich clothes and objects that proclaimed status. His drawings, many now in the Royal Collection at Windsor, are among the finest portrait studies ever made. His lost mural of Henry VIII for Whitehall Palace, destroyed by fire in 1698, fixed the king's image, legs apart and fists on hips, for posterity.

## Key artworks

### The Ambassadors (1533)

[![The Ambassadors](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/88/Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_The_Ambassadors_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/1280px-Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_The_Ambassadors_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_The_Ambassadors_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_The_Ambassadors_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1533
- **Type:** Oil on oak panel
- **Where to see it:** National Gallery, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Ambassadors%20Hans%20Holbein%20the%20Younger)

Painted in London in 1533, this double portrait shows Jean de Dinteville, French ambassador to Henry VIII, and his friend Georges de Selve, Bishop of Lavaur. Between them stands a two-tiered table covered with scientific instruments, a globe, a lute with a broken string and a Lutheran hymn book. These symbols of learning and the arts may also hint at discord in religion and politics during the Reformation. Across the floor floats a strange diagonal shape. Seen from the right side, close to the wall, it resolves into a human skull, an anamorphic memento mori reminding the viewer of death. A tiny crucifix half-hidden by the curtain at top left offers the hope of salvation. The National Gallery bought it in 1890.
