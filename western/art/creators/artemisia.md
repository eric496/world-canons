## Biography

Artemisia Gentileschi was the daughter of the painter Orazio Gentileschi, a follower of Caravaggio, and trained in his Roman studio. In 1611, at about 17, she was raped by the painter Agostino Tassi, her father's collaborator. At the trial that followed in 1612 she was tortured with thumbscrews to test her testimony. Tassi was convicted but barely punished. Soon after, she married and moved to Florence. There she won the patronage of the Medici and in 1616 became the first woman admitted to the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno. She later worked in Rome, Venice, Naples and London, where she joined her father at the court of Charles I around 1638–40, before settling in Naples.

Artemisia specialised in strong women from the Bible and history: Judith, Susanna, Esther, Cleopatra, Lucretia and Mary Magdalene. She painted them with powerful realism, dramatic light and psychological depth. She was celebrated in her lifetime and then largely forgotten. Feminist art historians rediscovered her from the 1970s on, and major exhibitions, including one at the National Gallery in London in 2020, have established her as one of the great painters of the Baroque.

## Key artworks

### Judith Slaying Holofernes (c. 1620)

[![Judith Slaying Holofernes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4e/Artemisia_Gentileschi_-_Judith_Beheading_Holofernes_-_WGA8563.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Artemisia_Gentileschi_-_Judith_Beheading_Holofernes_-_WGA8563.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Artemisia_Gentileschi_-_Judith_Beheading_Holofernes_-_WGA8563.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1620
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Uffizi Gallery, Florence
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Judith%20Slaying%20Holofernes%20Artemisia%20Gentileschi)

Artemisia painted the story of Judith and Holofernes several times. This version of around 1620, in the Uffizi, was made for the Medici court in Florence and is a reworking of an earlier version now in Naples. Unlike Caravaggio's hesitant Judith, Artemisia's heroine and her maid Abra work together with grim physical determination: the maid pins Holofernes down while Judith braces herself and drives the sword through his neck, and blood spurts in arcs across the white sheets. A bracelet on Judith's arm shows images of the huntress goddess Artemis, perhaps a reference to the painter's own name. Many viewers have read the painting in the light of her own experience of rape and trial, but it is above all a masterpiece of Baroque drama and physical realism.
