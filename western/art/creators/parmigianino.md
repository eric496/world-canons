# Parmigianino

**1503 – 1540** · Italian  
Born: Parma · Died: Casalmaggiore  
Movements: [Mannerism](../movements/mannerism.md)  

> A leading Mannerist known for elegant, elongated figures and virtuoso effects.

## Biography

Girolamo Francesco Maria Mazzola, called Parmigianino ('the little one from Parma'), was a prodigy. He went to Rome in 1524 to seek papal patronage, bringing his remarkable self-portrait in a convex mirror as a calling card. He was working there when imperial troops sacked the city in 1527, and he escaped to Bologna. Back in Parma he was commissioned to fresco the church of Santa Maria della Steccata, but he fell years behind, was briefly jailed for breach of contract, and died in 1540 at only 37. According to Vasari, he had become obsessed with alchemy.

Parmigianino is one of the defining artists of Mannerism. His figures are impossibly elegant, with elongated necks, hands and limbs, refined and self-consciously artificial, designed to dazzle the sophisticated viewer. He was also one of the first Italian painters to make etchings, and his many graceful drawings were eagerly collected.

### Self-portrait in a Convex Mirror (c. 1524)

[![Self-portrait in a Convex Mirror](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ab/Parmigianino_Selfportrait.jpg/1280px-Parmigianino_Selfportrait.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Parmigianino_Selfportrait.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Parmigianino_Selfportrait.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1524
- **Type:** Oil on convex panel
- **Where to see it:** Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Self-portrait%20in%20a%20Convex%20Mirror%20Parmigianino)

Painted around 1524, when Parmigianino was about 21, this self-portrait is on a wooden panel shaped like a section of a sphere, imitating the convex barber's mirror Vasari says he used as his model. His youthful face is undistorted at the centre, while his hand in the foreground swells enormously and the room behind curves around the edges. He took it to Rome as a showpiece and presented it to Pope Clement VII. It later belonged to the writer Pietro Aretino and is now in the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna. It also inspired John Ashbery's prize-winning 1975 poem of the same name.

### Madonna with the Long Neck (c. 1534–40)

[![Madonna with the Long Neck](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Parmigianino_-_Madonna_and_Child_with_Angels%2C_known_as_the_Madonna_with_the_Long_Neck.jpg/1280px-Parmigianino_-_Madonna_and_Child_with_Angels%2C_known_as_the_Madonna_with_the_Long_Neck.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Parmigianino_-_Madonna_and_Child_with_Angels%2C_known_as_the_Madonna_with_the_Long_Neck.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Parmigianino_-_Madonna_and_Child_with_Angels%2C_known_as_the_Madonna_with_the_Long_Neck.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1534–40
- **Type:** Oil on panel
- **Where to see it:** Uffizi Gallery, Florence
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Madonna%20with%20the%20Long%20Neck%20Parmigianino)

Commissioned in 1534 for a chapel in the church of Santa Maria dei Servi in Parma, this altarpiece was still unfinished when Parmigianino died in 1540. The Virgin, with a swan-like neck and tapering fingers, presides over an impossibly long, sleeping Christ Child, whose pose foreshadows the dead Christ of the Pietà. Crowded angels press in at the left, and in the strangely scaled background a tiny prophet unrolls a scroll beside a row of unfinished columns. The disproportion is deliberate: the elongated beauty refers to medieval poetic images of the Virgin's neck as an ivory tower, and it was meant to delight connoisseurs. It is now in the Uffizi.
