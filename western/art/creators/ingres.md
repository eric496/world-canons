## Biography

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres was born in Montauban, the son of a decorative artist, and showed early talent for both drawing and the violin. He trained in David's studio in Paris and won the Prix de Rome in 1801. He spent the years 1806–24 in Rome and Florence, often supporting himself by making exquisite portrait drawings of tourists. His Vow of Louis XIII at the Salon of 1824 made him the champion of the classical tradition against Delacroix's Romanticism. He later directed the French Academy in Rome and continued to paint into his eighties, finishing The Turkish Bath in 1862.

Ingres declared that 'drawing is the probity of art'. His paintings are defined by pure, flowing contour, smooth surfaces and an ideal beauty derived from Raphael and Greek vases, yet he often bent anatomy for the sake of graceful line. His portraits, such as Monsieur Bertin and Madame Moitessier, are among the most compelling of the 19th century. His mix of classicism and sensual distortion influenced Degas, Matisse and Picasso. In French, 'violon d'Ingres' means a hobby, after his love of the violin.

## Key artworks

### La Grande Odalisque (1814)

[![La Grande Odalisque](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/La_grande_odalisque_-_Jean-Auguste_Dominique_Ingres_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_1158.jpg/1280px-La_grande_odalisque_-_Jean-Auguste_Dominique_Ingres_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_1158.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:La_grande_odalisque_-_Jean-Auguste_Dominique_Ingres_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_1158.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:La_grande_odalisque_-_Jean-Auguste_Dominique_Ingres_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_1158.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1814
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée du Louvre, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=La%20Grande%20Odalisque%20Jean-Auguste-Dominique%20Ingres)

Ingres painted this nude in Rome in 1814 for Caroline Murat, Napoleon's sister and Queen of Naples, but she never received it because the Murats fell from power. A concubine of the harem (an odalisque) reclines on a couch with her back to us and turns her head to look over her shoulder. She holds a peacock-feather fan, and a hookah stands nearby. When the painting was shown at the Salon of 1819, critics complained that her back was too long: she seems to have two or three extra vertebrae, and her arm and pelvis are distorted. Ingres sacrificed anatomy for a long, sinuous line and cool, smooth sensuality. The Louvre bought it in 1899.
