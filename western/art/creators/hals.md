## Biography

Frans Hals was born in Antwerp, but his Protestant family moved north to Haarlem after Antwerp fell to Spanish forces in 1585. He spent his whole career there. He was the city's leading portraitist, painting merchants, preachers and their wives, as well as large group portraits of Haarlem's civic militia companies and the governors of charitable institutions. He also painted lively genre heads of drinkers, musicians and laughing children. Despite his success he often had money troubles, and in old age he received a small pension from the city. He died in Haarlem in 1666, and the museum there bears his name.

Hals is famous for his loose, rapid, visible brushwork, which seems to catch his sitters' expressions mid-word or mid-laugh. His portraits feel spontaneous and full of life compared with the polished finish of his contemporaries. After falling out of fashion he was enthusiastically rediscovered in the 19th century, when Manet, Courbet, Van Gogh and the American painter John Singer Sargent admired the freedom of his handling.

## Key artworks

### The Laughing Cavalier (1624)

[![The Laughing Cavalier](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/Cavalier_soldier_Hals-1624x.jpg/1280px-Cavalier_soldier_Hals-1624x.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cavalier_soldier_Hals-1624x.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cavalier_soldier_Hals-1624x.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1624
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Wallace Collection, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Laughing%20Cavalier%20Frans%20Hals)

An inscription records that this sitter was 26 years old in 1624, but his identity is unknown. The title is a Victorian invention: he is not laughing, though his raised eyebrows, upturned moustache and confident pose with his hand on his hip give him a cocky, amused air. The real showpiece is his richly embroidered sleeve, painted with dazzling freedom. It is decorated with motifs of arrows, flaming cornucopias, lovers' knots and bees, symbols of the pleasures and pains of love that suggest it may have been painted for a betrothal. The 4th Marquess of Hertford bought it in a famous Paris auction in 1865, and it is now one of the best-loved works in the Wallace Collection in London.
