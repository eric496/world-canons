# Jackson Pollock

**1912 – 1956** · American  
Born: Cody, Wyoming · Died: Springs, New York  
Movements: [Abstract Expressionism](../movements/abstract-expressionism.md)  

> Pollock poured and dripped paint onto canvas laid on the floor, making the act of painting the subject. He died in a car crash at 44.

## Biography

Paul Jackson Pollock was born in Cody, Wyoming, and grew up in Arizona and California. In New York he studied with the Regionalist Thomas Hart Benton at the Art Students League, worked for the government-funded Federal Art Project during the Depression, and attended an experimental workshop run by the Mexican muralist David Alfaro Siqueiros. He struggled with alcoholism all his life. In 1943 the collector Peggy Guggenheim gave him a contract and commissioned a large mural. In 1945 he married the painter Lee Krasner, and they moved to a farmhouse in Springs, Long Island, where his drip paintings developed between 1947 and 1950. In 1949 Life magazine asked whether he was the greatest living painter in the United States. He died in a car crash in August 1956, aged 44.

Pollock laid canvases on the floor and poured, dripped and flung paint from sticks, hardened brushes and cans, moving around and into the work. The finished paintings are dense, all-over webs of line without a central focus. Photographs and a film of him at work by Hans Namuth made the act of painting itself famous and shaped the idea of 'action painting'. He became the emblematic figure of Abstract Expressionism.

## Key artworks

### Autumn Rhythm (Number 30) (1950)

> 🖼️ **[View it at The Met →](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/488978)**  
> <sub>Still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here.</sub>

- **Date:** 1950
- **Type:** Enamel on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Autumn%20Rhythm%20%28Number%2030%29%20Jackson%20Pollock)

Pollock painted this huge canvas, more than 5 metres wide, at his studio in Springs, Long Island, in the autumn of 1950, at the height of his drip-painting period. He laid the unprimed canvas on the floor and poured and flung black, white, brown and teal enamel paint onto it with sticks and brushes, working from all sides. The result is a dense, rhythmic web of lines and splatters with no single focal point, which seems to extend beyond the edges of the canvas. It conveys the physical energy of its making. The Metropolitan Museum of Art acquired it in 1957, a year after Pollock's death.
