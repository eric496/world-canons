# Praxiteles

**active c. 375 BC – c. 330 BC** · Ancient Greek  
Born: Athens · Died: unknown  
Movements: [Classical Antiquity (Greece & Rome)](../movements/classical-antiquity.md)  

> The leading Athenian sculptor of the 4th century BC, known for softer, more sensuous marble figures. He made the first monumental fully nude statue of a goddess.

## Biography

Praxiteles was the leading Athenian sculptor of the mid-4th century BC and probably the son of the sculptor Kephisodotos. He worked mainly in marble and had it delicately painted. He moved away from the grandeur of the Parthenon generation towards softer, more relaxed and sensuous figures, often in an S-shaped pose that leans on a support. His works included the Apollo Sauroktonos (Apollo the Lizard-Slayer). A Hermes holding the infant Dionysus, found at Olympia in 1877, is often attributed to him, though scholars still debate whether it is his original.

Ancient writers linked him romantically with the celebrated courtesan Phryne, said to be the model for his Aphrodite. His gentle, intimate approach to the gods reshaped Hellenistic and Roman taste and, through Roman copies, the Renaissance ideal of the female nude.

## Key artworks

### Aphrodite of Knidos (c. 350 BC (Roman copies))

[![Aphrodite of Knidos](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Cnidus_Aphrodite_Altemps_Inv8619.jpg/1280px-Cnidus_Aphrodite_Altemps_Inv8619.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cnidus_Aphrodite_Altemps_Inv8619.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cnidus_Aphrodite_Altemps_Inv8619.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 350 BC (Roman copies)
- **Type:** Marble (original lost)
- **Where to see it:** Roman copies, incl. Palazzo Altemps, Rome, and the Colonna Venus, Vatican Museums
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Aphrodite%20of%20Knidos%20Praxiteles)

According to Pliny, Praxiteles made two statues of Aphrodite. The people of Kos chose the modestly draped one, and the city of Knidos bought the nude one, which made the city famous. The goddess is shown about to bathe, lowering her robe onto a water jar and lifting one hand before her body in a gesture later called the Venus Pudica (modest Venus). She stood in a round shrine that let visitors walk all the way around her, and ancient writers told stories of travellers overwhelmed by her beauty. The original is lost. Roman copies such as the Colonna Venus in the Vatican and the version in Rome's Palazzo Altemps preserve its composition, the first monumental fully nude goddess in Greek art.
