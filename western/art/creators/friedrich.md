## Biography

Caspar David Friedrich was born in Greifswald on the Baltic coast, then part of Swedish Pomerania. His childhood was marked by loss: his mother died when he was seven, and a brother drowned trying to save him when he fell through the ice. He studied at the Copenhagen Academy and from 1798 settled in Dresden. A prize from Goethe in 1805 and his controversial Tetschen Altarpiece (The Cross in the Mountains, 1808), a landscape used as an altarpiece, brought him attention. In 1810 the Prussian crown prince bought The Monk by the Sea and The Abbey in the Oakwood. After a stroke in 1835 he could barely paint, and he died in poverty and obscurity in 1840.

Friedrich turned landscape into a vehicle for religious and philosophical meaning: moonrises, Gothic ruins, misty mountains, Baltic shores and frozen seas, often with solitary figures seen from behind (Rückenfiguren) gazing into the infinite. His paintings embody the Romantic sublime and the individual's relationship with nature and God. Forgotten after his death, he was rediscovered in an exhibition in Berlin in 1906, and his influence can be traced to Munch and the Abstract Expressionists.

## Key artworks

### Wanderer above the Sea of Fog (c. 1818)

[![Wanderer above the Sea of Fog](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Caspar_David_Friedrich_-_Wanderer_above_the_Sea_of_Fog.jpeg/1280px-Caspar_David_Friedrich_-_Wanderer_above_the_Sea_of_Fog.jpeg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Caspar_David_Friedrich_-_Wanderer_above_the_Sea_of_Fog.jpeg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Caspar_David_Friedrich_-_Wanderer_above_the_Sea_of_Fog.jpeg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1818
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Hamburger Kunsthalle, Hamburg
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Wanderer%20above%20the%20Sea%20of%20Fog%20Caspar%20David%20Friedrich)

Painted around 1818 and now in the Hamburger Kunsthalle, this small painting has become the definitive image of Romanticism. A man in a dark green frock coat stands with his back to us on a rocky summit, leaning on a stick. He looks out over a sea of fog from which rocky peaks and distant mountains emerge. We see what he sees, and we are left to imagine his thoughts and feelings in the face of nature's vastness. The landscape is a composite of studies made in the Elbe Sandstone Mountains. The figure has been interpreted as a self-portrait, an image of the individual before God, or a memorial to a fallen officer.
