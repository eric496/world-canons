# John Singer Sargent

**1856 – 1925** · American  
Born: Florence · Died: London  
Movements: [Realism](../movements/realism.md)  

> The leading society portraitist of his era, known for dazzling, fluent brushwork.

## Biography

John Singer Sargent was born in Florence to American expatriate parents and grew up travelling around Europe. He trained in Paris from 1874 with the portraitist Carolus-Duran and made his name at the Salon with portraits and the Spanish dance scene El Jaleo. The scandal over Madame X in 1884 prompted him to move to London, where he painted Carnation, Lily, Lily, Rose (Tate) and became the most fashionable portraitist on both sides of the Atlantic. Around 1907 he largely gave up formal portraiture in favour of landscapes, watercolours and murals for the Boston Public Library and Museum of Fine Arts. As an official war artist in the First World War, he painted the monumental Gassed (Imperial War Museum).

Sargent's brushwork is fluent, bold and economical, influenced by Velázquez and Hals. He captured the glamour and nervous elegance of the Edwardian elite, as well as sparkling light in his watercolours of Venice, the Alps and the Middle East. He produced around 900 oil paintings and more than 2,000 watercolours.

- **Date:** 1883–84
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Madame%20X%20%28Madame%20Pierre%20Gautreau%29%20John%20Singer%20Sargent)

Virginie Amélie Avegno Gautreau was an American-born Parisian society beauty, married to a French banker, known for her lavender-powdered skin and striking profile. Sargent persuaded her to sit for him and painted her in 1883–84 in a daringly low-cut black satin gown with jewelled straps, her head in sharp profile and her body turned towards us. At the Salon of 1884, the pose and the original right strap, painted slipping off her shoulder, caused a scandal. Sargent repainted the strap, and he moved to London soon afterwards. He kept the painting for more than 30 years and sold it in 1916 to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, calling it the best thing he had ever done. The anonymous title was meant to protect the sitter's identity.
