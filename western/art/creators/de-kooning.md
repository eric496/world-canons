# Willem de Kooning

**1904 – 1997** · Dutch-American  
Born: Rotterdam, Netherlands · Died: East Hampton, New York  
Movements: [Abstract Expressionism](../movements/abstract-expressionism.md)  

> A leading gestural Abstract Expressionist whose violent, energetic brushwork never fully abandoned the figure.

## Biography

Willem de Kooning was born in Rotterdam and apprenticed at 12 to a firm of commercial artists and decorators while studying at night at the Rotterdam Academy. In 1926 he stowed away on a ship to the United States. He worked as a house painter and commercial artist and, during the Depression, for the Federal Art Project, and he formed a close friendship with Arshile Gorky. He married the painter Elaine Fried in 1943. His first solo exhibition, of black-and-white abstractions, was in 1948. The Woman series of the early 1950s caused a sensation. He moved permanently to Springs, East Hampton, in 1963. In his last years he developed Alzheimer's disease, and the late works he made in this period remain debated. He died in 1997.

De Kooning was a leading gestural Abstract Expressionist, but unlike Pollock he never fully abandoned the figure. His paintings are built from slashing, sweeping brushstrokes, layers of paint scraped and reworked, and a constant tension between abstraction and figuration, especially in his images of women and landscapes. One of his paintings, Interchange (1955), reportedly sold privately in 2015 for around $300 million.

## Key artworks

### Woman I (1950–52)

> 🖼️ **[View it at MoMA →](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79810)**  
> <sub>Still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here.</sub>

- **Date:** 1950–52
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Woman%20I%20Willem%20de%20Kooning)

De Kooning worked on Woman I from 1950 to 1952, painting, scraping and repainting it dozens of times. At one point he abandoned it, and the art historian Meyer Schapiro reportedly encouraged him to continue. The result is a ferocious female figure with huge staring eyes, a toothy grin and massive breasts, built up from slashing, violent brushstrokes of pink, yellow, green and blue that merge with the background. Drawing on ancient fertility figures and American billboard pin-ups, it shocked critics who saw abstraction as the only serious path. It has been criticised as misogynistic and praised as a powerful image. The Museum of Modern Art acquired it in 1953.
