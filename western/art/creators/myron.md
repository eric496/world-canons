# Myron

**active c. 470 BC – c. 440 BC** · Ancient Greek  
Born: Eleutherae, Greece · Died: unknown  
Movements: [Classical Antiquity (Greece & Rome)](../movements/classical-antiquity.md)  

> A bronze sculptor of the early Classical period, active in Athens,, admired for capturing athletes in motion. None of his originals survive; his work is known through Roman marble copies.

## Biography

Myron was a bronze sculptor working in Athens in the decades around 470–440 BC. This was the 'Severe' or early Classical phase, when Greek sculpture was moving away from stiff archaic poses. Ancient tradition says he trained under Ageladas of Argos, who also taught Polykleitos. His most celebrated works were athletes and animals. Many ancient poems praise a bronze cow by him that was supposedly so lifelike that calves tried to suckle it and herdsmen tried to drive it off.

None of Myron's originals survive, so he is known through Roman marble copies and the remarks of writers such as Pliny the Elder. Pliny admired his range and rhythm but said he paid less attention to the expression of feeling. Myron's achievement was to capture a body at the instant of greatest action while keeping it balanced and calm. That idea runs through Western sculpture from the Renaissance to the Olympic imagery of the modern age.

## Key artworks

### Discobolus (Discus Thrower) (c. 460–450 BC (Roman copy, 2nd century AD))

- **Date:** c. 460–450 BC (Roman copy, 2nd century AD)
- **Type:** Marble (Roman copy of lost bronze)
- **Where to see it:** Roman copies; the best-known marble (Lancellotti) is in the National Roman Museum, Palazzo Massimo, Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discobolus%20%28Discus%20Thrower%29%20Myron)

Myron's bronze original of about 460–450 BC is lost, but its fame led the Romans to make many marble copies. The best known is the Lancellotti Discobolus, found in Rome in 1781, now in the Palazzo Massimo. The athlete is caught at the top of his backswing, the instant before he unwinds to throw. His body forms two interlocking arcs, like a drawn bow, yet his face is completely calm. The pose is not quite anatomically possible, which shows that Myron was designing a perfect image rather than recording a real movement. It has become a universal emblem of athletics and the Olympic ideal.
