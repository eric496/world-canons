# Andrea Mantegna

**c. 1431 – 1506** · Italian  
Born: Isola di Carturo, near Padua · Died: Mantua  
Movements: [Early Italian Renaissance](../movements/early-renaissance.md)  

> Court painter to the Gonzaga in Mantua, famous for archaeological detail and daring foreshortening.

<sub>Portrait: Bronze bust in his funerary chapel, Sant'Andrea, Mantua · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mantegna_Bronze_Bust_San_Andrea_Mantua.png)</sub>

## Biography

Andrea Mantegna was adopted as a boy by the Paduan painter and collector Francesco Squarcione. In Padua he absorbed the university city's passion for antiquity and Donatello's recent sculpture there. He set up independently while still a teenager and married Nicolosia Bellini, which made him brother-in-law to Giovanni Bellini. His early frescoes in the Ovetari Chapel in the Eremitani church were largely destroyed by bombing in 1944. From 1460 until his death he was court painter to the Gonzaga family in Mantua.

Mantegna was the most archaeological painter of the Renaissance, filling his scenes with accurately studied Roman architecture, inscriptions and armour. His figures can look as hard and sharp as carved stone. He was a master of steep perspective and foreshortening and an influential engraver, whose prints spread his style north of the Alps to artists such as Dürer. His series The Triumphs of Caesar is now at Hampton Court.

### Camera degli Sposi (1465–74)

[![Camera degli Sposi](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Mantegna_-_Camera_degli_Sposi.jpg/1280px-Mantegna_-_Camera_degli_Sposi.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mantegna_-_Camera_degli_Sposi.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mantegna_-_Camera_degli_Sposi.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1465–74
- **Type:** Fresco
- **Where to see it:** Ducal Palace, Mantua
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Camera%20degli%20Sposi%20Andrea%20Mantegna)

Between 1465 and 1474 Mantegna frescoed a room in the Castello di San Giorgio, part of the Gonzaga palace in Mantua. It is now called the Camera degli Sposi ('Bridal Chamber'), though it was used as a reception and bedroom. The walls show Marquis Ludovico Gonzaga with his family, courtiers, servants and even a favourite dog, in one of the earliest group portraits of a court. The ceiling is the true marvel. Mantegna painted a circular opening, or oculus, to the sky, with women, a peacock and winged cherubs peering down over a balustrade. A plant pot balances precariously on the edge. It is the first di sotto in sù ('seen from below') ceiling illusion of the Renaissance and a model for Baroque ceilings.

### Lamentation of Christ (c. 1480)

[![Lamentation of Christ](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/The_dead_Christ_and_three_mourners%2C_by_Andrea_Mantegna.jpg/1280px-The_dead_Christ_and_three_mourners%2C_by_Andrea_Mantegna.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_dead_Christ_and_three_mourners%2C_by_Andrea_Mantegna.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_dead_Christ_and_three_mourners%2C_by_Andrea_Mantegna.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1480
- **Type:** Tempera on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Pinacoteca di Brera, Milan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lamentation%20of%20Christ%20Andrea%20Mantegna)

In this painting from around 1480, now in the Brera in Milan, the dead Christ lies on a marble slab seen from his feet. He is drastically foreshortened so that the wounds in his feet and hands are thrust towards the viewer. Mantegna actually shortened the legs and enlarged the head to make the image readable and heighten its impact. At the left, the weeping Virgin, St John and Mary Magdalene squeeze into the edge of the picture. The colours are muted, almost like stone. The painting was found in Mantegna's studio after his death, which suggests he kept it for his own devotion.
