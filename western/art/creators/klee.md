## Biography

Paul Klee was born at Münchenbuchsee near Bern to a musical family and was himself an accomplished violinist. He studied art in Munich, associated with the Blue Rider group, and in 1914 travelled to Tunisia with August Macke and Louis Moilliet. There he had a revelation about colour, writing in his diary that colour and he were now one, and that he was a painter. He taught at the Bauhaus from 1921 to 1931 and then at the Düsseldorf Academy, until the Nazis dismissed him in 1933. He returned to Bern, and more than 100 of his works were seized from German museums as 'degenerate'. Despite a serious illness from 1935 he was extraordinarily productive in his last years. He died in 1940.

Klee produced around 10,000 works of great variety: drawings, watercolours, oils and puppets. He combined childlike simplicity, irony, music, poetry, writing and signs, colour theory and fantasy. His teaching notes are among the most important texts on modern art. The Zentrum Paul Klee in Bern holds the largest collection of his work.

## Key artworks

### Twittering Machine (1922)

[![Twittering Machine](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7e/Die_Zwitscher-Maschine_%28Twittering_Machine%29%2C_1922_-_Paul_Klee.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Die_Zwitscher-Maschine_%28Twittering_Machine%29%2C_1922_-_Paul_Klee.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Die_Zwitscher-Maschine_%28Twittering_Machine%29%2C_1922_-_Paul_Klee.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1922
- **Type:** Watercolour and oil transfer drawing
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Twittering%20Machine%20Paul%20Klee)

In this small work of 1922, made with Klee's oil-transfer drawing technique and watercolour, four birdlike creatures with spindly legs perch on a thin wire or branch connected to a hand crank, beaks open. If the crank were turned, they would presumably twitter. Against a misty pinkish-blue background, the image is both whimsical and slightly sinister: the birds may be lures, and below them is a pit. It has been read as a satire on mechanised society and the mechanisation of nature and art. In 1937 the Nazis confiscated it from the National Gallery in Berlin as 'degenerate', and in 1939 the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired it.
