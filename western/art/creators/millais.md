## Biography

John Everett Millais was a child prodigy: at 11 he became the youngest student ever admitted to the Royal Academy Schools. In 1848, with William Holman Hunt and Dante Gabriel Rossetti, he founded the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. His Christ in the House of His Parents (1850) was attacked by Charles Dickens for its unidealised realism, but the critic John Ruskin defended the group. In 1855 he married Effie Gray, whose marriage to Ruskin had been annulled. In later life he became a hugely successful painter of portraits, landscapes and sentimental subjects. His painting Bubbles was famously used to advertise Pears soap. He was made a baronet and in 1896 became President of the Royal Academy, shortly before his death.

Millais's early Pre-Raphaelite work is distinguished by brilliant, jewel-like colour on a white ground, minute botanical accuracy painted outdoors from nature, and intense emotional storytelling. His later broader, looser style made him one of the richest artists of the Victorian age.

## Key artworks

### Ophelia (1851–52)

[![Ophelia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/John_Everett_Millais_-_Ophelia_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/1280px-John_Everett_Millais_-_Ophelia_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:John_Everett_Millais_-_Ophelia_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:John_Everett_Millais_-_Ophelia_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1851–52
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Tate Britain, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ophelia%20John%20Everett%20Millais)

Painted in 1851–52 and now in Tate Britain, this painting illustrates the death of Ophelia in Shakespeare's Hamlet: after her father's death and Hamlet's rejection, she falls into a stream and drowns while singing. Millais spent months painting the riverbank background on the spot beside the Hogsmill River in Surrey, recording every plant in meticulous detail, many with symbolic meaning: the poppy for death, violets for faithfulness, daisies for innocence. The model was Elizabeth Siddal, who posed in a bath of water in his London studio. The lamps under the bath went out, she caught a severe cold, and her father demanded that Millais pay the doctor's bills.
