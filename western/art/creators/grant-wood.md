# Grant Wood

**1891 – 1942** · American  
Born: near Anamosa, Iowa · Died: Iowa City, Iowa  
Movements: [American Modernism](../movements/american-modernism.md)  

> A leader of Regionalism who painted the rural Midwest in a crisp style inspired by Northern Renaissance painting.

## Biography

Grant Wood was born on a farm near Anamosa, Iowa, and grew up in Cedar Rapids after his father's death. He trained in crafts and design, taught art in the Cedar Rapids public schools, and made several trips to Europe in the 1920s. On a 1928 trip to Munich, to supervise the making of a stained-glass window, he was impressed by the clarity and detail of early Flemish and German painting. Back in Iowa, he developed his mature style and became, with Thomas Hart Benton and John Steuart Curry, one of the leaders of Regionalism, which celebrated rural Midwestern America. He founded an artists' colony at Stone City and taught at the University of Iowa. He died in 1942.

Wood's paintings, with their crisp outlines, smooth surfaces and stylised, rounded landscapes, depict the farms, people and legends of the Midwest. Whether his work is affectionate tribute, gentle satire or both remains a matter of debate. American Gothic made him famous overnight.

## Key artworks

### American Gothic (1930)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grant_Wood_-_American_Gothic_%281930%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1930
- **Type:** Oil on beaverboard
- **Where to see it:** Art Institute of Chicago
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=American%20Gothic%20Grant%20Wood)

In 1930 Grant Wood saw a small white house in Eldon, Iowa, with a Gothic Revival window in the gable, and imagined the kind of people who might live there. He painted a stern farmer holding a pitchfork and a woman beside him in front of the house. The models were his sister, Nan Wood Graham, and his dentist, Byron McKeeby. Wood said they were meant to be a father and his daughter, not husband and wife. The pitchfork's three tines are echoed in the stitching of the man's overalls and the window. The painting won a prize at the Art Institute of Chicago in 1930, which bought it. It quickly became an icon of rural America and one of the most parodied images in art. Whether it satirises small-town Midwestern values or celebrates them is still debated.
