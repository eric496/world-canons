## Biography

Johann Heinrich Füssli was born in Zürich, the son of a painter and writer, and was ordained as a Zwinglian minister. In 1762 he had to leave Switzerland after helping to expose a corrupt magistrate. He settled in London, where Sir Joshua Reynolds encouraged him to become a painter. He spent 1770–78 in Rome studying Michelangelo, then returned to London, anglicised his name to Henry Fuseli and became one of the most original artists of the Royal Academy, eventually its Professor of Painting and Keeper. He was a friend of William Blake and of the philosopher Mary Wollstonecraft.

Fuseli painted dramatic, sometimes violent and erotic scenes from Shakespeare, Milton, Dante and Germanic legend, as well as dreams and the supernatural. His figures are muscular, contorted and theatrically lit. He projected a Milton Gallery and contributed to John Boydell's Shakespeare Gallery. His fascination with the irrational and the subconscious makes him a forerunner of Romanticism and Symbolism.

## Key artworks

### The Nightmare (1781)

[![The Nightmare](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/15/Johann_Heinrich_F%C3%BCssli_-_The_Nightmare_55.5.A-d1-2019-04-15.jpg/1280px-Johann_Heinrich_F%C3%BCssli_-_The_Nightmare_55.5.A-d1-2019-04-15.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Johann_Heinrich_F%C3%BCssli_-_The_Nightmare_55.5.A-d1-2019-04-15.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Johann_Heinrich_F%C3%BCssli_-_The_Nightmare_55.5.A-d1-2019-04-15.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1781
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Detroit Institute of Arts
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Nightmare%20Henry%20Fuseli)

Fuseli's sensational painting of 1781, shown at the Royal Academy in 1782, made him famous overnight. A young woman in white lies sprawled across a bed, her head and arms hanging backwards in a deep, troubled sleep. On her chest squats a demonic incubus, and a wild-eyed horse, a pun on 'mare', thrusts its head through the curtains. The picture fused eroticism, horror and dream at a time of growing interest in the irrational. Engravings spread it widely, it was parodied in caricatures, and Fuseli painted several versions. This first version is in the Detroit Institute of Arts.
