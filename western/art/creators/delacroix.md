## Biography

Eugène Delacroix was born at Charenton-Saint-Maurice near Paris. Rumours have long suggested that the statesman Talleyrand was his real father. He trained with Pierre-Narcisse Guérin, studied Rubens and Venetian painters in the Louvre, and was profoundly influenced by Géricault. The Barque of Dante (1822), The Massacre at Chios (1824) and The Death of Sardanapalus (1827) made him the leader of the Romantic school. A journey to Morocco and Algeria in 1832 gave him a lifelong store of exotic subjects. He also painted vast decorative schemes in the Palais Bourbon, the Louvre and the church of Saint-Sulpice, and kept a celebrated journal.

Delacroix championed colour, movement and passionate feeling over the line-based classicism of Ingres. He was a master of vibrant juxtaposed colours that intensify each other, a lesson taken up by the Impressionists and Post-Impressionists, and his subjects came from literature, history, politics and the 'Orient'. Baudelaire praised him as the greatest painter of the age. Cézanne, Van Gogh and Matisse all acknowledged his influence.

## Key artworks

### Liberty Leading the People (1830)

[![Liberty Leading the People](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/La_Libert%C3%A9_guidant_le_peuple_-_Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_129_-_apr%C3%A8s_restauration_2024.jpg/1280px-La_Libert%C3%A9_guidant_le_peuple_-_Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_129_-_apr%C3%A8s_restauration_2024.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:La_Libert%C3%A9_guidant_le_peuple_-_Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_RF_129_-_apr%C3%A8s_restauration_2024.jpg)

- **Date:** 1830
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée du Louvre, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Liberty%20Leading%20the%20People%20Eug%C3%A8ne%20Delacroix)

This painting commemorates the July Revolution of 27–29 July 1830, the 'Three Glorious Days' in which Parisians overthrew King Charles X. Over a barricade of rubble and corpses, a bare-breasted woman, the personification of Liberty in a red Phrygian cap, holds aloft the tricolour flag and a musket and urges the people forward. Around her are a man in a top hat, possibly Delacroix himself, a worker with a sabre and a boy brandishing two pistols. The French state bought it in 1831, but it was soon hidden away as too inflammatory. Today it is a national symbol, used on the 100-franc banknote and in countless political images. It is in the Louvre, which restored it in 2023–24, revealing its original colours.
