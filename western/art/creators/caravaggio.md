# Caravaggio (Michelangelo Merisi)

**1571 – 1610** · Italian  
Born: Milan · Died: Porto Ercole  
Movements: [Baroque](../movements/baroque.md)  

> Caravaggio used street models and dramatic spotlighting (tenebrism) to make sacred scenes startlingly real. His violent life was as famous as his art, and his followers spread his style across Europe.

## Biography

Michelangelo Merisi was born in Milan in 1571 into a family from the nearby town of Caravaggio, which gave him his name. He trained with the Milanese painter Simone Peterzano and arrived in Rome in the early 1590s, poor and unknown. He first made his name with still lifes and half-length paintings of young men, then found a powerful patron in Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte. His public breakthrough came in 1600 with the Saint Matthew paintings for the Contarelli Chapel. Records show repeated arrests for brawling, carrying weapons and assault. In 1606 he killed Ranuccio Tomassoni in a fight and fled Rome under a death sentence. He worked in Naples, Malta (where he was briefly made a Knight of St John before being imprisoned and expelled), and Sicily. He died in 1610 at Porto Ercole, on his way back to Rome to seek a papal pardon.

Caravaggio painted saints and biblical figures from ordinary street models, with dirty feet, wrinkled faces and torn clothes, and plunged them into deep shadow cut by a raking spotlight. This dramatic technique is known as tenebrism. His radical realism shocked some patrons, and several altarpieces were rejected. It also inspired a generation of followers across Europe, the Caravaggisti, from Utrecht to Naples and Seville, and transformed Baroque painting.

### Judith Beheading Holofernes (c. 1598–99)

[![Judith Beheading Holofernes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Caravaggio_-_Giuditta_e_Oloferne_%28ca._1599%29.jpg/1280px-Caravaggio_-_Giuditta_e_Oloferne_%28ca._1599%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Caravaggio_-_Giuditta_e_Oloferne_%28ca._1599%29.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Caravaggio_-_Giuditta_e_Oloferne_%28ca._1599%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1598–99
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica (Palazzo Barberini), Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Judith%20Beheading%20Holofernes%20Caravaggio%20%28Michelangelo%20Merisi%29)

Painted around 1598–99 and now in the Palazzo Barberini in Rome, this canvas shows the Old Testament heroine Judith killing the Assyrian general Holofernes to save her people. Caravaggio shows the gruesome moment itself: Holofernes screams and twists on his bed as Judith saws through his neck with his own sword and blood spurts across the sheets. Judith holds herself at arm's length with a frown of concentration and distaste, and her old maid stands by eagerly holding the bag for the head. The strong light from the left, the deep black background and the red curtain intensify the drama. The model for Judith is thought to be the Roman courtesan Fillide Melandroni.

### The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599–1600)

[![The Calling of Saint Matthew](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Caravaggio_%E2%80%94_The_Calling_of_Saint_Matthew.jpg/1280px-Caravaggio_%E2%80%94_The_Calling_of_Saint_Matthew.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Caravaggio_%E2%80%94_The_Calling_of_Saint_Matthew.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Caravaggio_%E2%80%94_The_Calling_of_Saint_Matthew.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1599–1600
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Contarelli Chapel, San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Calling%20of%20Saint%20Matthew%20Caravaggio%20%28Michelangelo%20Merisi%29)

This was one of a pair of large canvases that made Caravaggio famous when they were unveiled in 1600 in the Contarelli Chapel of San Luigi dei Francesi, the French national church in Rome, where they still hang. Christ, almost hidden behind St Peter, enters a dim counting room and points at the tax collector Levi, who will become the apostle Matthew. His gesture echoes God's hand in Michelangelo's Creation of Adam. A shaft of light enters from the upper right, following the direction of the real window in the chapel, and falls on the faces of men in contemporary clothes counting money at a table. Scholars still debate whether Matthew is the bearded man pointing to himself, as if asking 'Me?', or the young man hunched over coins at the end of the table.

### The Supper at Emmaus (1601)

[![The Supper at Emmaus](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/1602-3_Caravaggio%2CSupper_at_Emmaus_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg/1280px-1602-3_Caravaggio%2CSupper_at_Emmaus_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1602-3_Caravaggio%2CSupper_at_Emmaus_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1602-3_Caravaggio%2CSupper_at_Emmaus_National_Gallery%2C_London.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1601
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** National Gallery, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Supper%20at%20Emmaus%20Caravaggio%20%28Michelangelo%20Merisi%29)

In Luke's Gospel, two disciples meet a stranger on the road to Emmaus after the Crucifixion and recognise him as the risen Christ only when he blesses bread at supper. Caravaggio painted the instant of recognition in 1601, for the Roman nobleman Ciriaco Mattei. One disciple starts to rise from his chair, the other flings his arms wide, and his outstretched hand seems to pierce the picture plane. The innkeeper looks on, puzzled. Christ is shown young and beardless, which is unusual. A basket of fruit perches precariously on the very edge of the table, about to fall into our space. It is in the National Gallery, London.
