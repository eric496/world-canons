# Agesander, Athenodoros & Polydorus

**active 1st century BC** · Ancient Greek (Rhodian)  
Born: Rhodes · Died: unknown  
Movements: [Classical Antiquity (Greece & Rome)](../movements/classical-antiquity.md)  

> Three sculptors from Rhodes named by the Roman writer Pliny the Elder as the makers of the Laocoön group. Their exact dates and whether the work is an original or a copy are debated.

## Biography

Agesander, Athenodoros and Polydorus were sculptors from the island of Rhodes, a major centre of late Hellenistic sculpture. We know them almost entirely through Pliny the Elder, who named them as the makers of the Laocoön group in the palace of the emperor Titus and called it a work superior to all others in painting or bronze. Their names also appear on the sculptures found in 1957 in the grotto of the emperor Tiberius's villa at Sperlonga, which show episodes from the Odyssey.

Whether the Laocoön is a Hellenistic original of about 200 BC, a Roman copy of a bronze, or a new work made for Roman patrons in the 1st century BC or AD is still debated. Its writhing, theatrical style is the high point of the 'Hellenistic baroque', and its rediscovery in 1506 had an enormous effect on Michelangelo and later artists.

## Key artworks

### Laocoön and His Sons (c. 1st century BC – 1st century AD)

[![Laocoön and His Sons](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a6/Laoco%C3%B6n_and_his_sons_group.jpg/1280px-Laoco%C3%B6n_and_his_sons_group.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Laoco%C3%B6n_and_his_sons_group.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Laoco%C3%B6n_and_his_sons_group.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1st century BC – 1st century AD
- **Type:** Marble sculpture
- **Where to see it:** Museo Pio-Clementino, Vatican Museums
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Laoco%C3%B6n%20and%20His%20Sons%20Agesander%2C%20Athenodoros%20%26%20Polydorus)

In Virgil's Aeneid, Laocoön was the Trojan priest who warned against the wooden horse. The gods sent two great sea serpents to crush him and his sons. The sculptors show the moment of agony: the father's body twists and strains, his face contorted, while the serpents coil around all three. The group was dug up in a Roman vineyard in January 1506. Michelangelo and the architect Giuliano da Sangallo identified it on the spot from Pliny's description, and Pope Julius II bought it for the Vatican's Belvedere courtyard. Laocoön's missing right arm was restored as stretched upward. The original bent arm was found by the archaeologist Ludwig Pollak in 1906 and reattached in the 1950s. The group inspired Renaissance and Baroque artists and Lessing's famous essay on the limits of painting and poetry, Laokoon (1766).
