## Biography

Claude Monet was born in Paris and grew up in Le Havre, where he made a name drawing caricatures. The landscape painter Eugène Boudin introduced him to painting outdoors. In Paris he met Renoir, Sisley and Bazille in Charles Gleyre's studio, and the friends developed painting directly from nature. He endured years of poverty with his companion and later wife Camille Doncieux, who died in 1879. He spent 1870–71 in London and then lived at Argenteuil on the Seine. He helped organise the first independent group exhibition in 1874, which gave Impressionism its name. From 1883 he lived at Giverny, where he created a flower garden and a water garden with a Japanese bridge. He married Alice Hoschedé in 1892.

Monet devoted his life to capturing fleeting light and atmosphere. From the 1890s he painted series of the same motif at different times of day and seasons, including Haystacks, Poplars, Rouen Cathedral, the Houses of Parliament in London and his water lilies. Despite cataracts, which were operated on in 1923, he worked on huge water lily paintings until his death in 1926, and these were later seen as forerunners of abstract art.

### Impression, Sunrise (1872)

[![Impression, Sunrise](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Monet_-_Impression%2C_Sunrise.jpg/1280px-Monet_-_Impression%2C_Sunrise.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Monet_-_Impression%2C_Sunrise.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Monet_-_Impression%2C_Sunrise.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1872
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Impression%2C%20Sunrise%20Claude%20Monet)

Monet painted this view of the port of Le Havre, his home town, in the early morning, probably in 1872. Through the mist and smoke of the industrial harbour, an orange sun rises over the water, and its reflection forms a broken line of orange strokes. Small rowing boats appear as dark silhouettes. The painting was shown at the first independent group exhibition in Paris in 1874. The critic Louis Leroy seized on its title to mock the whole group as 'Impressionists', and the name stuck. It was stolen from the Musée Marmottan in 1985 and recovered in 1990.

### Woman with a Parasol – Madame Monet and Her Son (1875)

[![Woman with a Parasol – Madame Monet and Her Son](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Claude_Monet_-_Woman_with_a_Parasol_-_Madame_Monet_and_Her_Son_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/1280px-Claude_Monet_-_Woman_with_a_Parasol_-_Madame_Monet_and_Her_Son_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Claude_Monet_-_Woman_with_a_Parasol_-_Madame_Monet_and_Her_Son_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Claude_Monet_-_Woman_with_a_Parasol_-_Madame_Monet_and_Her_Son_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1875
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C.
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Woman%20with%20a%20Parasol%20%E2%80%93%20Madame%20Monet%20and%20Her%20Son%20Claude%20Monet)

Painted in 1875 at Argenteuil, this painting in the National Gallery of Art, Washington, shows Monet's wife Camille and their seven-year-old son Jean on a hillside on a breezy summer day. Seen from below against a bright blue sky with white clouds, Camille holds a green parasol, and her white dress and veil flutter in the wind. Painted with rapid, fluid strokes, it captures a fleeting moment of light and movement rather than a formal portrait. Monet returned to the theme in 1886 with two paintings of a woman with a parasol, now in the Musée d'Orsay.

### Water Lilies (Nymphéas) murals (c. 1914–26)

[![Water Lilies (Nymphéas) murals](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Reflections_of_Clouds_on_the_Water-Lily_Pond.jpg/1280px-Reflections_of_Clouds_on_the_Water-Lily_Pond.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Reflections_of_Clouds_on_the_Water-Lily_Pond.jpg)

<sub>Reflections of Clouds on the Water-Lily Pond, c. 1920, Museum of Modern Art, New York: a related triptych, not the Orangerie cycle Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Reflections_of_Clouds_on_the_Water-Lily_Pond.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1914–26
- **Type:** Oil on canvas (cycle)
- **Where to see it:** Musée de l'Orangerie, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Water%20Lilies%20%28Nymph%C3%A9as%29%20murals%20Claude%20Monet)

In his last decades Monet painted his water garden at Giverny obsessively, producing around 250 paintings of water lilies. After the Armistice of November 1918 he offered the French state a monumental ensemble of water lily paintings as a monument to peace. With the help of his friend Georges Clemenceau, it was installed in two oval rooms in the Musée de l'Orangerie in Paris, which opened in 1927, a few months after his death. The eight huge panels, each about two metres high and together about 100 metres long, surround the visitor with the surface of the pond: water, lilies, reflected clouds, willows, at different times of day. They are often seen as the first immersive installation and a forerunner of abstract painting.
