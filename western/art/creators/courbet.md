## Biography

Gustave Courbet was born in Ornans in the Jura region to a prosperous farming family. He moved to Paris in 1839 and taught himself mostly by copying in the Louvre. After Dinner at Ornans won him a medal in 1849, and then The Stone Breakers (destroyed in 1945), A Burial at Ornans and other large paintings of rural life caused scandal at the Salon. When the 1855 World's Fair rejected some of his works, he built his own 'Pavilion of Realism' nearby and issued a Realist manifesto. A committed republican, he took part in the Paris Commune of 1871, was blamed for the toppling of the Vendôme Column, and was imprisoned and later ordered to pay for its reconstruction. He fled to Switzerland in 1873 and died in exile in 1877.

Courbet declared that painting should depict only 'real and existing things', and he refused to paint angels because he had never seen one. He gave peasants, labourers and provincial townspeople the monumental scale once reserved for history painting. He worked with thick paint, often applied with a palette knife, and painted landscapes, seascapes, hunting scenes and provocative nudes, including The Origin of the World (1866). He was a key precursor of Manet and modern art.

### A Burial at Ornans (1849–50)

[![A Burial at Ornans](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Gustave_Courbet_-_A_Burial_at_Ornans_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg/1280px-Gustave_Courbet_-_A_Burial_at_Ornans_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gustave_Courbet_-_A_Burial_at_Ornans_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gustave_Courbet_-_A_Burial_at_Ornans_-_Google_Art_Project_2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1849–50
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Burial%20at%20Ornans%20Gustave%20Courbet)

This enormous canvas, more than 6 metres wide, was painted in 1849–50 and shown at the Salon of 1850–51. It depicts the funeral of an ordinary person in Courbet's home town in the Jura. About 45 life-size townspeople, including the mayor, the priest, gravediggers, mourning women and Courbet's own father and sisters, gather around an open grave beneath grey cliffs. There is no heavenly vision and no heroic story, only the reality of provincial life and death. Critics were outraged that such a 'vulgar' subject was given the scale of history painting. The work became a manifesto of Realism. It is in the Musée d'Orsay.

### The Painter's Studio (1855)

[![The Painter's Studio](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Courbet_LAtelier_du_peintre.jpg/1280px-Courbet_LAtelier_du_peintre.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Courbet_LAtelier_du_peintre.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Courbet_LAtelier_du_peintre.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1855
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Painter%27s%20Studio%20Gustave%20Courbet)

Courbet's full title for this vast work of 1855 is The Painter's Studio: A Real Allegory Summing Up a Seven-Year Phase of My Artistic and Moral Life. At the centre he sits painting a landscape, watched by a nude model and a small boy. At the left are figures representing society and its victims and exploiters: a hunter, a rabbi, a laborer, a prostitute, a priest and others. At the right are his friends and supporters, including the poet Baudelaire reading, the socialist philosopher Proudhon, the critic Champfleury and his patron Alfred Bruyas. The jury of the 1855 World's Fair rejected it, and Courbet showed it in his own Pavilion of Realism. It is in the Musée d'Orsay.
