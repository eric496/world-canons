## Biography

Thomas Gainsborough was born in Sudbury, Suffolk. He went to London at about 13 and trained with the French engraver Hubert Gravelot. He returned to Suffolk, painting portraits and landscapes in Sudbury and Ipswich, then moved in 1759 to the fashionable spa town of Bath, where he became a successful society portraitist. In 1768 he was a founder member of the Royal Academy. He settled in London in 1774, where he was patronised by George III and Queen Charlotte, competing with Sir Joshua Reynolds as the leading portraitist of the age.

Gainsborough said he painted portraits for money and landscapes for love. His portraits are elegant and informal, painted with light, feathery strokes and silvery colour, and often set in landscape settings inspired by Van Dyck. His landscapes and 'fancy pictures' of rustic children helped found the English landscape tradition that led to Constable.

## Key artworks

### Mr and Mrs Andrews (c. 1750)

[![Mr and Mrs Andrews](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Thomas_Gainsborough_-_Mr_and_Mrs_Andrews.jpg/1280px-Thomas_Gainsborough_-_Mr_and_Mrs_Andrews.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Thomas_Gainsborough_-_Mr_and_Mrs_Andrews.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Thomas_Gainsborough_-_Mr_and_Mrs_Andrews.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1750
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** National Gallery, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mr%20and%20Mrs%20Andrews%20Thomas%20Gainsborough)

Gainsborough painted this double portrait around 1750, soon after the wedding of Robert Andrews and Frances Carter in 1748, at their estate, Auberies, near Sudbury. The couple are posed at the left under an oak tree, he standing with his gun and dog, she seated in a blue silk dress. Most of the canvas is given to a wide view of their well-kept fields, with sheaves of corn, sheep and distant woods, a proud display of land ownership and modern agriculture. An unfinished area on Mrs Andrews's lap may have been meant for a book, a bird, or perhaps a future child. The National Gallery bought it in 1960.
