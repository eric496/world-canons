# Polykleitos

**active c. 450 BC – c. 420 BC** · Ancient Greek  
Born: Argos, Greece · Died: unknown  
Movements: [Classical Antiquity (Greece & Rome)](../movements/classical-antiquity.md)  

> A bronze sculptor who wrote the Canon, a treatise on ideal human proportion. His relaxed, weight-shifted stance (contrapposto) became a model for Western sculpture.

## Biography

Polykleitos ran a leading bronze workshop in Argos in the Peloponnese in the second half of the 5th century BC and taught a whole school of followers. He was famous for statues of victorious athletes, such as the Diadoumenos (a youth tying a victory ribbon round his head), and for a gold-and-ivory statue of Hera at Argos that ancient visitors ranked alongside Phidias's work.

His lasting influence came from his treatise, the Canon. It set out a system of ideal proportions in which every part of the body relates to every other by measured ratios (symmetria). He illustrated it with a statue, generally identified as the Doryphoros. The treatise is lost, but its ideas passed through Roman writers such as Vitruvius to the Renaissance, where Leonardo's Vitruvian Man takes up the same search for perfect human proportion.

- **Type:** Marble (Roman copy of lost bronze)
- **Where to see it:** National Archaeological Museum, Naples (copy from Pompeii)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Doryphoros%20%28Spear-Bearer%29%20Polykleitos)

The Doryphoros is generally identified as the statue Polykleitos made to demonstrate his Canon of ideal proportions. The bronze original is lost. The most complete Roman marble copy was found at Pompeii and is now in Naples. A powerfully built young man walks forward with his weight on his right leg. His relaxed left leg trails behind, his hips and shoulders tilt in opposite directions, and his left arm once held a spear over the shoulder. This balance of tense and relaxed limbs, called contrapposto or chiasmus, makes the figure look alive and poised even at rest. It became the model for countless later statues, from Roman emperors to Michelangelo's David.
