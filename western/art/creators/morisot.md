## Biography

Berthe Morisot was born in Bourges, the daughter of a senior government official, and grew up in Paris. Women were not admitted to the École des Beaux-Arts, so she and her sister Edma studied privately, copying in the Louvre and learning from Camille Corot. She met Édouard Manet in 1868 and posed for several of his paintings, including The Balcony. In 1874 she married Manet's brother Eugène, and she exhibited at the first Impressionist exhibition the same year. She showed at all but one of the eight exhibitions, missing only the year her daughter Julie was born. She died in 1895, aged 54, of pneumonia caught while nursing Julie.

Morisot painted the private world she knew: mothers and children, women dressing, reading and resting, gardens and seaside scenes. She worked with rapid, feathery, almost abstract brushstrokes and luminous whites, and her technique was among the most radical of the group. Long overshadowed by her male colleagues, she is now recognised as one of the core members of Impressionism.

## Key artworks

### The Cradle (1872)

[![The Cradle](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Berthe_Morisot_008.jpg/1280px-Berthe_Morisot_008.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Berthe_Morisot_008.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Berthe_Morisot_008.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1872
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Cradle%20Berthe%20Morisot)

Morisot painted this picture in 1872. It shows her elder sister Edma, who had given up painting after her marriage, watching over her sleeping baby daughter Blanche. A gauzy white veil drawn across the cradle frames the child, and Edma's arm and gaze create a diagonal connecting mother and child. Its tender subject and delicate handling of whites and transparent fabric made it one of the most admired works at the first Impressionist exhibition in 1874. The Louvre acquired it in 1930, and it is now in the Musée d'Orsay.
