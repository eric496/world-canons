# James McNeill Whistler

**1834 – 1903** · American  
Born: Lowell, Massachusetts · Died: London  
Movements: [Realism](../movements/realism.md)  

> An American based in London and Paris who championed 'art for art's sake', titling his paintings like musical compositions.

## Biography

James Abbott McNeill Whistler was born in Lowell, Massachusetts, and spent part of his childhood in St Petersburg, where his father worked as a railway engineer for the tsar. He was dismissed from the West Point military academy, worked briefly as a map engraver, and in 1855 went to Paris to study art, never returning to America. He settled in London in 1859. His Symphony in White, No. 1 was a sensation at the Paris Salon des Refusés in 1863. In 1877 the critic John Ruskin accused him of 'flinging a pot of paint in the public's face' with a painted Nocturne. Whistler sued for libel and won, but the damages were a single farthing, and the legal costs bankrupted him in 1879.

Whistler believed in 'art for art's sake': a painting should be an arrangement of colours and forms, like music, rather than a story or moral lesson. So he titled works Symphonies, Harmonies, Arrangements and Nocturnes. He was influenced by Japanese prints, was a superb etcher (above all of Venice), designed the Peacock Room (now in the Freer Gallery, Washington), and was a witty, combative public figure who signed with a butterfly monogram.

## Key artworks

### Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1 (Whistler's Mother) (1871)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Whistlers_Mother_high_res.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1871
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée d'Orsay, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Arrangement%20in%20Grey%20and%20Black%20No.%201%20%28Whistler%27s%20Mother%29%20James%20McNeill%20Whistler)

Whistler painted his mother, Anna McNeill Whistler, in his London studio in 1871. She had stepped in when another model failed to appear and sat in profile because she was too tired to stand. Seated against a grey wall in a black dress and white lace cap, she is part of a carefully balanced composition of rectangles: a curtain with a Japanese-style pattern, a framed print on the wall and the footstool. Whistler insisted that the title expressed its true subject, an arrangement of colours and forms. The Royal Academy nearly rejected it. In 1891 the French state bought it, and in the 20th century it became an American icon of motherhood, even appearing on a US postage stamp. It is in the Musée d'Orsay.
