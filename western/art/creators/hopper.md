# Edward Hopper

**1882 – 1967** · American  
Born: Nyack, New York · Died: New York  
Movements: [American Modernism](../movements/american-modernism.md)  

> Hopper painted American cities, diners and roadsides with stark light and a mood of loneliness.

## Biography

Edward Hopper was born in Nyack, New York, and studied illustration and painting at the New York School of Art under Robert Henri. He made three trips to Paris between 1906 and 1910 but remained largely untouched by the avant-garde. For years he earned his living as a commercial illustrator. He sold a painting at the 1913 Armory Show and made a series of etchings, but real success came only with a 1924 exhibition of watercolours. That year he married the painter Josephine Nivison, who managed his records and was his only model for female figures. His House by the Railroad was the first painting acquired by the new Museum of Modern Art in 1930. He spent summers on Cape Cod and lived in a studio on Washington Square in New York until his death in 1967.

Hopper painted American cities, diners, offices, hotel rooms, theatres, gas stations and empty streets, as well as New England houses and lighthouses. His clear, simplified forms, strong light and shadow, and isolated figures convey a mood of silence and loneliness that has profoundly influenced film, photography and the popular image of modern America.

## Key artworks

### Nighthawks (1942)

[![Nighthawks](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Nighthawks_by_Edward_Hopper_1942.jpg/1280px-Nighthawks_by_Edward_Hopper_1942.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nighthawks_by_Edward_Hopper_1942.jpg)

<sub>Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nighthawks_by_Edward_Hopper_1942.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1942
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Art Institute of Chicago
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nighthawks%20Edward%20Hopper)

Hopper finished this painting in January 1942, shortly after the attack on Pearl Harbor. It shows a corner diner at night, seen through its large curved window from a deserted street. Inside, under harsh fluorescent light, a counterman in white serves three customers: a couple sitting side by side without speaking, and a man alone with his back to us. There is no visible door, which seals the figures inside the glass. Hopper said the diner was inspired by one on Greenwich Avenue in New York, simplified and enlarged, and admitted that unconsciously he was probably painting the loneliness of a big city. His wife Jo posed for the woman. The Art Institute of Chicago bought it a few months after it was painted, and it has become one of the most famous and parodied images of American art.
