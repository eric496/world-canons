## Biography

Jean-Siméon Chardin, the son of a Parisian maker of billiard tables, spent his whole life in Paris and almost never left it. In 1728 the Royal Academy admitted him as a painter 'of animals and fruit', the lowest category in its hierarchy of genres, on the strength of The Ray and The Buffet. From the 1730s he also painted quiet scenes of servants and middle-class family life, such as Saying Grace and Soap Bubbles. He later served as the Academy's treasurer and arranged the paintings at its Salon exhibitions. When failing eyesight made oil painting hard, he turned to pastel, producing memorable late self-portraits in spectacles.

Chardin's still lifes of copper pots, bread, fruit and game are painted with a thick, granular, almost tactile paint and soft harmonies of colour that give humble objects great dignity. The philosopher Diderot praised his work as magic in his Salon reviews. Chardin was admired as a counterweight to the frivolity of the Rococo, and he later inspired Cézanne, Morandi and many still-life painters.

## Key artworks

### The Ray (1728)

[![The Ray](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/La_Raie_-_Jean_Baptiste_Sim%C3%A9on_Chardin_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_INV_3197.jpg/1280px-La_Raie_-_Jean_Baptiste_Sim%C3%A9on_Chardin_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_INV_3197.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:La_Raie_-_Jean_Baptiste_Sim%C3%A9on_Chardin_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Peintures_INV_3197.jpg)

- **Date:** 1728
- **Type:** Oil on canvas
- **Where to see it:** Musée du Louvre, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Ray%20Jean-Sim%C3%A9on%20Chardin)

This large still life of 1728, in the Louvre, helped win Chardin admission to the Royal Academy. In a kitchen, a gutted skate (ray) hangs from a hook, its pale belly forming an eerie, almost human face, its red insides glistening. Beside it, a cat with bristling fur steps over a pile of oysters, eyeing the food. A knife sticks out from the table edge, and pots, a jug and a cloth complete the scene. Chardin's thick, tactile paint makes each texture palpable. Later artists including Cézanne, Matisse and Soutine studied and copied it.
