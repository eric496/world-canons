# Joseon potters

*조선 도공*

**1392 – 1910** · Korean  
Born: Gwangju (Gyeonggi) and kilns across Korea · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Joseon painting and ceramics](../movements/joseon-painting.md)  

> The potters of Joseon Korea who made buncheong ware and white porcelain, including the famous moon jars.

## Biography

After the fall of Goryeo, Korean potters turned from celadon to two new kinds of ware. Buncheong, a grey stoneware covered with white slip that was stamped, incised, brushed or painted, was made in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries in kilns across the country. At the same time the court's official kilns at Gwangju near Seoul produced white porcelain in keeping with the austere taste of Neo-Confucian scholars, sometimes painted in underglaze cobalt or iron. During the Japanese invasions of 1592–98 many Korean potters were taken to Japan, where they founded kilns such as Arita and Hagi.

Joseon ceramics are admired for their unforced, natural quality. The large white moon jars of the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, made in two halves joined at the middle, are slightly asymmetrical and softly glazed. Japanese tea masters treasured simple Korean bowls, and in the twentieth century the critic Yanagi Sōetsu made Joseon pottery central to his ideas of folk craft (mingei).

## Key artworks

### Moon jar (late 17th – 18th century)

- **Date:** late 17th – 18th century
- **Type:** White porcelain
- **Where to see it:** Leeum Museum of Art, Seoul; National Museum of Korea; British Museum and others
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Moon%20jar%20Joseon%20potters)

Moon jars were made at the official kilns of Gwangju near Seoul from the late seventeenth to the mid eighteenth century, a time when Joseon taste favoured plain white porcelain. Because they are so large, often around 40 centimetres high, they were made in two hemispherical halves joined at the middle, so that each is slightly uneven, and the milky glaze varies in colour from bluish to creamy. Their exact use is unclear, perhaps for storing food or as display pieces. Their unpretentious, natural beauty was celebrated by twentieth-century Korean painters and Western potters such as Bernard Leach, and the moon jar has become a symbol of Korean aesthetics. Several are National Treasures.
