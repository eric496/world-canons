# Korean Buddhist sculptors

*한국 불교 조각가*

**c. 550 – c. 800** · Korean  
Born: Three Kingdoms and Unified Silla Korea · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Buddhist sculpture and cave temples](../movements/buddhist-sculpture.md)  

> The anonymous makers of the gilt-bronze pensive bodhisattvas of the Three Kingdoms and the Seokguram Buddha of Unified Silla.

## Biography

Buddhism reached the Korean kingdoms of Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla in the fourth century, and by the sixth century Korean workshops were casting fine gilt-bronze images. Korean monks and craftsmen also travelled to Japan, where Baekje artisans helped build and furnish the first Buddhist temples. After Silla unified most of the peninsula in 668, its capital Gyeongju became a great Buddhist centre with temples such as Bulguksa.

The two great pensive bodhisattvas in the National Museum of Korea, seated with one leg crossed and a hand raised to the cheek, are among the masterpieces of world Buddhist sculpture, and a very similar wooden figure in Kōryū-ji in Kyoto is thought by many scholars to be Korean in origin. The granite Buddha of the Seokguram grotto, carved in the eighth century, is the classic image of Unified Silla's calm, balanced style.

- **Date:** early 7th century
- **Type:** Gilt bronze
- **Where to see it:** National Museum of Korea, Seoul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Pensive%20Bodhisattva%20%28National%20Treasure%20No.%2083%29%20Korean%20Buddhist%20sculptors)

The pensive pose, with the right leg crossed over the left knee and the fingers of the right hand lightly touching the cheek, was popular in East Asian Buddhism in the sixth and seventh centuries, often identified with Maitreya, the Buddha of the future. This figure, 93.5 centimetres high, is one of two great gilt-bronze examples in the National Museum of Korea. The body is slender, the bare torso smooth and the face wears a gentle smile under a low triple crown. Thin walls and a near-perfect casting show advanced technique. Whether it was made in Silla or Baekje is debated. A very similar wooden statue in Kōryū-ji in Kyoto, Japan's first National Treasure in sculpture, is thought by many to be Korean work or closely based on it.

### Seokguram Buddha (c. 751–774)

[![Seokguram Buddha](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/Front_view_of_Seokguram_from_front_chamber.jpg/1280px-Front_view_of_Seokguram_from_front_chamber.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Front_view_of_Seokguram_from_front_chamber.jpg)

<sub>The Buddha of Seokguram seen from the antechamber Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Front_view_of_Seokguram_from_front_chamber.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 751–774
- **Type:** Granite
- **Where to see it:** Seokguram Grotto, Gyeongju
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Seokguram%20Buddha%20Korean%20Buddhist%20sculptors)

According to the Samguk yusa, the minister Kim Daeseong began building Seokguram and the nearby temple Bulguksa in 751. Because the local granite was too hard to carve into caves, the builders constructed an artificial grotto of granite blocks covered with earth, with a rectangular antechamber and a round domed rotunda. The seated Buddha, about 3.5 metres high, sits facing east towards the sea, his right hand touching the earth, surrounded by reliefs of bodhisattvas, disciples and guardians. The figure combines volume, calm and precise proportion. Restored, sometimes badly, in the early twentieth century, the grotto is now sealed behind glass. With Bulguksa it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
