# Yamato-e painters

*大和絵の絵師*

**c. 1100 – c. 1333** · Japanese  
Born: Kyoto · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Yamato-e and narrative handscrolls](../movements/yamato-e-emaki.md)  
Also in: [Religion](../../religion/creators/yamato-e-painters.md)  

> The largely anonymous court and temple painters who made the great Japanese narrative handscrolls of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

## Biography

Narrative handscrolls (emakimono) were produced in the twelfth century for the imperial court, aristocrats and great temples in Kyoto, and many more followed in the Kamakura period. Their painters are mostly unknown. A few attributions are traditional: the Chōjū-giga have long been linked to the monk Toba Sōjō (Kakuyū), and the Ban Dainagon scrolls to the court painter Tokiwa Mitsunaga, but these attributions are debated and the scrolls were probably made by several hands. Court painting offices (e-dokoro) and temple workshops employed teams of painters, with a master designing the composition and assistants applying colour.

These painters developed distinctive conventions for telling stories across a scroll unrolled from right to left: the blown-off roof (fukinuki yatai) that lets the viewer look down into rooms, stylised faces with line-eyes and hook noses (hikime kagibana), sweeping diagonal compositions and continuous narrative in which the same figure appears several times. Their work ranges from the hushed melancholy of the Genji scrolls to slapstick comedy and roaring battle scenes, and it is often seen as a distant ancestor of modern manga.

### Illustrated Handscrolls of The Tale of Genji (Genji monogatari emaki) (c. 1130)

[![Illustrated Handscrolls of The Tale of Genji (Genji monogatari emaki)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Genji_emaki_Yadorigi.JPG/1280px-Genji_emaki_Yadorigi.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Genji_emaki_Yadorigi.JPG)

<sub>Yadorigi scene from the Genji scrolls, Tokugawa Art Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Genji_emaki_Yadorigi.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1130
- **Type:** Handscroll sections, ink and colour on paper
- **Where to see it:** Tokugawa Art Museum, Nagoya, and Gotoh Museum, Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Illustrated%20Handscrolls%20of%20The%20Tale%20of%20Genji%20%28Genji%20monogatari%20emaki%29%20Yamato-e%20painters)

Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji in the early eleventh century, and about a hundred years later the court produced lavish illustrated scrolls of it, perhaps for an imperial or Fujiwara patron. Of an original set of perhaps twenty scrolls, about twenty pictures and accompanying passages of calligraphy on decorated paper survive. The pictures use the conventions of tsukuri-e, built-up colour, with roofless interiors seen from above, steep diagonals, and stylised faces with slit eyes and hooked noses that leave emotion to posture and setting. Scenes such as Genji holding the baby Kaoru, who is not his son, express hidden sorrow with remarkable restraint. The scrolls are National Treasures, and the paintings were restored and reconstructed digitally in the 2000s.

### Frolicking Animals (Chōjū-jinbutsu-giga) (12th–13th century)

[![Frolicking Animals (Chōjū-jinbutsu-giga)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fa/Chouju_sumo2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chouju_sumo2.jpg)

<sub>Rabbit and frog wrestling, first scroll of the Chōjū-giga Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chouju_sumo2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 12th–13th century
- **Type:** Four handscrolls, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** Kōzan-ji, Kyoto (on deposit at Tokyo and Kyoto National Museums)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Frolicking%20Animals%20%28Ch%C5%8Dj%C5%AB-jinbutsu-giga%29%20Yamato-e%20painters)

The four scrolls preserved at the temple Kōzan-ji were painted by different hands over perhaps a century. The first and most famous shows rabbits, frogs and monkeys bathing, wrestling, holding archery contests and conducting a Buddhist service, in which a frog sits as the Buddha before a monkey monk. The second shows real and imaginary animals, the third and fourth people and animals at games. The scrolls use only ink line, drawn with lively speed, and have no text, so their satirical meaning is uncertain. They are traditionally attributed to the abbot Toba Sōjō, but this is unproven. Their humour and storytelling have made them famous as a forerunner of manga.

### Legends of Mount Shigi (Shigisan engi emaki) (12th century)

[![Legends of Mount Shigi (Shigisan engi emaki)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/da/Sigisanengi_tobikura.jpg/1280px-Sigisanengi_tobikura.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sigisanengi_tobikura.jpg)

<sub>The flying granary, Shigisan engi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sigisanengi_tobikura.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 12th century
- **Type:** Three handscrolls, ink and colour on paper
- **Where to see it:** Chōgosonshi-ji, Nara (on deposit at Nara National Museum)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Legends%20of%20Mount%20Shigi%20%28Shigisan%20engi%20emaki%29%20Yamato-e%20painters)

The scrolls tell stories of Myōren, a monk who lived on Mount Shigi near Nara in the tenth century. In the first, the 'flying granary', his begging bowl flies down to a rich man's house and, when the man refuses to fill it, lifts his whole storehouse and carries it to the mountain, while the household runs after it in panic. Other scrolls show Myōren curing an emperor and his sister's search for him. In contrast to the Genji scrolls, the painting uses lively, flowing line, many small figures with expressive faces and continuous narrative. The work shows how yamato-e handled crowds, movement and humour.

### Night Attack on the Sanjō Palace (Heiji monogatari emaki) (second half of the 13th century)

[![Night Attack on the Sanjō Palace (Heiji monogatari emaki)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/Heiji_Monogatari_Emaki_-_Sanjo_scroll_part_5_-_v2.jpg/1280px-Heiji_Monogatari_Emaki_-_Sanjo_scroll_part_5_-_v2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Heiji_Monogatari_Emaki_-_Sanjo_scroll_part_5_-_v2.jpg)

<sub>Night Attack on the Sanjō Palace (detail), Museum of Fine Arts, Boston Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Heiji_Monogatari_Emaki_-_Sanjo_scroll_part_5_-_v2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** second half of the 13th century
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on paper
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Night%20Attack%20on%20the%20Sanj%C5%8D%20Palace%20%28Heiji%20monogatari%20emaki%29%20Yamato-e%20painters)

In 1159 the warrior Minamoto no Yoshitomo and the courtier Fujiwara no Nobuyori attacked the Sanjō palace of the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa, carried him off and set the palace on fire. The scroll, about seven metres long, shows the attack as a mounting storm of horses, armour and flame, with soldiers killing courtiers and ladies trying to escape, then the procession of victors leaving. The masses of figures, red flames and swirling smoke give it an extraordinary energy. It belonged to a set of scrolls illustrating the war tale Heiji monogatari, of which parts are in Tokyo. The scroll left Japan in the nineteenth century and is one of the great treasures of the Boston museum.
