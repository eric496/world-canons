# Gu Kaizhi

*顧愷之*

**c. 344 – c. 406** · Chinese  
Born: Wuxi, Jiangsu · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Six Dynasties calligraphy & figure painting](../movements/six-dynasties-art.md)  

> The most famous painter of early China, remembered for figure scrolls such as the Admonitions Scroll and the Nymph of the Luo River, which survive only in later copies.

## Biography

Gu Kaizhi was born around 344 in Wuxi, into a family of officials, and served as an adviser to several generals of the Eastern Jin dynasty. Anecdotes in the collection A New Account of the Tales of the World portray him as witty, eccentric and a little naive, and say that he was called excellent in three things: painting, literary talent and foolishness. He was known for portraits and Buddhist murals, and is said to have left the eyes of his figures until last, because the spirit lay in them. Three essays on painting attributed to him survive.

No work by Gu Kaizhi survives in the original. The paintings associated with his name, the Admonitions Scroll in the British Museum and versions of the Nymph of the Luo River in Beijing, Washington and Liaoning, are copies made centuries later, perhaps preserving his compositions. They show figures in flowing robes drawn with an even, continuous line often compared to silk thread spun from a cocoon. Later critics placed him at the head of Chinese painting, and his name stands for the idea that a painting should capture the spirit of its subject.

### Admonitions Scroll (5th–7th century copy of a 4th-century composition)

<sub>Admonitions Scroll (detail), British Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ku_K%27ai-chih_001.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 5th–7th century copy of a 4th-century composition
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** British Museum, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Admonitions%20Scroll%20Gu%20Kaizhi)

The Admonitions of the Instructress to the Court Ladies was a poem written by Zhang Hua around 292 to criticise the behaviour of Empress Jia. The scroll illustrates it in a series of scenes separated by passages of text: Lady Feng throwing herself in front of a bear to protect the emperor, a lady at her mirror, a husband turning away from his wife in bed. Traditionally attributed to Gu Kaizhi, it is now generally thought to be a copy made between the fifth and seventh centuries, preserving an early composition. The figures are drawn with a fine, even line. The scroll entered the Qianlong Emperor's collection and was taken from Beijing by a British officer in 1900; it is now kept in the British Museum and shown only rarely.

### Nymph of the Luo River (Song-dynasty copy of a composition attributed to Gu Kaizhi)

[![Nymph of the Luo River](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bd/Gu_Kaizhi-Nymph_of_the_Luo_River_%28full%29%2C_Palace_Museum%2C_Beijing.jpg/1280px-Gu_Kaizhi-Nymph_of_the_Luo_River_%28full%29%2C_Palace_Museum%2C_Beijing.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gu_Kaizhi-Nymph_of_the_Luo_River_%28full%29%2C_Palace_Museum%2C_Beijing.jpg)

<sub>Nymph of the Luo River, Song copy, Palace Museum, Beijing Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gu_Kaizhi-Nymph_of_the_Luo_River_%28full%29%2C_Palace_Museum%2C_Beijing.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** Song-dynasty copy of a composition attributed to Gu Kaizhi
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Palace Museum, Beijing (other versions in Liaoning and Washington)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nymph%20of%20the%20Luo%20River%20Gu%20Kaizhi)

In his rhapsody of 222, the poet Cao Zhi describes stopping by the Luo River on his way home, seeing a beautiful goddess and falling in love with her, only for her to depart because humans and spirits cannot unite. The painting follows the poem from right to left: the prince on the bank, the nymph appearing over the water, her departure in a chariot drawn by dragons, and the prince's sorrowful journey home. Mountains and trees are small and archaic, like stage scenery, as in early Chinese painting. Several versions survive, all thought to be Song copies of a lost original associated with Gu Kaizhi. The work became a classic subject and is important evidence for early figure and landscape painting.
