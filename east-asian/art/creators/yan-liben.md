# Yan Liben

*閻立本*

**c. 601 – 673** · Chinese  
Born: Chang'an (Xi'an) · Died: Chang'an  
Movements & schools: [Tang painting & sancai](../movements/tang-art.md)  

> Leading figure painter of the early Tang court and a high minister, associated with Emperor Taizong Receiving the Tibetan Envoy and the Thirteen Emperors Scroll.

## Biography

Yan Liben came from a family of artists and engineers in the service of the Sui and Tang courts; his father and his brother Yan Lide were also painters and architects. He spent his career in government and rose to be one of the chief ministers under Emperor Gaozong. As a court painter he recorded important events and people, including the portraits of the twenty-four meritorious officials hung in the Lingyan Pavilion in 643, now lost. Tang sources say he was ashamed to be summoned to paint like a servant in front of courtiers and warned his sons against the profession.

The works attributed to him, Emperor Taizong Receiving the Tibetan Envoy in the Palace Museum in Beijing and the Thirteen Emperors Scroll in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, are probably later copies or partly so, and their attribution is debated. They show the dignified, carefully drawn figures of early Tang court art, with rank expressed by size. Yan Liben stands for the Tang ideal of painting as a record of moral example and political history.

### Emperor Taizong Receiving the Tibetan Envoy (Bunian tu) (7th century composition (probably a Song copy))

<sub>Emperor Taizong Receiving the Tibetan Envoy, Palace Museum, Beijing Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Emperor_Taizong_Receiving_the_Tibetan_Envoy%28Bunian_Tu%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 7th century composition (probably a Song copy)
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Palace Museum, Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Emperor%20Taizong%20Receiving%20the%20Tibetan%20Envoy%20%28Bunian%20tu%29%20Yan%20Liben)

In 640 the Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo sent his minister Gar Tongtsen to the Tang court to ask for a princess in marriage, and in 641 Princess Wencheng travelled to Lhasa. The painting shows Emperor Taizong seated on a litter carried by court women, some holding fans and a parasol, and on the left a court official in red, the envoy in a patterned robe and an interpreter. The emperor is much larger than everyone else, following the convention of hierarchic scale, while the women are more slender than in later Tang taste. The painting, traditionally attributed to Yan Liben, is probably a Song copy. It is a key document of Tang court painting and of relations with Tibet.

### Thirteen Emperors Scroll (7th century (attributed))

[![Thirteen Emperors Scroll](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/Yan_Liben._Thirteen_Emperors._Boston%2C_Museum_of_the_Fine_Arts.jpg/1280px-Yan_Liben._Thirteen_Emperors._Boston%2C_Museum_of_the_Fine_Arts.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yan_Liben._Thirteen_Emperors._Boston%2C_Museum_of_the_Fine_Arts.jpg)

<sub>Thirteen Emperors Scroll (detail), Museum of Fine Arts, Boston Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yan_Liben._Thirteen_Emperors._Boston%2C_Museum_of_the_Fine_Arts.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 7th century (attributed)
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Thirteen%20Emperors%20Scroll%20Yan%20Liben)

This long scroll presents thirteen rulers from the Western Han to the Sui dynasty, each standing or seated with attendants and identified by an inscription. The emperors are idealised types rather than true likenesses, and they are characterised morally: founders of dynasties appear powerful and dignified, while weak or wicked rulers look feeble. The scroll thus served as a kind of political lesson. Scholars believe the first part is a later replacement and debate how much of the rest is Yan Liben's own work. It entered the Boston museum in 1931 and is one of the most important early Chinese paintings outside China.
