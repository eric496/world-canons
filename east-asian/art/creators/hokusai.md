# Katsushika Hokusai

*葛飾北斎*

**1760 – 1849** · Japanese  
Born: Edo · Died: Edo  
Movements & schools: [Ukiyo-e](../movements/ukiyo-e.md)  

> Restless and prolific Edo artist, painter of the Great Wave and the Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, who worked under some thirty names over a seventy-year career.

<sub>Portrait: Self-portrait as an old man · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hokusai_as_an_old_man.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Hokusai was born in the Katsushika district of Edo and apprenticed as a woodblock carver before joining the studio of the actor-print artist Katsukawa Shunshō in 1779. He soon broke away, studied Kanō, Chinese and even European painting, and moved through many styles and names, illustrating novels, designing luxury privately issued surimono, and from 1814 publishing the sketchbooks known as Hokusai Manga. Around 1830, in his seventies, he designed the Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, his most famous work. He was poor for much of his life, moved house dozens of times, and was helped in his last years by his daughter Katsushika Ōi, herself a fine painter. He died in 1849 aged eighty-eight.

Hokusai's energy and curiosity were boundless: he drew everything, from gods and ghosts to fishermen, waterfalls and bridges, and he combined Japanese line with Western perspective and imported Prussian blue pigment. In the postscript to his One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji he wrote that nothing he had drawn before seventy was worth noticing, and hoped to keep improving until he was a hundred and ten. His prints and the Manga had a profound influence on European artists such as Monet, Degas and Van Gogh, and the Great Wave has become the most recognisable image of Japanese art.

### Hokusai Manga (1814–1878)

[![Hokusai Manga](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b3/Hokusai-MangaBathingPeople.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hokusai-MangaBathingPeople.jpg)

<sub>Bathers, from Hokusai Manga Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hokusai-MangaBathingPeople.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1814–1878
- **Type:** Woodblock-printed sketchbooks, 15 volumes
- **Where to see it:** Many collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hokusai%20Manga%20Katsushika%20Hokusai)

In 1812 Hokusai visited Nagoya and made hundreds of sketches for his pupils, and in 1814 a local publisher issued the first volume as a guide for students. Its success led to further volumes, the last three published after his death, fifteen in all. They contain thousands of images: wrestlers, bathers, fat and thin people, acrobats, ghosts, animals, plants, landscapes, buildings and tools, printed in black, grey and pink. Manga here means casual or random sketches. The books spread Hokusai's style and reached Europe, where they inspired artists such as Félix Bracquemond and the Impressionists. The term manga is now used for Japanese comics.

### The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c. 1831)

[![The Great Wave off Kanagawa](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Tsunami_by_hokusai_19th_century.jpg/1280px-Tsunami_by_hokusai_19th_century.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tsunami_by_hokusai_19th_century.jpg)

<sub>Hokusai, The Great Wave off Kanagawa Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tsunami_by_hokusai_19th_century.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1831
- **Type:** Polychrome woodblock print
- **Where to see it:** Many impressions, including the Metropolitan Museum of Art, British Museum and Tokyo National Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Great%20Wave%20off%20Kanagawa%20Katsushika%20Hokusai)

The Great Wave is the first print in Hokusai's series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, published by Nishimuraya Yohachi from about 1830. A huge wave with claw-like foam rears up over three narrow boats of rowers, while far away Mount Fuji appears as a small cone, echoing the shape of a smaller wave in the foreground. The composition, read from right to left in Japanese style, uses Western-style low horizon and perspective, and the print was one of the first to use the imported synthetic pigment Prussian blue. Thousands of impressions were printed. It reached Europe in the later nineteenth century, inspiring Debussy and many artists, and has become a global icon.

- **Date:** c. 1831
- **Type:** Polychrome woodblock print
- **Where to see it:** Many impressions, including the Metropolitan Museum of Art and British Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fine%20Wind%2C%20Clear%20Morning%20%28Red%20Fuji%29%20Katsushika%20Hokusai)

Also from Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, this print shows the mountain alone, its slopes red in the light of a late summer or early autumn morning when a south wind blows, as the title suggests. The summit still has streaks of snow, the base is dark green with forest, and the sky is filled with rows of small clouds. The print is one of the simplest and boldest in the series, reducing the landscape to a few areas of colour. Early impressions have a soft gradation of colour that later ones lost. With the Great Wave and the Thunderstorm below the Summit, it is one of the three most famous prints of the series.
