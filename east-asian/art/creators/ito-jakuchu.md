## Biography

Jakuchū was the eldest son of a wholesale greengrocer in Kyoto's Nishiki market and took over the business in 1739, but he had little interest in commerce. He studied Kanō painting and copied Chinese works in Zen temples, then kept chickens in his garden to observe them from life. Around 1755 he handed the business to his brother and devoted himself to painting, becoming a lay Buddhist. His house was destroyed in the great Kyoto fire of 1788, and he spent his last years near the temple of Sekihō-ji.

Between about 1757 and 1766 Jakuchū painted the thirty scrolls of the Colorful Realm of Living Beings, which he donated with a Shaka triad to Shōkoku-ji; they were later given to the imperial household. He also painted bold ink works and experimental screens made of thousands of small squares. Neglected for a long time, he became one of the most popular Japanese painters after exhibitions in the 2000s.

## Key artworks

### Colorful Realm of Living Beings (Dōshoku sai-e) (c. 1757–1766)

[![Colorful Realm of Living Beings (Dōshoku sai-e)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/It%C5%8D_Jakuch%C5%AB_-_Black_rooster_and_nandin_%28Colorful_Realm_of_Living_Beings%29.jpg/1280px-It%C5%8D_Jakuch%C5%AB_-_Black_rooster_and_nandin_%28Colorful_Realm_of_Living_Beings%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:It%C5%8D_Jakuch%C5%AB_-_Black_rooster_and_nandin_%28Colorful_Realm_of_Living_Beings%29.jpg)

- **Date:** c. 1757–1766
- **Type:** Set of thirty hanging scrolls, colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Museum of the Imperial Collections (Sannomaru Shōzōkan), Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Colorful%20Realm%20of%20Living%20Beings%20%28D%C5%8Dshoku%20sai-e%29%20It%C5%8D%20Jakuch%C5%AB)

Over about ten years Itō Jakuchū painted thirty large scrolls of birds, animals, fish, insects, shells and plants, and donated them with a triptych of the Buddha Shaka and two bodhisattvas to Shōkoku-ji, where they were hung for memorial ceremonies, representing all living beings gathered round the Buddha. Each scroll is dense with minutely observed detail: roosters with fiery combs, peacocks and phoenixes with white feathers painted in layers, and fish swimming in shoals. Jakuchū used the finest pigments and sometimes painted on the back of the silk to modulate colour. The temple gave the scrolls to the imperial household in 1889, and they were designated National Treasures in 2021.
