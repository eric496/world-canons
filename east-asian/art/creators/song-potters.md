# Song dynasty potters

*宋代陶工*

**c. 960 – c. 1279** · Chinese  
Born: Kilns across China · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Song and Goryeo ceramics](../movements/song-goryeo-ceramics.md)  

> The potters of the Ru, Jun, Ding, Guan, Longquan and Jian kilns, who made the refined monochrome ceramics of the Song dynasty.

## Biography

Song China had hundreds of kilns, some producing for the court and others for everyday use and export. Among the most admired were the Ru kilns near Kaifeng, which supplied the Northern Song court for only about twenty years around 1100; the Ding kilns of Hebei with their ivory-white porcelain; the Jun kilns with thick blue and purple glazes; the Guan kilns of the Southern Song capital at Hangzhou; Longquan with its green celadon; and Jian in Fujian, which made dark tea bowls for whisked tea. The potters are anonymous, though some families and workshops are known from inscriptions and excavations.

Song ceramics are prized for their quiet monochrome glazes, perfect forms and effects of crackle, which suited the taste of scholars and the court for antiquity and restraint. Later dynasties collected and imitated them, the Qianlong Emperor wrote poems on his Ru pieces, and Japanese tea masters treasured Jian bowls as tenmoku. Fewer than a hundred pieces of Ru ware are known today.

The Ru kilns at Qingliangsi in Baofeng, Henan, made ceramics for the court of the Northern Song for only a few decades around 1100, and fewer than a hundred pieces are known. Their glaze, which is said to contain powdered agate, is an opaque, soft bluish green like a sky after rain, with a fine network of crackle; the pieces were fired on tiny spurs, leaving small sesame-seed marks on the base. This oval basin, without any crackle, is one of the most perfect examples. It was later called a narcissus basin, though it was probably made for another use. The Qianlong Emperor had a poem incised on its base and a stand made for it. Ru ware set the ideal of quiet monochrome elegance for later Chinese ceramics.

- **Date:** 12th–13th century
- **Type:** Stoneware with iron-rich glaze
- **Where to see it:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (many examples worldwide)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jian%20ware%20tea%20bowl%20with%20hare%27s-fur%20glaze%20Song%20dynasty%20potters)

In Song China tea was made by whisking powdered leaves in hot water to a white froth, and tea contests judged how long the froth lasted. Dark bowls from the Jian kilns in Fujian showed it off best. Their thick iron-rich glaze breaks into fine brown and silvery streaks known as hare's fur, or, in rare cases, oil spots or iridescent rainbow spots. The emperor Huizong praised these bowls in his treatise on tea. Japanese monks studying at Zen monasteries on Mount Tianmu brought them home, and in Japan they were called tenmoku and became treasured utensils of the tea ceremony. Three iridescent yōhen tenmoku bowls in Japanese collections are National Treasures.
