# Goryeo celadon potters

*고려청자 도공*

**c. 950 – c. 1392** · Korean  
Born: Gangjin and Buan, Korea · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Song and Goryeo ceramics](../movements/song-goryeo-ceramics.md)  

> The anonymous potters of the Goryeo kingdom who made Korea's celebrated jade-green celadons and invented sanggam inlay.

## Biography

Korean potters learned to make high-fired greenwares from Chinese Yue kilns in the ninth and tenth centuries, and under the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392) the kilns at Gangjin and Buan in the south-west produced celadon for the court and aristocracy. By the early twelfth century the glaze had reached a clear, bluish green that the Chinese envoy Xu Jing, visiting in 1123, praised as kingfisher colour. Shapes included maebyeong vases, ewers, incense burners and cups, often modelled as gourds, melons, lotus or animals.

Goryeo potters developed sanggam, an inlay technique in which designs are incised into the clay and filled with white and black slip before glazing. Cranes among clouds, willows and chrysanthemums became classic motifs. Production declined with the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century and the fall of the dynasty, and the technique was largely forgotten until twentieth-century archaeologists and potters revived it. Goryeo celadons are now among Korea's most treasured national treasures.

### Celadon incense burner with openwork lid (12th century)

- **Date:** 12th century
- **Type:** Stoneware with celadon glaze
- **Where to see it:** National Museum of Korea, Seoul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Celadon%20incense%20burner%20with%20openwork%20lid%20Goryeo%20celadon%20potters)

This incense burner, National Treasure No. 95, is composed of three parts: a lid formed as an openwork sphere of interlocking circles, through which the smoke escapes; a bowl shaped like a lotus flower, decorated with inlaid details; and a base supported by three small crouching rabbits. Its pure kingfisher-coloured glaze is the kind described by the Chinese envoy Xu Jing in 1123. Combining modelling, openwork, inlay and white slip details, it shows the technical range of Goryeo potters at the height of their art in the twelfth century, before the inlaid wares became dominant.

- **Date:** mid-12th century
- **Type:** Stoneware with inlaid decoration under celadon glaze
- **Where to see it:** Kansong Art Museum, Seoul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Goryeo%20celadon%20maebyeong%20with%20inlaid%20cranes%20and%20clouds%20Goryeo%20celadon%20potters)

The maebyeong, a vase with broad shoulders and a small mouth, was used for wine or decoratively for a branch of plum blossom. This example, about 42 centimetres high, is covered with sixty-nine cranes, some flying up within circular medallions and some flying down between them, among clouds. The designs were incised into the clay and filled with white and black slip before glazing and firing, the inlay technique called sanggam that Goryeo potters perfected. The cranes, symbols of longevity, seem to float in the soft blue-green glaze. The collector Jeon Hyeong-pil bought the vase in 1935 to keep it in Korea, paying a famous sum; it is now National Treasure No. 68.
