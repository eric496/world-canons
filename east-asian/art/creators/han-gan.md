# Han Gan

*韓幹*

**c. 706 – c. 783** · Chinese  
Born: Chang'an or Lantian, Shaanxi · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Tang painting & sancai](../movements/tang-art.md)  

> Tang court painter celebrated for his horses, including Night-Shining White, the favourite charger of Emperor Xuanzong.

## Biography

Han Gan is said to have been a poor boy working for a wine shop when the poet-painter Wang Wei noticed his talent and paid for his training. He entered the court of Emperor Xuanzong, who loved horses and kept tens of thousands of them, including Central Asian breeds. According to tradition, when the emperor told Han Gan to study under the leading horse painter of the day, he replied that the horses in the imperial stables were his teachers.

Han Gan's horses were admired for their bulk and spirit, though the poet Du Fu complained that he painted the flesh and not the bones. Night-Shining White in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, a powerful tethered stallion covered with later collectors' seals and inscriptions, is the work most securely associated with him. He established the horse as a major subject of Chinese painting, followed by artists from Li Gonglin and Zhao Mengfu to Xu Beihong.

## Key artworks

### Night-Shining White (c. 750)

- **Date:** c. 750
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Night-Shining%20White%20Han%20Gan)

Emperor Xuanzong loved horses, and his stables held thousands of fine mounts brought from Central Asia. Night-Shining White (Zhaoye bai) was one of his favourite chargers. Han Gan shows it tied to a post, tossing its head with flaring nostrils and rolling eyes, its body powerfully rounded and modelled with light ink washes; the tail and part of the legs were retouched later. Over the centuries collectors, including the Southern Tang ruler Li Yu and the Qianlong Emperor, added seals and inscriptions, so that the picture is surrounded by a record of its ownership. The painting passed through several Western collections before entering the Metropolitan Museum in 1977.
