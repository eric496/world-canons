# Jingdezhen porcelain potters

*景德鎮窯工*

**c. 1300 – c. 1912** · Chinese  
Born: Jingdezhen, Jiangxi · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Yuan literati painting](../movements/yuan-literati.md), [Ming painting & porcelain](../movements/ming-painting-porcelain.md), [Qing painting: Orthodox masters and Individualists](../movements/qing-painting.md)  

> The potters and painters of Jingdezhen, China's porcelain capital, who made the blue-and-white and enamelled porcelains of the Yuan, Ming and Qing dynasties.

## Biography

Jingdezhen in Jiangxi province had abundant kaolin clay, porcelain stone and wood fuel, and by the fourteenth century it was the leading porcelain centre in China. Under the Yuan its potters began painting under the glaze with cobalt imported from Persia, creating blue-and-white ware for markets in the Islamic world. From 1369 the Ming set up an imperial kiln there, and production became a factory system in which, as the Jesuit Père d'Entrecolles observed in the 1710s, a single piece could pass through dozens of hands. Private kilns made export wares for Southeast Asia, Japan, the Middle East and Europe.

Jingdezhen porcelain set the standard for the world. Its blue-and-white was imitated in Vietnam, Persia, Japan and Delft; its Chenghua doucai cups and Qing famille rose enamels were technical marvels; and the European search for the secret of porcelain led to Meissen in 1708. Pieces bear reign marks rather than artists' names, and the potters remain anonymous, but their combined skill made porcelain synonymous with China, which in English took its very name.

### The David Vases (1351)

[![The David Vases](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/The_David_Vases.jpg/1280px-The_David_Vases.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_David_Vases.jpg)

<sub>The David Vases, British Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_David_Vases.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1351
- **Type:** Porcelain with underglaze cobalt-blue decoration
- **Where to see it:** British Museum, London
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20David%20Vases%20Jingdezhen%20porcelain%20potters)

These two tall vases, each about 63 centimetres high, are painted in underglaze cobalt blue with dragons among clouds, phoenixes, lotus and plantain leaves, and have handles shaped like elephant heads. An inscription on the neck states that a man named Zhang Wenjin donated them with an incense burner to a Daoist temple in Jiangxi in 1351, wishing for the protection and prosperity of his family. Because they are dated, they proved that sophisticated blue-and-white porcelain was being made at Jingdezhen during the Yuan dynasty, earlier than once thought. The vases were bought separately in the 1920s and 30s by Sir Percival David and reunited; his collection is now displayed at the British Museum.

### Blue-and-white palace bowl with a dragon (Xuande period (1426–1435))

[![Blue-and-white palace bowl with a dragon](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Blue_and_white_Ming_Xuande_1426_1435.jpg/1280px-Blue_and_white_Ming_Xuande_1426_1435.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Blue_and_white_Ming_Xuande_1426_1435.jpg)

<sub>Ming blue-and-white porcelain of the Xuande reign Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Blue_and_white_Ming_Xuande_1426_1435.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** Xuande period (1426–1435)
- **Type:** Porcelain with underglaze cobalt-blue decoration
- **Where to see it:** Palace Museum, Beijing (many in museums worldwide)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Blue-and-white%20palace%20bowl%20with%20a%20dragon%20Jingdezhen%20porcelain%20potters)

From the early fifteenth century the imperial kilns at Jingdezhen produced blue-and-white porcelain for the Ming court bearing the reign mark of the emperor, a practice that became standard. The Xuande reign is considered the high point of early Ming blue-and-white, with cobalt from Persia, called sumali, that gives a deep blue with dark spots where the pigment gathered. Dragons with five claws, reserved for the emperor, chase flaming pearls among clouds or waves. The porcelain was used at court and given as gifts to foreign rulers, and similar wares were traded to the Middle East, where collections such as the Ardebil shrine in Iran preserve them. Ming blue-and-white became the most imitated ceramic style in the world, from Vietnam and Persia to Delft.

- **Date:** Chenghua period (1465–1487)
- **Type:** Porcelain with underglaze blue and overglaze enamels
- **Where to see it:** Palace Museum, Beijing; National Palace Museum, Taipei; private collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Doucai%20chicken%20cup%20Jingdezhen%20porcelain%20potters)

The Chenghua emperor's imperial wares are small and delicate, and the chicken cups, only about eight centimetres across, are the most celebrated. Their decoration combines two techniques: outlines painted in underglaze cobalt blue and fired, then filled with overglaze enamels in red, yellow and green and fired again, a method called doucai, contending or fitting colours. On the cups a rooster and hen with chicks peck among rocks and flowers, a symbol of family harmony. Later emperors, including Yongzheng and Qianlong, had them copied, and even in the Ming they were expensive collectors' items. Very few are known, and one sold in 2014 for about 36 million US dollars.

### Famille rose porcelain (Yongzheng and Qianlong periods (1723–1795))

- **Date:** Yongzheng and Qianlong periods (1723–1795)
- **Type:** Porcelain with overglaze enamels
- **Where to see it:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York; Palace Museum, Beijing; and many other collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Famille%20rose%20porcelain%20Jingdezhen%20porcelain%20potters)

In the late Kangxi reign, the Qing court's enamel workshops, with the help of European missionaries, learned to make an opaque pink enamel derived from colloidal gold, together with an opaque white that could be mixed with other colours to create a range of soft shades. Applied over the glaze on Jingdezhen porcelain and fired again at low temperature, these enamels allowed painting with modelling and gradation, like painting on paper. Western collectors called the wares famille rose; Chinese terms include fencai and falangcai. The Yongzheng period produced the most refined examples, and under Qianlong famille rose was used for virtuoso vases and for export porcelain made for Europe in huge quantities.
