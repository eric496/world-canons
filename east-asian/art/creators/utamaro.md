# Kitagawa Utamaro

*喜多川歌麿*

**c. 1753 – 1806** · Japanese  
Born: ? · Died: Edo  
Movements & schools: [Ukiyo-e](../movements/ukiyo-e.md)  

> The supreme ukiyo-e designer of beautiful women, famous for his large-head bust portraits (ōkubi-e) of the 1790s.

## Biography

Utamaro's birthplace and parentage are unknown. He studied with the painter Toriyama Sekien and was promoted by the publisher Tsutaya Jūzaburō, in whose house he lived for a time. In the 1780s he produced illustrated books, including a celebrated book of insects. In the early 1790s he began designing bust portraits of courtesans, teahouse waitresses and townswomen that made him the most famous print artist in Edo. In 1804 he was handcuffed for fifty days for prints of the sixteenth-century warlord Hideyoshi, which broke censorship rules, and he died two years later.

Utamaro's women are tall and elegant, and he paid close attention to gestures, moods and the textures of hair, skin and fabric, sometimes using mica backgrounds. Series such as Ten Studies in Female Physiognomy suggest individual character and feeling. His work was among the first ukiyo-e to be collected in Europe, where Edmond de Goncourt wrote a book about him in 1891.

- **Where to see it:** Tokyo National Museum (and other impressions)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Woman%20Blowing%20a%20Poppin%20%28from%20Ten%20Types%20of%20Women%27s%20Physiognomy%29%20Kitagawa%20Utamaro)

The series Ten Types of Women's Physiognomy, published by Tsutaya Jūzaburō, presented types of character through faces, a playful use of the fashionable pseudo-science of physiognomy. In this print a young townswoman in a red and white chequered kimono turns to blow into a poppin, a glass toy that clicks when air is blown into it. The background is covered with glittering white mica. The drawing of hair, skin and fabric is refined, and the woman's absorbed expression gives a sense of a real moment. The print is among the earliest and most famous ōkubi-e.

### Three Beauties of the Present Day (c. 1793)

[![Three Beauties of the Present Day](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a8/Utamaro_%281793%29_Three_Beauties_of_the_Present_Time%2C_MFAB_21.6382.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Utamaro_%281793%29_Three_Beauties_of_the_Present_Time%2C_MFAB_21.6382.jpg)

<sub>Utamaro, Three Beauties of the Present Day, Museum of Fine Arts, Boston Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Utamaro_%281793%29_Three_Beauties_of_the_Present_Time%2C_MFAB_21.6382.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1793
- **Type:** Woodblock print, ink, colour and mica
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston (and other impressions)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Three%20Beauties%20of%20the%20Present%20Day%20Kitagawa%20Utamaro)

The print shows three young women celebrated in Edo in 1793: Tomimoto Toyohina, a geisha of the Yoshiwara, at the top, with Takashima Hisa and Naniwa Kita, daughters of tea shop owners, below, each identified by a crest on her kimono. Their faces look very similar at first, but on close inspection their features and expressions differ. The background is dusted with mica, giving a shimmer. The print is a remarkable example of the ōkubi-e, big-head picture, which Utamaro developed and which concentrated on the face. Such prints functioned partly as celebrity images. It became famous in Europe in the nineteenth century.
