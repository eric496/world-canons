# Asuka and Nara Buddhist workshops

*飛鳥・奈良の仏師*

**c. 600 – c. 800** · Japanese  
Born: Nara basin, Japan · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Buddhist sculpture and cave temples](../movements/buddhist-sculpture.md)  
Also in: [Religion](../../religion/creators/nara-buddhist-workshops.md)  

> The temple and government workshops of early Japan, including Tori Busshi and the Office for the Construction of Tōdai-ji, that made the first great Buddhist images in Japan.

## Biography

Buddhism was officially introduced to Japan from the Korean kingdom of Baekje in the sixth century, and the first images were made by immigrant craftsmen and their descendants. The best-known early sculptor is Tori Busshi, grandson of a Chinese or Korean immigrant, who signed the bronze Shaka Triad of Hōryū-ji in 623. In the eighth century the Nara court set up a government office to build Tōdai-ji and its colossal bronze Buddha, a project overseen, according to tradition, by Kuninaka no Kimimaro.

Asuka-period works such as the Shaka Triad are frontal and linear, close to Chinese Northern Wei and Korean models. Nara sculpture, made in bronze, clay and hollow dry lacquer, follows the full, naturalistic Tang style, and dry-lacquer figures such as the Ashura of Kōfuku-ji show an emotional subtlety that has made them some of the best-loved images in Japan. Many of these works survive because temples such as Hōryū-ji and the imperial storehouse of the Shōsōin preserved them for thirteen centuries.

### Shaka Triad of Hōryū-ji (623)

[![Shaka Triad of Hōryū-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2b/Horyuji_Shaka_Triad.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Horyuji_Shaka_Triad.jpg)

<sub>Shaka Triad by Tori Busshi, Hōryū-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Horyuji_Shaka_Triad.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 623
- **Type:** Gilt bronze
- **Where to see it:** Kondō (Golden Hall), Hōryū-ji, Ikaruga, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Shaka%20Triad%20of%20H%C5%8Dry%C5%AB-ji%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20Buddhist%20workshops)

An inscription on the back of the halo records that the triad was made in 623 by the sculptor Tori, of the Kuratsukuri clan of immigrant saddle-makers, in prayer for Prince Shōtoku, the great early patron of Japanese Buddhism, who died in 622. The historical Buddha Shaka sits cross-legged with hands raised in gestures of reassurance and gift-giving, flanked by two bodhisattvas, all set against a flame-patterned halo. The frontal, symmetrical design, archaic smile and cascading drapery follow Chinese Northern Wei models as transmitted through Korea. Hōryū-ji is among the oldest wooden buildings in the world, and the triad is one of the earliest dated works of Japanese sculpture and a National Treasure.

### Ashura (734)

[![Ashura](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Kofukuji_Ashura_1.jpg/1280px-Kofukuji_Ashura_1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kofukuji_Ashura_1.jpg)

<sub>Ashura, Kōfuku-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kofukuji_Ashura_1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 734
- **Type:** Hollow dry lacquer, painted
- **Where to see it:** Kōfuku-ji National Treasure Museum, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ashura%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20Buddhist%20workshops)

In 734 Empress Kōmyō commissioned a set of statues for the Western Golden Hall of Kōfuku-ji, the family temple of the Fujiwara, in memory of her mother. They included the Eight Legions, protectors of Buddhism originally drawn from Indian gods and demons. Ashura, in India a warlike demon, is shown as a thin boy with three faces and six arms, wearing only a scarf and skirt. The statue was made in hollow dry lacquer: layers of lacquer-soaked hemp cloth were built up over a clay core, which was then removed, giving a light figure with sensitive modelling. The faces express a gentle sorrow rather than fury, perhaps reflecting repentance. The statue survived the temple's fires and is a National Treasure.

### Great Buddha (Daibutsu) of Tōdai-ji (dedicated 752 (much rebuilt))

[![Great Buddha (Daibutsu) of Tōdai-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Daibutsu_Grand_bouddha_-_T%C5%8Ddai-ji_-_Nara.jpg/1280px-Daibutsu_Grand_bouddha_-_T%C5%8Ddai-ji_-_Nara.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daibutsu_Grand_bouddha_-_T%C5%8Ddai-ji_-_Nara.jpg)

<sub>The Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daibutsu_Grand_bouddha_-_T%C5%8Ddai-ji_-_Nara.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** dedicated 752 (much rebuilt)
- **Type:** Cast bronze, about 15 m
- **Where to see it:** Great Buddha Hall, Tōdai-ji, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Great%20Buddha%20%28Daibutsu%29%20of%20T%C5%8Ddai-ji%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20Buddhist%20workshops)

In 743 Emperor Shōmu issued an edict calling for a giant image of the cosmic Buddha Vairocana (Rushana) to protect the country, as head of a network of provincial temples. Casting took place in several stages between 747 and 749, using huge amounts of copper and gold from across Japan, and the eyes were painted in an eye-opening ceremony in 752 attended by monks from India and China. The statue has been damaged by earthquakes and fires, notably in 1180 and 1567, and the head and much of the body date from repairs in the Edo period; only parts of the base and lotus petals, with engraved images of the Buddhist universe, are original. The present hall, rebuilt in 1709, is still one of the largest wooden buildings in the world.
