# Kim Jeong-hui

*김정희 (金正喜)*

**1786 – 1856** · Korean  
Born: Yesan, Chungcheong province · Died: Gwacheon  
Movements & schools: [Joseon painting and ceramics](../movements/joseon-painting.md)  

> Scholar, epigrapher, calligrapher and painter (pen name Chusa) who created the Chusa calligraphic style and painted the literati masterpiece Sehando.

<sub>Portrait: Painted portrait of Kim Jeong-hui, attributed to Yi Han-cheol · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kim_Jeong-hui.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Kim Jeong-hui came from an aristocratic family related to the royal house. In 1809 he accompanied his father on an embassy to Beijing, where he met the Qing scholars Weng Fanggang and Ruan Yuan and became an enthusiast for the study of ancient inscriptions; back in Korea he identified the Bukhansan monument of King Jinheung of Silla. He held high office but fell victim to factional politics and spent nine years in exile on Jeju Island (1840–48) and later more in the north.

Kim Jeong-hui developed the Chusa style of calligraphy, based on ancient clerical script, with angular, unbalanced, powerful strokes. As a painter he advocated the Chinese literati ideal of expressing the mind, and his ink orchids and Sehando are its purest Korean expression. He was the central figure of Korean scholarly culture in the nineteenth century.

## Key artworks

### Sehando (Winter Scene) (1844)

[![Sehando (Winter Scene)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Sehando.jpg/1280px-Sehando.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sehando.jpg)

<sub>Kim Jeong-hui, Sehando, 1844 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sehando.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1844
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** National Museum of Korea, Seoul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sehando%20%28Winter%20Scene%29%20Kim%20Jeong-hui)

While Kim Jeong-hui was in exile on Jeju Island, his former interpreter-pupil Yi Sang-jeok, who travelled to Beijing with embassies, kept sending him rare books from China at great trouble. In gratitude Kim painted this scene in 1844, alluding to Confucius's saying that only when the year grows cold do we see that pine and cypress are the last to fade. A simple house stands between four bare trees on an empty ground, drawn with dry, sparse strokes. Yi took it to Beijing, where sixteen Qing scholars added colophons. The scroll was in Japan in the twentieth century, saved during the war by the calligrapher Son Jae-hyeong, and donated to the National Museum in 2020. It is National Treasure No. 180.
