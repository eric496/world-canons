# Ogata Kōrin

*尾形光琳*

**1658 – 1716** · Japanese  
Born: Kyoto · Died: Kyoto  
Movements & schools: [Momoyama and Edo decorative painting (Kanō and Rinpa)](../movements/kano-rinpa.md)  

> Edo-period painter and designer whose Irises and Red and White Plum Blossoms screens gave his name to the Rinpa school.

## Biography

Kōrin was the son of a wealthy Kyoto textile merchant who supplied the imperial court, and he grew up surrounded by fine design and distant links to Kōetsu's family. Having spent his inheritance, he turned to painting in his late thirties, receiving the rank of hokkyō in 1701. He spent some years in Edo working for daimyo patrons before returning to Kyoto. His brother Ogata Kenzan was a celebrated potter, and Kōrin often painted his ceramics.

Kōrin studied the work of Sōtatsu and developed an even more stylised, decorative manner, with abstract patterns, rhythmic repetition and areas of pure colour on gold. He also designed kimono, lacquer and fans. His style was revived a century later by Sakai Hōitsu, who used the name Rinpa, school of Kōrin, and in the late nineteenth century it influenced Art Nouveau in Europe.

### Irises (Kakitsubata-zu) (c. 1701–1705)

[![Irises (Kakitsubata-zu)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/KORIN-Irises-R.jpg/1280px-KORIN-Irises-R.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:KORIN-Irises-R.jpg)

<sub>Ogata Kōrin, Irises, right screen Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:KORIN-Irises-R.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1701–1705
- **Type:** Pair of six-panel folding screens, colour on gold leaf
- **Where to see it:** Nezu Museum, Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Irises%20%28Kakitsubata-zu%29%20Ogata%20K%C5%8Drin)

In The Tales of Ise, a tenth-century poem tale, a nobleman exiled from the capital stops at a place called Yatsuhashi, the eight bridges, where irises bloom by a stream, and composes a poem whose lines begin with the syllables of the word kakitsubata, iris, mourning the wife he has left behind. Kōrin left out the bridges, the stream and the travellers and painted only the flowers, in clumps of ultramarine and green on gold, rising and falling in a rhythm like music. Some groups are repeated almost exactly, perhaps using stencils, an idea from textile design. The screens are a National Treasure and among the most famous Japanese paintings.

### Red and White Plum Blossoms (Kōhakubai-zu) (c. 1710s)

[![Red and White Plum Blossoms (Kōhakubai-zu)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/77/Ogata_Korin_-_RED_AND_WHITE_PLUM_BLOSSOMS_%28National_Treasure%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/1280px-Ogata_Korin_-_RED_AND_WHITE_PLUM_BLOSSOMS_%28National_Treasure%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ogata_Korin_-_RED_AND_WHITE_PLUM_BLOSSOMS_%28National_Treasure%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

<sub>Ogata Kōrin, Red and White Plum Blossoms Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ogata_Korin_-_RED_AND_WHITE_PLUM_BLOSSOMS_%28National_Treasure%29_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1710s
- **Type:** Pair of two-panel folding screens, colour and gold on paper
- **Where to see it:** MOA Museum of Art, Atami
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Red%20and%20White%20Plum%20Blossoms%20%28K%C5%8Dhakubai-zu%29%20Ogata%20K%C5%8Drin)

On the left screen an old white plum bends in from the edge, on the right a young red plum rises, and between them a dark, swelling stream flows down, covered with rhythmic swirls that look almost like textile patterns. The trunks are painted with tarashikomi, the pooling ink technique of Sōtatsu, while the stream is thought to have been painted in silver that has darkened, with the swirls left reserved, though the technique has been debated. The contrast between the naturalistic trees and the abstract water, and between old and young, gives the screens a tense balance. Painted late in Kōrin's life, they are a National Treasure.
