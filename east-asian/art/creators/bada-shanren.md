## Biography

Bada Shanren, born Zhu Da (or Zhu Tong), was a descendant of the Ming imperial family living in Nanchang. After the Manchu conquest of 1644 he took refuge as a Buddhist monk. Around 1680 he suffered a mental crisis, said by some sources to have been feigned, during which he tore his monk's robes and returned to secular life. In later years he lived in Nanchang as a professional painter, and from 1684 signed his works Bada Shanren, written so that it seems to read both 'to cry' and 'to laugh'.

His paintings use a few bold strokes of ink and large empty spaces. Birds stand on one leg on rocks too small for them, fish look up with resentful eyes, and lotus leaves sway in splashed ink. Scholars debate how far these images carry loyalist political meaning. His radical simplification and expressive power influenced Qing and twentieth-century painters from the Yangzhou eccentrics to Qi Baishi and Zhang Daqian.

## Key artworks

### Fish and Rocks (c. 1690s)

[![Fish and Rocks](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Bada_Shanren_%28Chinese%2C_1626-1705%29_-_Fish_and_Rocks_-_1953.247_-_Cleveland_Museum_of_Art.jpg/1280px-Bada_Shanren_%28Chinese%2C_1626-1705%29_-_Fish_and_Rocks_-_1953.247_-_Cleveland_Museum_of_Art.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bada_Shanren_%28Chinese%2C_1626-1705%29_-_Fish_and_Rocks_-_1953.247_-_Cleveland_Museum_of_Art.jpg)

<sub>Bada Shanren, Fish and Rocks, Cleveland Museum of Art Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bada_Shanren_%28Chinese%2C_1626-1705%29_-_Fish_and_Rocks_-_1953.247_-_Cleveland_Museum_of_Art.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1690s
- **Type:** Handscroll or album leaves, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** Cleveland Museum of Art (and other collections)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fish%20and%20Rocks%20Bada%20Shanren)

Bada Shanren painted fish many times, usually without water: a single fish or a pair hang in the blank paper, their round eyes rimmed in black and turned upward with what seems like suspicion or disdain. Rocks above them are top-heavy and seem about to topple, and birds perch on one leg. A few strokes of ink, wet and dry, create each form. His inscriptions, often cryptic, play on classical texts. Whether the images are coded expressions of loyalty to the fallen Ming and contempt for the Qing is debated, but their wit and isolation are unmistakable. His radical economy influenced many modern painters, including Qi Baishi and Zhang Daqian.
