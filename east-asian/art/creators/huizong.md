## Biography

Zhao Ji, the eleventh son of Emperor Shenzong, became emperor in 1100. He was a passionate collector and patron, expanding the imperial collections of paintings, calligraphy and antique bronzes and commissioning catalogues of them. He reformed the court painting academy, setting examinations based on lines of poetry and insisting on close observation of nature. His government, however, was weakened by costly projects and corruption. In 1125 the Jurchen Jin invaded; Huizong abdicated in favour of his son, but both were captured when Kaifeng fell in 1127 and taken north, where Huizong died in captivity in 1135.

Huizong invented the slender, elegant slender-gold (shoujin) script, and his bird-and-flower paintings are precise and refined. Many works bearing his signature were probably made by academy painters in his name, and scholars debate which are truly his own hand. Auspicious Cranes in the Liaoning Provincial Museum is among the most celebrated. For later historians he was both the model of the artist-emperor and a warning of an artist who neglected the state.

### Ladies Preparing Newly Woven Silk (early 12th century, after Zhang Xuan (8th century))

<sub>Ladies Preparing Newly Woven Silk, Museum of Fine Arts, Boston Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Court_Ladies_Preparing_Newly_Woven_Silk.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** early 12th century, after Zhang Xuan (8th century)
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ladies%20Preparing%20Newly%20Woven%20Silk%20Emperor%20Huizong%20of%20Song)

The Tang painter Zhang Xuan, active in the court of Xuanzong in the eighth century, was famous for scenes of palace women. His painting of women preparing silk is lost, but this copy, with an inscription in Huizong's hand, preserves the composition. In three groups, the women pound silk with long pestles, sew and stretch out a length of cloth to be ironed with a pan of hot coals, while a little girl plays underneath. The full-figured women, bright colours and patterned clothes reflect Tang taste; the copy was probably made by Huizong's academy painters. It entered the Boston museum in 1912 and is a key document of Tang figure painting.

### Auspicious Cranes (1112)

[![Auspicious Cranes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/51/Auspicious_Cranes.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Auspicious_Cranes.jpg)

<sub>Auspicious Cranes, Liaoning Provincial Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Auspicious_Cranes.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1112
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Liaoning Provincial Museum, Shenyang
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Auspicious%20Cranes%20Emperor%20Huizong%20of%20Song)

According to the accompanying text written in Huizong's slender-gold script, on the evening of the Lantern Festival in 1112 a flock of cranes appeared over the Xuande Gate of the palace in Kaifeng, circling and calling, and two landed on the roof. The emperor took this as a sign of heaven's favour and recorded it in painting and poem. Against a blue sky of mineral pigment the white cranes, each in a different position, fly over the ridge of the roof, painted from directly in front. Such paintings of auspicious omens were part of Huizong's efforts to legitimise his reign; fifteen years later the dynasty lost the north. It is one of the most beautiful works associated with him.
