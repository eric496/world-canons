# Tōshūsai Sharaku

*東洲斎写楽*

**fl. 1794 – 1795** · Japanese  
Movements & schools: [Ukiyo-e](../movements/ukiyo-e.md)  

> Mysterious print designer who in ten months of 1794–95 produced about 140 kabuki actor prints of startling, almost caricatural intensity, then vanished.

## Biography

Nothing certain is known of Sharaku except his output. All his prints were published by Tsutaya Jūzaburō between the fifth month of 1794 and the first month of 1795. A nineteenth-century source identifies him as Saitō Jūrobei, a Noh actor in the service of the lord of Awa, and recent research has supported this, but his identity has been debated for over a century, with many alternative theories.

Sharaku's first and best works are large bust portraits of actors against dark mica backgrounds, which exaggerate their features and capture the moment of a dramatic pose. Their unflattering realism may not have pleased the public, and his later prints are weaker. Rediscovered by Western critics, notably Julius Kurth in 1910, he has been counted ever since among the greatest ukiyo-e artists.

## Key artworks

### Ōtani Oniji III as Yakko Edobei (1794)

[![Ōtani Oniji III as Yakko Edobei](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0e/Toshusai_Sharaku-_Otani_Oniji%2C_1794.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Toshusai_Sharaku-_Otani_Oniji%2C_1794.jpg)

<sub>Sharaku, Ōtani Oniji III as Yakko Edobei, 1794 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Toshusai_Sharaku-_Otani_Oniji%2C_1794.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1794
- **Type:** Woodblock print, ink, colour and dark mica
- **Where to see it:** Tokyo National Museum, Metropolitan Museum of Art and other collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=%C5%8Ctani%20Oniji%20III%20as%20Yakko%20Edobei%20T%C5%8Dsh%C5%ABsai%20Sharaku)

In the fifth month of 1794, the actor Ōtani Oniji III played Edobei, a henchman who robs another character, in the play The Colourful Reins of a Loving Wife at the Kawarazaki theatre. Sharaku's portrait shows him at a moment of menace, eyes narrowed, mouth pressed into a frown, hands thrust forward with splayed fingers. The dark mica background makes the figure stand out. The exaggeration of features, almost caricature, captures the intensity of the actor's performance. The print belongs to Sharaku's first series of 28 bust portraits, published by Tsutaya Jūzaburō, and is now one of the most recognised Japanese prints.
