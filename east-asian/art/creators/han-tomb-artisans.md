# Han tomb and court artisans

*漢代工匠*

**c. 206 BC – c. AD 220** · Chinese  
Born: Han empire · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Qin and Han tomb art](../movements/qin-han-tomb-art.md)  

> The anonymous jade carvers, bronze casters, lacquerers and silk painters who furnished the tombs of the Han nobility.

## Biography

Han dynasty China had state and princely workshops, including the imperial Shangfang, that produced luxury goods for court use and for burial. Their output survives mostly in excavated tombs: the jade suits of princes, painted lacquer boxes and cups, bronze lamps and incense burners inlaid with gold and silver, painted silk banners and, in eastern China, carved stone relief slabs showing history, myth and daily life. Some lacquer cups carry inscriptions listing every craftsman who worked on them, a sign of highly divided labour.

These artisans gave form to Han ideas about the afterlife, in which the soul needed protection, food and entertainment, and the body might be preserved by jade. Their realism and energy, seen in galloping bronze horses and lively pottery figures of dancers and storytellers, mark a shift from the solemn ritual art of the Bronze Age to a more human and narrative world.

### T-shaped silk banner from Mawangdui Tomb 1 (c. 168 BC)

[![T-shaped silk banner from Mawangdui Tomb 1](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2b/Mawangdui_silk_banner_from_tomb_no1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mawangdui_silk_banner_from_tomb_no1.jpg)

<sub>Silk banner from Mawangdui Tomb 1, Hunan Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mawangdui_silk_banner_from_tomb_no1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 168 BC
- **Type:** Ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Hunan Museum, Changsha
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=T-shaped%20silk%20banner%20from%20Mawangdui%20Tomb%201%20Han%20tomb%20and%20court%20artisans)

The tombs at Mawangdui in Changsha, excavated in 1972–74, belonged to the family of the Marquis of Dai. Tomb 1 contained the remarkably preserved body of Xin Zhui, Lady Dai, who died around 168 BC, and draped over her innermost coffin was this T-shaped silk banner about two metres long. At the top, in the heavens, are the sun with a crow, the moon with a toad and a serpent-bodied deity; in the middle the elderly lady herself, leaning on a staff, is attended by servants; below, offerings are laid out for her, and an underworld figure holds up the earth. The banner probably served in the funeral and may have guided her soul. It is one of the earliest major Chinese paintings to survive.

### Changxin Palace Lamp (2nd century BC)

[![Changxin Palace Lamp](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bc/Changxin_Palace_Lamp_%2811867682066%29.jpg/1280px-Changxin_Palace_Lamp_%2811867682066%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Changxin_Palace_Lamp_%2811867682066%29.jpg)

<sub>Changxin Palace Lamp, Hebei Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Changxin_Palace_Lamp_%2811867682066%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 2nd century BC
- **Type:** Gilt bronze lamp
- **Where to see it:** Hebei Museum, Shijiazhuang
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Changxin%20Palace%20Lamp%20Han%20tomb%20and%20court%20artisans)

This lamp was found in 1968 in the Mancheng tomb of Dou Wan, wife of Liu Sheng. Inscriptions show that it had belonged to the household of the Changxin Palace, the residence of the empress dowager, before passing to the prince's family. A kneeling young woman holds the lamp in one hand while her other arm and sleeve form a chimney, which carries smoke into the hollow body and kept the room clean. Sliding panels let the light be adjusted in direction and brightness, and the parts can be taken apart for cleaning. The calm, natural figure and the clever engineering make it one of the best-known works of Han art.

### Jade burial suit of Liu Sheng (c. 113 BC)

[![Jade burial suit of Liu Sheng](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/69/Jade_burial_suit_of_Liu_Sheng.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jade_burial_suit_of_Liu_Sheng.jpg)

<sub>Jade burial suit of Liu Sheng, Hebei Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jade_burial_suit_of_Liu_Sheng.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 113 BC
- **Type:** Jade plaques sewn with gold wire
- **Where to see it:** Hebei Museum, Shijiazhuang
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jade%20burial%20suit%20of%20Liu%20Sheng%20Han%20tomb%20and%20court%20artisans)

Liu Sheng, Prince Jing of Zhongshan and a brother of Emperor Wu of Han, died in 113 BC and was buried with his wife Dou Wan in rock-cut tombs at Mancheng in Hebei, excavated in 1968. Both were clothed in suits of jade, Liu Sheng's made of 2,498 thin plaques cut to fit the shape of head, body, hands and feet and sewn together with about a kilogram of gold wire. Han texts describe such suits as a privilege of emperors and high nobles, worn in the hope that jade would keep the body from decay; in fact the bodies had turned to dust. Making one required years of work by skilled lapidaries. These were the first complete jade suits ever found and caused a sensation.

### Flying Horse of Gansu (c. AD 150–200)

[![Flying Horse of Gansu](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Gansu_Museum_2007_257.jpg/1280px-Gansu_Museum_2007_257.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gansu_Museum_2007_257.jpg)

<sub>Flying Horse of Gansu, Gansu Provincial Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gansu_Museum_2007_257.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. AD 150–200
- **Type:** Cast bronze
- **Where to see it:** Gansu Provincial Museum, Lanzhou
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Flying%20Horse%20of%20Gansu%20Han%20tomb%20and%20court%20artisans)

This small bronze horse, about 35 centimetres high, was found in 1969 in a tomb at Leitai in Wuwei, Gansu, along with a procession of bronze horses, chariots and riders. The horse races forward with three hooves in the air, balanced on a fourth that rests on a flying bird, a brilliant solution to the problem of showing speed in sculpture. It probably represents one of the 'heavenly horses' from Central Asia prized by Han rulers. Its identity has been debated: the bird is usually called a swallow, but some argue it is a falcon or the horse is a mythical creature. Chosen in 1983 as the symbol of Chinese tourism, it is one of the most famous objects of Chinese art.
