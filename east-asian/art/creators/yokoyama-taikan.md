# Yokoyama Taikan

*横山大観*

**1868 – 1958** · Japanese  
Born: Mito, Ibaraki · Died: Tokyo  
Movements & schools: [Nihonga and yōga in modern Japan](../movements/nihonga-yoga.md)  

> Leading nihonga painter of modern Japan, a pupil of Okakura Kakuzō, famous for his misty 'blurred style' and countless images of Mount Fuji.

## Biography

Taikan was born in Mito the year of the Meiji Restoration and was in the first class of the Tokyo School of Fine Arts in 1889, studying under Hashimoto Gahō and Okakura Kakuzō. When Okakura was forced out in 1898, Taikan followed him to found the Japan Art Institute (Nihon Bijutsuin), and he travelled with Hishida Shunsō to India, the United States and Europe. He re-established the Institute in 1914 and dominated the nihonga world for decades. His paintings of Mount Fuji were associated with wartime nationalism. He received the Order of Culture in 1937.

With Shunsō, Taikan developed mōrōtai, the blurred or hazy style, in which outlines are replaced by washes of colour to suggest light and air, a method criticised at first but later central to nihonga. His long ink scroll The Wheel of Life (Seisei ruten, 1923) is an Important Cultural Property. Because he died in 1958, his works are not reproduced here.

## Key artworks

### The Wheel of Life (Seisei ruten) (1923)

> 🖼️ **[View it at National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo (MOMAT) →](https://www.momat.go.jp)**  
> <sub>Still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here.</sub>

- **Date:** 1923
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink on silk
- **Where to see it:** National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Wheel%20of%20Life%20%28Seisei%20ruten%29%20Yokoyama%20Taikan)

Taikan's longest work, about forty metres long, follows the journey of water: mist in the mountains, a stream in the forest, a river through villages, its broadening to the sea, a storm, and in the end a dragon rising into the clouds, suggesting the cycle of rebirth. It was painted entirely in ink, using the full range of traditional techniques and the hazy washes of Taikan's mōrōtai style. It was exhibited at the Japan Art Institute exhibition that opened on 1 September 1923, the day of the Great Kantō Earthquake, and survived. It is an Important Cultural Property. Because Taikan died in 1958, it is not reproduced here.
