# Wang Hui

*王翬*

**1632 – 1717** · Chinese  
Born: Changshu, Jiangsu · Died: Changshu  
Movements & schools: [Qing painting: Orthodox masters and Individualists](../movements/qing-painting.md)  

> Leading Orthodox landscape painter of the early Qing, one of the Four Wangs, who directed the scrolls of the Kangxi Emperor's Southern Inspection Tour.

## Biography

Wang Hui came from a family of painters in Changshu. Discovered as a young man by the scholar-painters Wang Jian and Wang Shimin, he studied old masterpieces in their collections and became one of the most skilful painters of his time. In 1691 he was summoned to Beijing to supervise a team painting the Kangxi Emperor's Southern Inspection Tour of 1689, a set of twelve huge handscrolls, which took six years.

Wang Hui aimed to unite the virtues of the Northern and Southern schools, combining the brushwork of the Yuan masters with the structure of Song landscape. With Wang Shimin, Wang Jian and Wang Yuanqi he is counted among the Four Wangs of the Orthodox school, whose synthesis of past styles dominated Qing court and scholarly painting. Twentieth-century reformers attacked the Orthodox school as stale, but its craftsmanship is again widely admired.

- **Date:** 1691–1698
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and colour on silk
- **Where to see it:** Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Kangxi%20Emperor%27s%20Southern%20Inspection%20Tour%2C%20Scroll%20Three%20Wang%20Hui)

In 1689 the Kangxi Emperor made his second tour of inspection of southern China, travelling down the Grand Canal to Hangzhou and Shaoxing. To commemorate it, the court commissioned twelve handscrolls, each about 70 centimetres high and up to 26 metres long, from a team led by Wang Hui. Scroll Three, in the Metropolitan Museum, follows the emperor from Jinan in Shandong to the sacred Mount Tai, with towns, markets, fields and crowds observed in detail. The project combined the Orthodox school's landscape tradition with documentary record and imperial propaganda. Several of the twelve scrolls survive in Beijing, Paris and New York, and they set the pattern for Qianlong's later tour scrolls.
