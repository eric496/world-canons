# Sesshū Tōyō

*雪舟等楊*

<a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Sesshu.jpg"><img align="right" width="240" src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/Portrait_of_Sesshu.jpg" alt="Sesshū Tōyō" title="Portrait of Sesshū, traditionally after a self-portrait"></a>

**1420 – 1506** · Japanese  
Born: Akahama, Bitchū province (Okayama) · Died: Iwami or Yamaguchi  
Movements & schools: [Muromachi ink painting](../movements/muromachi-ink.md)  

> Zen monk and the greatest master of Japanese ink painting, who travelled to Ming China and created a forceful, structured landscape style.

<sub>Portrait: Portrait of Sesshū, traditionally after a self-portrait · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Sesshu.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Sesshū was born in Bitchū province and entered the Zen temple Hōfuku-ji as a boy; a famous legend says he drew a mouse with his tears when tied to a pillar as punishment. He trained at Shōkoku-ji in Kyoto, where Shūbun was the leading painter, and in his thirties moved to Yamaguchi under the patronage of the Ōuchi family. In 1467–69 he joined an official mission to Ming China, where he visited monasteries and studied Chinese painting. On his return he settled in western Japan and travelled widely, painting until his death around 1506.

Sesshū's landscapes combine the Southern Song and Ming models he studied with strong, angular brushwork and a firm sense of structure, as in his Autumn and Winter Landscapes in the Tokyo National Museum. His Long Landscape Scroll of 1486 is a sixteen-metre journey through the seasons, while his splashed-ink (haboku) landscape of 1495 shows a few wet strokes. Six of his works are National Treasures, more than of any other Japanese painter, and the Kanō and Unkoku schools traced their lineage to him.

### Autumn and Winter Landscapes (late 15th century)

[![Autumn and Winter Landscapes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1f/Sesshu_%22Autumn_And_Winter_Landscapes%22_Winter.jpg/1280px-Sesshu_%22Autumn_And_Winter_Landscapes%22_Winter.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sesshu_%22Autumn_And_Winter_Landscapes%22_Winter.jpg)

<sub>Sesshū, Winter Landscape Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sesshu_%22Autumn_And_Winter_Landscapes%22_Winter.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 15th century
- **Type:** Pair of hanging scrolls, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** Tokyo National Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Autumn%20and%20Winter%20Landscapes%20Sessh%C5%AB%20T%C5%8Dy%C5%8D)

Each scroll is less than fifty centimetres high. In the autumn landscape a path winds past a temple and trees towards distant peaks; in the winter landscape a traveller climbs towards a temple in snow, while behind him a sharp, broken vertical stroke, perhaps the edge of a cliff, splits the sky. The brushwork is hard, angular and dark, and the forms are tightly locked together, quite unlike the misty manner of earlier Japanese ink painting. The pair may once have belonged to a set of the four seasons. Both are National Treasures and among the best-known works of Japanese ink painting.

### Long Landscape Scroll (Sansui chōkan) (1486)

[![Long Landscape Scroll (Sansui chōkan)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Landscapes_of_the_Four_Seasons_by_Sesshu_%28Mori_Museum%29.jpg/1280px-Landscapes_of_the_Four_Seasons_by_Sesshu_%28Mori_Museum%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Landscapes_of_the_Four_Seasons_by_Sesshu_%28Mori_Museum%29.jpg)

<sub>Sesshū, Long Landscape Scroll (section), Mōri Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Landscapes_of_the_Four_Seasons_by_Sesshu_%28Mori_Museum%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1486
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink and light colour on paper
- **Where to see it:** Mōri Museum, Hōfu, Yamaguchi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Long%20Landscape%20Scroll%20%28Sansui%20ch%C5%8Dkan%29%20Sessh%C5%AB%20T%C5%8Dy%C5%8D)

Sesshū painted this scroll in 1486 at his studio in Yamaguchi, under the patronage of the Ōuchi family, and it later passed to the Mōri lords who succeeded them. It begins with spring hills and moves through summer, autumn and winter, past fishing villages, mountain passes, a walled town and boats, ending in snow. Drawing on the Southern Song and Ming models he had seen in China, Sesshū combined them into a continuous journey with varied brushwork, crisp outlines and pale colour. At about sixteen metres, it is his longest work and a summary of his art. It is a National Treasure.

### Splashed-Ink Landscape (Haboku sansui) (1495)

[![Splashed-Ink Landscape (Haboku sansui)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8b/Sesshu_-_Haboku-Sansui_-_complete.jpg/1280px-Sesshu_-_Haboku-Sansui_-_complete.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sesshu_-_Haboku-Sansui_-_complete.jpg)

<sub>Sesshū, Splashed-Ink Landscape, 1495 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sesshu_-_Haboku-Sansui_-_complete.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1495
- **Type:** Hanging scroll, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** Tokyo National Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Splashed-Ink%20Landscape%20%28Haboku%20sansui%29%20Sessh%C5%AB%20T%C5%8Dy%C5%8D)

In 1495 Sesshū's pupil Sōen was about to return to Kamakura, and the old master painted this landscape as a certificate of his training. A few broad, wet strokes suggest a hill, trees, a tavern flag and boat, dissolving into mist. Above, Sesshū wrote a long inscription recalling his journey to China and his teachers Josetsu and Shūbun, followed by poems by six Zen monks. The splashed-ink technique, associated with the Chinese painter Yujian, depends on rapid, controlled accident. The painting is a National Treasure and an important document of Sesshū's view of his own lineage.
