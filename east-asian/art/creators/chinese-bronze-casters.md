# Bronze Age casters of China

*商周青銅工匠*

**c. 1600 BC – c. 221 BC** · Chinese  
Born: Yellow River valley and Sichuan · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Shang & Zhou ritual bronzes](../movements/shang-zhou-bronzes.md)  
Also in: [Music & Theatre](../../music/creators/chinese-bronze-casters.md)  

> The anonymous foundry workers of the Shang and Zhou dynasties, and of the Sanxingdui culture in Sichuan, who cast the ritual vessels, bells and figures of China's Bronze Age.

## Biography

The bronzes of ancient China were made in large royal and aristocratic workshops, not by named individual artists. At Shang sites such as Anyang (around 1250–1046 BC), archaeologists have found foundries with thousands of clay mould fragments. Rather than using the lost-wax method common elsewhere, Chinese casters built a clay model, took a multi-part clay mould from it, shaved down the model to create a gap and poured molten bronze into the space. The work required mining, smelting and fuel on an industrial scale and the labour of hundreds of people, which is why bronze was a monopoly of kings and great lineages.

The vessels were used to offer food and wine to ancestors, and their decoration, dominated by the taotie mask with its staring eyes, was probably meant to be powerful rather than beautiful. Under the Western Zhou, long inscriptions recording royal gifts and appointments were cast inside the vessels, making them precious historical documents. Far to the south-west, the Sanxingdui culture of Sichuan cast huge masks and standing figures unlike anything in the north. These objects were collected by later emperors and scholars, imitated in porcelain and jade, and are now among the treasures of Chinese museums.

### Houmuwu ding (c. 1200 BC)

[![Houmuwu ding](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/HouMuWuDingFullView.jpg/1280px-HouMuWuDingFullView.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:HouMuWuDingFullView.jpg)

<sub>Houmuwu ding, National Museum of China Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:HouMuWuDingFullView.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1200 BC
- **Type:** Cast bronze ritual vessel
- **Where to see it:** National Museum of China, Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Houmuwu%20ding%20Bronze%20Age%20casters%20of%20China)

This massive rectangular cauldron (ding), weighing about 830 kilograms, was found in 1939 near Anyang, the last capital of the Shang dynasty; villagers reburied it to hide it from the Japanese army, and it was recovered in 1946. The inscription inside, read as Hou Mu Wu, dedicates it to a royal mother, probably a consort of King Wu Ding. Such vessels held offerings of meat to the ancestors. It was cast in a set of clay moulds, with handles added in a separate pour, and its sides are framed with taotie masks and dragons. The scale of the casting, requiring hundreds of workers to melt and pour the metal at once, shows the organising power of the Shang state, and the vessel has become a national symbol of Chinese antiquity.

### Bronze heads and masks from Sanxingdui (c. 1200–1000 BC)

[![Bronze heads and masks from Sanxingdui](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Gold_Mask_%28%E9%BB%84%E9%87%91%E9%9D%A2%E7%BD%A9%29.jpg/1280px-Gold_Mask_%28%E9%BB%84%E9%87%91%E9%9D%A2%E7%BD%A9%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gold_Mask_%28%E9%BB%84%E9%87%91%E9%9D%A2%E7%BD%A9%29.jpg)

<sub>Gold mask from Sanxingdui Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gold_Mask_%28%E9%BB%84%E9%87%91%E9%9D%A2%E7%BD%A9%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1200–1000 BC
- **Type:** Cast bronze, some with gold foil
- **Where to see it:** Sanxingdui Museum, Guanghan, Sichuan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bronze%20heads%20and%20masks%20from%20Sanxingdui%20Bronze%20Age%20casters%20of%20China)

In 1986 workers at Sanxingdui near Chengdu uncovered two pits filled with deliberately broken and burned objects: elephant tusks, jades, a bronze tree nearly four metres tall, a standing figure of 2.6 metres, and dozens of life-size bronze heads, some covered with gold foil masks, along with huge masks with protruding cylindrical eyes. Six more pits excavated from 2019 have added many more finds. The objects were cast with the same piece-mould technology as Shang bronzes, but their imagery has no parallel in the Yellow River valley, and no writing explains them. They probably represent ancestors or deities of a kingdom of the ancient Shu people. Sanxingdui transformed the picture of early China, showing that the Bronze Age had several centres.

### Four-Ram Square Zun (c. 1200–1050 BC)

[![Four-Ram Square Zun](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ae/Square_zun_with_four_sheep_01.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Square_zun_with_four_sheep_01.jpg)

<sub>Four-Ram Square Zun, National Museum of China Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Square_zun_with_four_sheep_01.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1200–1050 BC
- **Type:** Cast bronze wine vessel
- **Where to see it:** National Museum of China, Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Four-Ram%20Square%20Zun%20Bronze%20Age%20casters%20of%20China)

This square wine vessel was found in 1938 by farmers in Ningxiang county, Hunan, far south of the Shang heartland. Its upper part is a flaring square mouth, and its body is formed by four rams whose heads and forelegs project from the corners, their horns curling round their faces; dragons rear up between them. The heads and horns were cast separately and then locked into the main mould, a complex procedure that shows the skill of southern bronze casters, who may have worked in a distinct regional tradition. The vessel was damaged in wartime and restored in the 1950s. It is one of the most celebrated of all Chinese bronzes, combining abstract surface decoration with sculptural animals.

### Mao Gong ding (c. 800 BC)

[![Mao Gong ding](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Ding_cauldron_of_Duke_Mao.jpg/1280px-Ding_cauldron_of_Duke_Mao.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ding_cauldron_of_Duke_Mao.jpg)

<sub>Mao Gong ding, National Palace Museum, Taipei Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ding_cauldron_of_Duke_Mao.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 800 BC
- **Type:** Cast bronze ritual vessel with inscription
- **Where to see it:** National Palace Museum, Taipei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mao%20Gong%20ding%20Bronze%20Age%20casters%20of%20China)

Found in Shaanxi province in the 1840s, the Mao Gong ding is a plain, round three-legged cauldron of the late Western Zhou period. Its importance lies in the inscription of 497 characters cast inside the bowl, in which a Zhou king, probably King Xuan, charges the Duke of Mao with governing the realm, warns him against abuses and lists rich gifts of chariots, robes and weapons. The duke then records that he made the vessel to commemorate the honour. The text is a key source for Zhou government and an admired model of bronze script (jinwen), studied by calligraphers such as Wu Changshuo. The vessel passed through several private collections before reaching the National Palace Museum in Taipei.

### Bianzhong of Marquis Yi of Zeng (c. 433 BC)

[![Bianzhong of Marquis Yi of Zeng](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/%E6%B9%96%E5%8C%97%E5%8D%9A%E7%89%A9%E9%A4%A8%E6%9B%BE%E4%BE%AF%E4%B9%99%E7%B7%A8%E9%90%98.jpg/1280px-%E6%B9%96%E5%8C%97%E5%8D%9A%E7%89%A9%E9%A4%A8%E6%9B%BE%E4%BE%AF%E4%B9%99%E7%B7%A8%E9%90%98.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%B9%96%E5%8C%97%E5%8D%9A%E7%89%A9%E9%A4%A8%E6%9B%BE%E4%BE%AF%E4%B9%99%E7%B7%A8%E9%90%98.jpg)

<sub>The bells of Marquis Yi, Hubei Provincial Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%B9%96%E5%8C%97%E5%8D%9A%E7%89%A9%E9%A4%A8%E6%9B%BE%E4%BE%AF%E4%B9%99%E7%B7%A8%E9%90%98.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 433 BC
- **Type:** Set of 65 cast bronze bells
- **Where to see it:** Hubei Provincial Museum, Wuhan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bianzhong%20of%20Marquis%20Yi%20of%20Zeng%20Bronze%20Age%20casters%20of%20China)

In 1978 the tomb of Marquis Yi, ruler of the small state of Zeng, was excavated at Suizhou in Hubei. Among more than 15,000 objects was a complete orchestra, including this set of 65 bronze bells hanging from a lacquered wooden frame supported by bronze figures of warriors. Each bell, because of its almond-shaped cross-section, produces two different notes depending on where it is struck. Inscriptions in gold on the bells name the notes and the relations between the tuning systems of different states, giving unique evidence for ancient Chinese music theory. The set covers a range of five octaves. Its discovery showed the astonishing technical precision of Warring States casters, and replicas are used in concerts.
