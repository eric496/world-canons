# Ming and Qing imperial workshops

*明清宮廷作坊*

**1368 – 1912** · Chinese  
Born: Beijing and Suzhou · Died: ?  
Movements & schools: [Ming painting & porcelain](../movements/ming-painting-porcelain.md), [Qing painting: Orthodox masters and Individualists](../movements/qing-painting.md)  

> The palace workshops of the Ming and Qing courts, which produced carved lacquer, cloisonné, jade and other luxury objects.

## Biography

The Ming court maintained workshops for lacquer, metalwork and textiles, among them the Orchard Factory (Guoyuanchang) in Beijing, known for its deeply carved red lacquer of the Yongle and Xuande reigns. The Qing emperors expanded the system in the Zaobanchu, the Imperial Household's Office of Manufacture, with dozens of specialist workshops in the Forbidden City staffed by craftsmen from the south and sometimes by European Jesuits. Emperors, above all Yongzheng and Qianlong, commented on designs and commissioned pieces in person.

The workshops' output, now mostly in the Palace Museum in Beijing and the National Palace Museum in Taipei, shows extreme technical virtuosity: carved lacquer built up from hundreds of coats, cloisonné enamels, and jade and hardstone carvings that turned the natural colours of stones into cabbages, mountains and animals. Their objects were given as diplomatic gifts and helped define the court taste of East Asia.

### Carved red lacquer of the Yongle period (Yongle period (1403–1424))

[![Carved red lacquer of the Yongle period](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/55/WLA_vanda_Box_Carved_lacquer_on_wood_Yongle_reign_period.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WLA_vanda_Box_Carved_lacquer_on_wood_Yongle_reign_period.jpg)

<sub>Carved red lacquer box, Yongle period, Victoria and Albert Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WLA_vanda_Box_Carved_lacquer_on_wood_Yongle_reign_period.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** Yongle period (1403–1424)
- **Type:** Carved cinnabar lacquer on wood
- **Where to see it:** Palace Museum, Beijing; Victoria and Albert Museum, London; and other collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Carved%20red%20lacquer%20of%20the%20Yongle%20period%20Ming%20and%20Qing%20imperial%20workshops)

Lacquer, the sap of the lacquer tree, hardens into a waterproof, durable film, and Chinese craftsmen had used it since the Neolithic. In carved lacquer (tihong), dozens or even hundreds of thin coats, each dried and polished, are built up on a wooden core and then carved in relief. The imperial Orchard Factory in Beijing, staffed by craftsmen from the south, produced dishes, boxes and cup-stands of extraordinary quality in the Yongle and Xuande reigns, often with large peonies, camellias or landscapes, the reign mark incised and filled with gold. The deep, rounded carving and polished edges are typical of early Ming work. Such pieces were given as diplomatic gifts to Japan and other states and greatly influenced Japanese lacquer.

### Jadeite Cabbage (late 19th century)

[![Jadeite Cabbage](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Jadeite_Cabbage%2C_National_Palace_Museum.jpg/1280px-Jadeite_Cabbage%2C_National_Palace_Museum.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jadeite_Cabbage%2C_National_Palace_Museum.jpg)

<sub>Jadeite Cabbage, National Palace Museum, Taipei Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jadeite_Cabbage%2C_National_Palace_Museum.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 19th century
- **Type:** Jadeite carving
- **Where to see it:** National Palace Museum, Taipei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jadeite%20Cabbage%20Ming%20and%20Qing%20imperial%20workshops)

The carving, about 19 centimetres long, uses a single piece of jadeite that shades from white to green; the carver made the white part the stem and the green part the leaves of a Chinese cabbage, with a locust and a katydid hiding in the leaves. The flaws and cracks in the stone became the veins of the leaves. The cabbage is thought to have belonged to the Guangxu Emperor's consort Jin, perhaps as part of her dowry, symbolising purity, with the insects suggesting fertility. It was displayed in the Forbidden City and later evacuated with the imperial collections to Taiwan. The carver is unknown. It is a favourite of visitors to the museum and a symbol of Qing virtuoso craftsmanship.
