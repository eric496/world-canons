# Unkei

*運慶*

**c. 1150 – 1223** · Japanese  
Born: Nara · Died: Kyoto  
Movements & schools: [Yamato-e and narrative handscrolls](../movements/yamato-e-emaki.md)  

> The greatest sculptor of the Kamakura period, leader of the Kei school, whose powerful, realistic figures include the giant Kongō Rikishi of Tōdai-ji.

<sub>Portrait: Seated wooden statue of Unkei, Rokuharamitsu-ji, Kyoto · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Unkei_Rokuharamitsuji.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Unkei was the son of the sculptor Kōkei, head of a Nara workshop. When the Taira burned Tōdai-ji and Kōfuku-ji in 1180, the Kei school was engaged in their rebuilding, and Unkei also worked for the new warrior rulers in eastern Japan. In 1203, with Kaikei and their assistants, he carved the two eight-metre guardian kings at the Great South Gate of Tōdai-ji in about seventy days. His sons, including Tankei, continued the workshop.

Unkei's figures have a new physical presence: muscular bodies, tense poses and faces of individual character, sometimes with inlaid crystal eyes. His portrait statues of the Indian monks Muchaku and Seshin at Kōfuku-ji are among the finest portrait sculptures in the world. His realism reflected both a study of Nara and Song Chinese art and the vigorous taste of the warrior class.

### Kongō Rikishi (Niō) of the Great South Gate, Tōdai-ji (1203)

[![Kongō Rikishi (Niō) of the Great South Gate, Tōdai-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/07/Nio_guardians_by_Unkei_in_Nara.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nio_guardians_by_Unkei_in_Nara.jpg)

<sub>Niō guardian at the Great South Gate of Tōdai-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nio_guardians_by_Unkei_in_Nara.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1203
- **Type:** Joined wood, about 8.4 m
- **Where to see it:** Nandaimon (Great South Gate), Tōdai-ji, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kong%C5%8D%20Rikishi%20%28Ni%C5%8D%29%20of%20the%20Great%20South%20Gate%2C%20T%C5%8Ddai-ji%20Unkei)

After Tōdai-ji was burned in 1180 during the Genpei War, its rebuilding was led by the monk Chōgen. In 1203 the Kei school sculptors carved two guardians for the new Great South Gate: the open-mouthed Agyō and the closed-mouthed Ungyō, representing the first and last sounds of the Sanskrit alphabet. Each is about 8.4 metres tall and built from about three thousand pieces of wood. Inscriptions found during restoration in 1988–93 confirmed that Unkei and Kaikei directed the work with about twenty assistants. The swelling muscles, flying draperies and fierce faces, made to be seen from below, are the most dramatic expression of Kamakura realism. Both figures are National Treasures.

### Muchaku (Asanga) (c. 1212)

[![Muchaku (Asanga)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Kofukuji_Hokuendo_Muchaku_Unkei.jpg/1280px-Kofukuji_Hokuendo_Muchaku_Unkei.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kofukuji_Hokuendo_Muchaku_Unkei.jpg)

<sub>Unkei and workshop, Muchaku, Kōfuku-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kofukuji_Hokuendo_Muchaku_Unkei.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1212
- **Type:** Painted wood with inlaid crystal eyes
- **Where to see it:** Hokuendō, Kōfuku-ji, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Muchaku%20%28Asanga%29%20Unkei)

Asanga, called Muchaku in Japanese, and his brother Vasubandhu (Seshin) were fourth-century Indian monks who founded the Yogācāra school, important to the Hossō sect of Kōfuku-ji. Their statues were made for the rebuilt Northern Octagonal Hall around 1212, at the end of Unkei's career, and carved by his sons and workshop under his direction. Muchaku, nearly two metres high, stands holding a wrapped cloth bundle, his head slightly bowed, his face lined and inward-looking, with crystal eyes that catch the light. Although no likeness of Asanga existed, the statue has the presence of a portrait of a real person. It is often considered the summit of Japanese portrait sculpture and is a National Treasure.
