## Biography

Born Lu Jian in Changshu, Huang Gongwang was adopted as a child by an old man named Huang and took his name. He worked as a minor clerk, was imprisoned for a time after his superior was implicated in a scandal, and then turned to Daoism, joining the Quanzhen sect. He made a living as a fortune-teller and teacher and began to paint seriously only in his fifties.

Huang Gongwang wrote a short treatise on painting landscape and developed a style of layered, dry hemp-fibre strokes that build up rounded hills. His masterpiece, Dwelling in the Fuchun Mountains, painted between 1347 and 1350, was later burned in a collector's fire and survives in two pieces in Hangzhou and Taipei. Ming and Qing painters regarded him as the greatest of the Four Masters, and the Orthodox school took him as its chief model.

## Key artworks

### Dwelling in the Fuchun Mountains (1347–1350)

[![Dwelling in the Fuchun Mountains](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/Dwelling_in_the_Fuchun_Mountains_%28first_half%29.JPG/1280px-Dwelling_in_the_Fuchun_Mountains_%28first_half%29.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dwelling_in_the_Fuchun_Mountains_%28first_half%29.JPG)

<sub>Dwelling in the Fuchun Mountains (part) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dwelling_in_the_Fuchun_Mountains_%28first_half%29.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1347–1350
- **Type:** Handscroll, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** National Palace Museum, Taipei, and Zhejiang Provincial Museum, Hangzhou
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dwelling%20in%20the%20Fuchun%20Mountains%20Huang%20Gongwang)

Huang Gongwang painted this scroll for a Daoist friend, Wuyong, over three or four years, adding to it whenever he had time. It shows the scenery of the Fuchun River near Hangzhou: gently rolling hills, trees, villages and fishing boats, built up with dry, layered hemp-fibre strokes and pale ink, an image of quiet seclusion rather than grandeur. In 1650 its owner, Wu Hongyu, ordered it to be burned as he was dying so that it would go with him, but his nephew pulled it from the fire. The small first section, The Remaining Mountain, is in Hangzhou, the longer part in Taipei. The Qianlong Emperor wrote many inscriptions on what turned out to be a copy, and he dismissed the genuine one. The two parts were shown together in Taipei in 2011.
