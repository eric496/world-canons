# Muqi

*牧谿*

**c. 1210 – c. 1269** · Chinese  
Born: Sichuan · Died: Hangzhou  
Movements & schools: [Song academy & literati painting](../movements/song-academy-literati.md)  

> Chan Buddhist monk-painter of the Southern Song, whose Six Persimmons and splashed-ink works are among the most revered paintings in Japan.

## Biography

Muqi, whose monastic name was Fachang, came from Sichuan and lived at the Liutong temple near the West Lake in Hangzhou. According to some sources he offended the powerful minister Jia Sidao and had to go into hiding. Japanese monks who studied in Hangzhou brought his paintings back to Japan, where they entered the collections of the Ashikaga shoguns and Zen temples.

Muqi painted Buddhist figures, animals, birds, fruit and landscapes in a free, often wet ink style. Chinese critics found him coarse and few of his works were kept in China, but in Japan he was regarded as one of the supreme painters. His triptych of Guanyin, a crane and gibbons in Daitoku-ji, his views of the Xiao and Xiang rivers and the Six Persimmons became models for Zen painting and the tea ceremony.

## Key artworks

### Six Persimmons (13th century)

[![Six Persimmons](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c7/%E5%85%AD%E6%9F%BF%E5%9B%BE.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%85%AD%E6%9F%BF%E5%9B%BE.jpg)

<sub>Six Persimmons, attributed to Muqi, Daitoku-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%85%AD%E6%9F%BF%E5%9B%BE.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 13th century
- **Type:** Hanging scroll, ink on paper
- **Where to see it:** Daitoku-ji (Ryūkō-in), Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Six%20Persimmons%20Muqi)

Six persimmons sit in a line on an empty ground, some outlined, some filled with dark or pale ink, their stems painted with quick strokes. Nothing else is shown. The painting is traditionally attributed to Muqi, though it is not signed and the attribution is debated. It was brought to Japan and kept in the Zen temple Daitoku-ji in Kyoto, where it is shown only rarely. In the twentieth century it became celebrated in the West as an emblem of Zen Buddhism, its plainness and balance seen as expressing emptiness and the suchness of things. Whatever its meaning, it shows a remarkable control of ink tone.
