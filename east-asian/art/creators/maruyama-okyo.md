## Biography

Ōkyo was born to a farming family in Tanba and went to Kyoto as a youth, working in a toy shop where he painted megane-e, perspective pictures viewed through a lens device, which introduced him to Western linear perspective. He studied with a Kanō painter but filled sketchbooks with studies from life of plants, animals and people. With the patronage of the abbot Yūjō of Enman-in and wealthy merchants, he became the most sought-after painter in Kyoto.

Ōkyo's work combines direct observation with elegant composition, as in his Pine Trees in Snow screens in the Mitsui Memorial Museum, painted with almost no outline. His Maruyama school, and its offshoot the Shijō school of Matsumura Goshun, dominated Kyoto painting into the twentieth century and laid the ground for the realism of modern nihonga.

## Key artworks

### Pine Trees in Snow (Sesshō-zu) (late 18th century)

[![Pine Trees in Snow (Sesshō-zu)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Okyo_Pines_right.jpg/1280px-Okyo_Pines_right.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Okyo_Pines_right.jpg)

<sub>Maruyama Ōkyo, Pine Trees in Snow, right screen Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Okyo_Pines_right.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 18th century
- **Type:** Pair of six-panel folding screens, ink and gold on paper
- **Where to see it:** Mitsui Memorial Museum, Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Pine%20Trees%20in%20Snow%20%28Sessh%C5%8D-zu%29%20Maruyama%20%C5%8Ckyo)

Two great pines, one bent and one upright, stand on the two screens against a ground of gold. The snow lying on their branches is simply the white of the paper left blank, while the needles and bark are painted around it in ink with strokes that model form rather than outline it, and a faint sprinkle of gold dust suggests cold light. Ōkyo developed this method from long study of nature and Western-style shading. The screens were painted for the Mitsui merchant family, important patrons of Ōkyo, and are a National Treasure, the defining work of the Maruyama school's naturalism.
