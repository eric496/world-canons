## Biography

Su Shi passed the imperial examinations in 1057 with his brother Su Zhe and began a career in government that was repeatedly interrupted by factional politics. Opposed to Wang Anshi's reforms, he was arrested in 1079 on charges of slandering the emperor in his poems and banished to Huangzhou, where he farmed a plot on an eastern slope (Dongpo), which gave him his literary name. Later he held high office again before further exile to Huizhou and finally to Hainan island. He died in 1101 on his way back north.

Su Shi is among the greatest Chinese poets, and in calligraphy one of the Four Masters of the Song. His Cold Food Observance, written in exile, is regarded as one of the finest works of running script. As a painter he made ink bamboo and withered trees, and as a critic he argued that judging a painting by likeness was the view of a child: painting should express the mind and character of the artist, as poetry does. These ideas became the foundation of literati painting in China, Korea and Japan.

## Key artworks

### Cold Food Observance (Hanshi tie) (c. 1082)

[![Cold Food Observance (Hanshi tie)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Su_Shi-Cold_Food_Observance.jpg/1280px-Su_Shi-Cold_Food_Observance.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Su_Shi-Cold_Food_Observance.jpg)

<sub>Su Shi, Cold Food Observance Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Su_Shi-Cold_Food_Observance.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1082
- **Type:** Calligraphy, ink on paper, handscroll
- **Where to see it:** National Palace Museum, Taipei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Cold%20Food%20Observance%20%28Hanshi%20tie%29%20Su%20Shi)

In 1082, three years into his banishment at Huangzhou, Su Shi wrote two poems on the Cold Food festival, when fires are put out before the Qingming festival. They describe the rain, his poor hut, his cold kitchen, and his despair at being far from the capital and his ancestors' graves. In the writing the characters grow larger and heavier as the emotion intensifies, with some strokes stretched out dramatically. The poet Huang Tingjian added a colophon praising it. The scroll, scorched when the Yuanmingyuan was burned in 1860, was in Japan for some decades before returning to China. It is often called the third greatest work of running script, after Wang Xizhi's Orchid Pavilion and Yan Zhenqing's Requiem to My Nephew.
