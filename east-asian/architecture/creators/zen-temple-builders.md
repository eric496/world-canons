# Muromachi Zen temple builders

*禅宗寺院の工人*

**active 13th – 16th century** · Japanese  
Styles & eras: [Kamakura Buddhist & Zen architecture](../movements/kamakura-zen.md), [Muromachi gardens & shoin](../movements/muromachi-gardens-shoin.md)  

> The carpenters, monks and garden workers who built Japan's Zen monasteries and gardens; this entry gathers works without firmly known designers.

## Biography

Zen monasteries in Kamakura and Kyoto were founded by shōguns and warriors and planned by monks, some of them Chinese, following the monastic layout of Song China. Their gardens were made by monks and by specialist gardeners, including the low-status kawaramono ('riverbank people') such as Zen'ami, who worked for the Ashikaga shōguns.

They introduced the Chinese 'Zen style' of architecture and developed the dry landscape garden, the shoin room and the pavilions of the Kitayama and Higashiyama villas. Many famous gardens have traditional attributions, for instance to Sōami, that cannot be confirmed.

### Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion) (1397; rebuilt 1955)

[![Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Golden_Pavilion_Kinkaku-ji_water_mirror_2024.jpg/960px-Golden_Pavilion_Kinkaku-ji_water_mirror_2024.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Golden_Pavilion_Kinkaku-ji_water_mirror_2024.jpg)

<sub>Kinkaku-ji reflected in its pond Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Golden_Pavilion_Kinkaku-ji_water_mirror_2024.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1397; rebuilt 1955
- **Type:** Pavilion and garden
- **Location:** Kita, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kinkaku-ji%20%28Golden%20Pavilion%29%20Muromachi%20Zen%20temple%20builders)

In 1397 the retired shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu acquired an aristocratic estate in the northern hills of Kyoto and built a villa, which became the Zen temple Rokuon-ji after his death in 1408. Its three-storey pavilion stands at the edge of a pond: the first floor is in the style of an aristocratic palace, the second in the style of a warrior residence and the third in the form of a Zen Buddhist hall with cusped windows, and the upper two storeys are covered in gold leaf. The pavilion, which housed relics of the Buddha, survived until 1950, when a young monk burned it down, an event that inspired Yukio Mishima's novel The Temple of the Golden Pavilion. It was rebuilt in 1955 and its gilding renewed in 1987. The pond garden with its islands and rocks dates largely from Yoshimitsu's time. It is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto World Heritage Site.

### Shariden of Engaku-ji (15th century)

[![Shariden of Engaku-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Engaku-ji_Shariden_1.jpg/1280px-Engaku-ji_Shariden_1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Engaku-ji_Shariden_1.jpg)

<sub>Shariden of Engaku-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Engaku-ji_Shariden_1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 15th century
- **Type:** Zen relic hall
- **Location:** Kamakura, Kanagawa Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Shariden%20of%20Engaku-ji%20Muromachi%20Zen%20temple%20builders)

Engaku-ji was founded in 1282 by the regent Hōjō Tokimune for the Chinese Chan master Wuxue Zuyuan (Mugaku Sogen), in memory of those who died fighting the Mongol invasions. Its relic hall (Shariden), traditionally said to house a tooth of the Buddha, is a small building of the fifteenth century, moved to Engaku-ji from another temple. It is the classic example of the Zenshūyō or 'Zen style' adopted from Song China: a steep thatched roof with sharply upturned corners above a pent roof, closely set bracket sets including between the columns, rafters fanning out at the corners, cusped bell-shaped windows (katōmado) and panelled doors. It is a National Treasure and is usually open to the public only on a few days a year.

### Ginkaku-ji (Silver Pavilion) (1482–1490)

[![Ginkaku-ji (Silver Pavilion)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/06/Ginkakuji_Kyoto03-r.jpg/1280px-Ginkakuji_Kyoto03-r.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ginkakuji_Kyoto03-r.jpg)

<sub>Kannon Hall (Silver Pavilion), Ginkaku-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ginkakuji_Kyoto03-r.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1482–1490
- **Type:** Pavilion and garden
- **Location:** Sakyō, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ginkaku-ji%20%28Silver%20Pavilion%29%20Muromachi%20Zen%20temple%20builders)

The shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimasa, having retired amid the devastation of the Ōnin War, began a villa at the foot of the eastern hills of Kyoto in 1482, modelled on Saihō-ji and his grandfather's Golden Pavilion. It became the Zen temple Jishō-ji after his death in 1490. The two-storey Kannon Hall, completed in 1489, was never covered in silver, despite its popular name; its lower floor is in residential style and its upper floor in the Zen style. The nearby Tōgu-dō, of 1486, contains the Dōjinsai, a small study of four and a half mats with a built-in desk and staggered shelves, the oldest surviving example of the shoin room that became the basis of the formal Japanese interior. Yoshimasa's circle at the villa made it a centre of Higashiyama culture: tea, ink painting, flower arrangement and Noh. The raked sand terrace and cone in the garden were added in the Edo period.

### Rock Garden of Ryōan-ji (late 15th century (debated))

[![Rock Garden of Ryōan-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5c/Kare-sansui_zen_garden%2C_Ry%C5%8Dan-ji%2C_Kyoto_20190416_1.jpg/1280px-Kare-sansui_zen_garden%2C_Ry%C5%8Dan-ji%2C_Kyoto_20190416_1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kare-sansui_zen_garden%2C_Ry%C5%8Dan-ji%2C_Kyoto_20190416_1.jpg)

<sub>Rock garden of Ryōan-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kare-sansui_zen_garden%2C_Ry%C5%8Dan-ji%2C_Kyoto_20190416_1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 15th century (debated)
- **Type:** Dry landscape garden
- **Location:** Ukyō, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rock%20Garden%20of%20Ry%C5%8Dan-ji%20Muromachi%20Zen%20temple%20builders)

Ryōan-ji was founded in 1450 by the warrior Hosokawa Katsumoto and rebuilt after the Ōnin War in 1499. In front of its abbot's quarters lies a rectangular courtyard enclosed on two sides by an earthen wall, in which fifteen stones, set in five groups with moss at their bases, rest on a bed of white gravel raked in lines. From any point on the viewing veranda at least one stone is hidden. The garden's date, designer and meaning are unknown. It is often attributed to the painter Sōami, but this is not documented, and the names of two workmen are carved on one stone; scholars have proposed dates from the late fifteenth to the seventeenth century. Interpretations range from islands in the sea to a tigress with cubs. Little noticed before the twentieth century, it became a modern emblem of Zen and of abstract composition. It is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto World Heritage Site.

### Daisen-in (1509–1513)

[![Daisen-in](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Daisen-in1.jpg/1280px-Daisen-in1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daisen-in1.jpg)

<sub>Garden of Daisen-in Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daisen-in1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1509–1513
- **Type:** Zen subtemple and garden
- **Location:** Daitoku-ji, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Daisen-in%20Muromachi%20Zen%20temple%20builders)

Daisen-in, a subtemple of the great Zen monastery of Daitoku-ji, was founded in 1509 by the monk Kogaku Sōkō, and its abbot's quarters, of 1513, are among the oldest surviving buildings of their type. The narrow garden wrapped around the building's north-east corner is a celebrated dry landscape: tall upright stones and clipped shrubs represent mountains and a waterfall, white gravel a river that flows under a stone bridge and past a stone 'treasure ship', widening into a sea of raked gravel on the south side. Its compression of a whole landscape into a few square metres recalls Chinese ink landscape painting. The designer is unknown, though the garden is sometimes attributed to Kogaku Sōkō himself or to Sōami. The main hall is a National Treasure.
