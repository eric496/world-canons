# Builders of the Three Kingdoms of Korea

*삼국시대 장인*

**active 4th – 7th century** · Korean  
Styles & eras: [Three Kingdoms, Silla & Goryeo Buddhist architecture](../movements/korean-buddhist.md)  

> The builders and craftsmen of Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla, whose temples and tombs are known mainly from stone remains.

## Biography

The three Korean kingdoms adopted Buddhism from the fourth century and built royal tombs, palaces and great temples, with craftsmen trained in Chinese methods and developing distinctive local styles. Baekje builders were so renowned that they were sent to Japan and to Silla to build temples, and a single name, Abiji, survives in the historical record.

Because their timber buildings were all destroyed, their work is known from stone pagodas, foundations, tiles and the murals of Goguryeo tombs. Through Japan's first temples, built by Baekje craftsmen, their influence extended across East Asia.

### Goguryeo Tombs (4th – 7th century)

[![Goguryeo Tombs](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/Tomb_of_King_Tongmyong.jpg/1280px-Tomb_of_King_Tongmyong.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tomb_of_King_Tongmyong.jpg)

<sub>Tomb attributed to King Tongmyong (Dongmyeong), near Pyongyang Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tomb_of_King_Tongmyong.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 4th – 7th century
- **Type:** Royal and noble tombs
- **Location:** Pyongyang and Nampo region, North Korea; Ji'an, Jilin, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Goguryeo%20Tombs%20Builders%20of%20the%20Three%20Kingdoms%20of%20Korea)

Goguryeo, the northernmost of the Three Kingdoms of Korea, ruled a territory spanning present-day northern Korea and north-east China until 668. Its kings and nobles were buried in tombs of two main types: stepped pyramids of cut stone, such as the so-called General's Tomb near Ji'an, and earth mounds over stone chambers. Many chambers have corbelled 'lantern' ceilings and walls painted with hunting scenes, processions, houses, stars and the guardian animals of the four directions, which are valuable evidence for the lost timber architecture of the period. The tomb attributed to King Dongmyeong, the kingdom's legendary founder, near Pyongyang was heavily restored in the 1990s. The tombs in North Korea and in China were inscribed on the World Heritage List in 2004.

### Five-storey Stone Pagoda of Jeongnimsa (6th – 7th century)

[![Five-storey Stone Pagoda of Jeongnimsa](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ea/%EC%98%A4%EC%B8%B5%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%EA%B3%BC_%EC%A0%95%EB%A6%BC%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%A0%84%EA%B2%BD2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%EC%98%A4%EC%B8%B5%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%EA%B3%BC_%EC%A0%95%EB%A6%BC%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%A0%84%EA%B2%BD2.jpg)

<sub>Five-storey stone pagoda and site of Jeongnimsa Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%EC%98%A4%EC%B8%B5%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%EA%B3%BC_%EC%A0%95%EB%A6%BC%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%A0%84%EA%B2%BD2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 6th – 7th century
- **Type:** Stone pagoda
- **Location:** Buyeo, South Chungcheong Province, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Five-storey%20Stone%20Pagoda%20of%20Jeongnimsa%20Builders%20of%20the%20Three%20Kingdoms%20of%20Korea)

Jeongnimsa was the central temple of Sabi (present-day Buyeo), the last capital of Baekje from 538 to 660. Its five-storey granite pagoda, about 8.3 metres high, has slender proportions and thin, broad eaves that turn up slightly at the corners, a refined translation of timber forms into stone. When the Tang and Silla armies conquered Baekje in 660, the Tang general carved a victory inscription on its base, which led to its being misnamed for centuries as a Tang monument. It is one of only two surviving Baekje pagodas, with that of Mireuksa, and is part of the Baekje Historic Areas World Heritage Site.

### Stone Pagoda of Mireuksa (639)

[![Stone Pagoda of Mireuksa](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/%EC%9D%B5%EC%82%B0_%EB%AF%B8%EB%A5%B5%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%282019%EB%85%84%29_%EC%95%BC%EA%B2%BD.jpg/1280px-%EC%9D%B5%EC%82%B0_%EB%AF%B8%EB%A5%B5%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%282019%EB%85%84%29_%EC%95%BC%EA%B2%BD.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%EC%9D%B5%EC%82%B0_%EB%AF%B8%EB%A5%B5%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%282019%EB%85%84%29_%EC%95%BC%EA%B2%BD.jpg)

<sub>Stone pagoda of Mireuksa, Iksan Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%EC%9D%B5%EC%82%B0_%EB%AF%B8%EB%A5%B5%EC%82%AC%EC%A7%80_%EC%84%9D%ED%83%91%282019%EB%85%84%29_%EC%95%BC%EA%B2%BD.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 639
- **Type:** Stone pagoda
- **Location:** Iksan, North Jeolla Province, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Stone%20Pagoda%20of%20Mireuksa%20Builders%20of%20the%20Three%20Kingdoms%20of%20Korea)

Mireuksa was the largest Buddhist temple of the Baekje kingdom, founded by King Mu in the early seventh century with three precincts, each with a pagoda and hall, dedicated to the future Buddha Maitreya. The western pagoda, of granite, imitates the forms of a timber pagoda, with corner posts, beams and flaring eaves cut in stone. A reliquary found during restoration in 2009, with an inscription dated 639, revealed that it was commissioned by a Baekje queen. By modern times only six storeys of one side survived, propped up with concrete by Japanese restorers in 1915. After a dismantling and reconstruction lasting from 2001 to 2018, it again stands about 14.5 metres high. It is part of the Baekje Historic Areas World Heritage Site.
