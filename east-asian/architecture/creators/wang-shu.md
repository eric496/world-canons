# Wang Shu

*王澍*

**1963** · Chinese  
Born: Ürümqi, Xinjiang · Died: ?  
Styles & eras: [Contemporary architecture](../movements/east-asian-contemporary-arch.md)  

> Chinese architect, Pritzker Prize winner in 2012, who builds with recycled tiles and bricks and the craft of traditional builders.

## Biography

Wang Shu studied architecture in Nanjing and at Tongji University in Shanghai, and in the early 1990s spent years working with craftsmen on building sites rather than designing. In 1997 he founded the Amateur Architecture Studio in Hangzhou with his wife, the architect Lu Wenyu. He heads the school of architecture at the China Academy of Art, whose Xiangshan campus in Hangzhou he designed in the 2000s. In 2012 he became the first Chinese citizen to win the Pritzker Prize.

Wang's work is a response to the demolition of old Chinese towns during the building boom. He reuses millions of salvaged bricks and tiles in a technique derived from the wapan walls of Zhejiang villages, and draws on the composition of Chinese landscape painting and gardens. The Ningbo Museum and the Xiangshan campus are his best-known buildings; with Lu Wenyu he has also worked on the regeneration of villages.

### Xiangshan Campus, China Academy of Art (2002–2007)

- **Date:** 2002–2007
- **Type:** University campus
- **Location:** Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Xiangshan%20Campus%2C%20China%20Academy%20of%20Art%20Wang%20Shu)

For the new campus of the China Academy of Art at Xiangshan, on the outskirts of Hangzhou, Wang Shu and Lu Wenyu designed a group of buildings in two phases between 2002 and 2007. Instead of a monumental plan, they arranged the buildings informally around a small hill, keeping fields, ponds and streams between them as in a village. More than seven million old tiles and bricks salvaged from demolished buildings in the region were used for walls and roofs, together with bamboo, timber and rammed earth, and the roofs and walkways were shaped with reference to Chinese landscape painting. The campus became the main manifesto of Wang's idea of an architecture rooted in craft and local memory, and he heads the architecture school housed there.

### Ningbo Museum (2003–2008)

[![Ningbo Museum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/South_Gate_of_Ningbo_Museum.jpg/1280px-South_Gate_of_Ningbo_Museum.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:South_Gate_of_Ningbo_Museum.jpg)

<sub>South gate of Ningbo Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:South_Gate_of_Ningbo_Museum.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 2003–2008
- **Type:** Museum
- **Location:** Yinzhou, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ningbo%20Museum%20Wang%20Shu)

The Ningbo Museum (Ningbo History Museum) was built in a new district of the city where the old villages had been cleared. Wang Shu and Lu Wenyu of Amateur Architecture Studio designed it as a massive block that splits at the top into a fractured, mountain-like silhouette, with irregular openings and inner courtyards and valleys. Its walls are built partly in the wapan technique of local builders, with reused bricks and tiles of many sizes and dates laid in a dense, varied mosaic, and partly in concrete cast in bamboo formwork. Some two million salvaged pieces were used. Opened in 2008, the museum was cited when Wang received the Pritzker Prize in 2012 as an example of his concern for memory and craft in a period of rapid destruction.
