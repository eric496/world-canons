# Joseon royal builders

*조선 왕실 장인*

**active 1392–1910** · Korean  
Styles & eras: [Joseon palaces & seowon](../movements/joseon-palaces-seowon.md)  

> The officials and craftsmen who built the palaces, shrines and walls of the Joseon capital Seoul.

## Biography

The Joseon court organised construction through government offices that recorded each project in illustrated reports called uigwe, which list materials, costs and the names of craftsmen. The overall plans of the capital were set by scholar-officials such as Jeong Do-jeon, following Confucian ritual texts and geomancy.

Their palaces and shrines are restrained, carefully fitted to the terrain and decorated with painted dancheong patterns. Most were rebuilt after the Japanese invasions of the 1590s, and many again in the nineteenth century and after the damage of colonial rule and war, often with the help of the uigwe records.

### Gyeongbokgung (1395; rebuilt 1867)

[![Gyeongbokgung](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Front_view_of_the_Imperial_Throne_Hall_Geunjeongjeon_at_Gyeongbokgung_Palace_with_blue_sky_in_Seoul.jpg/1280px-Front_view_of_the_Imperial_Throne_Hall_Geunjeongjeon_at_Gyeongbokgung_Palace_with_blue_sky_in_Seoul.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Front_view_of_the_Imperial_Throne_Hall_Geunjeongjeon_at_Gyeongbokgung_Palace_with_blue_sky_in_Seoul.jpg)

<sub>Geunjeongjeon throne hall, Gyeongbokgung Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Front_view_of_the_Imperial_Throne_Hall_Geunjeongjeon_at_Gyeongbokgung_Palace_with_blue_sky_in_Seoul.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1395; rebuilt 1867
- **Type:** Royal palace
- **Location:** Jongno, Seoul, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gyeongbokgung%20Joseon%20royal%20builders)

After founding the Joseon dynasty, King Taejo moved the capital to Hanyang (Seoul) and in 1395 completed Gyeongbokgung, the 'Palace of Shining Blessings', whose halls were named by the scholar-official Jeong Do-jeon. Laid out to the north of the city on a site chosen by geomancy, with Mount Bugak behind it, it was planned along a north–south axis from the Gwanghwamun gate through courts to the throne hall Geunjeongjeon. The palace burned during the Japanese invasion of 1592 and lay in ruins for over two centuries until the regent Heungseon Daewongun rebuilt it in 1867. Under Japanese rule much of it was demolished and the Government-General building erected in front of the throne hall. That building was removed in 1995–96, and restoration of the palace has continued since 1990. The two-storey Geunjeongjeon and the Gyeonghoeru banquet pavilion on its lotus pond are among the finest Joseon buildings.

### Jongmyo Shrine (1395; rebuilt 1608, enlarged 18th–19th century)

[![Jongmyo Shrine](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Jeongjeon%2C_Jongmyo_%28Autumn%2C_2012%29.jpg/1280px-Jeongjeon%2C_Jongmyo_%28Autumn%2C_2012%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jeongjeon%2C_Jongmyo_%28Autumn%2C_2012%29.jpg)

<sub>Jeongjeon hall of Jongmyo Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jeongjeon%2C_Jongmyo_%28Autumn%2C_2012%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1395; rebuilt 1608, enlarged 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Royal ancestral shrine
- **Location:** Jongno, Seoul, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jongmyo%20Shrine%20Joseon%20royal%20builders)

Jongmyo was built in 1395 to hold the spirit tablets of the Joseon kings and queens, following the Confucian principle that the royal ancestral shrine should stand to the east of the main palace. Burned in 1592, it was rebuilt in 1608, and the main hall, Jeongjeon, was lengthened several times as tablets accumulated, reaching nineteen chambers and a length of about 101 metres; a second hall, Yeongnyeongjeon, holds further tablets. The halls stand on a vast stone terrace, austere and almost unadorned, so that the long, low roof dominates the courtyard. The Jongmyo Jerye ancestral rite, with its court music, is still performed every May. The shrine became a World Heritage Site in 1995, and the rite and music are inscribed as intangible heritage.

### Sungnyemun (Namdaemun) (1398; rebuilt 2008–2013)

[![Sungnyemun (Namdaemun)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Sungnyemun_Gate%2C_front%2C_2013.jpg/1280px-Sungnyemun_Gate%2C_front%2C_2013.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sungnyemun_Gate%2C_front%2C_2013.jpg)

<sub>Sungnyemun after its reconstruction, 2013 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sungnyemun_Gate%2C_front%2C_2013.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1398; rebuilt 2008–2013
- **Type:** City gate
- **Location:** Jung, Seoul, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sungnyemun%20%28Namdaemun%29%20Joseon%20royal%20builders)

Sungnyemun was the main southern gate of the walls of Hanyang, completed in 1398 and rebuilt in 1448. A two-storey timber gate tower under a hipped and gabled roof stands on a massive stone platform with an arched passage. It was the oldest timber structure in Seoul and was designated National Treasure No. 1 in 1962. In February 2008 an arsonist set fire to it and the upper structure was largely destroyed. It was rebuilt by 2013 using traditional tools and methods, including hand-forged nails and roof tiles, and short sections of the city wall on either side were restored. The reconstruction was criticised for some errors, but the loss led to a national debate on heritage protection.

### Changdeokgung and its Rear Garden (1405; rebuilt from 1610)

[![Changdeokgung and its Rear Garden](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Biwon_Huwon_of_Changdeokgung.jpg/1280px-Biwon_Huwon_of_Changdeokgung.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Biwon_Huwon_of_Changdeokgung.jpg)

<sub>The Rear Garden (Huwon) of Changdeokgung Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Biwon_Huwon_of_Changdeokgung.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1405; rebuilt from 1610
- **Type:** Royal palace and garden
- **Location:** Jongno, Seoul, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Changdeokgung%20and%20its%20Rear%20Garden%20Joseon%20royal%20builders)

Changdeokgung was built in 1405 as a secondary palace, and after the destruction of all of Seoul's palaces in 1592 it was the first to be rebuilt, from 1610, serving as the main royal residence for some 270 years. Unlike Gyeongbokgung's formal axis, its buildings are arranged freely to fit the foothills of Mount Bugak. Behind them lies the Rear Garden (Huwon), often called the Secret Garden, which spreads over wooded valleys with ponds, pavilions and halls for study and leisure, such as the Buyongji pond with the Juhamnu library pavilion built under King Jeongjo. The garden shows the Korean preference for working with natural terrain. The palace was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1997.
