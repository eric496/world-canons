# Chinese Buddhist cave and pagoda builders

*佛教石窟與佛塔工匠*

**active 4th – 11th century** · Chinese  
Styles & eras: [Buddhist cave temples & early pagodas](../movements/buddhist-caves-pagodas.md)  

> The monks, donors, sculptors and masons who carved China's cave temples and built its brick and stone pagodas; this entry gathers works without a known designer.

## Biography

Buddhist cave temples and pagodas were commissioned by emperors, officials, monks and lay societies, whose names often survive in dedicatory inscriptions, while the stone carvers, painters and masons who made them are rarely recorded. Workshops moved along the Silk Road and between capitals, carrying styles from Central Asia into China.

These builders adapted the Indian rock-cut sanctuary and the stupa to Chinese conditions, creating the multi-storey pagoda in timber, brick and stone and imitating timber forms in stone and brick. Their works preserve the best evidence for early Chinese architecture, since so few early timber buildings survive.

### Mogao Caves (366 – 14th century)

[![Mogao Caves](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cc/Jiucenglou_of_Mogao_Caves.jpg/1280px-Jiucenglou_of_Mogao_Caves.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jiucenglou_of_Mogao_Caves.jpg)

<sub>The nine-storey facade (Jiucenglou) of the Mogao Caves Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jiucenglou_of_Mogao_Caves.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 366 – 14th century
- **Type:** Buddhist cave temples
- **Location:** Dunhuang, Gansu, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mogao%20Caves%20Chinese%20Buddhist%20cave%20and%20pagoda%20builders)

According to an inscription, the monk Lezun began carving the first cave into the cliff south-east of Dunhuang in 366. Over the next thousand years monks, merchants and local rulers commissioned hundreds of caves, of which 492 decorated shrines survive, together with more than 2,000 painted sculptures and some 45,000 square metres of murals. The caves range from small meditation cells to great halls with a central pillar representing a stupa and colossal Buddhas housed behind multi-storey timber facades, such as the nine-storey Jiucenglou. The murals preserve images of Tang palaces, temples and cities that no longer exist. In 1900 a sealed library cave was found containing tens of thousands of manuscripts, many later removed by foreign expeditions. The site, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987, is managed by the Dunhuang Academy, which limits visits to protect the fragile paintings.

### Yungang Grottoes (c. 460–525)

[![Yungang Grottoes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/90/61292-Yungang-Grottoes_%2828498548881%29.jpg/1280px-61292-Yungang-Grottoes_%2828498548881%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:61292-Yungang-Grottoes_%2828498548881%29.jpg)

<sub>Yungang Grottoes, Datong Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:61292-Yungang-Grottoes_%2828498548881%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 460–525
- **Type:** Buddhist cave temples
- **Location:** Datong, Shanxi, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yungang%20Grottoes%20Chinese%20Buddhist%20cave%20and%20pagoda%20builders)

The Northern Wei, a dynasty of Xianbei origin, made Buddhism a state religion, and around 460 the monk Tanyao began carving five great caves, each with a colossal Buddha said to represent one of the dynasty's emperors, into the sandstone cliff near the capital Pingcheng, present-day Datong. Over the following decades some 250 caves and niches with more than 50,000 statues were cut along a kilometre of cliff. The early figures show strong influence from Gandhara and Central Asia, while later caves have carved imitations of timber buildings, with bracketed eaves and pagodas, which are among the best evidence for the architecture of the period. Work slowed after the court moved to Luoyang in 494. The grottoes became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001.

### Longmen Grottoes (c. 493 – 10th century)

[![Longmen Grottoes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b4/27427-Luoyang_%2849067744628%29.jpg/1280px-27427-Luoyang_%2849067744628%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:27427-Luoyang_%2849067744628%29.jpg)

<sub>Longmen Grottoes, Luoyang Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:27427-Luoyang_%2849067744628%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 493 – 10th century
- **Type:** Buddhist cave temples
- **Location:** Luoyang, Henan, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Longmen%20Grottoes%20Chinese%20Buddhist%20cave%20and%20pagoda%20builders)

After the Northern Wei moved their capital to Luoyang in 494, carving began in the limestone cliffs on both banks of the Yi River south of the city. Work continued through the Sui and Tang dynasties, producing about 2,300 caves and niches and over 100,000 statues, with thousands of inscriptions that are a major source for the history of Chinese calligraphy. The most famous ensemble, the open-air Fengxian Temple completed in 675, centres on a Vairocana Buddha about 17 metres high, traditionally associated with the patronage of Empress Wu Zetian. Holes in the rock show that some groups were once sheltered by timber roofs. Longmen marks the shift from the elongated Northern Wei style to the full, rounded Tang ideal. It was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2000.

### Songyue Pagoda (c. 523)

[![Songyue Pagoda](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e1/Pagoda_of_Songyue_Temple%2C_2015-09-25_20.jpg/960px-Pagoda_of_Songyue_Temple%2C_2015-09-25_20.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pagoda_of_Songyue_Temple%2C_2015-09-25_20.jpg)

<sub>Songyue Pagoda, Dengfeng Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pagoda_of_Songyue_Temple%2C_2015-09-25_20.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 523
- **Type:** Brick pagoda
- **Location:** Mount Song, Dengfeng, Henan, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Songyue%20Pagoda%20Chinese%20Buddhist%20cave%20and%20pagoda%20builders)

The Songyue Pagoda stands in a temple on Mount Song, one of China's sacred mountains, and was built around 523 under the Northern Wei. Of yellow brick bonded with clay, it rises about 37 metres from a tall base storey to fifteen closely spaced eaves that curve inward to a pointed spire, giving it a bullet-like profile. Its twelve-sided plan is unique among Chinese pagodas. The doorways and niches of the lower storey carry carved arches and lion motifs that show the Indian roots of the form. As the oldest standing brick pagoda in China, it proves how quickly Chinese builders adapted the stupa into a tower of many eaves. It forms part of the World Heritage Site of the historic monuments of Dengfeng.

### Giant Wild Goose Pagoda (652; rebuilt 704)

[![Giant Wild Goose Pagoda](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/Giant_Wild_Goose_Pagoda.jpg/1280px-Giant_Wild_Goose_Pagoda.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Giant_Wild_Goose_Pagoda.jpg)

<sub>Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, Xi'an Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Giant_Wild_Goose_Pagoda.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 652; rebuilt 704
- **Type:** Brick pagoda
- **Location:** Da Ci'en Temple, Xi'an, Shaanxi, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Giant%20Wild%20Goose%20Pagoda%20Chinese%20Buddhist%20cave%20and%20pagoda%20builders)

The pilgrim monk Xuanzang returned to Chang'an in 645 after sixteen years in India with hundreds of Buddhist texts, which he translated at the Da Ci'en Temple. In 652 a pagoda was built there to store them, reportedly following Xuanzang's own wishes and Indian models. That first structure of rammed earth faced with brick soon decayed, and it was rebuilt under Empress Wu Zetian around 704. The present square tower, about 64 metres high, has seven storeys of diminishing size, its brick walls modelled with flat pilasters and brackets in imitation of timber construction. Its steady, massive outline became an emblem of Xi'an. The outer brick casing was renewed in the Ming dynasty. It is part of the Silk Roads World Heritage Site.

### Small Wild Goose Pagoda (707–709)

[![Small Wild Goose Pagoda](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/43/Xi%27anwildgoosepagoda2.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xi%27anwildgoosepagoda2.JPG)

<sub>Small Wild Goose Pagoda, Xi'an Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xi%27anwildgoosepagoda2.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 707–709
- **Type:** Brick pagoda
- **Location:** Jianfu Temple, Xi'an, Shaanxi, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Small%20Wild%20Goose%20Pagoda%20Chinese%20Buddhist%20cave%20and%20pagoda%20builders)

The Small Wild Goose Pagoda was built between 707 and 709 at the Jianfu Temple in Chang'an, where the monk Yijing translated sutras he had brought back from India by sea. Unlike the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, it is of the multi-eaved type, with fifteen originally (now thirteen) closely spaced brick eaves on a square plan, rising in a graceful convex curve. Its height today is about 43 metres; the top storeys were lost in an earthquake in 1556. Tradition records that earlier quakes split the tower open and later closed it again, and its foundation of rammed earth shaped like a hemisphere is thought to have helped it survive. It is among the best preserved Tang brick buildings and part of the Silk Roads World Heritage Site.
