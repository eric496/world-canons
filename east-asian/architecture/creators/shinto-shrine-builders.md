# Shinto shrine carpenters

*宮大工*

**active 7th century – present** · Japanese  
Styles & eras: [Shinto shrine architecture](../movements/shinto-shrines.md)  

> The shrine carpenters (miya-daiku) and priestly communities who have built and periodically rebuilt Japan's Shinto shrines.

## Biography

The great shrines of Japan were built by carpenters attached to shrine and court, and their designs were preserved less in drawings than in the practice of periodic rebuilding. At Ise, every twenty years, the carpenters build new sanctuaries in the same form beside the old, a cycle that takes years of preparation, from cutting cypress trees to the transfer of the deity.

These rebuildings transmit the skills and the ancient forms of Japanese architecture from one generation to the next. Their work raises the question, often debated by modern architects and conservators, of whether authenticity lies in old material or in a continuously renewed form.

### Ise Grand Shrine (form from late 7th century; rebuilt every 20 years, most recently 2013)

[![Ise Grand Shrine](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/Naiku_04.jpg/1280px-Naiku_04.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Naiku_04.jpg)

<sub>The Inner Shrine (Naikū), Ise Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Naiku_04.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** form from late 7th century; rebuilt every 20 years, most recently 2013
- **Type:** Shinto shrine
- **Location:** Ise, Mie Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ise%20Grand%20Shrine%20Shinto%20shrine%20carpenters)

Ise Jingū consists of two main shrines, the Inner Shrine (Naikū) dedicated to the sun goddess Amaterasu, ancestor of the imperial line, and the Outer Shrine (Gekū) dedicated to Toyouke, goddess of food, together with more than a hundred smaller shrines. The main sanctuaries are built in the shinmei-zukuri style: raised floors, unpainted cypress, thatched gable roofs crowned by crossed finials (chigi) and short logs (katsuogi), and freestanding posts carrying the ridge, forms that recall ancient granaries. Every twenty years, in the ceremony of shikinen sengū, the sanctuaries and many other buildings are rebuilt on an adjacent plot and the old ones dismantled, a tradition dating back, according to shrine records, to 690. The sixty-second rebuilding took place in 2013. The practice preserves not the material but the form and the skills of carpenters, and it led modern architects such as Bruno Taut and Kenzō Tange to see Ise as an archetype of Japanese architecture. The innermost precincts may not be entered by the public.

### Itsukushima Shrine (1168; rebuilt 13th–16th century; torii 1875)

[![Itsukushima Shrine](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ef/Itsukushima_Shrine_Torii_Gate_%2813890465459%29.jpg/1280px-Itsukushima_Shrine_Torii_Gate_%2813890465459%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Itsukushima_Shrine_Torii_Gate_%2813890465459%29.jpg)

<sub>The great torii of Itsukushima Shrine Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Itsukushima_Shrine_Torii_Gate_%2813890465459%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1168; rebuilt 13th–16th century; torii 1875
- **Type:** Shinto shrine
- **Location:** Miyajima, Hatsukaichi, Hiroshima Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Itsukushima%20Shrine%20Shinto%20shrine%20carpenters)

The island of Itsukushima (Miyajima) in the Inland Sea was considered sacred, and a shrine to three sea goddesses is recorded there from the sixth century. Its present plan was created in 1168 by Taira no Kiyomori, the dominant warrior statesman of the age, who made it his family shrine. The halls, painted vermilion and linked by long corridors, stand on piers over the tidal flats, so that at high tide the shrine seems to float, and its arrangement echoes the shinden-zukuri palace style of the Heian aristocracy. The buildings were rebuilt after fires in the thirteenth century and later, and include a Noh stage. The great torii in the sea, about 16 metres high, dates in its present form from 1875. The shrine became a World Heritage Site in 1996 and is one of Japan's most famous views.

### Izumo Taisha (main hall 1744; origins ancient)

[![Izumo Taisha](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Izumo-taisha14bs4592.jpg/1280px-Izumo-taisha14bs4592.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Izumo-taisha14bs4592.jpg)

<sub>Izumo Taisha Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Izumo-taisha14bs4592.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** main hall 1744; origins ancient
- **Type:** Shinto shrine
- **Location:** Izumo, Shimane Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Izumo%20Taisha%20Shinto%20shrine%20carpenters)

Izumo Taisha is dedicated to Ōkuninushi, a god of the land, of marriage and good relations, and appears in the eighth-century chronicles Kojiki and Nihon Shoki. Its main hall (honden) is built in the taisha-zukuri style, regarded as one of the oldest forms of shrine architecture: a square plan of two by two bays with a central pillar, an entrance at one side of the gable end and a steep, curved roof of cypress bark. The present hall, of 1744, is about 24 metres tall, the largest of its kind, but shrine legends describe much taller halls of the past, raised on long stilts. In 2000 excavations found the bases of giant columns, each made of three tree trunks bound together, from a thirteenth-century rebuilding, lending weight to these traditions. The main hall is periodically re-roofed, most recently in 2013, and is a National Treasure.
