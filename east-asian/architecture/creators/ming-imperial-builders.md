# Ming imperial builders

*明代宮廷工匠*

**active 1368–1644** · Chinese  
Styles & eras: [Ming–Qing imperial architecture](../movements/ming-qing-imperial.md)  

> The official designers, craftsmen and labourers who built the palaces, altars and tombs of the Ming court.

## Biography

Ming imperial construction was directed by the Ministry of Works and eunuch-run offices, which mobilised craftsmen from across the empire, many from the Suzhou region, together with soldiers and conscripted labourers. Timber was brought from the forests of the south-west, bricks fired at specified kilns and stamped with makers' names, and stone quarried near Beijing.

A few master craftsmen such as Kuai Xiang are named in records, but most of the designers are anonymous. Their buildings follow strict conventions of rank, colour and number, and they established the form of Beijing that the Qing inherited.

### Ming Tombs (1409–1644)

[![Ming Tombs](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Thirteen_tombs_entance_dagong_gate201909.jpg/1280px-Thirteen_tombs_entance_dagong_gate201909.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Thirteen_tombs_entance_dagong_gate201909.jpg)

<sub>Great Red Gate at the entrance to the Ming Tombs Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Thirteen_tombs_entance_dagong_gate201909.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1409–1644
- **Type:** Imperial necropolis
- **Location:** Changping, Beijing, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ming%20Tombs%20Ming%20imperial%20builders)

In 1409 the Yongle Emperor chose a valley north of Beijing, sheltered by mountains on three sides according to principles of geomancy, for his tomb, and thirteen of the sixteen Ming emperors were later buried there. A single approach serves the whole necropolis: a stone archway of 1540, a great red gate and a Spirit Way lined with pairs of stone animals and officials. Each tomb follows the same plan of a sacrificial hall in front of a walled, tree-covered burial mound. Yongle's Changling has the largest surviving hall, the Hall of Eminent Favour, supported by huge columns of single nanmu tree trunks. The underground palace of the Wanli Emperor's Dingling was excavated in the 1950s, but poor conservation damaged its contents, and no further tombs have been opened. The Ming and Qing imperial tombs are a World Heritage Site.

### Temple of Heaven (1420; Hall of Prayer rebuilt 1889)

[![Temple of Heaven](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Temple_of_Heaven_20160323_01.jpg/1280px-Temple_of_Heaven_20160323_01.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Temple_of_Heaven_20160323_01.jpg)

<sub>Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, Temple of Heaven Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Temple_of_Heaven_20160323_01.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1420; Hall of Prayer rebuilt 1889
- **Type:** Imperial altar complex
- **Location:** Beijing, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Temple%20of%20Heaven%20Ming%20imperial%20builders)

The Temple of Heaven was built in the same years as the Forbidden City, south-east of it in the outer city of Beijing, and was enlarged in the sixteenth century. Its precinct, larger than the palace, is enclosed by a wall rounded at the north and square at the south, reflecting the ancient idea of a round Heaven over a square Earth. Along a raised causeway lie the open-air Circular Mound Altar, of three white marble tiers where the emperor sacrificed at the winter solstice, the Imperial Vault of Heaven, and the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, a round hall with a triple roof of blue tiles on a three-tiered marble terrace. Its timber frame of concentric rings of columns symbolises the seasons, months and hours. The hall burned after a lightning strike in 1889 and was rebuilt to the old design. The complex is a World Heritage Site and the supreme expression of Chinese ritual architecture.
