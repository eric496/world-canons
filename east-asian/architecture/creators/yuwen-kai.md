# Yuwen Kai

*宇文愷*

**555 – 612** · Chinese (Sui)  
Styles & eras: [Tang & Liao timber halls](../movements/tang-liao-timber.md)  

> Sui dynasty engineer and city planner who laid out the new capital Daxing, later Tang Chang'an, the largest planned city of its age.

## Biography

Yuwen Kai came from an aristocratic family of Xianbei descent that had served the Northern Zhou, and entered the service of the new Sui dynasty when it reunified China in 581. The following year Emperor Wen commissioned him to plan a new capital south-east of the old Han city of Chang'an. The result, Daxing, was a vast walled rectangle of about 84 square kilometres. Under Emperor Yang he went on to plan the eastern capital at Luoyang, worked on canals, and produced designs for imperial ritual buildings and portable palaces.

Yuwen Kai's Daxing was a grid of 108 walled wards around broad avenues, with the palace and government city at the north and markets to east and west. Renamed Chang'an by the Tang, it became the cosmopolitan metropolis of the Silk Road and the model for capitals across East Asia, including Heijō-kyō (Nara) and Heian-kyō (Kyoto) in Japan. None of his buildings survives above ground, but excavations have confirmed the regularity of his plan.

## Key buildings & sites

### Daxing (Sui–Tang Chang'an) (582–583)

- **Date:** 582–583
- **Type:** Planned capital city
- **Location:** Xi'an, Shaanxi, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Daxing%20%28Sui%E2%80%93Tang%20Chang%27an%29%20Yuwen%20Kai)

In 582 Emperor Wen of the Sui ordered a new capital to be built south-east of the ruined Han city of Chang'an, and Yuwen Kai planned it with remarkable speed; the walls and palace were occupied the following year. Named Daxing by the Sui and Chang'an by the Tang, the city formed a rectangle about 9.7 by 8.6 kilometres enclosed by rammed-earth walls. The palace and the government city lay at the north centre; a broad avenue some 150 metres wide ran south from them to the main gate, and the rest of the city was divided into walled residential wards and two great markets. With perhaps a million inhabitants under the Tang, it was the largest city in the world and a meeting place of merchants, monks and envoys from across Asia. The plan was copied in Japan at Nara and Kyoto. The city was largely destroyed at the end of the Tang, and little survives above ground apart from its pagodas; its gate towers are known from tomb murals.
