## Biography

Toshihito was a younger brother of Emperor Go-Yōzei. As a boy he was adopted by Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who had no heir at the time, but the adoption was dissolved when Hideyoshi's son was born, and he was given a new princely house, the Hachijō-no-miya. He was a leading scholar of the Kokinshū and received its secret teachings, and he devoted himself to poetry, tea and the arts. Around 1615 he began a villa on the Katsura River west of Kyoto, an area associated with the Tale of Genji.

Toshihito built the Old Shoin and the first gardens; his son Prince Toshitada (1619–1662) restored and greatly enlarged the estate from the 1640s, adding the Middle Shoin, the New Palace and tea houses. The design, a stroll garden around a pond with buildings in the sukiya style, is traditionally credited to Kobori Enshū, but it is now generally seen as the work of the princes themselves with their craftsmen.

## Key buildings & sites

### Katsura Imperial Villa (c. 1615–1660s)

[![Katsura Imperial Villa](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Katsura_Rikyu_%283264799678%29.jpg/1280px-Katsura_Rikyu_%283264799678%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Katsura_Rikyu_%283264799678%29.jpg)

<sub>Katsura Imperial Villa Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Katsura_Rikyu_%283264799678%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1615–1660s
- **Type:** Villa and stroll garden
- **Location:** Nishikyō, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Katsura%20Imperial%20Villa%20Prince%20Hachij%C5%8D%20Toshihito)

Prince Hachijō Toshihito began the villa around 1615 on land associated with the Tale of Genji, and his son Prince Toshitada enlarged it from the 1640s, so that the main building is a staggered line of three shoin linked diagonally: the Old Shoin with its bamboo moon-viewing platform, the Middle Shoin and the New Palace. Around a pond with islands, a stroll garden leads past tea houses such as the Shōkintei, each revealing a new view. The buildings combine the formal shoin style with the rustic materials and asymmetry of the tea house: unpainted wood, paper screens, tatami and refined but simple details. The design was long credited to Kobori Enshū, but this is now considered a legend. In 1933 the German architect Bruno Taut praised it as a model of functional modern design, and Walter Gropius and Kenzō Tange later wrote on it. It is administered by the Imperial Household Agency and can be visited by reservation.
