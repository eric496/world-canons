## Biography

Musō Soseki was born in Ise Province, studied esoteric Buddhism and then Zen, and spent years in retreat in remote hermitages before being drawn into the service of Emperor Go-Daigo and then of the Ashikaga shōguns. He headed major monasteries in Kyoto and Kamakura and in 1339 became founding abbot of Tenryū-ji, built by Ashikaga Takauji in memory of Go-Daigo. Seven emperors honoured him with the title of National Master. His disciples made the Five Mountains monasteries centres of Chinese learning and literature.

Musō treated garden making as a form of Zen practice and wrote that a garden can be a means to awakening. Gardens associated with him include those of Zuisen-ji in Kamakura, Tenryū-ji and Saihō-ji, the 'Moss Temple', which he restored in 1339 with a pond garden below and a dry stone cascade above. These gardens, combining pond, rock arrangement and borrowed scenery, shaped Japanese garden design for centuries. Some attributions are traditional rather than documented.

### Saihō-ji (Moss Temple) (1339)

[![Saihō-ji (Moss Temple)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/Matsuojingatanicho%2C_Nishikyo_Ward%2C_Kyoto%2C_Kyoto_Prefecture_615-8286%2C_Japan_-_panoramio_%281%29.jpg/1280px-Matsuojingatanicho%2C_Nishikyo_Ward%2C_Kyoto%2C_Kyoto_Prefecture_615-8286%2C_Japan_-_panoramio_%281%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Matsuojingatanicho%2C_Nishikyo_Ward%2C_Kyoto%2C_Kyoto_Prefecture_615-8286%2C_Japan_-_panoramio_%281%29.jpg)

- **Date:** 1339
- **Type:** Zen garden
- **Location:** Matsuo, Nishikyō, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Saih%C5%8D-ji%20%28Moss%20Temple%29%20Mus%C5%8D%20Soseki)

Saihō-ji was an old temple of the Pure Land school that Musō Soseki restored as a Zen temple in 1339. He reshaped its garden into two parts. The lower garden is a strolling garden around a pond shaped like the character for heart (kokoro), with islands and pavilions. The upper garden, on the hillside, contains an arrangement of rocks that forms a dry waterfall, often regarded as one of the earliest examples of the dry landscape (karesansui) garden. The Ashikaga shōguns Yoshimitsu and Yoshimasa admired it and modelled their own gardens on it. The moss for which it is now famous grew in later centuries after the buildings decayed and the garden was flooded. To protect it, visits are by advance application, with copying of sutras before the tour. It is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto World Heritage Site.

### Garden of Tenryū-ji (1339–1345)

[![Garden of Tenryū-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Tenryuji_Kyoto.jpg/1280px-Tenryuji_Kyoto.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tenryuji_Kyoto.jpg)

<sub>Tenryū-ji, Kyoto Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tenryuji_Kyoto.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1339–1345
- **Type:** Zen temple garden
- **Location:** Arashiyama, Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Garden%20of%20Tenry%C5%AB-ji%20Mus%C5%8D%20Soseki)

Tenryū-ji was founded in 1339 by the shōgun Ashikaga Takauji to console the spirit of Emperor Go-Daigo, whom he had defeated, and Musō Soseki was its founding abbot; trading ships were sent to China to help pay for it. The temple buildings have burned many times and most date from the Meiji period, but the Sōgenchi pond garden behind the abbot's quarters is thought to preserve Musō's design. A pond with a beach of pebbles lies beneath a composition of tall stones on the far shore that represents a waterfall and a carp climbing it, a Chinese image of enlightenment, with a stone bridge before it. The hills of Arashiyama form a borrowed background. Tenryū-ji was ranked first among Kyoto's Five Mountain Zen temples and is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto World Heritage Site.
