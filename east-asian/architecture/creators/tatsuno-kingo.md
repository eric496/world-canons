# Tatsuno Kingo

*辰野金吾*

**1854 – 1919** · Japanese  
Born: Karatsu, Hizen Province · Died: Tokyo  
Styles & eras: [Meiji Western-style & early modern](../movements/meiji-western.md)  

> Leading Meiji architect, pupil of Josiah Conder, who designed the Bank of Japan and Tokyo Station.

## Biography

Tatsuno Kingo was born into a low-ranking samurai family of the Karatsu domain and graduated in 1879 at the top of the first architecture class of the Imperial College of Engineering, taught by Josiah Conder. He studied further in London, including in the office of William Burges, and toured Europe. Returning in 1883 he succeeded Conder as professor, helped found the Architectural Institute of Japan and trained a generation of architects. He died in the influenza pandemic of 1919.

Tatsuno's major works are the Bank of Japan head office (1896), a stone building in a Renaissance manner, and Tokyo Station (1914), a long red-brick building banded with white stone and crowned by domes. This 'Tatsuno style', derived from the English Queen Anne manner, was repeated in banks and offices across Japan, Korea and Taiwan. It symbolised Japan's determination to build a modern state on Western lines.

### Bank of Japan Head Office (1890–1896)

[![Bank of Japan Head Office](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/Bank_of_Japan_2010.jpg/1280px-Bank_of_Japan_2010.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bank_of_Japan_2010.jpg)

<sub>Bank of Japan head office, Tokyo Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bank_of_Japan_2010.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1890–1896
- **Type:** Central bank
- **Location:** Nihonbashi, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bank%20of%20Japan%20Head%20Office%20Tatsuno%20Kingo)

The Bank of Japan was founded in 1882, and Tatsuno Kingo, after studying central bank buildings in Europe, designed its head office in Nihonbashi, completed in 1896. It is a building of brick faced with granite and andesite, in a restrained Neo-Baroque manner, arranged around a courtyard and entered through a gateway, with a central dome. Its design was adapted for the risk of earthquakes, and the upper floors were partly lightened after the Nōbi earthquake of 1891 during construction. It survived the Kantō earthquake of 1923 and the war. The building, still part of the bank, is an Important Cultural Property and houses the Bank of Japan's currency museum nearby.

### Tokyo Station (1908–1914; restored 2012)

[![Tokyo Station](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/Tokyo-STA_Marunouchi-Entrance_2023.jpg/1280px-Tokyo-STA_Marunouchi-Entrance_2023.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokyo-STA_Marunouchi-Entrance_2023.jpg)

<sub>Marunouchi building of Tokyo Station Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokyo-STA_Marunouchi-Entrance_2023.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1908–1914; restored 2012
- **Type:** Railway station
- **Location:** Marunouchi, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tokyo%20Station%20Tatsuno%20Kingo)

Tokyo Station opened in 1914 as the central terminus of Japan's railways, facing the Imperial Palace across the Marunouchi business district. Tatsuno Kingo's Marunouchi building is about 335 metres long, of red brick with bands of white stone, with domed halls at the north and south ends and a central entrance reserved for the imperial family. Its steel frame helped it survive the Kantō earthquake of 1923. In the air raids of 1945 the roof and domes burned, and the building was repaired with only two storeys and simplified roofs. A major restoration completed in 2012 rebuilt the third storey and the domes to the original design and added seismic isolation beneath the building. It is an Important Cultural Property.
