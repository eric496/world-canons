# Li Jie

*李誡*

**c. 1065 – 1110** · Chinese (Song)  
Styles & eras: [Song architecture & the Yingzao Fashi](../movements/song-yingzao-fashi.md)  

> Song dynasty official of the Directorate of Buildings who compiled the Yingzao Fashi, the most important pre-modern Chinese treatise on architecture.

## Biography

Li Jie, courtesy name Mingzhong, came from Guancheng in present-day Henan and served for many years in the Directorate of Buildings and Construction at the Northern Song capital Kaifeng, supervising work on palaces, government offices and temples. Around 1097 he was ordered to revise an earlier, unsatisfactory building code. He interviewed carpenters and craftsmen, gathered practice from the workshops and completed the new text in 1100; it was printed by imperial order in 1103.

The Yingzao Fashi ('Treatise on Architectural Methods') set out a modular system based on the cross-section of a standard timber, the cai, from which the sizes of columns, beams and bracket sets were derived in eight grades. It also covered masonry, carpentry, painting and tiling, with labour and material quotas and illustrations. Rediscovered in the twentieth century, it became the key with which Liang Sicheng and his colleagues learned to read surviving Chinese buildings.

- **Date:** 1100; printed 1103
- **Type:** Building manual
- **Location:** Kaifeng (Bianjing), Northern Song capital
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yingzao%20Fashi%20Li%20Jie)

The Yingzao Fashi ('Treatise on Architectural Methods' or 'State Building Standards') was compiled by Li Jie of the Directorate of Buildings and Construction and printed in 1103 to control costs and standardise imperial building. Its thirty-four chapters cover terminology, foundations, stone, carpentry, joinery, carving, painting, tiling and brickwork, with labour quotas and material estimates, and six chapters of illustrations of bracket sets, cross-sections and decorative patterns. Its central principle is a modular unit, the cai, defined by the section of a standard bracket arm, with eight grades for buildings of different rank, so that all dimensions followed from the building's status. Only later copies survive, and the text was long almost unreadable. A copy found in 1919 was published, and Liang Sicheng spent much of his career decoding it by comparing it with surviving buildings.
