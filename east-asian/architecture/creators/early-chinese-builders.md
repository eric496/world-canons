# Qin and Han imperial builders

*秦漢工匠*

**active 3rd century BC – 3rd century AD** · Chinese  
Styles & eras: [Early Chinese palaces & tombs](../movements/early-chinese-palaces-tombs.md)  

> The conscript labourers, craftsmen and officials who built the palaces and tombs of the first Chinese empires; this entry gathers works whose designers are unknown.

## Biography

The Qin and Han states built on a colossal scale by mobilising conscripted labour, convicts and specialist craftsmen under ministries of works. Historians such as Sima Qian record the numbers involved, and the names of a few officials and many workshop marks survive on bricks, tiles and figures, but no architect in the modern sense can be identified for their palaces and tombs.

Their buildings of rammed earth and timber rose on high terraces arranged along axes within walled enclosures, a model of imperial planning that lasted two thousand years. Almost everything above ground has vanished, but excavated foundations, pottery models and tombs designed as underground palaces show the beginnings of the Chinese architectural tradition.

## Key buildings & sites

### Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor (c. 246–208 BC)

[![Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Tomb_of_the_First_Emperor_Qin_Shi_Huang_Di%2C_Xi%27an%2C_China_-_panoramio.jpg/1280px-Tomb_of_the_First_Emperor_Qin_Shi_Huang_Di%2C_Xi%27an%2C_China_-_panoramio.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tomb_of_the_First_Emperor_Qin_Shi_Huang_Di%2C_Xi%27an%2C_China_-_panoramio.jpg)

<sub>The tomb mound of the First Emperor near Xi'an Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tomb_of_the_First_Emperor_Qin_Shi_Huang_Di%2C_Xi%27an%2C_China_-_panoramio.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 246–208 BC
- **Type:** Imperial tomb complex
- **Location:** Lintong, near Xi'an, Shaanxi, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mausoleum%20of%20the%20First%20Qin%20Emperor%20Qin%20and%20Han%20imperial%20builders)

Work on the tomb of Ying Zheng began soon after he became king of Qin in 246 BC and continued after he unified China and took the title of First Emperor in 221 BC; according to the historian Sima Qian, hundreds of thousands of conscripts laboured on it. The burial mound, originally a stepped pyramid of rammed earth now about 50 metres high, stood within two rectangular walled enclosures, the plan of a palace city for the afterlife. Sima Qian describes a burial chamber with a ceiling of heavenly bodies and rivers of mercury, and soil tests have found high mercury levels, but the central tomb has never been opened. Around it, pits discovered from 1974 contain the Terracotta Army, bronze chariots, stables and figures of officials and entertainers. The complex shows how early Chinese architecture organised space by walls, axes and hierarchy, and how much of it is now known only through tombs.
