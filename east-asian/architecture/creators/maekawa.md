# Kunio Maekawa

*前川國男*

**1905 – 1986** · Japanese  
Born: Niigata · Died: Tokyo  
Styles & eras: [Japanese modernism & Metabolism](../movements/metabolism-modernism.md)  

> Japanese architect who worked for Le Corbusier and Antonin Raymond and became a founder of postwar Japanese modernism.

## Biography

Maekawa graduated from Tokyo Imperial University in 1928 and left immediately for Paris, where he worked for two years in Le Corbusier's studio. Back in Tokyo he worked for Antonin Raymond before opening his own office in 1935, entering competitions with modernist designs that challenged the official 'imperial crown' style of the 1930s. Kenzō Tange was one of his assistants.

After the war Maekawa built concert halls, libraries and museums in exposed concrete and tile, among them the Kanagawa Prefectural Music Hall and Library (1954), the Tokyo Bunka Kaikan (1961) facing Le Corbusier's National Museum of Western Art in Ueno, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Art Museum (1975). He developed durable fired-tile cladding for concrete and argued for architecture as a public, democratic art.

## Key buildings & sites

### Tokyo Bunka Kaikan (1961)

[![Tokyo Bunka Kaikan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/94/Tokyo_bunka_kaikan01s3200.jpg/1280px-Tokyo_bunka_kaikan01s3200.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokyo_bunka_kaikan01s3200.jpg)

<sub>Tokyo Bunka Kaikan Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokyo_bunka_kaikan01s3200.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1961
- **Type:** Concert hall
- **Location:** Ueno Park, Taitō, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tokyo%20Bunka%20Kaikan%20Kunio%20Maekawa)

The Tokyo Bunka Kaikan, opened in 1961 to mark the 500th anniversary of the founding of Edo, was Tokyo's main hall for opera, ballet and orchestral music for decades. Kunio Maekawa placed it in Ueno Park opposite the National Museum of Western Art, completed in 1959 to a design by Le Corbusier, for whom Maekawa had worked in Paris. A massive concrete roof with a deep upturned edge, like the eaves of a temple, shelters the foyers and the main hall of over 2,000 seats, whose walls are lined with sculptural acoustic panels. The building is a key example of the heroic concrete modernism of postwar Japan, and has been renovated several times.
