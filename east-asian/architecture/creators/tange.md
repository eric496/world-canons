# Kenzō Tange

*丹下健三*

**1913 – 2005** · Japanese  
Born: Sakai, Osaka · Died: Tokyo  
Styles & eras: [Japanese modernism & Metabolism](../movements/metabolism-modernism.md), [Contemporary architecture](../movements/east-asian-contemporary-arch.md)  

> The leading Japanese architect of the postwar era, designer of the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park and the Yoyogi National Gymnasium.

## Biography

Tange grew up partly in Imabari on Shikoku, studied at Tokyo Imperial University and worked for Kunio Maekawa. In 1949 he won the competition for the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, completed in 1955 with its museum raised on pilotis aligned with the ruined Genbaku Dome. As a professor at the University of Tokyo his laboratory trained Fumihiko Maki, Arata Isozaki, Kishō Kurokawa and others. His 1960 Plan for Tokyo, a linear city across Tokyo Bay, inspired the Metabolists. He received the Pritzker Prize in 1987.

Tange's buildings of the 1950s and 1960s, such as the Kagawa Prefectural Office (1958), translated the post-and-beam of Japanese timber building into concrete. His Yoyogi gymnasiums for the 1964 Olympics, hung from cables in great swooping curves, and the stainless-steel St Mary's Cathedral in Tokyo are masterpieces of structural expression. Later his office worked worldwide, and the twin-towered Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building (1991) became a symbol of the city.

### Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum and Park (1949–1955)

[![Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum and Park](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cd/Hiroshima_Peace_Memorial_Museum_2009.jpg/1280px-Hiroshima_Peace_Memorial_Museum_2009.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hiroshima_Peace_Memorial_Museum_2009.jpg)

<sub>Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hiroshima_Peace_Memorial_Museum_2009.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1949–1955
- **Type:** Museum and memorial park
- **Location:** Naka, Hiroshima, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hiroshima%20Peace%20Memorial%20Museum%20and%20Park%20Kenz%C5%8D%20Tange)

In 1949 Kenzō Tange won the competition for a peace park on the delta island at the centre of the atomic explosion of 6 August 1945. He drew a single axis from the main museum building, through a saddle-shaped cenotaph inspired by ancient clay house models, to the ruined dome of the former Industrial Promotion Hall, now the Genbaku Dome. The museum, completed in 1955, is a long concrete building raised on pilotis, with a facade of vertical louvres, through which the axis passes unobstructed. The design combined Le Corbusier's modernism with the proportions of traditional timber architecture. The main building was designated an Important Cultural Property in 2006, the first postwar buildings to be so designated, and the Genbaku Dome became a World Heritage Site in 1996.

### Yoyogi National Gymnasium (1961–1964)

[![Yoyogi National Gymnasium](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/18/Kokuritsu_Yoyogi_Ky%C5%8Dgij%C5%8D_1.jpg/1280px-Kokuritsu_Yoyogi_Ky%C5%8Dgij%C5%8D_1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kokuritsu_Yoyogi_Ky%C5%8Dgij%C5%8D_1.jpg)

<sub>Yoyogi National Gymnasium Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kokuritsu_Yoyogi_Ky%C5%8Dgij%C5%8D_1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1961–1964
- **Type:** Sports arena
- **Location:** Shibuya, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yoyogi%20National%20Gymnasium%20Kenz%C5%8D%20Tange)

For the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, the first held in Asia, Kenzō Tange and the structural engineer Yoshikatsu Tsuboi designed two arenas in Yoyogi Park. In the larger, built for swimming and diving, two main steel cables are slung between two concrete masts and anchored to the ground, and the roof is suspended from them on secondary cables, producing sweeping curves that recall both the roofs of temples and the forms of shells. The smaller arena, for basketball, has a spiralling roof hung from a single mast. The arenas were built in about eighteen months and impressed the world as a sign of Japan's postwar recovery. They have remained in use for sports and concerts, and were designated an Important Cultural Property in 2021.

### St Mary's Cathedral, Tokyo (1961–1964)

[![St Mary's Cathedral, Tokyo](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/20030702_2_July_2003_Tokyo_Cathedorale_1_Tange_Kenzou_Sekiguchi_Tokyo_Japan_b.jpg/1280px-20030702_2_July_2003_Tokyo_Cathedorale_1_Tange_Kenzou_Sekiguchi_Tokyo_Japan_b.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20030702_2_July_2003_Tokyo_Cathedorale_1_Tange_Kenzou_Sekiguchi_Tokyo_Japan_b.jpg)

<sub>St Mary's Cathedral, Tokyo Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20030702_2_July_2003_Tokyo_Cathedorale_1_Tange_Kenzou_Sekiguchi_Tokyo_Japan_b.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1961–1964
- **Type:** Cathedral
- **Location:** Sekiguchi, Bunkyō, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=St%20Mary%27s%20Cathedral%2C%20Tokyo%20Kenz%C5%8D%20Tange)

The Catholic cathedral of Tokyo, a timber Gothic building, was destroyed in an air raid in 1945. For the new cathedral, completed in 1964, Kenzō Tange and Yoshikatsu Tsuboi designed eight hyperbolic paraboloid concrete shells that lean together and rise from a diamond-shaped plan to a height of about 40 metres. Between the shells narrow skylights form a cross that runs the length of the roof and down the facades, bringing light into the tall, unadorned concrete interior. The exterior is clad in stainless steel. A separate bell tower stands about 60 metres high. The cathedral is one of the most admired works of Tange's mature period.

### Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building (1988–1991)

[![Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building_2024.jpg/1280px-Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building_2024.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building_2024.jpg)

<sub>Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokyo_Metropolitan_Government_Building_2024.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1988–1991
- **Type:** Government offices
- **Location:** Nishi-Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tokyo%20Metropolitan%20Government%20Building%20Kenz%C5%8D%20Tange)

When the Tokyo metropolitan government moved its headquarters from Marunouchi to the new skyscraper district of Shinjuku, Kenzō Tange won the competition with a design completed in 1991. The main building is 243 metres high and divides at its 33rd floor into two towers, an outline often compared to a Gothic cathedral, and is accompanied by a second tower and an assembly building around a semicircular plaza. The granite and glass facades carry a fine grid pattern that has been compared both to traditional lattice and to the circuits of a computer chip. It was the tallest building in Tokyo until 2007, and its free observation decks are among the city's most visited viewpoints.
