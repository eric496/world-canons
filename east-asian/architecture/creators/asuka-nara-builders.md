# Asuka and Nara temple builders

*飛鳥・奈良の工人*

**active late 6th – 8th century** · Japanese (with Korean and Chinese craftsmen)  
Styles & eras: [Asuka & Nara temple architecture](../movements/asuka-nara-temples.md)  

> The Korean, Chinese and Japanese craftsmen who built Japan's first Buddhist temples; this entry gathers works without known designers.

## Biography

According to the Nihon Shoki, in 588 the Korean kingdom of Baekje sent temple carpenters, a roof-tile maker, a metalworker and a painter to build Asuka-dera, Japan's first full Buddhist temple. Their descendants and Japanese apprentices, organised later in the government's Office for the Construction of Tōdai-ji and similar bodies, built the temples of Asuka and Nara.

These builders introduced to Japan the timber frame with bracket sets, tiled roofs and stone platforms, the temple compound with its pagoda and halls, and the grid capital. Their works at Hōryū-ji, Yakushi-ji, Tōdai-ji and Tōshōdai-ji include the oldest timber buildings in the world.

### Hōryū-ji (founded 607; rebuilt c. 670–711)

[![Hōryū-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/17/Horyu-ji11s3200.jpg/1280px-Horyu-ji11s3200.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Horyu-ji11s3200.jpg)

<sub>Golden hall and pagoda of Hōryū-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Horyu-ji11s3200.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** founded 607; rebuilt c. 670–711
- **Type:** Buddhist temple
- **Location:** Ikaruga, Nara Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=H%C5%8Dry%C5%AB-ji%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20temple%20builders)

Hōryū-ji was founded in 607 by Prince Shōtoku and Empress Suiko. According to the Nihon Shoki it burned down in 670, and its western precinct was rebuilt by the early eighth century, though the exact dates and whether parts survive from the first temple are long-standing scholarly debates. Its golden hall (kondō) and five-storey pagoda stand side by side within a cloister entered through a middle gate, an asymmetrical arrangement unlike Chinese practice. The buildings show features of the Chinese style of the sixth and seventh centuries, transmitted through Korea: swelling columns, cloud-shaped brackets and railings with swastika patterns. Tree-ring dating has shown that the pagoda's central pillar was felled in 594. The eastern precinct includes the octagonal Yumedono (739). A fire in 1949 that damaged the golden hall's murals led to Japan's Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties. In 1993 Hōryū-ji became, with Himeji Castle, one of Japan's first World Heritage Sites.

### East Pagoda of Yakushi-ji (730)

[![East Pagoda of Yakushi-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Yakushiji_Nara03s3s4350.jpg/1280px-Yakushiji_Nara03s3s4350.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yakushiji_Nara03s3s4350.jpg)

<sub>Yakushi-ji, Nara Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yakushiji_Nara03s3s4350.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 730
- **Type:** Pagoda
- **Location:** Nara, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=East%20Pagoda%20of%20Yakushi-ji%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20temple%20builders)

Yakushi-ji was founded by Emperor Tenmu in 680, who vowed it for the recovery of his consort, the future Empress Jitō, and was moved to the new capital Heijō-kyō (Nara) after 710. Its East Pagoda, dated to 730, is the only building to survive from the Nara period temple. It has three main storeys, each with a smaller pent roof (mokoshi) below, so that it appears to have six roofs of alternating size, a lively rhythm that is often described as frozen music. A bronze finial at the top carries openwork flying celestials. The West Pagoda, burned in 1528, was rebuilt in 1981 in bright Tang colours, showing how the East Pagoda may once have looked. The East Pagoda underwent a full dismantling and restoration completed in 2020. Yakushi-ji is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara World Heritage Site.

### Tōdai-ji (743–752; Great Buddha Hall rebuilt 1709)

[![Tōdai-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/T%C5%8Ddai-ji_Kon-d%C5%8D.jpg/1280px-T%C5%8Ddai-ji_Kon-d%C5%8D.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:T%C5%8Ddai-ji_Kon-d%C5%8D.jpg)

<sub>Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) of Tōdai-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:T%C5%8Ddai-ji_Kon-d%C5%8D.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 743–752; Great Buddha Hall rebuilt 1709
- **Type:** Buddhist temple
- **Location:** Nara, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=T%C5%8Ddai-ji%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20temple%20builders)

In 743 Emperor Shōmu ordered the casting of a colossal bronze Buddha, Vairocana, as the centre of a national network of temples, and the image was consecrated in 752 in a ceremony attended by monks from across Asia. The Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) that sheltered it was flanked by two seven-storey pagodas. The temple was burned in 1180 during the Genpei War and rebuilt under the monk Chōgen, then burned again in fighting in 1567. The present hall, completed in 1709, is about 57 metres wide, roughly a third narrower than the original, but still one of the largest timber buildings in the world; its enormous roof beams required giant pines found with difficulty. The Shōsōin storehouse, the Hokke-dō with its eighth-century sculptures and Chōgen's Great South Gate also survive. Tōdai-ji is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara World Heritage Site.

### Shōsōin (mid-8th century)

[![Shōsōin](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Shoso-in.jpg/1280px-Shoso-in.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shoso-in.jpg)

<sub>Shōsōin storehouse, Nara Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shoso-in.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** mid-8th century
- **Type:** Treasure storehouse
- **Location:** Tōdai-ji, Nara, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sh%C5%8Ds%C5%8Din%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20temple%20builders)

After Emperor Shōmu's death in 756 his widow, Empress Kōmyō, dedicated his personal possessions to the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji, and they were placed in this storehouse. The building is raised on forty tall wooden posts and its walls are built in the azekura technique of horizontal logs of triangular section, interlocked at the corners, under a single hipped roof. It is divided into three sections. The collection, some 9,000 objects including musical instruments, mirrors, textiles, glass and documents from China, Persia and Central Asia as well as Japan, has survived in remarkable condition. The treasures were moved to modern fireproof storehouses in the twentieth century; a selection is shown each autumn at the Nara National Museum. The building, administered by the Imperial Household Agency, is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara World Heritage Site.

### Golden Hall of Tōshōdai-ji (late 8th century)

[![Golden Hall of Tōshōdai-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Toshodaiji-GoldenHall%28NT%29.jpg/1280px-Toshodaiji-GoldenHall%28NT%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Toshodaiji-GoldenHall%28NT%29.jpg)

<sub>Golden hall of Tōshōdai-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Toshodaiji-GoldenHall%28NT%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 8th century
- **Type:** Buddhist hall
- **Location:** Nara, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Golden%20Hall%20of%20T%C5%8Dsh%C5%8Ddai-ji%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20temple%20builders)

Tōshōdai-ji was founded in 759 by Ganjin (Jianzhen), a Chinese monk who reached Japan in 753 after five failed voyages and the loss of his sight, to train Japanese monks in the proper rules of ordination. Its golden hall (kondō), built in the late eighth century after his death, is the only main hall of a Nara temple to survive from the period. Seven bays wide, it has an open colonnade along the front, a single great hipped roof and three-stepped bracket sets, and houses large dry-lacquer and wooden statues of Vairocana, the Healing Buddha and a thousand-armed Kannon. The roof was made steeper in later repairs. Between 2000 and 2009 the hall was completely dismantled and rebuilt using its original timbers. Tōshōdai-ji is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara World Heritage Site.
