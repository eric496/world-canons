# Heian court builders

*平安の工人*

**active 9th – 12th century** · Japanese  
Styles & eras: [Heian Pure Land halls & shinden-zukuri](../movements/heian-pure-land.md)  

> The carpenters and craftsmen who built the palaces and Pure Land temples of the Heian aristocracy.

## Biography

In the Heian period the court and great families such as the Fujiwara commissioned mansions and temples from carpenters and craftsmen in Kyoto, some of them attached to the government, others in workshops patronised by aristocrats. Patrons such as Fujiwara no Yorimichi are known by name, while the builders are not.

They developed a more Japanese architecture, with cypress-bark roofs, raised wooden floors, pond gardens and halls designed to be seen across water, and created the Amida halls of Pure Land Buddhism. Their palaces are all lost, but are known from picture scrolls such as the Genji Monogatari Emaki.

### Phoenix Hall of Byōdō-in (1053)

[![Phoenix Hall of Byōdō-in](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/Phoenix_Hall%2C_Byodo-in%2C_November_2016_-01.jpg/1280px-Phoenix_Hall%2C_Byodo-in%2C_November_2016_-01.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Phoenix_Hall%2C_Byodo-in%2C_November_2016_-01.jpg)

<sub>Phoenix Hall (Hōōdō), Byōdō-in Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Phoenix_Hall%2C_Byodo-in%2C_November_2016_-01.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1053
- **Type:** Buddhist hall
- **Location:** Uji, Kyoto Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Phoenix%20Hall%20of%20By%C5%8Dd%C5%8D-in%20Heian%20court%20builders)

Byōdō-in was a villa of the regent Fujiwara no Michinaga that his son Yorimichi converted into a temple in 1052, the year many believed the age of the 'decline of the Dharma' had begun. The Amida Hall, dedicated in 1053, houses a gilded wooden statue of the Buddha Amida by the sculptor Jōchō, surrounded by carved celestial musicians on the walls. The hall stands on an island in a pond, with a central pavilion flanked by L-shaped wings on raised corridors and a tail corridor at the back, a shape later likened to a bird with outspread wings; bronze phoenixes stand on the ridge, giving it the name Hōōdō. Seen across the water, it was meant to evoke Amida's palace in the Western Paradise. The hall survived the wars that destroyed most of Kyoto and appears on the ten-yen coin. It is part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto World Heritage Site.

### Konjikidō of Chūson-ji (1124)

[![Konjikidō of Chūson-ji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/230728_Chusonji_Hiraizumi_Iwate_pref_Japan01s3.jpg/1280px-230728_Chusonji_Hiraizumi_Iwate_pref_Japan01s3.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:230728_Chusonji_Hiraizumi_Iwate_pref_Japan01s3.jpg)

<sub>Chūson-ji, Hiraizumi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:230728_Chusonji_Hiraizumi_Iwate_pref_Japan01s3.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1124
- **Type:** Buddhist mortuary hall
- **Location:** Hiraizumi, Iwate Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Konjikid%C5%8D%20of%20Ch%C5%ABson-ji%20Heian%20court%20builders)

In the early twelfth century Fujiwara no Kiyohira, ruler of the northern frontier region, built at his capital Hiraizumi a group of temples to rival those of Kyoto. The Konjikidō, or Golden Hall, completed in 1124, is a square hall of only about 5.5 metres a side, its interior and exterior covered with gold leaf over black lacquer and its altars inlaid with mother-of-pearl from the southern seas and decorated with gilt bronze. Beneath the altars lie the mummified remains of Kiyohira and the three succeeding lords of the family. A protective enclosing hall was built over it in the thirteenth century; the present concrete shelter, with the hall behind glass, dates from 1965. It is the most opulent surviving work of late Heian architecture and part of the Hiraizumi World Heritage Site.

### Sanjūsangen-dō (1164; rebuilt 1266)

[![Sanjūsangen-dō](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Sanjusangendo_2022.jpg/1280px-Sanjusangendo_2022.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sanjusangendo_2022.jpg)

<sub>Sanjūsangen-dō, Kyoto Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sanjusangendo_2022.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1164; rebuilt 1266
- **Type:** Buddhist hall
- **Location:** Kyoto, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sanj%C5%ABsangen-d%C5%8D%20Heian%20court%20builders)

Rengeō-in, known as Sanjūsangen-dō, the 'hall of thirty-three bays', was built in 1164 by Taira no Kiyomori for the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa. It burned in 1249 and was rebuilt in 1266. The hall is about 120 metres long, the longest timber building in Japan, with a single row of thirty-three bays between its columns. Inside stand a seated thousand-armed Kannon by the sculptor Tankei and a thousand standing Kannon statues in ranks, many made in the thirteenth century by sculptors of the Kei school. The long veranda was used from the Edo period for an archery contest in which archers shot along its full length. The building, a National Treasure, shows the late Heian style in its simplest monumental form.
