# Vernacular builders of China, Japan and Korea

*民居建造者*

**active 15th – 20th century** · Chinese, Japanese and Korean  
Styles & eras: [Vernacular houses & villages](../movements/vernacular.md)  

> The farmers, clans and village carpenters who built the houses of East Asia's countryside; this entry gathers vernacular works without known designers.

## Biography

Vernacular houses were built by families and communities with local carpenters and masons, following inherited practice rather than drawings. Their forms responded to climate, materials, family structure and security: earthen walls in Fujian, steep thatch in the snowy Japanese Alps, heated ondol floors and separate quarters in Korean gentry houses.

Long overlooked by historians, these buildings were studied from the twentieth century onward as expressions of everyday life, and several villages have been inscribed as World Heritage Sites. Communal maintenance, such as the rethatching of roofs in Shirakawa-gō, keeps some of them alive.

### Fujian Tulou (12th – 20th century)

[![Fujian Tulou](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/90/Three_round%2C_one_oval%2C_one_square_%2816831811084%29.jpg/1280px-Three_round%2C_one_oval%2C_one_square_%2816831811084%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Three_round%2C_one_oval%2C_one_square_%2816831811084%29.jpg)

<sub>Tianluokeng tulou cluster, Nanjing County, Fujian Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Three_round%2C_one_oval%2C_one_square_%2816831811084%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 12th – 20th century
- **Type:** Fortified communal houses
- **Location:** South-western Fujian, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Fujian%20Tulou%20Vernacular%20builders%20of%20China%2C%20Japan%20and%20Korea)

In the mountains of south-western Fujian, Hakka and Minnan clans built tulou, fortified houses of rammed earth that could hold several hundred people of one lineage. Most surviving examples date from the seventeenth to the twentieth centuries. A typical round tulou has outer walls of earth up to two metres thick at the base, with no windows on the lower floors and a single gate, enclosing a ring of three or four storeys of identical family rooms, built of timber, opening on to galleries that face a courtyard with an ancestral hall at the centre. Square, oval and other forms also occur. The design combined defence against bandits with an egalitarian distribution of space. In 2008 forty-six tulou were inscribed as a World Heritage Site, and groups such as the Tianluokeng cluster of four round houses and one square one are now well known.

### Hahoe Folk Village (15th – 19th century)

[![Hahoe Folk Village](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Hahoe_Folk_Village_02.jpg/1280px-Hahoe_Folk_Village_02.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hahoe_Folk_Village_02.jpg)

<sub>Hahoe Folk Village Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hahoe_Folk_Village_02.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 15th – 19th century
- **Type:** Clan village
- **Location:** Andong, North Gyeongsang Province, South Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hahoe%20Folk%20Village%20Vernacular%20builders%20of%20China%2C%20Japan%20and%20Korea)

Hahoe has been the home of the Pungsan Ryu clan since the fifteenth or sixteenth century, and its name refers to the river that winds around it in an S-shaped loop. Large tile-roofed houses of the yangban gentry, arranged in courtyards with separate quarters for men and women, stand among the thatched houses of the commoners who once served them. The village includes a shrine and study halls associated with the statesman Ryu Seong-ryong, chief minister during the Japanese invasions of the 1590s, and Byeongsan Seowon academy lies nearby. Hahoe also preserves a famous masked dance drama. With Yangdong, it was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2010 as the Historic Villages of Korea.

### Gasshō Houses of Shirakawa-gō (17th – 19th century)

[![Gasshō Houses of Shirakawa-gō](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Ogi_Shirakawa-g%C5%8D%2C_Gifu%2C_Japan.jpg/1280px-Ogi_Shirakawa-g%C5%8D%2C_Gifu%2C_Japan.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ogi_Shirakawa-g%C5%8D%2C_Gifu%2C_Japan.jpg)

<sub>Ogimachi village, Shirakawa-gō Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ogi_Shirakawa-g%C5%8D%2C_Gifu%2C_Japan.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 17th – 19th century
- **Type:** Farmhouses
- **Location:** Shirakawa, Gifu Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gassh%C5%8D%20Houses%20of%20Shirakawa-g%C5%8D%20Vernacular%20builders%20of%20China%2C%20Japan%20and%20Korea)

In the isolated mountain valleys of Shirakawa-gō and Gokayama, farmers built large houses in the gasshō-zukuri style, named after the resemblance of their steep roofs to hands joined in prayer. The thatched roofs, at about sixty degrees, shed the heavy snow of the region, and the large attic spaces under them, on several floors, were used for raising silkworms and producing saltpetre. Extended families lived on the ground floor around an open hearth, whose smoke preserved the thatch and timbers. The roofs are rethatched every few decades by the villagers working together in a tradition of mutual help called yui. Ogimachi in Shirakawa-gō preserves the largest group, and the villages were inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1995.
