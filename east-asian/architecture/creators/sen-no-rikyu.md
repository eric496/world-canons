## Biography

Sen no Rikyū, born Tanaka Yoshirō to a merchant family in the port city of Sakai, studied tea with Takeno Jōō and practised Zen at Daitoku-ji. He became tea master to Oda Nobunaga and then to Toyotomi Hideyoshi, whose great tea gatherings he organised and for whom he served as a political confidant. In 1591, for reasons that are still debated, Hideyoshi ordered him to commit suicide. His descendants founded the Omotesenke, Urasenke and Mushakōjisenke schools of tea.

Rikyū shrank the tea room to two tatami mats or less, with walls of rough earth plaster, small paper windows, a low crawl-through entrance (nijiriguchi) and an alcove for a single scroll or flower, so that host and guests met as equals in a dim, quiet space. This aesthetic of poverty and restraint, wabi, transformed Japanese architecture and design. The Tai-an at Myōki-an in Yamazaki, of about 1582, is traditionally attributed to him.

## Key buildings & sites

### Tai-an tea room (c. 1582)

[![Tai-an tea room](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/TAIAN_in_MyokiAN_Kyoto.jpg/1280px-TAIAN_in_MyokiAN_Kyoto.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:TAIAN_in_MyokiAN_Kyoto.jpg)

<sub>The Tai-an at Myōki-an Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:TAIAN_in_MyokiAN_Kyoto.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1582
- **Type:** Tea house
- **Location:** Myōki-an, Ōyamazaki, Kyoto Prefecture, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tai-an%20tea%20room%20Sen%20no%20Riky%C5%AB)

The Tai-an, at the Zen temple Myōki-an in Yamazaki south-west of Kyoto, is traditionally said to have been built by Sen no Rikyū around 1582, when Toyotomi Hideyoshi had a base at Yamazaki after defeating Akechi Mitsuhide. It may have been moved to its present site. The room has just two tatami mats for host and guests, with a small alcove (tokonoma) whose corners and ceiling are plastered over to make it seem deeper, walls of rough clay mixed with straw, small windows of paper and lattice and a low ceiling of different heights. Guests enter by crawling through a small square opening, the nijiriguchi, leaving rank and swords outside. It is the oldest surviving tea house in Japan and a National Treasure, and can be visited only by advance appointment. It embodies Rikyū's ideal of tea in a space of humility and concentration.
