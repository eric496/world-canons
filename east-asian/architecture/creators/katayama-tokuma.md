# Katayama Tōkuma

*片山東熊*

**1854 – 1917** · Japanese  
Born: Hagi, Chōshū Domain · Died: Tokyo  
Styles & eras: [Meiji Western-style & early modern](../movements/meiji-western.md)  

> Architect to the Imperial Household who designed the neo-Baroque Akasaka Palace and the national museums of Nara and Kyoto.

## Biography

Katayama Tōkuma came from the Chōshū domain, whose leaders dominated the Meiji government, and was a member of the first class of Josiah Conder's architecture students, graduating in 1879. He entered the Imperial Household Ministry and spent his career designing for the imperial family, travelling in Europe to study palaces and furnishings.

His works include the Nara Imperial Museum (1894) and the Kyoto Imperial Museum (1895), both in a French Renaissance manner, and above all the Tōgū Palace, now the State Guest House at Akasaka (1909), modelled on European palaces such as Versailles and the Louvre. It is the most ambitious work of Japanese Western-style architecture; the Meiji Emperor reportedly found it too extravagant. It became the first postwar building designated a National Treasure, in 2009.

## Key buildings & sites

### Akasaka Palace (1899–1909)

<sub>Akasaka Palace, Tokyo Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2019_Akasaka_Palace_02.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1899–1909
- **Type:** Palace (State Guest House)
- **Location:** Moto-Akasaka, Minato, Tokyo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Akasaka%20Palace%20Katayama%20T%C5%8Dkuma)

The Tōgū Palace was built between 1899 and 1909 as the residence of the crown prince, the future Emperor Taishō. Katayama Tōkuma, architect of the Imperial Household, modelled it on European palaces, with a symmetrical facade of granite, a central entrance and curving wings, and lavish interiors decorated by Japanese and European craftsmen, including panels of cloisonné and paintings by leading artists. Its steel frame and brick walls were designed with earthquakes in mind. It was little used as a residence, and after the Second World War served as the National Diet Library and other offices. Renovated by Murano Tōgo and others, it became the State Guest House in 1974 for visiting heads of state. In 2009 it was designated a National Treasure, the first building of the Meiji era or later to be so honoured.
