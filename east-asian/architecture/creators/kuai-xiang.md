# Kuai Xiang

*蒯祥*

**1398 – 1481** · Chinese (Ming)  
Born: Xiangshan, Wu County (Suzhou) · Died: Beijing  
Styles & eras: [Ming–Qing imperial architecture](../movements/ming-qing-imperial.md)  

> Ming dynasty master carpenter from Suzhou who is credited with the design of the Forbidden City's main halls and the Tiananmen gate.

## Biography

Kuai Xiang came from a family of carpenters at Xiangshan near Suzhou, a district famous for its building craftsmen. When the Yongle Emperor moved the capital to Beijing he was among the craftsmen summoned to build the new palace, completed in 1420. He worked on the palace through several reigns, rebuilding halls after fires, and on tombs and other imperial works, rising from craftsman to a vice minister of works, an extraordinary career for an artisan.

Kuai Xiang is traditionally credited with the design of the Chengtian Gate, the predecessor of today's Tiananmen, and of the three great halls of the Forbidden City, though the palace was the work of a whole bureaucracy of designers and thousands of workers, and the extent of his role is hard to establish. Later sources praised his skill at drawing and his ability to estimate exactly the timber a building would need. He became the patron figure of the Xiangshan builders.

### Forbidden City (1406–1420)

[![Forbidden City](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ef/The_Forbidden_City_-_View_from_Coal_Hill.jpg/1280px-The_Forbidden_City_-_View_from_Coal_Hill.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Forbidden_City_-_View_from_Coal_Hill.jpg)

<sub>The Forbidden City seen from Jingshan (Coal Hill) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Forbidden_City_-_View_from_Coal_Hill.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1406–1420
- **Type:** Imperial palace
- **Location:** Beijing, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Forbidden%20City%20Kuai%20Xiang)

When the Yongle Emperor moved the Ming capital to Beijing, he ordered a new palace built on the site of the Yuan palace; construction ran from 1406 to 1420, using timber floated from the south-west and stone and bricks from across northern China. The rectangular walled precinct, about 960 by 750 metres and surrounded by a moat, contains some 980 buildings. It is laid out along a north–south axis: an outer court of three great halls on a triple marble terrace, led by the Hall of Supreme Harmony, for state ceremonies, and an inner court of residential palaces and a garden for the emperor and his household. Yellow glazed roofs, red walls and the number and form of roofs and ornaments express the strict hierarchy of the empire. Most halls have been rebuilt after fires, many in the Qing. Twenty-four emperors lived there until 1912; since 1925 it has been the Palace Museum. Kuai Xiang is traditionally credited with its design, though it was the work of an imperial bureaucracy.

### Tiananmen (1420; rebuilt 1651)

[![Tiananmen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/20200110_Tiananmen-3.jpg/1280px-20200110_Tiananmen-3.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20200110_Tiananmen-3.jpg)

<sub>Tiananmen, Beijing Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20200110_Tiananmen-3.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1420; rebuilt 1651
- **Type:** Palace gate
- **Location:** Beijing, China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tiananmen%20Kuai%20Xiang)

The gate first built in 1420 as the Chengtian Gate, the main southern entrance to the Imperial City in front of the Forbidden City, is traditionally attributed to the master carpenter Kuai Xiang. It was destroyed in fighting at the fall of the Ming in 1644 and rebuilt under the Qing in 1651 with its present name. A gate tower of nine bays by five, under a double-eaved hipped and gabled roof of yellow tiles, stands on a massive red platform pierced by five arched passages, the central one reserved for the emperor. Imperial edicts were proclaimed from its balcony. On 1 October 1949 Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic there, and the gate appears on the national emblem designed by Liang Sicheng and Lin Huiyin's team. It was entirely reconstructed in 1969–70 to the old design.
