# Ji Cheng

*計成*

**1582 – after 1634** · Chinese (Ming)  
Born: Wujiang (Suzhou) · Died: ?  
Styles & eras: [Chinese scholar gardens](../movements/chinese-scholar-gardens.md)  

> Late Ming garden designer and author of the Yuanye ('The Craft of Gardens'), the only classical Chinese treatise devoted entirely to garden making.

## Biography

Ji Cheng was born in Wujiang near Suzhou and trained as a painter, admiring the landscapes of Jing Hao and Guan Tong. After travelling in his youth he settled in Zhenjiang and began making artificial rockeries, then whole gardens for officials in Changzhou and Yangzhou, including the Garden of Reflections (Yingyuan) of Zheng Yuanxun. His treatise Yuanye, with a preface of 1631, was printed in 1634 with the backing of the politician Ruan Dacheng, whose later disgrace may explain why the book was little read in China for three centuries.

The Yuanye covers siting, buildings, walls, windows, paving, rockeries and the 'borrowing' of views from the surrounding landscape, illustrated with lattice and window patterns. Its central idea is that a garden, though made by people, should seem to have arisen naturally. The book survived in Japan, where it was known as Dakaten, and was rediscovered in China in the 1930s. None of Ji Cheng's own gardens survives.

## Key buildings & sites

### Yuanye (The Craft of Gardens) (1631; printed 1634)

- **Date:** 1631; printed 1634
- **Type:** Garden treatise
- **Location:** Printed in the lower Yangzi region
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yuanye%20%28The%20Craft%20of%20Gardens%29%20Ji%20Cheng)

Ji Cheng wrote the Yuanye after decades of building gardens for officials in the Yangzi delta, and it was printed in 1634 with prefaces by friends and patrons. In three books it discusses the choice of site, the types of buildings, railings, doors and windows, walls, paving, rockeries and the selection of stones, with diagrams of lattice and paving patterns. Ji Cheng insisted that a garden should be adapted to its site and that the designer, not the builders, determines its quality. He gave a famous name to the practice of 'borrowing scenery' (jiejing), incorporating views beyond the walls. Written in elegant parallel prose, the book was hardly known in China after the Ming, partly because of its association with the disgraced Ruan Dacheng, but survived in Japan and was republished in China in the 1930s. It is now a classic of garden history.
