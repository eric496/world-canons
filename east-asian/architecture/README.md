## Notes on dates and rebuilding

**Timber is renewed, not preserved.** Much of East Asia's architecture is built of wood, and fire, war, earthquakes and decay have meant that most famous buildings have been repaired or rebuilt many times. Dates in this section give the founding or design date first and note major rebuildings: the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests at the Temple of Heaven was rebuilt after a fire in 1889, Tōdai-ji's Great Buddha Hall dates from 1709, Kinkaku-ji from 1955, Sungnyemun from 2013 and most of Gyeongbokgung from 1867 or after 1990.

**Periodic rebuilding as tradition.** At Ise Grand Shrine the main sanctuaries are rebuilt every twenty years on an adjacent plot in the ceremony of *shikinen sengū*, a practice the shrine traces to 690; the 62nd rebuilding took place in 2013 and the next is due in 2033. Izumo Taisha is re-roofed in similar cycles. Here authenticity lies in the continuity of form and craft rather than in old material, an idea recognised in the Nara Document on Authenticity of 1994.

**Dismantling and reassembly.** Japanese and Korean conservators traditionally take a timber building apart, replace decayed members and put it back together, as at the Golden Hall of Tōshōdai-ji (2000–2009), the East Pagoda of Yakushi-ji (completed 2020) and the stone pagoda of Mireuksa (2001–2018).

**Lost buildings.** A few entries record buildings that no longer stand (the Hwangnyongsa pagoda, the Rokumeikan, the Nakagin Capsule Tower) or survive only in part (the Old Summer Palace, Frank Lloyd Wright's Imperial Hotel, whose lobby was reconstructed at Meiji Mura), because of their importance in the history of architecture.

**Attributions.** Many gardens and buildings carry traditional attributions, to Kobori Enshū, Sōami or the Silla minister Kim Dae-seong for example, that are not documented. The descriptions say where this is so. Anonymous works are grouped under collective entries such as *Tang dynasty carpenters* or *Shinto shrine carpenters*.
