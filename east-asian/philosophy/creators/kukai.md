## Biography

Kūkai, posthumously Kōbō Daishi, was born in 774 to the Saeki family on Shikoku and studied Chinese classics at the court college before leaving to practise as a wandering ascetic. In 804 he sailed to Tang China with the same embassy as Saichō, and in Chang'an received initiation into esoteric Buddhism from the master Huiguo. Returning in 806, he won the patronage of Emperor Saga, founded the monastic centre on Mount Kōya in 816 and was given Tō-ji in Kyoto. He also founded a school open to commoners, and he is counted among the three great calligraphers of his era. He died on Mount Kōya in 835.

Kūkai's philosophy holds that the cosmic Buddha Mahāvairocana preaches directly through all phenomena, so that sounds, letters and forms are themselves the dharma, and that one can 'attain buddhahood in this very body' through ritual of body, speech and mind. His Ten Stages of Mind ranks every philosophy he knew, from ethical Confucianism through the Buddhist schools, as steps toward esoteric insight. He is also the subject of countless legends and of the Shikoku pilgrimage.

### Indications of the Goals of the Three Teachings (Sangō shiiki) (797)

[![Indications of the Goals of the Three Teachings (Sangō shiiki)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a0/Rokoshiiki.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rokoshiiki.jpg)

<sub>Kūkai's autograph of the earlier draft, Rōko shiiki, Kongōbu-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rokoshiiki.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 797
- **Type:** Philosophical fiction
- **Written / first published:** Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Indications%20of%20the%20Goals%20of%20the%20Three%20Teachings%20%28Sang%C5%8D%20shiiki%29%20K%C5%ABkai)

Written in elegant Chinese parallel prose when Kūkai was twenty-four, the Sangō shiiki explained his decision to abandon a Confucian career. A prodigal youth is lectured in turn by a Confucian gentleman, who recommends loyalty, filial piety and learning; by a Daoist recluse, who recommends the arts of immortality; and by a ragged Buddhist mendicant, modelled on Kūkai himself, who shows that all things are impermanent and that the bodhisattva path surpasses the others. It is the first work in Japan to compare the three teachings and an early masterpiece of Japanese writing in Chinese; an autograph of its earlier draft, the Rōko shiiki, survives at Kongōbu-ji on Mount Kōya.

### Attaining Enlightenment in This Very Body (Sokushin jōbutsu gi) (c. 820s)

- **Date:** c. 820s
- **Type:** Doctrinal treatise
- **Written / first published:** Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Attaining%20Enlightenment%20in%20This%20Very%20Body%20%28Sokushin%20j%C5%8Dbutsu%20gi%29%20K%C5%ABkai)

In this short treatise Kūkai defends the central claim of Shingon: buddhahood is not reached after countless lifetimes but can be realised 'in this very body'. He explains it through a verse of eight lines, arguing that the six great elements, earth, water, fire, wind, space and consciousness, constitute both the Buddha and all beings and interpenetrate without obstruction; that the four kinds of mandala express this reality; and that the 'three mysteries' of body, speech and mind, practised through mudrā, mantra and meditation, unite the practitioner with Mahāvairocana. With his works on the meanings of sound and of the syllable 'hūṃ', it forms the core of Kūkai's philosophy of language and embodiment.

### Ten Stages of Mind (Jūjūshinron) (830)

- **Date:** 830
- **Type:** Doctrinal treatise
- **Written / first published:** Presented to Emperor Junna
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ten%20Stages%20of%20Mind%20%28J%C5%ABj%C5%ABshinron%29%20K%C5%ABkai)

In 830 Emperor Junna asked the Buddhist schools to submit summaries of their doctrines, and Kūkai responded with the ten-fascicle Himitsu mandara jūjūshinron and its abridgement, the Precious Key to the Secret Treasury (Hizō hōyaku). The ten stages lead from the mind that, like a goat, knows only food and sex, through the ethical mind awakened by Confucianism and the mind seeking heaven, to the Hīnayāna, Yogācāra, Mādhyamaka, Tendai and Kegon schools, and finally to the esoteric mind of Shingon, which contains all the others. Each lower stage is a partial truth rather than an error. The work is both a doctrinal classification in the tradition of Tiantai and Huayan and the first systematic comparative philosophy written in Japan.
