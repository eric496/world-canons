# Han Fei

*韓非*

**c. 280 BC – 233 BC** · Chinese  
Born: State of Han · Died: Qin  
Schools & eras: [Legalism](../movements/legalism.md)  

> Prince of the state of Han and student of Xunzi who synthesised Legalist statecraft into a theory of law, technique and positional power.

## Biography

Han Fei was a member of the ruling house of the state of Han, one of the weakest of the Warring States. According to Sima Qian he studied with Xunzi alongside Li Si, suffered from a stammer and so put his ideas in writing, and repeatedly urged the Han king to reform without success. The king of Qin, the future First Emperor, admired his writings; when Han Fei was sent to Qin as an envoy in 233 BC he was imprisoned, apparently through Li Si's intrigue, and died by poison.

The book Han Feizi brings together three strands of earlier thought: fa (published law with fixed rewards and punishments), shu (the ruler's techniques for controlling officials) and shi (the power of position). Han Fei assumed that people act from self-interest, mocked appeals to the ancient sages with parables like the farmer waiting by a tree stump, and wrote the earliest surviving commentary on the Laozi. Qin's unification put his ideas into practice; later dynasties officially condemned him but quietly used his methods.

## Key key texts

### Han Feizi (c. 250–233 BC)

- **Date:** c. 250–233 BC
- **Type:** Political treatise
- **Written / first published:** State of Han / Qin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Han%20Feizi%20Han%20Fei)

The Han Feizi collects essays mostly attributed to Han Fei, though some are probably by others. It argues that rulers can rely neither on sages nor on moral persuasion; they must publish clear laws, attach certain rewards and punishments to them, keep officials in check by comparing their words with their results, and hide their own preferences so that no minister can manipulate them. Han Fei illustrates his points with a store of anecdotes, among them the man who sold a spear that could pierce any shield and a shield that no spear could pierce, the origin of the Chinese word for contradiction. Chapters explaining and illustrating the Laozi give Daoist wuwei a political reading. Qin's First Emperor admired the book, and despite official condemnation it remained required reading for statesmen.
