# Jeong Do-jeon

*鄭道傳*

**1342 – 1398** · Korean  
Born: Yeongju, Goryeo · Died: Hanyang (Seoul)  
Schools & eras: [Korean Neo-Confucianism](../movements/korean-neo-confucianism.md)  

> Scholar-statesman who designed the institutions of the new Joseon dynasty and wrote its sharpest Confucian critique of Buddhism.

## Biography

Jeong Do-jeon was a Neo-Confucian official of the late Goryeo who was exiled in the 1370s for opposing a pro-Mongol policy. He became the chief strategist of the general Yi Seong-gye, and when Yi founded the Joseon dynasty in 1392 Jeong drafted its legal code, planned its new capital at Hanyang and shaped its institutions. He was killed in 1398 by the prince Yi Bang-won, the future King Taejong, in a struggle over the succession.

His Array of Critiques of Buddhism (Bulssi japbyeon) attacked Buddhist doctrines of transmigration, karma and the mind as empty and socially harmful, using Zhu Xi's philosophy of principle and material force. He made Cheng-Zhu learning the ideology of a state that would last five centuries.

## Key key texts

### Array of Critiques of Buddhism (Bulssi japbyeon) (1398)

- **Date:** 1398
- **Type:** Polemical treatise
- **Written / first published:** Hanyang (Seoul)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Array%20of%20Critiques%20of%20Buddhism%20%28Bulssi%20japbyeon%29%20Jeong%20Do-jeon)

In nineteen short chapters Jeong Do-jeon attacks the Buddhist teachings of transmigration, karma, the mind as source of all things, the illusoriness of the world, and the renunciation of family life, using the Cheng-Zhu philosophy of principle and material force: the dispersal of qi at death disproves rebirth; the Buddhists see the mind but not the principle within it; and their withdrawal from human relations destroys the moral order. He also blames the decline of past dynasties on Buddhist patronage. Completed in 1398, the year of his murder, it was published in 1456 and provided the intellectual basis for the Joseon state's long suppression of Buddhism.
