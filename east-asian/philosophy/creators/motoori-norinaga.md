## Biography

Motoori Norinaga was born into a merchant family in Matsusaka, near the Ise shrines. He studied medicine and Confucian learning in Kyoto, then returned home to practise as a pediatrician, teaching literature and the classics in his spare hours. A single meeting with the scholar Kamo no Mabuchi in 1763 confirmed his life's work, the decipherment of the Kojiki, completed in 1798. He had nearly five hundred registered students.

Norinaga held that Chinese learning, with its moralising and rationalising, had overlaid an older Japanese way of sincere feeling expressed in the ancient myths and poetry. In writings on The Tale of Genji and waka he identified the core of literature as mono no aware, a deep sensitivity to things, and he took the Kojiki myths at their word as the record of the age of the gods. His philology was rigorous; his nativism became an important source for later Shinto nationalism.

### The Essentials of the Tale of Genji (Shibun yōryō) (1763)

- **Date:** 1763
- **Type:** Literary-philosophical treatise
- **Written / first published:** Matsusaka, Ise
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Essentials%20of%20the%20Tale%20of%20Genji%20%28Shibun%20y%C5%8Dry%C5%8D%29%20Motoori%20Norinaga)

In 1763 Norinaga wrote two treatises, the Shibun yōryō on The Tale of Genji and the Isonokami sasamegoto on poetry. Rejecting Buddhist and Confucian readings of Genji as a moral allegory or warning against lust, he argued that the novel's purpose is to make readers know mono no aware, the capacity to be deeply moved by things, and that its characters are judged good or bad by their sensitivity, not by conventional morality. This theory of literature as the expression and cultivation of feeling became one of the cornerstones of Japanese aesthetics and of Norinaga's wider critique of Chinese rationalism.

### Commentary on the Kojiki (Kojiki-den) (1764–1798; printed 1790–1822)

[![Commentary on the Kojiki (Kojiki-den)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/Kojiki_den.jpg/1280px-Kojiki_den.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kojiki_den.jpg)

<sub>Printed volume of the Kojiki-den Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kojiki_den.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1764–1798; printed 1790–1822
- **Type:** Philological commentary
- **Written / first published:** Matsusaka, Ise
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Kojiki%20%28Kojiki-den%29%20Motoori%20Norinaga)

The Kojiki, compiled in 712, records Japan's myths and early imperial history in a mixture of Chinese characters used for meaning and sound that had become almost unreadable. Motoori Norinaga spent thirty-five years reconstructing its readings in ancient Japanese, word by word, and commenting on every passage. In the introductory essay Naobi no mitama he argued that the Kojiki records the true Way of the gods, received from the sun goddess Amaterasu, which needs no Chinese moralising and can only be accepted, not rationalised. The philology was a monumental scholarly achievement still used today; the theology made the Kojiki a central text of later Shinto nationalism.
