## Biography

Zhu Xi was born in 1130 in Fujian, where his father, a minor official, had moved after the Jurchen conquest of the north. He passed the jinshi at eighteen, studied with Li Tong, a follower of the Cheng school, and held relatively few offices, preferring temple sinecures that left time for writing and teaching. He revived the White Deer Grotto Academy, debated Lu Jiuyuan at the Goose Lake Temple in 1175, and served as a lecturer to the emperor for a few weeks in 1194. At his death in 1200 his teaching was proscribed as 'false learning' in a factional purge.

Zhu Xi built a comprehensive system from the ideas of Zhou Dunyi, Zhang Zai and the Cheng brothers: all things combine li (principle) and qi (vital stuff); human nature is principle and therefore good, but is obscured by turbid qi, and one restores it through the investigation of things and reverent self-discipline. He selected the Great Learning, the Analects, the Mencius and the Doctrine of the Mean as the Four Books and wrote commentaries on them that became the official examination standard in China from 1313, and dominant in Joseon Korea and Tokugawa Japan.

### Family Rituals (Zhuzi jiali) (c. 1170s)

- **Date:** c. 1170s
- **Type:** Ritual manual
- **Written / first published:** Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Family%20Rituals%20%28Zhuzi%20jiali%29%20Zhu%20Xi)

Zhu Xi compiled the Family Rituals to give ordinary gentry households a simplified, practical version of ancient ritual, adapted from Sima Guang and others. It covers the ancestral hall, coming-of-age capping, weddings, funerals and mourning grades, and ancestral sacrifices. The manuscript was reportedly stolen and reappeared only after Zhu's death, which later scholars used to question its authenticity, though most now accept it as substantially his. Through official promotion it shaped family life in Ming and Qing China, and above all in Joseon Korea, where its funeral and ancestral rites became the norm of elite society and the subject of fierce controversies.

### Reflections on Things at Hand (Jinsilu) (1175)

[![Reflections on Things at Hand (Jinsilu)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a7/%E6%9C%B1%E7%86%B9%2C_%E5%91%82%E7%A5%96%E8%AC%99%E3%80%8E%E8%BF%91%E6%80%9D%E9%8C%B2%E3%80%8F.jpg/1280px-%E6%9C%B1%E7%86%B9%2C_%E5%91%82%E7%A5%96%E8%AC%99%E3%80%8E%E8%BF%91%E6%80%9D%E9%8C%B2%E3%80%8F.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%9C%B1%E7%86%B9%2C_%E5%91%82%E7%A5%96%E8%AC%99%E3%80%8E%E8%BF%91%E6%80%9D%E9%8C%B2%E3%80%8F.jpg)

<sub>Early printed edition of the Jinsilu by Zhu Xi and Lü Zuqian Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%9C%B1%E7%86%B9%2C_%E5%91%82%E7%A5%96%E8%AC%99%E3%80%8E%E8%BF%91%E6%80%9D%E9%8C%B2%E3%80%8F.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1175
- **Type:** Anthology
- **Written / first published:** Cold Spring Study, Fujian; compiled with Lü Zuqian
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Reflections%20on%20Things%20at%20Hand%20%28Jinsilu%29%20Zhu%20Xi)

In 1175 Lü Zuqian visited Zhu Xi at his retreat in Fujian, and in ten days the two friends selected passages from the four Northern Song masters and arranged them in fourteen chapters, from the substance of the Way through learning, self-cultivation and family life to government and the critique of heterodoxy. The title comes from the Analects, where reflecting on things at hand is called the method of humaneness. Zhu meant it as a ladder to the Four Books. It became the standard introduction to Neo-Confucianism, was used across Korea and Japan with numerous commentaries, and in Wing-tsit Chan's English translation remains the classic entry point to the school.

### Collected Commentaries on the Four Books (Sishu zhangju jizhu) (1190 (revised until 1200))

- **Date:** 1190 (revised until 1200)
- **Type:** Classical commentary
- **Written / first published:** Zhangzhou, Fujian (first printing)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Collected%20Commentaries%20on%20the%20Four%20Books%20%28Sishu%20zhangju%20jizhu%29%20Zhu%20Xi)

Zhu Xi worked on these commentaries for most of his life and first had the four books printed together at Zhangzhou in 1190, continuing to revise the commentary on the Great Learning until days before his death. By grouping the four texts and prescribing the order of reading, first the Great Learning, then the Analects, the Mencius and finally the Doctrine of the Mean, he made them a curriculum, and his glosses read them through his philosophy of principle, nature and the investigation of things. In 1313 the Yuan court made the commentaries the basis of the civil-service examinations, as they remained under the Ming and Qing until 1905; Joseon Korea and Edo Japan took them as standard too. Few works of philosophy have shaped so many lives.

### Classified Conversations of Master Zhu (Zhuzi yulei) (compiled 1270)

- **Date:** compiled 1270
- **Type:** Recorded conversations
- **Written / first published:** Edited by Li Jingde
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Classified%20Conversations%20of%20Master%20Zhu%20%28Zhuzi%20yulei%29%20Zhu%20Xi)

Zhu Xi's many students took notes of his answers to their questions over some thirty years, and in 1270 Li Jingde combined the various collections into a single work of 140 chapters arranged by topic: principle and qi, heaven and earth, ghosts and spirits, human nature, learning, the classics, the Song masters, Buddhism and Daoism, history and literature. Lively and colloquial, the conversations show Zhu working out problems, changing his mind and correcting students. They are indispensable for understanding his metaphysics and were studied intensely in Korea, where Yi Hwang and others compiled selections, and in Japan.
