## Biography

Prince Umayado, posthumously Shōtoku Taishi, was a son of Emperor Yōmei and served as regent for his aunt, Empress Suiko, from 593. The Nihon shoki, compiled a century after his death, credits him with founding temples such as Shitennō-ji and Hōryū-ji, sending embassies to Sui China, establishing the cap-rank system and writing the Seventeen-article Constitution of 604. He died in 622 at his palace in Ikaruga.

Historians debate how many of these achievements are his; the Constitution and the commentaries on three sutras (Sangyō gisho) attributed to him may be later or the work of his court. Yet the figure of Shōtoku became central to Japanese identity as the patron who joined Confucian ideals of harmony and loyal service to Buddhist faith, and he has long been venerated as a bodhisattva, notably by Shinran.

### Seventeen-article Constitution (Jūshichijō kenpō) (604 (traditional date))

- **Date:** 604 (traditional date)
- **Type:** Moral and political injunctions
- **Written / first published:** Asuka, Yamato; preserved in the Nihon shoki (720)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Seventeen-article%20Constitution%20%28J%C5%ABshichij%C5%8D%20kenp%C5%8D%29%20Prince%20Sh%C5%8Dtoku)

The Nihon shoki dates this text to 604 and credits it to Prince Shōtoku. It is not a constitution in the modern sense but a set of moral precepts for officials: it opens by declaring harmony to be precious, urges reverence for the Three Treasures of Buddhism, obedience to imperial commands, decorum, impartial judgement of lawsuits, the appointment of worthy officials and consultation on important matters rather than deciding alone. Its language draws on the Analects, the Book of Rites, Legalist texts and Buddhist sutras. Whether it was written by Shōtoku, by his court, or by the compilers of the Nihon shoki is debated. It has been invoked ever since as the founding statement of Japanese political ethics.

### Commentaries on the Three Sutras (Sangyō gisho) (c. 609–615 (traditional))

[![Commentaries on the Three Sutras (Sangyō gisho)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Lotus_Sutra_written_by_Prince_Sh%C5%8Dtoku.jpg/1280px-Lotus_Sutra_written_by_Prince_Sh%C5%8Dtoku.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lotus_Sutra_written_by_Prince_Sh%C5%8Dtoku.jpg)

<sub>Manuscript of the Lotus Sutra commentary (Hokke gisho) traditionally held to be in Prince Shōtoku's hand Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lotus_Sutra_written_by_Prince_Sh%C5%8Dtoku.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 609–615 (traditional)
- **Type:** Sutra commentaries
- **Written / first published:** Ikaruga, Yamato; manuscript of the Lotus commentary in the Imperial Household collection
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentaries%20on%20the%20Three%20Sutras%20%28Sangy%C5%8D%20gisho%29%20Prince%20Sh%C5%8Dtoku)

Tradition credits Prince Shōtoku with commentaries on three Mahāyāna sutras: the Lotus Sutra, the Śrīmālā Sutra, in which a laywoman queen expounds the dharma, and the Vimalakīrti Sutra, whose hero is a lay bodhisattva. The choice of sutras centred on lay figures suited a lay ruler. The commentaries draw heavily on Chinese works of the Liang dynasty, and whether Shōtoku wrote them or they were brought from China or compiled by his court is debated; a four-scroll manuscript of the Lotus commentary, Hokke gisho, long kept at Hōryū-ji and now in the Imperial collection, is claimed to be in his hand. They stand at the beginning of Japanese Buddhist scholarship.
