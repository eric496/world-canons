# Wang Chong

*王充*

**27 – c. 97** · Chinese  
Born: Shangyu, Kuaiji (Zhejiang) · Died: ?  
Schools & eras: [Yin-Yang & Han Cosmology](../movements/yin-yang-han.md)  

> Eastern Han rationalist whose Lunheng criticised the superstitions and portent-reading of his age.

## Biography

Wang Chong came from a modest family in Kuaiji, in the southeast. He studied at the imperial academy in Luoyang, where, the story goes, he was too poor to buy books and read them standing in the bookshops. He held only minor local posts, quarrelled with superiors, and spent much of his life in retirement writing.

His Lunheng (Discourses Weighed in the Balance) attacks belief in ghosts, portents and Heaven's moral responses as unsupported by evidence, insisting that Heaven acts spontaneously and without purpose, and that claims must be tested by experience. He even criticised Confucius and Mencius where he found them inconsistent. Neglected for centuries, Wang Chong was celebrated in the twentieth century as a forerunner of scientific and materialist thought.

## Key key texts

### Discourses Weighed in the Balance (Lunheng) (c. 70–90)

- **Date:** c. 70–90
- **Type:** Critical essays
- **Written / first published:** Eastern Han China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discourses%20Weighed%20in%20the%20Balance%20%28Lunheng%29%20Wang%20Chong)

Wang Chong wrote the Lunheng over many years as a 'balance' to weigh claims. He argues that Heaven is not a purposive being and does not send portents in response to human conduct, that thunder is not Heaven's anger, that the dead do not become ghosts because the vital fluids that animated them disperse, and that fate, not merit, determines success. He criticised Confucius and Mencius where he found their reasoning faulty and pointed out exaggerations in the classics. The book was little valued in imperial times, but twentieth-century scholars hailed it as an early triumph of naturalism and critical thinking.
