# Nishitani Keiji

*西谷啓治*

**1900 – 1990** · Japanese  
Born: Ishikawa Prefecture · Died: Kyoto  
Schools & eras: [Kyoto School](../movements/kyoto-school.md)  

> Nishida's leading heir, who in Religion and Nothingness confronted European nihilism with the Buddhist standpoint of emptiness.

## Biography

Nishitani Keiji was born in 1900 and studied with Nishida at Kyoto Imperial University, graduating in 1924, and practised Zen at Shōkoku-ji. He studied with Heidegger in Freiburg in 1937–39. Involved in the controversial wartime symposia on 'overcoming modernity', he was purged from teaching during the Occupation, then reinstated in 1952; he taught philosophy and religion at Kyoto and later at Ōtani University until his death in 1990.

In Religion and Nothingness (1961) Nishitani argued that modern science and the death of God open a 'field of nihility' beneath the self, which must be passed through to the 'field of emptiness' (śūnyatā), where things appear in their own suchness. His dialogue with Nietzsche, Meister Eckhart and Heidegger made him a central figure in the global philosophy of religion.

## Key key texts

### Religion and Nothingness (Shūkyō to wa nanika) (1961)

- **Date:** 1961
- **Type:** Philosophy of religion
- **Written / first published:** Tokyo: Sōbunsha
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Religion%20and%20Nothingness%20%28Sh%C5%ABky%C5%8D%20to%20wa%20nanika%29%20Nishitani%20Keiji)

The essays collected as Shūkyō to wa nanika ('What is religion?') ask how religion is possible after science and Nietzsche. Nishitani argues that the modern self, which treats everything as an object of its own representation, is underlain by a 'field of nihility' revealed in anxiety and the sense of meaninglessness. Rather than fleeing it, one must pass through it to the 'field of emptiness' (śūnyatā), where self and things are no longer opposed and each appears in its own mode of being, in a circuminsession with all others. He engages Christianity, Eckhart, Nietzsche and Heidegger, as well as Zen. Translated by Jan Van Bragt in 1982, it is the most influential Kyoto School book in the West.
