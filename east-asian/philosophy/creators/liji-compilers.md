# Compilers of the Book of Rites

*禮記編者*

**c. 400 BC – c. 50 BC** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: ?  
Schools & eras: [Classical Confucianism](../movements/classical-confucianism.md)  
Also in: [Religion](../../religion/creators/liji-compilers.md)  

> The Confucian ritual specialists of the Warring States and Han whose essays were gathered into the Book of Rites, among them the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean.

## Biography

The Liji, or Book of Rites, is an anthology of forty-nine essays on ritual, music, government and moral cultivation compiled in the Former Han; tradition credits the final selection to the scholar Dai Sheng in the first century BC. The essays themselves come from many Confucian hands of the late Warring States and early Han. Tradition attributed two of them to named disciples, the Great Learning to Confucius's pupil Zengzi and the Doctrine of the Mean to his grandson Zisi; modern scholars regard both attributions as uncertain, though excavated manuscripts from Guodian show that related ideas circulated in Zisi's era.

Treated for a thousand years as part of the ritual classics, the two short chapters were lifted out by Song Neo-Confucians, who saw in them a complete programme of self-cultivation and a metaphysics of sincerity. Zhu Xi grouped them with the Analects and the Mencius as the Four Books, which became the core of East Asian schooling and examinations from the fourteenth to the twentieth century.

### Great Learning (Daxue) (c. 3rd–2nd century BC)

- **Date:** c. 3rd–2nd century BC
- **Type:** Classical essay (chapter of the Book of Rites)
- **Written / first published:** China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Great%20Learning%20%28Daxue%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Rites)

The Great Learning is a brief chapter of the Book of Rites, traditionally attributed to Confucius's disciple Zengzi, an attribution modern scholars doubt. It sets out a chain of eight steps: investigating things, extending knowledge, making the will sincere, rectifying the mind, cultivating the person, regulating the family, governing the state and bringing peace to all under heaven. Zhu Xi rearranged the text, added a passage of his own on the investigation of things, and placed it first among the Four Books as the framework of learning. Wang Yangming insisted on the older text and reread its key terms, so arguments over this short essay defined the split between the Cheng-Zhu and Lu-Wang schools, and its eight steps were memorised across East Asia.

### Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong) (c. 3rd–2nd century BC)

- **Date:** c. 3rd–2nd century BC
- **Type:** Classical essay (chapter of the Book of Rites)
- **Written / first published:** China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Doctrine%20of%20the%20Mean%20%28Zhongyong%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Rites)

The Zhongyong, traditionally attributed to Confucius's grandson Zisi, opens by declaring that what Heaven confers is called human nature, following that nature is called the Way, and cultivating the Way is called teaching. It describes a state of equilibrium before emotions arise and harmony when they arise in due measure, and makes sincerity, being true to oneself, the force by which the sage forms a triad with heaven and earth. Song Neo-Confucians valued it as the most metaphysical Confucian text and Zhu Xi made it the culminating book of the Four Books; it was also read closely by Buddhists, and in the West it has been translated as The Doctrine of the Mean or Focusing the Familiar.

### Book of Rites (Liji) (compiled 1st century BC)

<sub>Page of an annotated edition of the Book of Rites Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Annotated_Classic_of_Rites_59.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** compiled 1st century BC
- **Type:** Anthology of ritual essays
- **Written / first published:** Han China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Rites%20%28Liji%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Rites)

The Liji collects writings by Confucian ritual specialists of the late Warring States and Han, ranging from detailed rules of mourning and etiquette to essays on the meaning of ritual, the role of music in shaping emotion, and the ideal society. The chapter 'The Conveyance of Rites' (Liyun) describes an age of Great Unity in which the world was shared by all, a passage Kang Youwei took as his utopian programme. Compiled in the Former Han and associated with Dai Sheng, it became one of the Five Classics, and two of its chapters, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean, were later raised to independent canonical status.
