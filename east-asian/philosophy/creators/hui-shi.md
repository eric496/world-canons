# Hui Shi

*惠施*

**c. 370 BC – c. 310 BC** · Chinese  
Born: State of Song · Died: ?  
Schools & eras: [School of Names](../movements/school-of-names.md)  

> Statesman and logician, friend and sparring partner of Zhuangzi, remembered for ten paradoxes about space, time and sameness.

## Biography

Hui Shi served as chief minister to King Hui of Wei (Liang) in the late fourth century BC and was involved in its diplomacy. The Zhuangzi presents him as Zhuangzi's friend and favourite opponent, notably in the debate over whether one can know the happiness of fish, and records Zhuangzi's grief at his death, since he had lost the one person worth arguing with. The same book says that his writings filled five carts.

None of those writings survives. What remains is the list of ten propositions preserved in the final chapter of the Zhuangzi, such as that the south has no limit and yet has a limit, and that heaven and earth are one body, which seem to argue that all distinctions are relative and to conclude in universal love for all things. Hui Shi is the best-attested member of the School of Names and an important figure for the history of Chinese logic.

## Key key texts

### Hui Shi's Ten Theses (c. 4th century BC)

- **Date:** c. 4th century BC
- **Type:** Paradoxes (preserved in Zhuangzi ch. 33)
- **Written / first published:** China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hui%20Shi%27s%20Ten%20Theses%20Hui%20Shi)

The final chapter of the Zhuangzi, 'All Under Heaven', a Han-era survey of the schools, lists ten theses of Hui Shi: the greatest has nothing outside it and the smallest nothing inside it; heaven is as low as earth; the sun at noon is setting; one sets off for Yue today and arrived yesterday; and finally, love all things, for heaven and earth are one body. It also lists the debaters' paradoxes, such as that an egg has feathers. The text gives no arguments, and scholars reconstruct them as reflections on the relativity of spatial and temporal distinctions. Short as it is, the passage is our main evidence for the ideas of the most important early Chinese logician.
