# Feng Youlan

*馮友蘭*

**1895 – 1990** · Chinese  
Born: Tanghe, Henan · Died: Beijing  
Schools & eras: [New Confucianism](../movements/new-confucianism.md)  

> Author of the standard modern history of Chinese philosophy and of a 'new rational learning' that restated Neo-Confucianism in logical terms.

## Biography

Feng Youlan studied at Peking University and took his doctorate at Columbia in 1923. He taught for decades at Tsinghua University and, during the war, at the Southwest Associated University in Kunming. His two-volume History of Chinese Philosophy (1931–34), translated by Derk Bodde, became the standard work in Chinese and English. After 1949 he stayed on the mainland, repeatedly criticised himself under political pressure, and during the Cultural Revolution was drawn into the campaign against Confucius. He died in Beijing in 1990.

In six books written during the war, beginning with A New Treatise on Neo-Confucianism (Xin lixue, 1939), Feng reconstructed the Cheng-Zhu concept of principle with the tools of Western logical analysis, and described four levels of human life culminating in the 'sphere of heaven and earth'. His histories shaped how both Chinese and Western readers have understood Chinese philosophy.

### A History of Chinese Philosophy (Zhongguo zhexue shi) (1931–1934)

- **Date:** 1931–1934
- **Type:** History of philosophy
- **Written / first published:** Shanghai: Commercial Press
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20History%20of%20Chinese%20Philosophy%20%28Zhongguo%20zhexue%20shi%29%20Feng%20Youlan)

Feng Youlan's history, whose first volume appeared in 1931 and second in 1934, divided Chinese philosophy into an 'Age of the Philosophers' up to the early Han and an 'Age of Classical Learning' from Dong Zhongshu to the end of the Qing. Combining careful use of sources with conceptual analysis on Western lines, it treated each thinker's ideas systematically and gave unusual attention to the School of Names, the later Mohists and Neo-Confucian metaphysics. Unlike Hu Shi, Feng accepted traditional dating less critically but went further into philosophical interpretation. Bodde's English translation (1937–53) made it the main source for Western knowledge of the subject for half a century.

### A New Treatise on Neo-Confucianism (Xin lixue) (1939)

- **Date:** 1939
- **Type:** Philosophical treatise
- **Written / first published:** Changsha: Commercial Press
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20New%20Treatise%20on%20Neo-Confucianism%20%28Xin%20lixue%29%20Feng%20Youlan)

Written while Feng's university had fled the Japanese invasion to the southwest, the Xin lixue 'continues', rather than merely repeats, the Neo-Confucianism of the Cheng brothers and Zhu Xi. Using logical analysis influenced by the new realism he had encountered at Columbia, Feng sets out four formal concepts, principle, qi, the substance of the Way and the Great Whole, which say nothing about actual facts but describe what must be the case for anything to exist. The book was followed by works on society, human life and method, including A New Treatise on the Nature of Man, which describes four spheres of life from the natural to that of heaven and earth. Together they form the most ambitious system of Chinese philosophy in the twentieth century.
