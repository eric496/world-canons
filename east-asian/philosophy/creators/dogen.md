## Biography

Dōgen Kigen was born in Kyoto in 1200 into the aristocracy and was orphaned young. He was ordained on Mount Hiei but, troubled by the question of why practice is needed if all beings already have buddha-nature, moved to Kennin-ji, the Rinzai Zen temple, and in 1223 went to Song China. There he received dharma transmission in the Caodong lineage from Rujing at Mount Tiantong. Returning in 1227, he taught near Kyoto, and in 1243 moved to remote Echizen, where he founded Eihei-ji. He died in 1253.

Dōgen taught shikantaza, 'just sitting', as itself the enactment of awakening: practice and realisation are one. His Shōbōgenzō, written in Japanese rather than classical Chinese, explores 'being-time' (uji), the identity of existence and time, buddha-nature as impermanence, and the dropping off of body and mind. Largely confined to his school for centuries, it was rediscovered in the twentieth century by Watsuji Tetsurō and the Kyoto School and is now read worldwide.

### Universal Recommendation for Zazen (Fukan zazengi) (1227; autograph 1233)

[![Universal Recommendation for Zazen (Fukan zazengi)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/ca/Fukan_Zazengi_Tenpuku_Copy.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fukan_Zazengi_Tenpuku_Copy.JPG)

<sub>Dōgen's autograph of the Fukan zazengi, Tenpuku-era copy of 1233, Eihei-ji Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fukan_Zazengi_Tenpuku_Copy.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1227; autograph 1233
- **Type:** Meditation instruction
- **Written / first published:** Kōshō-ji, Kyoto; autograph at Eihei-ji
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Universal%20Recommendation%20for%20Zazen%20%28Fukan%20zazengi%29%20D%C5%8Dgen)

Dōgen composed the Fukan zazengi soon after returning from China in 1227, to make the practice of zazen available to everyone, and revised it in 1233; the autograph of that version, in elegant Chinese, survives at Eihei-ji and is designated a National Treasure. It explains how to sit, breathe and hold the mind, but also insists that zazen is not a method for attaining awakening: it is 'the dharma gate of ease and joy', the practice-realisation of awakening itself. Recited daily in Sōtō temples, it is the shortest statement of the conviction that runs through all of Dōgen's work.

### Treasury of the True Dharma Eye (Shōbōgenzō) (1231–1253)

- **Date:** 1231–1253
- **Type:** Essays (Zen discourses)
- **Written / first published:** Kōshō-ji, Kyoto and Eihei-ji, Echizen
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Treasury%20of%20the%20True%20Dharma%20Eye%20%28Sh%C5%8Db%C5%8Dgenz%C5%8D%29%20D%C5%8Dgen)

The Shōbōgenzō is a collection of essays, many delivered as talks to Dōgen's monks between 1231 and 1253, that survives in compilations of 75, 12 and later 95 chapters. Written in a Japanese dense with Chinese Buddhist terms, puns and deliberate rereadings of classical phrases, it explores 'Genjōkōan', where to study the self is to forget the self; 'Uji' (being-time), which identifies existence with time; 'Busshō' (buddha-nature), which reads the phrase 'all sentient beings have buddha-nature' as 'all beings are buddha-nature' and identifies buddha-nature with impermanence; and topics of monastic practice. Little studied outside Sōtō for centuries, it was rediscovered by Watsuji Tetsurō in the 1920s and is now recognised as one of the summits of Japanese thought, often compared with Heidegger.
