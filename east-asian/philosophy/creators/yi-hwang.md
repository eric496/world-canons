# Yi Hwang

*李滉*

**1501 – 1570** · Korean  
Born: Andong, Gyeongsang · Died: Andong  
Schools & eras: [Korean Neo-Confucianism](../movements/korean-neo-confucianism.md)  
Also in: [Architecture](../../architecture/creators/yi-hwang.md)  

> Toegye, the most revered Korean Neo-Confucian, who interpreted Zhu Xi with great depth and moral seriousness and is honoured on the 1000-won note.

## Biography

Yi Hwang, known by his pen name Toegye, was born in 1501 near Andong. He passed the civil examination in 1534 and held a succession of posts, but repeatedly retired to the countryside, where he founded the Dosan Seodang, the school around which the Dosan Seowon academy grew after his death. Hundreds of students came to him, and King Seonjo repeatedly called him to court. He died in 1570.

Toegye's interpretation stressed reverent seriousness (gyeong) and the active role of principle: in the Four–Seven debate with Gi Dae-seung he argued that the four moral feelings named by Mencius issue from principle, while the seven ordinary emotions issue from material force. His Ten Diagrams on Sage Learning, presented to the young king, summarises the path of cultivation. His work strongly influenced Japanese Confucians such as Yamazaki Ansai, and the Yeongnam school of his followers dominated southern Korean scholarship.

### Four–Seven Debate Letters (Sadan chiljeong non) (1559–1566)

- **Date:** 1559–1566
- **Type:** Philosophical correspondence
- **Written / first published:** Andong and Hanyang; letters between Yi Hwang and Gi Dae-seung
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Four%E2%80%93Seven%20Debate%20Letters%20%28Sadan%20chiljeong%20non%29%20Yi%20Hwang)

In 1559 Gi Dae-seung (Gobong), twenty-six years Toegye's junior, questioned a formulation in which Yi Hwang had said that the four beginnings of Mencius (compassion, shame, deference and the sense of right) issue from principle and the seven emotions of the Book of Rites from material force. Gi argued that the four are simply the good portion of the seven and that principle and qi cannot be separated. Over seven years of respectful letters Toegye refined his view: in the four, principle issues and qi follows; in the seven, qi issues and principle rides on it. The exchange, later continued by Yi I and Seong Hon, raised questions about the relation of moral and ordinary emotion that Chinese Neo-Confucians had left untouched, and it defined Korean philosophy for three centuries.

### Ten Diagrams on Sage Learning (Seonghak sipdo) (1568)

- **Date:** 1568
- **Type:** Diagrams with commentary
- **Written / first published:** Hanyang; presented to King Seonjo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ten%20Diagrams%20on%20Sage%20Learning%20%28Seonghak%20sipdo%29%20Yi%20Hwang)

In 1568, at the age of sixty-eight, Yi Hwang presented ten diagrams to the seventeen-year-old King Seonjo, so that the king might keep them before him as a screen or album. Seven derive from earlier Chinese and Korean scholars, among them Zhou Dunyi's diagram of the Supreme Ultimate and the Western Inscription, and three are Toegye's own; each is accompanied by a text and his commentary. They lead from the cosmos through the Great Learning and elementary learning to the relation of mind, nature and feelings, the Four-Seven question and the practice of reverence in daily life. Compact and profound, it is the best-known summary of Korean Neo-Confucianism and was frequently printed in Korea and Japan.
