## Biography

Wang Shouren, called Yangming, was the son of a top-ranked examination graduate. As a young man he tried to follow Zhu Xi's method literally by investigating the principle of bamboo for seven days, and fell ill. After passing the jinshi in 1499 he offended the powerful eunuch Liu Jin, was flogged and exiled to Longchang in remote Guizhou, where in 1508 he experienced a sudden enlightenment: principle is not outside the mind. Rehabilitated, he became a highly successful official and general, suppressing rebellions and the uprising of the Prince of Ning in 1519. He died in 1529 returning from a campaign.

Wang taught that the mind is principle, that knowing and acting are one, since to really know filial piety is already to practise it, and in his later years that everyone possesses liangzhi, innate knowing of the good, which one must extend in every situation. His teaching, recorded in the Chuanxi lu, spread rapidly in the late Ming, was carried to Japan as Yōmeigaku, and inspired reformers there and in China down to the twentieth century.

### Instructions for Practical Living (Chuanxi lu) (1518; expanded 1524 and 1556)

- **Date:** 1518; expanded 1524 and 1556
- **Type:** Recorded sayings and letters
- **Written / first published:** Ganzhou, Jiangxi (first printing)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Instructions%20for%20Practical%20Living%20%28Chuanxi%20lu%29%20Wang%20Yangming)

The Chuanxi lu, whose title comes from the Analects phrase about practising what one has been taught, was first printed by Wang Yangming's disciple Xue Kan in 1518; further parts added in 1524 and after Wang's death brought it to three books. In lively dialogues Wang argues that there is no principle outside the mind, so that filial piety is not found in one's parents but in one's own mind; that knowing and acting are one, since seeing a beautiful colour already involves liking it; and that liangzhi, the innate knowing of good, is complete in everyone. The text includes his 'Four-Sentence Teaching' on the original substance of mind beyond good and evil, which split his followers. Its English title comes from Wing-tsit Chan's 1963 translation; in Japan, as Denshūroku, it was a favourite of reformers and activists.

### Inquiry on the Great Learning (Daxue wen) (1527)

- **Date:** 1527
- **Type:** Philosophical essay
- **Written / first published:** Shaoxing, Zhejiang
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Inquiry%20on%20the%20Great%20Learning%20%28Daxue%20wen%29%20Wang%20Yangming)

Written down by his disciple Qian Dehong just before Wang set out on his final campaign in 1527, the Inquiry on the Great Learning was intended as an introduction to his teaching for new students. Wang explains that the great person regards heaven, earth and all things as one body, as shown when we feel alarm at a child about to fall into a well, pity for a frightened animal or regret at a broken tile, and that self-centred desire obscures this humaneness. 'Investigating things' means correcting the mind's intentions in each affair, and 'extending knowledge' means extending innate knowing. It is the most concise statement of his mature philosophy.
