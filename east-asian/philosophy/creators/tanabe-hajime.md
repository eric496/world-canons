# Tanabe Hajime

*田辺元*

**1885 – 1962** · Japanese  
Born: Tokyo · Died: Kita-Karuizawa, Gunma  
Schools & eras: [Kyoto School](../movements/kyoto-school.md)  

> Nishida's successor and critic at Kyoto, whose 'logic of species' and wartime 'philosophy as metanoetics' made him the second pillar of the Kyoto School.

## Biography

Tanabe Hajime studied mathematics and then philosophy at Tokyo Imperial University, and in 1919 Nishida invited him to Kyoto. He studied in Germany in 1922–24 with Husserl and Heidegger. Succeeding Nishida in 1927, he soon criticised his teacher's philosophy. During the 1930s he developed a 'logic of species' that gave the nation a mediating role between individual and humanity. He retired in 1945 to a mountain cottage in Kita-Karuizawa, where he lived until his death in 1962.

In Philosophy as Metanoetics (1946) Tanabe confessed that his reason had failed in the face of the war and that philosophy must pass through repentance (zange) and self-surrender to an 'other power', drawing on Shinran. He also wrote on mathematics and physics, and later on death and Christianity.

## Key key texts

### Philosophy as Metanoetics (Zangedō to shite no tetsugaku) (1946)

- **Date:** 1946
- **Type:** Philosophical treatise
- **Written / first published:** Tokyo: Iwanami Shoten
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Philosophy%20as%20Metanoetics%20%28Zanged%C5%8D%20to%20shite%20no%20tetsugaku%29%20Tanabe%20Hajime)

Tanabe delivered the substance of this book as lectures in the autumn of 1944, as Japan's defeat loomed, and published it in 1946. Feeling that he had neither resisted nor rightly guided his nation, he concluded that philosophy itself must undergo zange, repentance or metanoia: reason must recognise its own powerlessness and surrender to an 'other power' that restores it in a new form. Drawing on Shinran's Pure Land faith and on Kant, Hegel and Kierkegaard, he called this 'metanoetics', a philosophy that is not his own work but the action of absolute nothingness through him. The book is a rare example of a philosopher's public self-criticism after the war.
