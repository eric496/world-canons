# Dai Zhen

*戴震*

**1724 – 1777** · Chinese  
Born: Xiuning, Anhui · Died: Beijing  
Schools & eras: [Ming–Qing Critique & Evidential Learning](../movements/evidential-learning.md)  

> Leading kaozheng scholar of the Qing, who used philology to attack the Neo-Confucian opposition of principle and desire.

<sub>Portrait: Traditional portrait of Dai Zhen · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dai_Zhen.png)</sub>

## Biography

Dai Zhen was the son of a cloth merchant in Huizhou. Largely self-taught in mathematics, astronomy and phonology, he made his name in Beijing as a brilliant textual scholar but repeatedly failed the metropolitan examination. In 1773 he was appointed to the editorial board of the imperial Complete Library of the Four Treasuries, and was given the jinshi degree by special decree in 1775. He died in Beijing in 1777.

Dai made philology a philosophical method: by recovering the ancient meanings of key words one could understand the sages directly. In his Evidential Study of the Meaning of Terms in the Mencius he argued that principle is the pattern found within things and feelings, not a separate entity, and that Song Confucians, by setting principle against desire, had let the powerful 'kill people with principle'. Neglected in his time, this critique was celebrated by Hu Shi and other modern thinkers.

## Key key texts

### Evidential Study of the Meaning of Terms in the Mencius (Mengzi ziyi shuzheng) (1777)

- **Date:** 1777
- **Type:** Philosophical lexicon
- **Written / first published:** Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Evidential%20Study%20of%20the%20Meaning%20of%20Terms%20in%20the%20Mencius%20%28Mengzi%20ziyi%20shuzheng%29%20Dai%20Zhen)

Completed shortly before his death, this work takes the form of a lexicon of key terms in the Mencius, principle (li), the Way, nature, talent, humaneness and others, each explained through evidence from ancient texts. Dai Zhen argues that principle originally meant the fine pattern within things, discovered by careful examination, not a metaphysical entity implanted in the mind; that desires and feelings are part of human nature and principle is their proper fulfilment; and that Song Confucians, by opposing principle to desire, enabled the powerful to condemn the weak in the name of principle. Little read in his lifetime, it was championed by Zhang Binglin, Liang Qichao and Hu Shi as a precursor of modern humanism.
