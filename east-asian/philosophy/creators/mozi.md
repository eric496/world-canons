# Mozi

*墨子*

**c. 470 BC – c. 391 BC** · Chinese  
Born: probably Lu or Song · Died: ?  
Schools & eras: [Mohism](../movements/mohism.md)  

> Founder of Mohism, who argued against the Confucians for impartial care, frugality and the condemnation of offensive war.

## Biography

Mo Di, called Mozi ('Master Mo'), lived in the fifth century BC, probably in the state of Lu or Song; his dates are uncertain. He may have come from the artisan class and seems to have been trained in Confucian learning before turning against it. He founded a tightly organised community with its own leaders and discipline, whose members served as defensive engineers for states threatened with attack; a famous story tells how he walked ten days to Chu and dissuaded its king from besieging Song.

Mozi judged doctrines by three tests, including whether they benefit the people, and on this basis argued for jian ai, impartial concern for everyone, against the graded, family-first love of the Confucians. He condemned aggressive war, extravagant funerals and music as wasteful, and held that Heaven wills human welfare. Mohism was a major rival of Confucianism for two centuries, then disappeared after the Han; Qing philologists and modern thinkers rediscovered it, praising its logic and its consequentialist ethics.

### Mozi (c. 430–250 BC)

- **Date:** c. 430–250 BC
- **Type:** Philosophical treatise (school text)
- **Written / first published:** China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mozi%20Mozi)

The Mozi survives in fifty-three of an original seventy-one chapters, written by Mozi's followers over several generations. Its core consists of ten doctrines, each in up to three versions that may reflect rival factions: elevating the worthy, conforming upward, impartial care, condemning offensive war, frugal use, frugal funerals, Heaven's intent, explaining ghosts, condemning music and condemning fatalism. The essays argue methodically, appealing to the precedents of the sage-kings, the evidence of ordinary people's eyes and ears, and benefit to the people. Further chapters record dialogues and describe techniques for defending cities. Long neglected, the book was edited by Qing scholars, especially Sun Yirang, and modern readers have compared its ethics to utilitarianism.

### Mohist Canons (c. 300 BC)

- **Date:** c. 300 BC
- **Type:** Logical and scientific treatise
- **Written / first published:** China (preserved in the Mozi)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mohist%20Canons%20Mozi)

Six chapters of the Mozi, the Canons with their Explanations and the Greater and Lesser Selections, were written by later Mohists in the third century BC, not by Mozi himself. They define terms such as knowledge, benefit, duration and space, distinguish kinds of names, and analyse argument by analogy; other entries describe the pinhole image, plane mirrors and the lever. Badly corrupted in transmission, they became readable only after Qing and twentieth-century reconstruction, notably by A. C. Graham, and are now recognised as the high point of ancient Chinese logic and science.
