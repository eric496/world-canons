> Warring States thinker whose name is attached to the Zhuangzi, a dazzling book of parables, paradoxes and arguments about perspective, freedom and the Dao.

<sub>Portrait: Zhuangzi dreaming of a butterfly, Chinese painting (an imagined scene) · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dschuang-Dsi-Schmetterlingstraum-Zhuangzi-Butterfly-Dream.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Very little is known of Zhuang Zhou. Sima Qian says he came from Meng in the state of Song, served for a time as a minor official in a lacquer garden, and declined an invitation to become chief minister of Chu, preferring, in his own famous image, to drag his tail in the mud like a living turtle rather than be revered as a dead one in a temple. Anecdotes in the book show him debating with the logician Hui Shi, apparently his friend. He lived in the late fourth century BC.

The book that bears his name was assembled by many hands; scholars generally attribute the seven 'Inner Chapters' to Zhuangzi himself or his circle. It questions whether any viewpoint can claim final authority, as in the story of Zhuang Zhou dreaming he was a butterfly, and recommends a free, wandering responsiveness to the transformations of things. Its craftsmen, cripples and talking trees made it one of the great works of Chinese literature as well as philosophy, and it deeply shaped Chan Buddhism, painting and poetry.

### Discussion on Making All Things Equal (Qiwulun) (c. 300 BC)

[![Discussion on Making All Things Equal (Qiwulun)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c1/Dschuang-Dsi-Schmetterlingstraum-Zhuangzi-Butterfly-Dream.jpg/1280px-Dschuang-Dsi-Schmetterlingstraum-Zhuangzi-Butterfly-Dream.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dschuang-Dsi-Schmetterlingstraum-Zhuangzi-Butterfly-Dream.jpg)

<sub>Zhuangzi dreaming of a butterfly, Chinese painting Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dschuang-Dsi-Schmetterlingstraum-Zhuangzi-Butterfly-Dream.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 300 BC
- **Type:** Philosophical essay (chapter 2 of the Zhuangzi)
- **Written / first published:** China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discussion%20on%20Making%20All%20Things%20Equal%20%28Qiwulun%29%20Zhuangzi)

The second of the Inner Chapters begins with a sage who has 'lost himself' and hears the piping of heaven, and proceeds through a dense argument about how every judgement of right and wrong depends on a viewpoint, so that Confucians and Mohists affirm what the other denies. Zhuangzi recommends a 'clarity' that follows things as they are rather than taking sides, and plays with logical puzzles of the School of Names. It ends with Zhuang Zhou dreaming he is a butterfly and, on waking, wondering whether he is a butterfly dreaming he is Zhuang Zhou. The chapter is one of the most discussed texts in Chinese philosophy and has been compared to Pyrrhonian scepticism and to relativism.

### Zhuangzi (c. 4th–2nd century BC)

[![Zhuangzi](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/95/Zhuangzi_Song_Edition.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zhuangzi_Song_Edition.jpg)

<sub>Page of a Song-dynasty edition of the Zhuangzi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zhuangzi_Song_Edition.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 4th–2nd century BC
- **Type:** Philosophical anthology
- **Written / first published:** China; arranged by Guo Xiang c. 300 AD
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Zhuangzi%20Zhuangzi)

The Zhuangzi opens with a giant fish that becomes a bird flying ninety thousand li, mocked by a cicada and a dove, and goes on to unsettle every fixed standpoint: the cook Ding carves an ox effortlessly by following its natural joints, the useless tree survives because no carpenter wants it, and Zhuangzi drums on a pot after his wife's death. The Inner Chapters are generally ascribed to Zhuangzi or his close circle; the Outer and Miscellaneous Chapters contain material by Primitivists, Yangists, syncretists and others. Guo Xiang's edition of about 300 AD fixed the received text. The book is both a philosophical masterpiece and one of the great works of Chinese prose, and it shaped Xuanxue, Chan Buddhism, landscape painting and poetry.
