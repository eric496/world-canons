# Fukuzawa Yukichi

*福澤諭吉*

**1835 – 1901** · Japanese  
Born: Osaka · Died: Tokyo  
Schools & eras: [Reform & Enlightenment](../movements/modern-reform.md)  

> The leading advocate of Western learning in Meiji Japan, founder of Keiō University, whose books preached individual independence and civilisation.

## Biography

Fukuzawa Yukichi was born in Osaka to a low-ranking samurai of the Nakatsu domain. He studied Dutch learning in Nagasaki and with Ogata Kōan in Osaka, then taught himself English, and travelled to the United States in 1860 and to Europe in 1862 with shogunate missions. His Conditions in the West (Seiyō jijō) became a bestseller. He founded the school that became Keiō University and the newspaper Jiji shinpō, and refused government office throughout his life.

An Encouragement of Learning (1872–76) opens by denying that heaven creates one person above another, and urges practical learning as the basis of personal and national independence. In An Outline of a Theory of Civilization (1875) he argued that civilisation is the progress of knowledge and virtue of a whole people. Late in life he wrote in favour of Japan's leaving Asia behind, a position whose meaning and authorship are still debated. His portrait appeared on the ten-thousand-yen note from 1984 to 2024.

### An Encouragement of Learning (Gakumon no susume) (1872–1876)

- **Date:** 1872–1876
- **Type:** Pamphlet series
- **Written / first published:** Tokyo; published by Keiō Gijuku
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=An%20Encouragement%20of%20Learning%20%28Gakumon%20no%20susume%29%20Fukuzawa%20Yukichi)

Fukuzawa Yukichi's series opens with the famous statement that, it is said, heaven does not create one person above or below another, and argues that differences in status come from learning. By learning he meant practical knowledge, reading, writing, arithmetic, geography, physics, economics and ethics, which enables people to stand on their own and so to build an independent nation. Later pamphlets discuss the equality of nations, the duties of citizens, the evils of servility and the role of private initiative. Written in plain Japanese, the series sold, by Fukuzawa's estimate, over three million copies, about one for every ten Japanese, and shaped the values of Meiji education.

### An Outline of a Theory of Civilization (Bunmeiron no gairyaku) (1875)

[![An Outline of a Theory of Civilization (Bunmeiron no gairyaku)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/04/%E6%96%87%E6%98%8E%E8%AB%96%E4%B9%8B%E6%A6%82%E7%95%A5.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%96%87%E6%98%8E%E8%AB%96%E4%B9%8B%E6%A6%82%E7%95%A5.png)

<sub>Title page of the first edition of Bunmeiron no gairyaku, 1875 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E6%96%87%E6%98%8E%E8%AB%96%E4%B9%8B%E6%A6%82%E7%95%A5.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1875
- **Type:** Treatise
- **Written / first published:** Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=An%20Outline%20of%20a%20Theory%20of%20Civilization%20%28Bunmeiron%20no%20gairyaku%29%20Fukuzawa%20Yukichi)

Fukuzawa's most systematic work describes civilisation as a relative and progressive state, measured by the knowledge and virtue of the whole people rather than by its rulers. Drawing on the historians François Guizot and Henry Thomas Buckle, he argued that Western nations were 'civilised', China and Japan 'half-civilised', and that Japan's traditional imbalance of power, which concentrated authority in the government, had stunted the people's spirit. The goal of civilisation in Japan's present situation, he concluded, was the preservation of national independence. The book is the classic of the Meiji enlightenment and was re-examined in detail by Maruyama Masao.
