# Authors of the Book of Changes

*易經作者*

**c. 1000 BC – c. 200 BC** · Chinese  
Born: Zhou China · Died: ?  
Schools & eras: [Yin-Yang & Han Cosmology](../movements/yin-yang-han.md)  

> The anonymous diviners and commentators who built the Book of Changes over most of a millennium, from the Western Zhou line texts to the Warring States and Han 'Ten Wings'.

## Biography

The Yijing (I Ching) is not the work of one person. Tradition credited the eight trigrams to the mythical sage Fu Xi, the sixty-four hexagrams and their judgements to King Wen of Zhou, the line statements to the Duke of Zhou, and the appended commentaries, the 'Ten Wings', to Confucius. Modern scholarship sees instead a divination manual whose core texts took shape among court diviners of the Western Zhou, around the ninth century BC, and commentaries written by many hands in the late Warring States and early Han.

Excavated manuscripts have transformed the picture. A silk Yijing with otherwise unknown commentaries was found at Mawangdui (buried 168 BC), and a bamboo text of the Changes at Shanghai Museum dates from the fourth century BC. The commentators turned an oracle into a philosophy of change, in which yin and yang alternate and the sage learns to read the moment. Every later school, from Wang Bi to Zhu Xi to Wang Fuzhi, wrote on it.

### Book of Changes (Yijing) (c. 9th century BC – 2nd century BC)

[![Book of Changes (Yijing)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/I_Ching_Song_Dynasty_print.jpg/960px-I_Ching_Song_Dynasty_print.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:I_Ching_Song_Dynasty_print.jpg)

<sub>Page of a Song-dynasty printed edition of the Book of Changes Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:I_Ching_Song_Dynasty_print.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 9th century BC – 2nd century BC
- **Type:** Divination classic with commentaries
- **Written / first published:** Zhou China; received text fixed in the Han
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Changes%20%28Yijing%29%20Authors%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Changes)

The Yijing, or Zhouyi, began as a manual for divining with yarrow stalks: each of its sixty-four hexagrams, figures of six broken or unbroken lines, carries a short judgement and a statement for each line, terse images of journeys, marriages, battles and sacrifices. Its core probably took shape at the Western Zhou court. In the late Warring States and early Han, commentators added the Ten Wings, which read the lines as yin and yang, the hexagrams as stages of cosmic transformation, and the book as a guide for the sage to act in harmony with the times. Made the first of the Five Classics under the Han, it attracted more commentary than any other Chinese book, from Wang Bi's metaphysics to Cheng Yi's ethics and Zhu Xi's return to divination, and it travelled to Korea, Japan and, from the seventeenth century, Europe, where Leibniz saw binary arithmetic in its diagrams.

### Great Commentary (Xici zhuan) (c. 3rd–2nd century BC)

- **Date:** c. 3rd–2nd century BC
- **Type:** Commentary (one of the Ten Wings)
- **Written / first published:** China; a version is among the Mawangdui silk manuscripts
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Great%20Commentary%20%28Xici%20zhuan%29%20Authors%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Changes)

The Xici, or Appended Statements, is a two-part essay attached to the Book of Changes. It explains how the sages created the trigrams by observing heaven, earth and animals, how divination works, and how the hexagrams model the ceaseless 'production and reproduction' of the world. Its formulas, that the alternation of yin and yang is called the Dao and that the Changes has the Great Ultimate (taiji) which produces the two modes, became the starting point for Zhou Dunyi's diagram and much of Neo-Confucian cosmology. A version slightly different from the received text was found among the Mawangdui silk manuscripts buried in 168 BC, showing that the commentary was circulating in the early Han.
