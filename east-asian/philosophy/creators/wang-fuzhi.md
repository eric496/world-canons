## Biography

Wang Fuzhi, called Chuanshan, came from a scholarly family in Hengyang. He passed the provincial examination in 1642, fought against the Manchus, and served the Southern Ming court of the Yongli emperor before factional strife drove him away. From about 1660 he lived in poverty in the hills of Hunan, refusing to shave his head in the Manchu fashion, and wrote ceaselessly. His work circulated hardly at all until the nineteenth century, when the Hunanese statesman Zeng Guofan published it.

Wang developed Zhang Zai's philosophy of qi into a thoroughgoing view that principle exists only in concrete things and their changes, and that the Way is inseparable from the instruments that embody it. In works such as his commentary on the Comprehensive Mirror he read history as a process whose institutions change with circumstances. His anti-Manchu passages made him a hero of late Qing revolutionaries, and Marxist historians later claimed him as a materialist.

### Outer Commentary on the Book of Changes (Zhouyi waizhuan) (1655)

- **Date:** 1655
- **Type:** Philosophical commentary
- **Written / first published:** Hunan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Outer%20Commentary%20on%20the%20Book%20of%20Changes%20%28Zhouyi%20waizhuan%29%20Wang%20Fuzhi)

Written during Wang Fuzhi's years of hiding in Hunan, this 'outer' commentary, as opposed to his line-by-line 'inner' commentary, uses the Book of Changes to develop his own philosophy. Against Neo-Confucian talk of a principle prior to things, he insists that the Way exists only in concrete instruments (qi 器), that without the thing there is no Way, and that reality is a continuous process of the vital stuff (qi 氣) giving rise to ever-new forms. Unknown in his lifetime, it was printed in the nineteenth century and has since been read as a culmination of Chinese philosophies of change.

### On Reading the Comprehensive Mirror (Du Tongjian lun) (1691)

- **Date:** 1691
- **Type:** Historical essays
- **Written / first published:** Hunan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20Reading%20the%20Comprehensive%20Mirror%20%28Du%20Tongjian%20lun%29%20Wang%20Fuzhi)

In one of his last works Wang Fuzhi commented, reign by reign, on the events recorded in Sima Guang's Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government, from the Warring States to the end of the Five Dynasties. He judges rulers and ministers, but also argues that historical circumstances (shi) shape what is possible, that the abolition of feudal domains under the Qin was a necessary development despite the Qin's wickedness, and that laws suited to one era are unsuitable to another. His fierce distinctions between Chinese and 'barbarians' made the book a resource for anti-Manchu revolutionaries; its sense of historical process has interested modern philosophers of history.
