## Biography

Wang Bi came from a distinguished family of scholars and grew up in Luoyang, the Wei capital, where his precocious talent impressed the leaders of the 'pure conversation' circles. He held a minor court post, became caught up in the factional purges of 249, and died of illness that autumn at the age of twenty-three.

In his short life Wang Bi wrote commentaries on the Laozi and on the Yijing, together with an outline of the Changes, that set the terms of Xuanxue, the 'learning of the mysterious'. He argued that all things are rooted in wu, nothingness or the formless, which cannot be named, and that the meaning of a text lies beyond its words and images, which should be forgotten once grasped. His Laozi commentary is the basis of the standard received text, and his Changes commentary was the official one for the Tang examinations.

### Commentary on the Laozi (c. 245)

- **Date:** c. 245
- **Type:** Philosophical commentary
- **Written / first published:** Luoyang, Wei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Laozi%20Wang%20Bi)

Written when Wang Bi was barely twenty, the commentary reads the Laozi as a single coherent argument: all things that have form and name arise from wu, the formless and nameless, which is the 'root' to which the ruler must hold in order to govern without imposing. Wang draws out the political consequences, that a ruler who holds the One allows the many to settle themselves, and ties the text to his reading of the Book of Changes. His essay Outline of the Laozi accompanies it. The version of the Daodejing transmitted with his commentary became the standard text, and his interpretation remains one of the two classical readings of the book, alongside that of Heshang Gong.

### Outline of the Book of Changes (Zhouyi lüeli) (c. 245)

- **Date:** c. 245
- **Type:** Philosophical essay and commentary
- **Written / first published:** Luoyang, Wei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Outline%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Changes%20%28Zhouyi%20l%C3%BCeli%29%20Wang%20Bi)

Wang Bi wrote a commentary on the Book of Changes and this accompanying outline, which swept away the numerological and omen-based interpretation of the Han. He argued that each hexagram captures a single situation, governed by one ruling line, and that words exist to explain images and images to express meaning, so the interpreter, like the fisherman who forgets the trap once he has the fish (an image from the Zhuangzi), should not cling to them. Wang Bi's commentary, completed by Han Kangbo, became the officially recognised one in the Tang, and his principle of 'getting the meaning and forgetting the words' influenced later Chinese hermeneutics and Buddhist readings of scripture.
