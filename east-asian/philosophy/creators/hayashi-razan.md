## Biography

Hayashi Razan studied Zen at Kennin-ji in Kyoto as a youth but turned to Neo-Confucianism, becoming a student of Fujiwara Seika. In 1605 he entered the service of Tokugawa Ieyasu, drafting documents and advising on law, ritual and diplomacy for him and his successors, and took the tonsure as a lay Buddhist to fit court conventions. In 1630 the shogunate gave him land in Edo for a school, the ancestor of the Shōheikō academy, which his descendants headed until the 1860s.

Razan expounded Zhu Xi's metaphysics of principle and material force and applied it to the Tokugawa order: the hierarchy of ruler and subject, like heaven above and earth below, reflects principle itself. He attacked Buddhism and Christianity, sought to harmonise Confucianism with Shinto, and compiled a history of Japan. His family's position made Cheng-Zhu learning the orthodox Confucianism of Edo Japan.

## Key key texts

### Treatise on the Three Virtues (Santokushō) (early 17th century)

- **Date:** early 17th century
- **Type:** Confucian primer
- **Written / first published:** Edo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Treatise%20on%20the%20Three%20Virtues%20%28Santokush%C5%8D%29%20Hayashi%20Razan)

The Santokushō takes its theme from the Doctrine of the Mean, which names wisdom, humaneness and courage as the three universal virtues, and expounds them in simple Japanese for samurai and other readers who could not read Chinese. Hayashi Razan explains Zhu Xi's teaching that the virtues are principle inherent in the mind and that cultivation requires reverent seriousness, and applies it to the hierarchy of Tokugawa society. Works of this kind, alongside his lectures and his role at the shogunate, did much to make Neo-Confucian vocabulary part of Edo culture, though the dating and attribution of some of Razan's Japanese writings are uncertain.
