## Biography

Cheng Yi, known as Yichuan, studied as a youth with Zhou Dunyi, impressed the imperial academy with an essay on what Yan Hui loved to learn, but held little office, spending most of his life teaching in Luoyang. Briefly a lecturer to the young emperor Zhezong, he was later banished as a member of the conservative faction, and his teachings were banned for a time. He outlived his brother by over twenty years.

Cheng Yi made li, principle, the central concept of Confucian philosophy: every thing has its principle, all principles are ultimately one, and the way to sagehood is to investigate things and extend knowledge while cultivating reverential seriousness (jing). He wrote a major commentary on the Book of Changes. Zhu Xi took him as his principal model, so that the orthodox school bears both their names.

### Commentary on the Book of Changes (Yichuan yizhuan) (1099)

- **Date:** 1099
- **Type:** Philosophical commentary
- **Written / first published:** Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Book%20of%20Changes%20%28Yichuan%20yizhuan%29%20Cheng%20Yi)

Cheng Yi worked on his commentary to the Book of Changes for decades and completed its preface in 1099, during his political exile in Fuzhou. Discarding numerology, he read each hexagram as a situation in human affairs, in which the cultivated person discerns the principle of the moment and responds rightly. The preface states one of the central formulas of Neo-Confucianism: substance and function come from one source, and there is no gap between the manifest and the hidden. Zhu Xi supplemented it with his own commentary that restored the divinatory dimension, and the two together were official for the Ming examinations.

### Surviving Works of the Chengs of Henan (Henan Chengshi yishu) (compiled 1168)

- **Date:** compiled 1168
- **Type:** Recorded sayings
- **Written / first published:** Edited by Zhu Xi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Surviving%20Works%20of%20the%20Chengs%20of%20Henan%20%28Henan%20Chengshi%20yishu%29%20Cheng%20Yi)

After the deaths of Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi, their disciples circulated notes of their conversations. Zhu Xi gathered these into the Yishu (Surviving Works) of twenty-five chapters in 1168, later adding a supplement. Many sayings are attributed simply to 'the master', so distinguishing the brothers' views has been a perennial scholarly problem. Here appear the Chengs' famous statements that 'nature is principle', that cultivation requires reverence and learning requires the extension of knowledge, and that principle is one while its manifestations are many. The collection was the main source from which Zhu Xi built his system and the model for the genre of Neo-Confucian recorded sayings.
