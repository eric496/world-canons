> Northern Song thinker who grounded ethics in a philosophy of qi and wrote the Western Inscription on the unity of all beings.

## Biography

Zhang Zai, known as Hengqu after his home in Shaanxi, was drawn as a young man to military strategy and then, on the advice of the reforming statesman Fan Zhongyan, to the Doctrine of the Mean. He studied Buddhism and Daoism before returning to the classics, passed the jinshi in 1057, and served briefly at court before withdrawing to teach. He was an uncle of the Cheng brothers, with whom he debated.

Zhang argued that everything is made of qi, which condenses into things and disperses into the Great Void, so that the void is not nothing, as Buddhists and Daoists claimed. From this he drew a moral vision: in the Western Inscription, heaven is father and earth mother, and all people are siblings. His naturalism was revived by Wang Fuzhi in the seventeenth century and his idea of qi underlies much later Korean Neo-Confucian debate.

### Western Inscription (Ximing) (c. 1070s)

- **Date:** c. 1070s
- **Type:** Inscription (short essay)
- **Written / first published:** Study of Zhang Zai, Hengqu, Shaanxi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Western%20Inscription%20%28Ximing%29%20Zhang%20Zai)

Zhang Zai inscribed two short texts on the walls of his study; the western one, originally titled 'Correcting Obstinacy', became one of the most loved texts of Neo-Confucianism. It begins by calling Heaven father and Earth mother and the self a small being dwelling in their midst, and from this draws an ethic of universal kinship: all people are siblings and all things companions; caring for the old and orphaned is serving one's parents; and, at its close, living means following and serving, and death is peace. Cheng Yi defended it against the charge of Mohist impartial love by formulating the principle that 'principle is one but its manifestations are many', and Zhu Xi and Korean scholars wrote commentaries on it.

### Correcting Youthful Ignorance (Zhengmeng) (1076)

- **Date:** 1076
- **Type:** Philosophical treatise
- **Written / first published:** Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Correcting%20Youthful%20Ignorance%20%28Zhengmeng%29%20Zhang%20Zai)

In the Zhengmeng Zhang Zai sets out his systematic thought in seventeen sections. All things are condensations of qi, which in its dispersed state is the Great Void, so that being and void are two states of one stuff, and Buddhist and Daoist claims that the world arises from emptiness are mistaken. The Great Harmony is the Dao; human beings have both a heavenly nature and a physical nature, and learning transforms the latter. The Western Inscription is drawn from its final section. The book was later the main inspiration for Wang Fuzhi, whose commentary on it is one of his chief works, and for Korean thinkers who stressed qi.
