# Liang Qichao

*梁啓超*

**1873 – 1929** · Chinese  
Born: Xinhui, Guangdong · Died: Beijing  
Schools & eras: [Reform & Enlightenment](../movements/modern-reform.md)  

> Journalist and scholar, Kang Youwei's disciple, whose essays introduced a generation of Chinese readers to nationalism, citizenship and Western thought.

## Biography

Liang Qichao passed the provincial examination at sixteen and became Kang Youwei's leading student. He was a central figure in the 1898 reforms and, after their collapse, fled to Japan, where he edited the journals Qingyi bao and Xinmin congbao from Yokohama, reaching readers across the Chinese world. After the 1911 revolution he returned and served briefly as minister of justice and of finance in the Republic, then devoted himself to teaching and scholarship, notably at Tsinghua University.

Liang's brilliant, accessible prose popularised social Darwinism, nationalism and political theory, and his essay series on the 'new citizen' argued that China needed citizens with public virtue rather than loyal subjects. He wrote pioneering intellectual histories, including a survey of Qing scholarship. After visiting Europe in 1919 he grew critical of Western materialism and more sympathetic to Chinese tradition.

### On the New Citizen (Xinmin shuo) (1902–1906)

- **Date:** 1902–1906
- **Type:** Essay series
- **Written / first published:** Yokohama, in the Xinmin congbao
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=On%20the%20New%20Citizen%20%28Xinmin%20shuo%29%20Liang%20Qichao)

Published serially from 1902 in Liang Qichao's Yokohama journal Xinmin congbao (New Citizen Journal), these essays argued that China's weakness lay less in its government than in its people, who had been subjects of dynasties rather than citizens of a nation. Liang called for public morality, national consciousness, a spirit of adventure, rights and duties, freedom, self-government and progress, drawing on Japanese translations of Western political thought and social Darwinism. Written in a vivid new style, the essays were read by a generation of young Chinese, including Mao Zedong and Hu Shi.

### Intellectual Trends in the Qing Period (Qingdai xueshu gailun) (1920)

- **Date:** 1920
- **Type:** Intellectual history
- **Written / first published:** Shanghai
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Intellectual%20Trends%20in%20the%20Qing%20Period%20%28Qingdai%20xueshu%20gailun%29%20Liang%20Qichao)

Written in two weeks in 1920 as a preface to another book and published separately the next year, this survey describes Qing learning as a cycle of birth, flourishing, transformation and decline, in which reaction against Ming philosophy led to Gu Yanwu and Huang Zongxi, then to the evidential learning of Dai Zhen and the Qianlong era, and finally to the New Text school of Kang Youwei and Liang himself. He compared the movement to the European Renaissance. The book, translated into English by Immanuel Hsü, shaped the modern understanding of Qing thought for decades.
