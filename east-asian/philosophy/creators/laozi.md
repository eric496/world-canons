> The legendary sage to whom the Daodejing is attributed. Whether he was a single historical person, and when he lived, has been debated since antiquity.

<sub>Portrait: Zhang Lu, Laozi Riding an Ox, Ming dynasty, National Palace Museum, Taipei (an imagined portrait) · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zhang_Lu-Laozi_Riding_an_Ox_%28cropped%29.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

According to the historian Sima Qian, writing around 100 BC, Laozi ('Old Master') was Li Er, an archivist at the Zhou court and an older contemporary of Confucius, who on leaving China through a western pass wrote down a book in five thousand characters for the gatekeeper and vanished. Yet Sima Qian himself recorded rival identifications, and modern scholars widely doubt that the Daodejing was written by one person in the sixth century BC. The Guodian bamboo slips of about 300 BC contain only part of the text, arranged differently, which suggests the book grew over time from sayings collected in the fifth and fourth centuries BC.

Whoever Laozi was, the text bearing his name became one of the most translated books in the world. It describes the Dao as a nameless source that produces all things without striving, and urges rulers and individuals toward wuwei (non-coercive action), softness, humility and few desires. Han Feizi, Huang-Lao statecraft, Wang Bi's metaphysics and the Daoist religion, which deified Laozi as Lord Lao, each read him differently; he is a central figure of Chinese thought regardless of his historicity.

### Daodejing (c. 4th century BC (earliest manuscript c. 300 BC))

- **Date:** c. 4th century BC (earliest manuscript c. 300 BC)
- **Type:** Philosophical poem
- **Written / first published:** China; earliest witness from the Guodian tomb, Hubei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Daodejing%20Laozi)

The Daodejing ('Classic of the Way and its Power'), also called the Laozi, opens with the claim that the Dao that can be spoken of is not the constant Dao. In compressed, rhythmic chapters it describes the Dao as an empty, generative source, like a valley or a mother, and recommends that rulers and individuals practise wuwei, acting without forcing, so that things order themselves; water, which yields yet wears away stone, is its emblem. Its date and authorship are debated: the Guodian bamboo manuscripts of about 300 BC contain about two-fifths of the text in a different order, and the two Mawangdui silk copies of the early Han place the 'De' part first. The text has been read as political strategy, mystical philosophy, a manual of self-cultivation and Daoist scripture, and it is among the most translated books in the world.

### Guodian Laozi (c. 300 BC (buried))

[![Guodian Laozi](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/TaiYiShengShui1.jpg/1280px-TaiYiShengShui1.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:TaiYiShengShui1.jpg)

<sub>Guodian slips of 'The Great One Gave Birth to Water', found bundled with the third Laozi group Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:TaiYiShengShui1.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 300 BC (buried)
- **Type:** Bamboo-slip manuscript
- **Written / first published:** Tomb 1, Guodian, Jingmen, Hubei; Hubei Provincial Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Guodian%20Laozi%20Laozi)

In 1993 archaeologists excavating a Chu tomb at Guodian in Hubei found about eight hundred bamboo slips with writings sealed around 300 BC. Among them were three bundles containing passages that correspond to about a third to two-fifths of the received Daodejing, in a different order and without some of its anti-Confucian lines, together with a previously unknown cosmogony, 'The Great One Gave Birth to Water', and many Confucian essays possibly related to Zisi. The find showed that Laozi sayings circulated in the fourth century BC, but also that the book was still taking shape, sparking intense debate about its formation. The other Guodian texts have transformed the study of Confucianism between Confucius and Mencius.

### Mawangdui Silk Manuscripts of the Laozi (before 168 BC)

[![Mawangdui Silk Manuscripts of the Laozi](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ed/Mawangdui_LaoTsu_Ms2.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mawangdui_LaoTsu_Ms2.JPG)

<sub>Section of a Mawangdui silk manuscript of the Laozi, Hunan Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mawangdui_LaoTsu_Ms2.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** before 168 BC
- **Type:** Silk manuscripts
- **Written / first published:** Tomb 3, Mawangdui, Changsha; Hunan Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mawangdui%20Silk%20Manuscripts%20of%20the%20Laozi%20Laozi)

Tomb 3 at Mawangdui near Changsha, the burial of a son of the Marquis of Dai sealed in 168 BC, contained a library of texts written on silk, among them two complete copies of the Laozi, known as A and B, together with versions of the Book of Changes and texts associated with Huang-Lao thought. Both Laozi copies reverse the received order of the book's two halves and differ in many readings from the version transmitted by Wang Bi. Discovered in 1973 and published soon after, they became the basis of a new wave of translations and reopened questions about how the classics were transmitted.
