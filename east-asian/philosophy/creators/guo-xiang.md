# Guo Xiang

*郭象*

**c. 252 – 312** · Chinese  
Born: Luoyang · Died: ?  
Schools & eras: [Xuanxue (Learning of the Mysterious)](../movements/xuanxue.md)  

> Jin-dynasty official and philosopher whose edition and commentary created the received thirty-three-chapter Zhuangzi.

## Biography

Guo Xiang was an official of the Western Jin and an admired speaker in pure-conversation circles. He served the prince Sima Yue as a high official during the turbulent War of the Eight Princes. Even in antiquity he was accused of having appropriated an unfinished commentary by Xiang Xiu; modern scholars think he built on Xiang's work but developed his own philosophy.

Guo Xiang edited the sprawling Zhuangzi materials into the thirty-three chapters we read today and wrote the commentary that accompanied them. Against Wang Bi he denied that there is any creator or root: every thing 'self-so' produces itself and has its own allotted nature, and fulfilling that nature is freedom, so even a sage-ruler busy in government can be at ease. The idea of self-transformation (duhua) made his commentary a cornerstone of Xuanxue.

## Key key texts

### Commentary on the Zhuangzi (c. 300)

- **Date:** c. 300
- **Type:** Philosophical commentary
- **Written / first published:** Luoyang, Western Jin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentary%20on%20the%20Zhuangzi%20Guo%20Xiang)

Guo Xiang reduced the fifty-two-chapter Zhuangzi then in circulation to thirty-three, cutting material he considered spurious, and wrote a running commentary, probably building on the lost work of Xiang Xiu. In it he denies that there is any creator, Dao or nothingness from which things come: each thing is 'self-so' and transforms on its own, and contentment lies in fulfilling one's allotted nature, whether one is a great bird or a small one. The sage, he argued, can be fully engaged in affairs while remaining inwardly free. The commentary fixed the text that all later readers used and was a major source of Xuanxue and of Chinese Buddhist thought.
