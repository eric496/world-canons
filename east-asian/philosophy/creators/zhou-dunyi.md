## Biography

Zhou Dunyi, known as Lianxi, spent his career as a conscientious provincial official in the south, admired for his fairness as a judge. For a short time he taught the teenage Cheng brothers. In his last years he retired to a study by a stream below Mount Lu, which he named after the Lian stream of his home. His love of the lotus, rising clean from the mud, became the subject of a celebrated short essay.

His two short works, the Taijitu shuo and the Tongshu, were made central by Zhu Xi, who placed him at the head of the Neo-Confucian lineage. Drawing on a Daoist diagram, Zhou described how the Supreme Ultimate generates yin and yang and the Five Phases and so all things, and how the sage, by stillness and sincerity, embodies that order. Whether his ideas derive from Daoist sources was hotly debated by later Confucians.

### Penetrating the Book of Changes (Tongshu) (c. 1060s)

- **Date:** c. 1060s
- **Type:** Philosophical aphorisms
- **Written / first published:** Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Penetrating%20the%20Book%20of%20Changes%20%28Tongshu%29%20Zhou%20Dunyi)

The Tongshu, also known as Yi Tongshu, is a series of brief chapters that develop the ethical side of Zhou Dunyi's cosmology. Sincerity (cheng), he argues, is the foundation of the sage and the source of the five constant virtues; it is quiescent and without action, yet at the subtle point where thought first stirs, good and evil divide. Sagehood can be learned, and its key is having 'no desire'. The text joined the language of the Book of Changes to the Doctrine of the Mean and helped set the agenda for the Cheng brothers and Zhu Xi.

### Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate (Taijitu shuo) (c. 1070)

[![Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate (Taijitu shuo)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a0/ZhoushiTaijitu.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ZhoushiTaijitu.png)

<sub>Zhou Dunyi's Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ZhoushiTaijitu.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1070
- **Type:** Diagram with commentary
- **Written / first published:** Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Explanation%20of%20the%20Diagram%20of%20the%20Supreme%20Ultimate%20%28Taijitu%20shuo%29%20Zhou%20Dunyi)

Zhou Dunyi's short text explains a diagram of concentric circles and linked shapes, probably adapted from a Daoist chart, and opens with the phrase 'Non-polar and yet Supreme Polarity' (wuji er taiji). Movement and stillness of the Supreme Ultimate produce yang and yin, which transform into the Five Phases, and their union produces the myriad things and human beings, the most spiritually responsive of them; the sage settles himself with the mean, correctness, humaneness and righteousness, and makes stillness primary. Zhu Xi placed it at the head of his anthology Reflections on Things at Hand. Its opening words provoked a famous debate between Zhu Xi and Lu Jiuyuan over whether 'non-polar' betrayed a Daoist source.
