# Daoist alchemists

*煉丹家*

**c. 300 – c. 1100** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: China  
Eras & fields: [Chinese alchemy & pharmacology](../movements/chinese-alchemy.md), [Gunpowder & the compass](../movements/gunpowder-compass.md)  

> The mostly anonymous Daoist adepts of Tang and earlier China whose experiments with saltpetre, sulphur and carbon produced the first gunpowder.

## Biography

Chinese alchemy, waidan or 'outer elixir', sought drugs of immortality by heating minerals in sealed vessels. Its texts, preserved in the Daoist Canon, were written by many hands, often under the names of immortals, from Ge Hong's time onward.

A ninth-century alchemical text, the Zhenyuan Miaodao Yaolüe, warns that heating saltpetre, sulphur and realgar with honey has caused flames to burn the hands and face and even the house. This is usually taken as the earliest record of gunpowder, whose military use followed within a century.

## Key works & inventions

### Gunpowder (c. 850)

<sub>'Flying crow with magic fire', a gunpowder rocket bomb, from the Ming military manual Huolongjing Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%27Flying_Crow_With_Magic_Fire%27%2C_a_winged_rocket_bomb.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 850
- **Type:** Invention
- **Published / where:** Tang China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gunpowder%20Daoist%20alchemists)

Gunpowder seems to have been discovered by alchemists heating saltpetre with sulphur and carbonaceous materials in search of elixirs; a ninth-century text warns of the fires such mixtures cause. By the tenth century it was used in incendiary arrows, and the Wujing Zongyao of 1044 gives the first recipes. Over the following centuries the Song, Jin and Mongols developed fire lances, exploding bombs, rockets and, by the late thirteenth century, metal-barrelled guns; the oldest surviving bronze guns date from about 1290. The Mongol conquests and trade carried knowledge of gunpowder west, and it appears in European and Islamic sources in the second half of the thirteenth century, with transformative effects on warfare everywhere.
