# Chinese compass makers

*指南針製作者*

**c. 200 BC – c. 1100** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: China  
Eras & fields: [Gunpowder & the compass](../movements/gunpowder-compass.md)  

> The anonymous geomancers, artisans and sailors of China who developed the magnetic compass, from the lodestone spoon to the floating needle used at sea.

## Biography

Lodestone was known in China by the third century BC, and Han texts mention a 'south-governor' used in divination, often interpreted as a lodestone spoon turning on a polished board, though this reading is debated. Geomancers, who sited houses and tombs by the lie of the land, were the main users.

By the eleventh century needles magnetised by rubbing on lodestone were floated on water or hung on a silk thread, as Shen Kuo describes, and around 1100 Zhu Yu records sailors using a needle at sea in dark weather. The mariner's compass reached Europe and the Islamic world by about 1190–1230, though whether by transmission or independent invention is still debated.

## Key works & inventions

### Magnetic compass (c. 1040–1117)

[![Magnetic compass](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/Chinese_compass_nach_fabris.jpg/1280px-Chinese_compass_nach_fabris.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chinese_compass_nach_fabris.jpg)

<sub>Model of a Chinese lodestone spoon on a divination board Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chinese_compass_nach_fabris.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1040–1117
- **Type:** Navigational instrument
- **Published / where:** Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Magnetic%20compass%20Chinese%20compass%20makers)

Chinese texts describe the attraction of lodestone by the third century BC, and Han and later diviners may have used a lodestone spoon on a board for geomancy, though the evidence is disputed. By the eleventh century both the floating iron fish of the Wujing Zongyao and Shen Kuo's needle rubbed on lodestone were known, and Shen Kuo observed magnetic declination, the difference between magnetic and true south. Zhu Yu's Pingzhou Ketan, written about 1117 about Guangzhou around 1100, records that pilots used the needle at night or in overcast weather. The compass appears in European writing by Alexander Neckam about 1190 and in the Islamic world soon after. It made open-sea navigation possible and, with gunpowder and printing, figures in Francis Bacon's list of inventions that changed the world.
