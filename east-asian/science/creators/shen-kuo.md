# Shen Kuo

*沈括*

**1031 – 1095** · Chinese  
Born: Qiantang (Hangzhou) · Died: Runzhou (Zhenjiang)  
Eras & fields: [Song science & engineering](../movements/song-science.md), [Gunpowder & the compass](../movements/gunpowder-compass.md)  

> Song statesman and polymath whose Dream Pool Essays contain the first clear description of the magnetic compass and notes on fossils, optics, printing and mathematics.

## Biography

Shen Kuo passed the civil-service examination in 1063 and served the reforming chancellor Wang Anshi as director of the astronomical bureau, finance commissioner, diplomat to the Liao and military commander on the north-western frontier. After a defeat in 1082 he was dismissed and retired to his garden, the Dream Brook, at Runzhou.

There he wrote the Dream Pool Essays, notes on everything from music and divination to astronomy and engineering. He described the magnetic needle and its declination from true north, argued from fossil shells in mountains and petrified bamboo that land and climate had changed over time, explained the camera obscura and recorded Bi Sheng's movable type. Western historians since Joseph Needham have treated him as one of the most remarkable scientific minds in Chinese history.

## Key works & inventions

### Dream Pool Essays (1088)

[![Dream Pool Essays](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/41/Mengxi_bitan.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mengxi_bitan.jpg)

<sub>Page of a printed edition of the Mengxi Bitan Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mengxi_bitan.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1088
- **Type:** Scientific notebook
- **Published / where:** Runzhou (Zhenjiang), Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dream%20Pool%20Essays%20Shen%20Kuo)

Shen Kuo wrote the Mengxi Bitan in retirement at his estate, the Dream Brook, and it was published around 1088, with two supplements. Its entries mix anecdote and history with first-hand observation. He describes the magnetic needle, suspended by a silk thread, and notes that it points slightly east of south; explains the formation of mountains and alluvial plains by erosion and sedimentation, citing marine fossils high inland; infers climate change from petrified bamboo in the dry north; discusses the camera obscura and concave mirrors; works out the sum of stacked objects and the length of arcs; and records Bi Sheng's movable type and the iron-smelting and oil of his time. Its range has made it a favourite source for historians of Chinese science.
