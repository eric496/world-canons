# Tu Youyou

*屠呦呦*

**1930** · Chinese  
Born: Ningbo, Zhejiang · Died: ?  
Eras & fields: [Science since 1945](../movements/contemporary-science.md), [Chinese alchemy & pharmacology](../movements/chinese-alchemy.md)  

> Chinese pharmaceutical chemist who discovered artemisinin, a key malaria drug, in 1972 and in 2015 became the first Chinese woman to win a Nobel Prize in science.

## Biography

Tu Youyou studied pharmacy at Beijing Medical College and joined the Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine in 1955. In 1969 she was appointed to lead a group in Project 523, a secret military programme to find drugs against chloroquine-resistant malaria during the Vietnam War.

Screening traditional remedies, her team found Ge Hong's fourth-century advice to steep sweet wormwood in cold water, and she extracted the active compound at low temperature. Artemisinin and its derivatives became the core of malaria treatment worldwide, saving millions of lives. She shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

## Key works & inventions

### Discovery of artemisinin (1972)

> 🖼️ **[View it at Nobel Prize →](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2015/tu/facts/)**  
> <sub>Still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here.</sub>

- **Date:** 1972
- **Type:** Pharmacological discovery
- **Published / where:** Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discovery%20of%20artemisinin%20Tu%20Youyou)

In Project 523 Tu Youyou's group screened some 2,000 traditional remedies for activity against malaria. Extracts of qinghao, Artemisia annua, gave inconsistent results until Tu, rereading Ge Hong's Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies, noticed that it called for steeping the plant in cold water, and reasoned that heat destroyed the active principle. In 1971 an ether extraction at low temperature completely cleared malaria parasites in mice and monkeys, Tu tested it on herself, and in 1972 the pure compound, qinghaosu or artemisinin, was isolated; its structure was determined later in the decade. Published mainly in Chinese during the Cultural Revolution, the work became widely known only later. Artemisinin-based combination therapies are now the standard treatment for malaria, and Tu shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2015.
