# Qin Jiushao

*秦九韶*

**c. 1202 – c. 1261** · Chinese  
Born: Puzhou, Sichuan (probably) · Died: Meizhou, Guangdong  
Eras & fields: [Song–Yuan mathematics](../movements/song-yuan-mathematics.md)  

> Southern Song mathematician whose Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections (1247) gave the general solution of the Chinese remainder problem and a numerical method for equations of any degree.

## Biography

Qin Jiushao, son of an official from Sichuan, learned astronomy at the Song capital and served in military and civil posts amid the Mongol wars. Contemporaries described him as able but greedy and ruthless, and he died in a provincial post in the far south after falling from favour.

His treatise of 1247 contains the dayan rule for solving simultaneous congruences, now called the Chinese remainder theorem, and solves polynomial equations up to the tenth degree by a method equivalent to the one later named after Horner. He also gave a formula for the area of a triangle from its three sides.

## Key works & inventions

### Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections (1247)

[![Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/42/Shu_Shu_Jiu_Zhang_skqs.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shu_Shu_Jiu_Zhang_skqs.jpg)

<sub>Page of the Shushu Jiuzhang in the Siku Quanshu copy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shu_Shu_Jiu_Zhang_skqs.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1247
- **Type:** Mathematical treatise
- **Published / where:** Southern Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mathematical%20Treatise%20in%20Nine%20Sections%20Qin%20Jiushao)

Qin Jiushao's Shushu Jiuzhang contains 81 problems in nine sections on calendars, land, surveying, taxes, construction, military matters and trade, many deliberately complicated. The first section gives the dayan qiuyi method for finding a number with given remainders when divided by several numbers, including non-coprime ones, a full solution of what became known as the Chinese remainder theorem, used for calendar computations. Other problems lead to polynomial equations up to the tenth degree, solved on the counting board by a step-by-step procedure equivalent to Horner's method of 1819. The book also gives Qin's formula for the area of a triangle from its sides, equivalent to Heron's. It circulated in manuscript and was printed only in 1842.
