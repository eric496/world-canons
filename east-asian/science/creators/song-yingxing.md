# Song Yingxing

*宋應星*

**1587 – c. 1666** · Chinese  
Born: Fengxin, Jiangxi · Died: Fengxin, Jiangxi  
Eras & fields: [Ming technology & natural history](../movements/ming-science.md)  

> Late Ming scholar whose illustrated Tiangong Kaiwu (1637) is the classic survey of Chinese agriculture and industry.

## Biography

Song Yingxing passed the provincial examination in 1615 but failed the metropolitan examination repeatedly. He served as a county teacher and minor official in Jiangxi and withdrew to private life when the Ming fell in 1644.

His Exploitation of the Works of Nature describes in eighteen chapters, with over a hundred woodcuts, the production of grain, salt, sugar, ceramics, metals, weapons, paper, ink, textiles, dyes and ships. Ignored in Qing China, it survived in Japan and was rediscovered in the twentieth century as a unique record of pre-industrial technology.

## Key works & inventions

### Tiangong Kaiwu (1637)

[![Tiangong Kaiwu](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/91/Tiangong_Kaiwu_-_Page_1.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tiangong_Kaiwu_-_Page_1.png)

<sub>First page of the Tiangong Kaiwu Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tiangong_Kaiwu_-_Page_1.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1637
- **Type:** Encyclopedia of technology
- **Published / where:** Ming China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tiangong%20Kaiwu%20Song%20Yingxing)

In eighteen sections, arranged from things most needed for life to the least, Song Yingxing described the growing and processing of grain, clothing fibres and dyes, salt, sugar, oils, pottery, bronze casting, boats and carts, iron forging, lime and coal, paper, metal smelting, weapons including gunpowder, ink and pearls and jade. More than a hundred woodcuts show workers at furnaces, looms and mills. Song wrote from close observation and criticised scholars who scorned practical knowledge. Published in 1637, it was soon forgotten in Qing China but reprinted in Japan in 1771, where it influenced Edo technology; Chinese scholars recovered it from Japanese copies in the 1920s, and it is now a classic of the history of technology.
