# Su Song

*蘇頌*

**1020 – 1101** · Chinese  
Born: Tong'an, Quanzhou (Fujian) · Died: Runzhou (Zhenjiang)  
Eras & fields: [Song science & engineering](../movements/song-science.md)  

> Song statesman and scientist who built a 12-metre water-powered astronomical clock tower at Kaifeng and described it in an illustrated treatise.

## Biography

Su Song rose through the Song bureaucracy to become chancellor in 1092. Sent on embassy to the Liao in 1077, he noticed that the Liao calendar was more accurate than the Song's, and was commissioned to build a new armillary clock.

Completed in 1092 with the official Han Gonglian, his tower had an armillary sphere on top, a rotating celestial globe inside and a pagoda of puppets announcing the hours, all driven by a waterwheel controlled by an escapement. His treatise Xin Yixiang Fayao records it in exact drawings, and he also edited a great illustrated materia medica. The tower was lost when the Jurchen took Kaifeng in 1127, but has since been reconstructed in models.

### Su Song's astronomical clock tower (1088–1092)

[![Su Song's astronomical clock tower](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/46/Clock_Tower_from_Su_Song%27s_Book_desmear.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Clock_Tower_from_Su_Song%27s_Book_desmear.JPG)

<sub>The clock tower, illustration from Su Song's Xin Yixiang Fayao Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Clock_Tower_from_Su_Song%27s_Book_desmear.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1088–1092
- **Type:** Astronomical clock
- **Published / where:** Kaifeng, Song China (destroyed)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Su%20Song%27s%20astronomical%20clock%20tower%20Su%20Song)

Su Song's tower stood in the Song capital Kaifeng from about 1090. On its open roof was a bronze armillary sphere for observation; in the middle storey a celestial globe turned in step with the sky; below, a five-tiered pagoda displayed jacks that rang bells, struck gongs and held up tablets to mark the hours and quarters. All were driven by a large waterwheel with 36 scoops, filled one at a time from a constant-level tank and released by a weighbridge and trip lever acting as an escapement, so that the wheel advanced in regular steps. A chain drive transmitted the power upward. The tower was dismantled after the Jurchen captured Kaifeng in 1127, and no later Chinese builder matched it, but Su Song's book made full-scale reconstructions possible in the twentieth century.

### Xin Yixiang Fayao (1094)

[![Xin Yixiang Fayao](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e1/Chain_drive%2C_Su_Song%27s_book_of_1092.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chain_drive%2C_Su_Song%27s_book_of_1092.jpg)

<sub>Drawing of the chain drive of the clock tower, from Su Song's treatise Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chain_drive%2C_Su_Song%27s_book_of_1092.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1094
- **Type:** Illustrated technical treatise
- **Published / where:** Kaifeng, Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Xin%20Yixiang%20Fayao%20Su%20Song)

Su Song wrote the Xin Yixiang Fayao to record the design of his clock tower so that it could be built again. In three chapters it gives an overview drawing of the tower and some fifty detailed drawings of the armillary, the globe, the waterwheel, the escapement, the chain drive and the jacks, with dimensions. It also includes five star maps showing over 1,400 stars, among the oldest printed star charts. The book survived in later copies, and its drawings allowed Joseph Needham and others to explain the mechanism and to build working models.
