## Biography

Xu Guangqi, from a modest family in Shanghai, met the Jesuits in the 1590s, was baptised Paul in 1603 and passed the highest examination in 1604. Working with Matteo Ricci in Beijing he translated the first six books of Euclid's Elements (1607). He rose to become Grand Secretary, the highest office in the Ming government, shortly before his death.

From 1629 he directed the Calendar Bureau, bringing in Jesuit astronomers to compile the Chongzhen calendar, and he promoted Western-style cannon for defence against the Manchus. His Complete Treatise on Agriculture, published in 1639, gathered Chinese and Western knowledge of farming, irrigation and famine relief, including the sweet potato. He is honoured as a pioneer of Chinese science and the district of Xujiahui in Shanghai is named after his family.

### Chongzhen calendar (1629–1634)

- **Date:** 1629–1634
- **Type:** Astronomical compendium
- **Published / where:** Beijing, Ming China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chongzhen%20calendar%20Xu%20Guangqi)

After Xu Guangqi's prediction of a solar eclipse in 1629 proved more accurate than that of the official astronomers, the Chongzhen Emperor put him in charge of a new calendar bureau. With the Jesuits Johann Schreck, Giacomo Rho and Adam Schall, and Chinese scholars, he compiled the Chongzhen Lishu, 137 chapters on Tychonic planetary theory, spherical trigonometry, logarithm-free tables and instruments, presented between 1631 and 1634. The Ming fell before the calendar was adopted, but Schall presented a revised version to the Qing in 1645 as the Shixian calendar, and it guided Chinese astronomy for the next two centuries.

### Nongzheng Quanshu (1639)

[![Nongzheng Quanshu](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Nongzhengquanshu.jpg/1280px-Nongzhengquanshu.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nongzhengquanshu.jpg)

<sub>Page of the Nongzheng Quanshu Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nongzhengquanshu.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1639
- **Type:** Agricultural encyclopedia
- **Published / where:** Ming China (published posthumously)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nongzheng%20Quanshu%20Xu%20Guangqi)

Xu Guangqi worked on agricultural questions for decades, experimenting with crops in Shanghai and Tianjin. His Nongzheng Quanshu, edited by Chen Zilong and published in 1639, has sixty chapters on land policy, soils, irrigation, tools, crops, sericulture, forestry, animal husbandry and famine plants. It includes the Taixi Shuifa, a treatise on Western hydraulic machines written with the Jesuit Sabatino de Ursis, and promotes the sweet potato, recently arrived from the Americas, as a famine crop. It became a standard work for officials concerned with food supply.
