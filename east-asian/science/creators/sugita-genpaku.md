## Biography

Sugita Genpaku was physician to the Obama domain in Edo, trained in surgery. In 1771 he and Maeno Ryōtaku watched the dissection of an executed criminal and found that the body matched the plates of the Dutch Ontleedkundige Tafelen and not Chinese medical charts.

With little Dutch, they and Nakagawa Jun'an spent over three years translating the book, published as Kaitai Shinsho, 'New Book of Anatomy', in 1774. In old age Sugita wrote Rangaku Kotohajime, a memoir of these beginnings. He trained many students and is seen as a founder of modern medicine in Japan.

### Kaitai Shinsho (1774)

[![Kaitai Shinsho](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/91/Kaitai_shinsyo01.jpg/1280px-Kaitai_shinsyo01.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kaitai_shinsyo01.jpg)

<sub>Frontispiece of the Kaitai Shinsho, 1774 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kaitai_shinsyo01.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1774
- **Type:** Anatomical translation
- **Published / where:** Edo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kaitai%20Shinsho%20Sugita%20Genpaku)

After seeing a dissection in 1771 that matched the plates of a Dutch anatomy book rather than Chinese diagrams, Sugita Genpaku, Maeno Ryōtaku, Nakagawa Jun'an and others began translating the Ontleedkundige Tafelen, a Dutch version of the German anatomy of Johann Adam Kulmus. With only Maeno's limited Dutch and no dictionary, they worked for three and a half years, sometimes spending a day on one line. The result, published in four volumes of text and one of illustrations by Odano Naotake, introduced Japanese readers to European anatomy and coined words such as shinkei, nerve, still in use in Japanese and Chinese. Its success showed that Dutch books could be read and launched the movement of Dutch learning.

### Rangaku Kotohajime (1815)

[![Rangaku Kotohajime](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a4/Rangakukoto-hajime-1869.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rangakukoto-hajime-1869.jpg)

<sub>The 1869 printed edition of Rangaku Kotohajime Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rangakukoto-hajime-1869.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1815
- **Type:** Memoir
- **Published / where:** Edo, Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rangaku%20Kotohajime%20Sugita%20Genpaku)

Written when Sugita was over eighty, Rangaku Kotohajime, 'The Beginnings of Dutch Learning', recounts the early contacts of Japanese physicians and interpreters with Dutch books, the dissection of 1771 at Kotsugahara, and the struggle to translate the anatomy text word by word, as well as the spread of rangaku afterwards. It circulated in manuscript until Fukuzawa Yukichi had it printed in 1869, presenting it as a story of Japan's early opening to Western science. It remains the classic account of the movement and a vivid picture of scholarly collaboration.
