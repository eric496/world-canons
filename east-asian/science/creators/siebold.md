## Biography

Siebold arrived at Dejima in 1823 as physician to the Dutch trading post and was allowed to open a school, the Narutaki-juku, outside Nagasaki, where he taught medicine and natural history to Japanese students. In 1828 he was found to possess forbidden maps of Japan, derived from Inō's surveys, and was expelled.

Back in Leiden he published Nippon, Flora Japonica and Fauna Japonica, the fullest European descriptions of Japan for decades, and introduced many Japanese plants to European gardens. He returned to Japan in 1859–62. His daughter Kusumoto Ine became the first Japanese woman trained in Western medicine.

## Key works & inventions

### Flora Japonica (1835–1870)

[![Flora Japonica](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4d/Siebold-Flora_Japonica-Teil1-1835-Kupfertitel.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Siebold-Flora_Japonica-Teil1-1835-Kupfertitel.png)

<sub>Engraved title page of Flora Japonica, part 1, 1835 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Siebold-Flora_Japonica-Teil1-1835-Kupfertitel.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1835–1870
- **Type:** Illustrated botany
- **Published / where:** Leiden, Netherlands
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Flora%20Japonica%20Philipp%20Franz%20von%20Siebold)

From his years at Dejima, Siebold brought back to Europe thousands of herbarium specimens, seeds and living plants, as well as drawings by the Nagasaki artist Kawahara Keiga and others. With the Munich botanist Zuccarini he published Flora Japonica in instalments from 1835, with 150 hand-coloured plates describing hydrangeas, camellias, lilies, maples and many other species, several named for the first time. His companion works, Fauna Japonica and the multi-volume Nippon, described Japan's animals, geography and culture. His nursery in Leiden spread Japanese plants through Europe, and his collections are in Leiden and Munich.
