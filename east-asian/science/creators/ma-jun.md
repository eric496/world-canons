# Ma Jun

*馬鈞*

**c. 200 – c. 265** · Chinese  
Born: Fufeng, Shaanxi · Died: China  
Eras & fields: [Paper & Han technology](../movements/han-technology.md)  

> Engineer of the Cao Wei state who reconstructed the south-pointing chariot and improved the silk loom and the square-pallet chain pump.

## Biography

Ma Jun was a minor official of Wei known for his stammer and his mechanical talent; the main account of him is an essay by the writer Fu Xuan. In a famous court debate he insisted that the legendary south-pointing chariot could be built, and then built it.

The chariot used differential gearing, not magnetism, to keep a figure pointing south whatever way the vehicle turned. He is also credited with a simplified silk loom, a water-powered puppet theatre and an improved chain pump for irrigation. His machines are lost, but his story shows the honoured place of engineering in early China.

## Key works & inventions

### South-pointing chariot (c. 235)

[![South-pointing chariot](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/South-pointing_chariot_%28Science_Museum_model%29.jpg/1280px-South-pointing_chariot_%28Science_Museum_model%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:South-pointing_chariot_%28Science_Museum_model%29.jpg)

<sub>Model of a south-pointing chariot, Science Museum, London Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:South-pointing_chariot_%28Science_Museum_model%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 235
- **Type:** Machine
- **Published / where:** Cao Wei, China (lost)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=South-pointing%20chariot%20Ma%20Jun)

The south-pointing chariot carried a figure with an outstretched arm that pointed in one direction however the vehicle turned. Legends credited it to the Yellow Emperor, but the first reliable accounts concern Ma Jun, who built one for the Wei court around 235 after a debate about whether such a thing was possible. Since it used no magnet, it must have worked by gears linking the two wheels, so that when one wheel moved further than the other in a turn, the figure turned by the same angle the other way; this amounts to a differential gear. Later versions were built under the Song, when the engineers Yan Su and Wu Deren left detailed descriptions of the gearing.
