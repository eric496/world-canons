## Biography

Cai Lun entered the palace as a eunuch under Emperor Ming and rose to direct the imperial workshops that made weapons and instruments. The official History of the Later Han records that in 105 he presented to Emperor He a paper made from tree bark, hemp ends, old rags and fishing nets, and was rewarded with a title. Caught up in court intrigue, he took poison in 121.

Archaeology has found paper made of hemp fibre from the second century BC, so Cai Lun did not invent paper outright. His achievement was to systematise the use of cheap raw materials and promote paper as a writing surface. He was later honoured as the patron of papermakers, and the craft spread to Korea and Japan by the seventh century and to the Islamic world by the eighth.

## Key works & inventions

### Invention of paper (105)

[![Invention of paper](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5b/Making_Paper.gif)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Making_Paper.gif)

<sub>Lifting a sheet of paper from the vat, woodcut from Song Yingxing's Tiangong Kaiwu, 1637 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Making_Paper.gif).</sub>

- **Date:** 105
- **Type:** Invention
- **Published / where:** Luoyang, Han China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Invention%20of%20paper%20Cai%20Lun)

Paper is made by beating plant fibres in water until they separate, catching the pulp on a fine screen and drying the mat into a sheet. Fragments of hemp paper from western China date from the second century BC, used at first for wrapping, but the Han history credits the court eunuch Cai Lun with presenting in 105 a paper made from mulberry bark, hemp waste, rags and old fishing nets, and he is remembered as its inventor. Over the next centuries paper replaced bamboo strips and silk for writing in China. Papermaking reached Korea and Japan by about the seventh century, Samarkand and Baghdad in the eighth, and Spain and Italy in the twelfth and thirteenth, where it made the printing revolution possible.
