## Biography

Zhang Ji, known as Zhongjing, lived through the epidemics and wars of the late Han. In his preface he says that more than two-thirds of his large clan died within ten years, most of them of 'cold damage', and that this drove him to collect old prescriptions and study the classics. Later tradition made him governor of Changsha, but this is uncertain.

His treatise organised fevers into six stages, each matched to a pattern of symptoms and a specific prescription, joining the theory of the Inner Classic to practical treatment. Its formulas, many still in use, are the basis of kampō medicine in Japan and of much traditional prescribing in China and Korea.

## Key works & inventions

### Shanghan Zabing Lun (c. 200–210)

- **Date:** c. 200–210
- **Type:** Clinical medical treatise
- **Published / where:** China; later divided into Shanghan Lun and Jingui Yaolüe
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Shanghan%20Zabing%20Lun%20Zhang%20Zhongjing)

Written around 200–210 amid the epidemics of the late Han, Zhang Zhongjing's treatise was scattered in the following wars and rearranged by Wang Shuhe; the Song editorial office printed it in two parts, the Treatise on Cold Damage (Shanghan Lun) and the Essentials from the Golden Cabinet (Jingui Yaolüe). The first describes the progression of feverish illness through six stages, from the 'greater yang' on the surface to the 'reverting yin' deep in the body, each defined by a set of signs, and gives 113 prescriptions with exact ingredients and preparation. The method of 'pattern identification and treatment' it embodies remains the core of Chinese clinical medicine, and from the seventeenth century its formulas became the basis of the Japanese koihō school of kampō.
