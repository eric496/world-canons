## Biography

Ricci studied law in Rome, joined the Jesuits in 1571 and learned mathematics from Christopher Clavius. Sent to Goa and Macau, he entered China in 1583 with Michele Ruggieri, learned classical Chinese, adopted the dress of a Confucian scholar and reached Beijing in 1601, where he lived under the Wanli Emperor's protection until his death.

He won the respect of scholars with his memory, his clocks and prisms, and above all his world maps and his translations with Xu Guangqi and Li Zhizao of Euclid and other works. His policy of accommodation to Chinese culture made him a controversial figure within the Church, but he is remembered in China and the West as the founder of a long scientific and cultural exchange.

### Kunyu Wanguo Quantu (1602)

<sub>The six panels of the 1602 map Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kunyu_Wanguo_Quantu_by_Matteo_Ricci_All_panels.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1602
- **Type:** World map
- **Published / where:** Beijing, Ming China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kunyu%20Wanguo%20Quantu%20Matteo%20Ricci)

Ricci made a first world map in Chinese at Zhaoqing in 1584, and in 1602 in Beijing, with the official Li Zhizao, printed a large version on six panels about 3.6 metres wide. Based on European maps such as those of Ortelius, it placed China near the centre by putting the Pacific in the middle, showed the five continents, gave notes on many countries and explained the spherical earth, latitude and longitude, and the size of the globe. Copies were made in colour for the Wanli Emperor, and versions reached Korea and Japan, where they shaped geographical knowledge for two centuries. Only a few original prints survive, including one in the James Ford Bell Library in Minnesota.

### Jihe Yuanben (Euclid's Elements) (1607)

[![Jihe Yuanben (Euclid's Elements)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/14/Jiheyuanben_Kyoto_p36.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jiheyuanben_Kyoto_p36.jpg)

<sub>Page of the Jihe Yuanben (Kyoto University copy) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jiheyuanben_Kyoto_p36.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1607
- **Type:** Translation
- **Published / where:** Beijing, Ming China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jihe%20Yuanben%20%28Euclid%27s%20Elements%29%20Matteo%20Ricci)

Working from his teacher Clavius's Latin edition of Euclid, Ricci explained the text orally and Xu Guangqi wrote it in classical Chinese, in sessions over about a year; the first six books, on plane geometry, were printed in Beijing in 1607 as Jihe Yuanben, 'Origins of Quantity'. They had to invent much of the vocabulary, including the word jihe, now the Chinese and Japanese word for geometry, and terms for point, line, angle and parallel. Xu praised the work for its rigour, saying it could not be doubted, refuted or improved; Chinese mathematics had not used axiomatic proof of this kind. The remaining nine books were translated only in 1857 by Li Shanlan and Alexander Wylie.
