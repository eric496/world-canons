# Heo Jun

*許浚*

**1539 – 1615** · Korean  
Born: Yangcheon (Seoul) · Died: Korea  
Eras & fields: [Joseon science under Sejong](../movements/joseon-science.md), [Silhak and late Joseon science](../movements/silhak-science.md)  

> Joseon court physician whose Dongui Bogam (1613) became the standard encyclopedia of Korean medicine and is inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.

## Biography

Heo Jun was born to a military aristocratic family but, as the son of a concubine, was barred from the highest offices. He entered the royal medical service and became personal physician to King Seonjo, accompanying him in flight during the Japanese invasions of 1592.

In 1596 Seonjo commissioned him to compile a new medical encyclopedia. Completed in exile in 1610 and printed in 1613, the Dongui Bogam organises Chinese and Korean medicine by internal medicine, external conditions, miscellaneous diseases, pharmacology and acupuncture, using many local Korean herbs. It was reprinted in China and Japan and remains a classic of Korean medicine.

## Key works & inventions

### Dongui Bogam (1613)

[![Dongui Bogam](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/77/Dongibogam.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dongibogam.jpg)

<sub>Volumes of the Dongui Bogam Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dongibogam.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1613
- **Type:** Medical encyclopedia
- **Published / where:** Hanseong (Seoul), Joseon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dongui%20Bogam%20Heo%20Jun)

Commissioned by King Seonjo in 1596 and completed by Heo Jun in 1610, the Dongui Bogam was printed in 25 volumes in 1613. It is divided into five parts: internal bodily functions, external parts of the body, miscellaneous diseases, drugs and acupuncture. Heo Jun drew on over 200 Chinese and Korean medical books but organised them with an emphasis on nourishing life and preventing illness, and listed Korean names for 637 local drugs in the Korean alphabet so that ordinary people could find them. Reprinted many times in China and Japan, it remains the central text of Korean medicine, and it was added to the Memory of the World Register in 2009.
