# Li Shizhen

*李時珍*

**1518 – 1593** · Chinese  
Born: Qizhou, Hubei · Died: Qizhou, Hubei  
Eras & fields: [Ming technology & natural history](../movements/ming-science.md), [Chinese alchemy & pharmacology](../movements/chinese-alchemy.md)  

> Ming physician and naturalist whose Bencao Gangmu (1578) is the greatest work of Chinese materia medica, describing nearly 1,900 drugs.

## Biography

Li Shizhen was the son of a physician in Hubei. After failing the provincial examinations three times he turned to medicine, and served briefly in a princely household and the imperial medical bureau, where he read rare books.

From about 1552 he spent 27 years revising earlier pharmacopoeias, correcting errors from his own field observations and travels and from over 800 sources. The Bencao Gangmu, published after his death in 1596, classifies 1,892 substances into 16 divisions and 60 categories and gives more than 11,000 prescriptions. It was reprinted throughout East Asia, translated in part into European languages, and probably the 'ancient Chinese encyclopaedia' that Darwin cited.

## Key works & inventions

### Bencao Gangmu (1578 (printed 1596))

[![Bencao Gangmu](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6e/Bencao_Gangmu0_33-36.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bencao_Gangmu0_33-36.jpg)

<sub>Illustrated pages of the Bencao Gangmu Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bencao_Gangmu0_33-36.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1578 (printed 1596)
- **Type:** Materia medica
- **Published / where:** Nanjing, Ming China (first edition)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bencao%20Gangmu%20Li%20Shizhen)

Li Shizhen worked on the Bencao Gangmu for nearly three decades, finishing it in 1578; it was printed in Nanjing in 1596, three years after his death. In 52 chapters it describes 1,892 drugs, 374 of them newly added, arranged in sixteen divisions from water, fire and earth through minerals, herbs, grains, vegetables, fruit and trees to insects, fish, birds, beasts and humans, with an entry for each giving names, origin, preparation, properties and uses. Li corrected many errors of earlier pharmacopoeias, rejected some claims of immortality drugs and gave careful descriptions of plants and animals, making it the greatest work of Chinese natural history as well as pharmacology. Reprinted many times, it shaped honzōgaku, the study of natural products, in Edo Japan, and was partly translated into Latin, French, English and Russian.
