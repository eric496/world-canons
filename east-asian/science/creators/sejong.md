# Sejong the Great

*世宗大王*

**1397 – 1450** · Korean  
Born: Hanseong (Seoul) · Died: Hanseong (Seoul)  
Eras & fields: [Joseon science under Sejong](../movements/joseon-science.md)  
Also in: [Music & Theatre](../../music/creators/sejong.md), [History](../../history/creators/sejong.md)  

> Fourth king of Joseon (r. 1418–50), who created the Hangul alphabet and sponsored an extraordinary programme of astronomy, instrument-making, medicine and agriculture.

## Biography

Sejong, third son of King Taejong, came to the throne in 1418. He gathered scholars in the Hall of Worthies and promoted learning in every field, while also strengthening the northern frontiers and reforming taxes.

In science he commissioned the royal observatory, water clocks, sundials and rain gauges; the Chiljeongsan calendar, which calculated the motions of the sun, moon and planets for the latitude of Seoul; farming manuals adapted to Korean conditions; and the medical collections Hyangyak Jipseongbang and Uibang Yuchwi. He is the most honoured king in Korean history and appears on the 10,000-won note.

### Nongsa Jikseol (1429)

- **Date:** 1429
- **Type:** Agricultural manual
- **Published / where:** Hanseong (Seoul), Joseon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nongsa%20Jikseol%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

Sejong found that Chinese farming manuals did not suit Korean soils and climate, and in 1428 ordered provincial governors to ask old farmers about their practices. The official Jeong Cho edited the results into the Nongsa Jikseol, printed in 1429 and distributed to local officials. It describes the preparation of seed, ploughing, manuring, and the cultivation of rice, millet, beans, barley and other crops, including transplanting and dry sowing of rice. It is the first Korean agricultural book written from Korean experience.

### Hyangyak Jipseongbang (1433)

- **Date:** 1433
- **Type:** Pharmacopoeia and prescription collection
- **Published / where:** Hanseong (Seoul), Joseon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hyangyak%20Jipseongbang%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

Imported Chinese drugs were expensive and scarce, and Korean physicians had long sought local substitutes, hyangyak. In 1431–33 the scholars Yu Hyo-tong, No Jung-rye and Pak Yun-deok compiled the Hyangyak Jipseongbang in 85 chapters, listing 959 disease patterns and over 10,000 prescriptions drawn from Korean and Chinese books, with 694 local drugs. Officials were sent with the embassy to China to check the identity of Korean plants. With the later Uibang Yuchwi, a huge medical encyclopedia of 1445, it laid the basis for Heo Jun's Dongui Bogam.

### Cheugugi rain gauge (1441)

[![Cheugugi rain gauge](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c5/Jang_Yeong-sil_Science_Garden-Rain_Gauges_13-11789_Busan%2C_South_Korea_03.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jang_Yeong-sil_Science_Garden-Rain_Gauges_13-11789_Busan%2C_South_Korea_03.JPG)

<sub>Replicas of Joseon rain gauges at the Jang Yeong-sil Science Garden, Busan Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jang_Yeong-sil_Science_Garden-Rain_Gauges_13-11789_Busan%2C_South_Korea_03.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1441
- **Type:** Meteorological instrument
- **Published / where:** Joseon Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Cheugugi%20rain%20gauge%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

In 1441 Sejong's government, at the suggestion of Crown Prince Munjong, made a cylinder of iron or bronze of fixed size, about 30 cm tall and 14 cm across, and the next year installed it on a stone base at the royal observatory and at every provincial and county office. After each rain, officials measured the depth of water with a ruler and reported it to the capital, and the records were used to plan farming and assess taxes. The system, revived in the eighteenth century, produced a continuous Korean rainfall record from 1777. The only surviving cheugugi, made in 1837 for Gongju, is a national treasure of Korea. It predates Benedetto Castelli's rain gauge of 1639.

### Chiljeongsan (1442)

- **Date:** 1442
- **Type:** Astronomical calendar
- **Published / where:** Hanseong (Seoul), Joseon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chiljeongsan%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

Sejong ordered his astronomers Yi Sun-ji and Kim Dam and others to master the Chinese Shoushi system and the Islamic Huihui calendar and adapt them to Korea. The result, the Chiljeongsan Naepyeon and Oepyeon, was completed in 1442. It calculated the positions of the sun, moon and five visible planets, eclipses and the times of sunrise and sunset for Seoul, so that Korea no longer depended on Chinese predictions. It shows the high mathematical level of the Sejong court and was used until the Shixian calendar was adopted in 1653.
