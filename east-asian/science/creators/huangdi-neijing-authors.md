# Authors of the Huangdi Neijing

*黃帝內經作者*

**c. 300 BC – c. 100 BC** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: China  
Eras & fields: [Classical Chinese medicine](../movements/classical-chinese-medicine.md)  

> The anonymous physicians whose writings, framed as dialogues between the Yellow Emperor and his ministers, became the Huangdi Neijing, the founding classic of Chinese medicine.

## Biography

The Inner Classic of the Yellow Emperor is attributed to the legendary sage-ruler Huangdi, but it is a compilation of many shorter texts written by different hands, probably between the late Warring States and the first century BC. Medical manuscripts found at Mawangdui, buried in 168 BC, show earlier stages of the same ideas.

The authors explained health and disease through yin and yang, the five phases, and the flow of qi and blood through a network of channels, and gave detailed rules for diagnosis by the pulse and for acupuncture. Their correlative way of thinking, linking the body to seasons, climate and emotions, remains the theoretical basis of traditional medicine in China, Korea and Japan.

## Key works & inventions

### Huangdi Neijing (c. 2nd–1st century BC)

- **Date:** c. 2nd–1st century BC
- **Type:** Medical classic
- **Published / where:** China; Suwen edited by Wang Bing, 762
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Huangdi%20Neijing%20Authors%20of%20the%20Huangdi%20Neijing)

The Huangdi Neijing survives in two parts, the Suwen ('Basic Questions') and the Lingshu ('Spiritual Pivot'), each of 81 chapters, mostly cast as dialogues between the Yellow Emperor and his physicians Qibo and others. Compiled from many shorter texts, probably between the third and first centuries BC, it presents the body as a microcosm governed by yin and yang and the five phases, with organs linked to seasons, colours, tastes and emotions, and with qi and blood circulating through twelve main channels. Disease arises from imbalance caused by climate, emotion or diet; the physician diagnoses by observation and the pulse and treats with needles, moxibustion and drugs, ideally before illness appears. Edited and expanded by Wang Bing in the Tang and by the Song editorial office, it remains the theoretical root of Chinese, Korean and Japanese traditional medicine.
