## Notes

**Dating inventions.** Dates for early inventions are usually dates of the first surviving description, not of invention. Paper is older than Cai Lun's report of 105; gunpowder is first described by alchemists in the ninth century and first given as a recipe in 1044; the magnetic needle is described in the eleventh century, while earlier lodestone "south-pointers" are debated. Many works were compiled over centuries and are credited here to collective authors.

**Transmission.** Knowledge moved constantly across East Asia: Chinese mathematics, medicine and calendars were adapted in Korea and Japan, and some Chinese books (Zhu Shijie's primer, the Tiangong Kaiwu, many Tang medical texts) survived only in Korean or Japanese copies. Paper, printing, the compass and gunpowder reached the Islamic world and Europe over centuries, though how directly is often debated. From 1582 the Jesuits brought European astronomy and geometry to Beijing, and from the eighteenth century Dutch books at Nagasaki brought European anatomy and physics to Japan. The Diamond Sutra of 868 and Korea's Jikji of 1377, landmarks of printing, are covered in the religion section.

**The Needham Question.** The historian Joseph Needham (1900–1995), whose *Science and Civilisation in China* began in 1954, asked why China, so inventive for centuries, did not produce the kind of modern science that emerged in seventeenth-century Europe. Answers point to bureaucracy, the examination system, economics, language or the framing of the question itself, and many historians now doubt that it has a single answer or treat it as a question about Europe as much as China.
