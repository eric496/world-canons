## Biography

Very little is known about Guan Hanqing. Sources of the Yuan period describe him as a resident of Dadu, the Mongol capital, possibly a physician by training, who belonged to a 'writing club' of playwrights and was intimately familiar with the theatre, perhaps acting himself. He is credited with more than sixty plays, of which about eighteen survive.

Guan's plays include courtroom dramas, historical pieces and comedies, many with clever, courageous women at their centre, such as the courtesan who outwits a cruel husband in Rescuing a Fellow Courtesan. Their vigorous colloquial arias and sympathy for victims of corrupt officials have made him the most performed and studied of Yuan dramatists, and in the twentieth century he was celebrated in China as a people's playwright.

### The Injustice to Dou E (late 13th century)

[![The Injustice to Dou E](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2a/Dou_E_and_Donkey_Zhang.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dou_E_and_Donkey_Zhang.jpg)

<sub>Dou E and Donkey Zhang, woodblock illustration Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dou_E_and_Donkey_Zhang.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 13th century
- **Type:** Zaju drama
- **Written / first published:** Dadu (Beijing)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Injustice%20to%20Dou%20E%20Guan%20Hanqing)

Dou E is sold as a child to pay her father's debts and becomes a widow while still young. A rogue, Donkey Zhang, tries to force her to marry him and poisons her mother-in-law's soup, but kills his own father by mistake. Accused, Dou E refuses to confess until the corrupt magistrate threatens to torture her mother-in-law; she is sentenced to death. On the execution ground she makes three vows: that her blood will fly up onto a white banner, that snow will fall in midsummer, and that the district will suffer three years of drought, all of which come true. Years later her ghost appears to her father, now a high official, and he rights the wrong. In four acts plus a wedge, with Dou E singing all the arias, the play combines Buddhist and Confucian ideas of karma with a sharp protest against corruption, and its full title, 'Moving Heaven and Shaking Earth', became proverbial.

### Rescuing a Fellow Courtesan (late 13th century)

- **Date:** late 13th century
- **Type:** Zaju drama
- **Written / first published:** Dadu (Beijing)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rescuing%20a%20Fellow%20Courtesan%20Guan%20Hanqing)

Song Yin-zhang, a young courtesan, ignores the warnings of her shrewd friend Zhao Pan'er and marries the rich, charming Zhou She, who begins beating her as soon as they are married. When Song sends a desperate letter, Zhao Pan'er travels to Zhengzhou, dresses up, and seduces Zhou She into promising to marry her if he divorces Song. Having obtained the divorce paper, she escapes with her friend, and when Zhou She takes them to court, the magistrate rules in the women's favour. Lively, colloquial and full of sympathy for the courtesans' resourcefulness, it is one of the masterpieces of Yuan comedy and is still performed in Chinese opera.
