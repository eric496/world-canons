## Biography

Su Shi passed the jinshi in 1057 together with his brother Su Zhe, and both were praised by the examiner Ouyang Xiu. He opposed Wang Anshi's New Policies and in 1079 was arrested for poems judged to slander the court, the 'Crow Terrace poetry case'; after months in prison he was banished to Huangzhou, where he farmed a plot on an eastern slope and took the name Dongpo. Recalled and promoted in the 1080s, he was again exiled when the reformers returned, ending on the island of Hainan, and died on the way home after a pardon.

Su Shi wrote over 2,000 shi poems, broadened the ci beyond love songs into a vehicle for history and philosophy, and wrote some of the finest prose in the language, including the two Red Cliff rhapsodies. He was also one of the four great calligraphers of the Song and a theorist of scholar painting, valuing the expression of the mind over likeness. His good humour in adversity, Buddhist and Daoist detachment, and even the pork dish named after him have made him a beloved figure.

### Prelude to Water Melody (1076)

- **Date:** 1076
- **Type:** Ci (lyric)
- **Written / first published:** Mizhou (Zhucheng), Shandong
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Prelude%20to%20Water%20Melody%20Su%20Shi)

Su Shi's preface says that he drank until dawn at the Mid-Autumn Festival of 1076, became very drunk and wrote this lyric thinking of his brother Su Zhe, whom he had not seen for seven years. Holding up his wine cup, he asks the moon how long it has existed and what year it is in the palaces of heaven; he wishes to ride the wind there but fears the cold, and dances with his shadow below. The second stanza turns to human separation: people have sorrow and joy, parting and reunion, as the moon waxes and wanes, and this has never been perfect; he hopes only that they may both live long and share the moon's beauty across a thousand miles. Set to music in modern times, it remains one of the most popular Chinese poems.

### Odes on the Red Cliff (1082)

[![Odes on the Red Cliff](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Su_Shi-Former_Ode_on_the_Red_Cliff.jpg/1280px-Su_Shi-Former_Ode_on_the_Red_Cliff.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Su_Shi-Former_Ode_on_the_Red_Cliff.jpg)

<sub>Su Shi's own calligraphy of the Former Ode on the Red Cliff, National Palace Museum, Taipei Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Su_Shi-Former_Ode_on_the_Red_Cliff.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1082
- **Type:** Fu (prose rhapsodies)
- **Written / first published:** Huangzhou (Huanggang), Hubei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Odes%20on%20the%20Red%20Cliff%20Su%20Shi)

Exiled to Huangzhou after the poetry trial of 1079, Su Shi made two boat excursions below a cliff on the Yangtze that he took to be the site of the battle of Red Cliff (208), where Cao Cao's fleet was destroyed. In the Former Ode, written in the seventh month of 1082, a guest plays a melancholy flute and laments that even the hero Cao Cao is gone, while human life is a mayfly between heaven and earth. Su replies that, seen from the viewpoint of change, nothing lasts an instant, but from the viewpoint of constancy, we and all things are endless; the clear breeze and bright moon belong to everyone. The Latter Ode, three months later, is a mysterious winter night climb ending with a crane and a Daoist in a dream. Written in a free 'prose fu' style, the two pieces are among the most celebrated works of Chinese prose, and Su's own calligraphy of the first survives in the National Palace Museum in Taipei.

### Remembering the Past at Red Cliff (1082)

- **Date:** 1082
- **Type:** Ci (lyric)
- **Written / first published:** Huangzhou, Hubei
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Remembering%20the%20Past%20at%20Red%20Cliff%20Su%20Shi)

Written to the tune 'The Charms of Niannu', the lyric opens with the great river flowing east, its waves washing away the romantic heroes of a thousand ages. At the old fortress, said to be Zhou Yu's Red Cliff, rocks pierce the clouds and waves pile up like snow. Su imagines the young general Zhou Yu, newly married, with feather fan and silk turban, destroying Cao Cao's fleet amid laughter, and then mocks his own sentimental, prematurely grey self, ending by pouring a libation to the river moon, since life is like a dream. With its sweep of history and landscape it is the classic example of the 'heroic and unrestrained' ci, which Su Shi created by breaking the genre's association with love songs.
