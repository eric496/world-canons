# Kawabata Yasunari

*川端康成*

**1899 – 1972** · Japanese  
Born: Osaka · Died: Zushi, Kanagawa  
Movements & eras: [Meiji to early Shōwa modern literature](../movements/meiji-modern.md), [Postwar Japanese literature](../movements/postwar-japanese.md)  

> Novelist of melancholy beauty and the first Japanese winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature (1968).

## Biography

Orphaned in early childhood, Kawabata was raised by his grandfather, who also died when he was fifteen. At Tokyo Imperial University he co-founded the Neo-Perceptionist group of modernist writers. His story The Dancing Girl of Izu (1926) brought success, and he worked on Snow Country from 1935 to 1948. He was a generous supporter of younger writers, including Mishima. He died in 1972 of gas poisoning, generally believed to be suicide.

Kawabata's novels, including Snow Country, Thousand Cranes and The Sound of the Mountain, use haiku-like juxtaposition and silence to portray loneliness, love and the transience of beauty, often with a sense of a traditional Japan slipping away. His very short 'palm-of-the-hand stories' are also admired.

### Snow Country (1935–48)

- **Date:** 1935–48
- **Type:** Novel
- **Written / first published:** Serialised from 1935; final version Tokyo, 1948
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Snow%20Country%20Kawabata%20Yasunari)

The novel opens with a train emerging from a long tunnel into the snow country on the Sea of Japan side of the mountains, one of the most famous opening sentences in Japanese. Shimamura, a wealthy idler from Tokyo who writes on Western ballet he has never seen, visits a hot-spring resort several times and has an affair with Komako, a passionate young geisha, whose sincere love he cannot return. He is also fascinated by the girl Yōko, whose face he first sees reflected in the train window. The novel ends with a fire in a cocoon warehouse and Yōko's fall, as Shimamura feels the Milky Way flowing into him. Written in fragments over thirteen years, with haiku-like ellipses, it is Kawabata's best-known work and was cited in his Nobel Prize award.

### The Sound of the Mountain (1949–54)

- **Date:** 1949–54
- **Type:** Novel
- **Written / first published:** Serialised 1949–54; book Tokyo, 1954
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Sound%20of%20the%20Mountain%20Kawabata%20Yasunari)

Ogata Shingo, a Tokyo businessman in his sixties living in Kamakura, hears one night a rumbling from the mountain behind his house and takes it as a premonition of death. His memory is failing, his son Shūichi has a mistress, his daughter has left her husband, and he is drawn, with a mixture of affection and suppressed desire, to Shūichi's gentle wife Kikuko. In a series of quiet episodes, dreams and seasonal scenes, the novel portrays old age, family breakdown and postwar Japan. Many critics regard it as Kawabata's finest novel.
