> Scholar and writer of Tales of Moonlight and Rain, the classic of Japanese supernatural fiction.

## Biography

Akinari was born in Osaka to an unknown mother and adopted by a merchant family; a childhood illness left his fingers deformed. He wrote comic fiction, studied Japanese classics in the National Learning movement, debated Motoori Norinaga, and practised medicine after his house burned in 1771. His last years in Kyoto were spent in poverty and near blindness.

Tales of Moonlight and Rain (1776), nine stories adapted from Chinese and Japanese sources in an elegant classical style, combine ghosts and demons with psychological intensity. Akinari later wrote the more personal Tales of Spring Rain. Mizoguchi's film Ugetsu (1953) drew on two of his tales.

## Key works

### Tales of Moonlight and Rain (Ugetsu monogatari) (1776)

[![Tales of Moonlight and Rain (Ugetsu monogatari)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0c/Tales_of_Moonlight_and_Rain_cover.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tales_of_Moonlight_and_Rain_cover.jpg)

<sub>Cover of an early edition Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tales_of_Moonlight_and_Rain_cover.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1776
- **Type:** Yomihon (story collection)
- **Written / first published:** Osaka and Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tales%20of%20Moonlight%20and%20Rain%20%28Ugetsu%20monogatari%29%20Ueda%20Akinari)

Ugetsu monogatari, published in five illustrated volumes, contains nine stories adapted from Chinese vernacular fiction and Japanese sources and set in Japan's past. In The House in the Thicket, a man who left his wife to make money in the capital returns years later to find her waiting, spends the night with her, and wakes in a ruined house beside her grave; in The Lust of the White Serpent, a young man is pursued by a beautiful woman who is a serpent spirit; in The Blue Hood, a priest's obsessive love for a dead boy turns him into a cannibal demon. Written in a refined pseudo-classical style rich in allusion, the tales explore passion, loyalty and the power of the mind. They are the finest of the early yomihon and inspired Mizoguchi Kenji's film Ugetsu (1953).
