## Biography

According to the biography written by Sima Qian two centuries later, Qu Yuan was an aristocrat and minister at the court of King Huai of Chu who urged alliance against the rising state of Qin. Slandered by rivals, he was dismissed and later banished to the south by King Huai's successor. After hearing that Qin had taken the Chu capital, he is said to have drowned himself in the Miluo River clasping a stone. Modern scholars debate how much of this story, and how many of the poems ascribed to him, are historical.

The poems of the Chuci attributed to Qu Yuan, above all the Li Sao, created the figure of the loyal minister rejected by an unworthy ruler, expressed through fragrant plants, divine journeys and courtship of goddesses. This voice of wronged integrity became a model for countless later poets in exile. The Dragon Boat Festival, held on the fifth day of the fifth month, is popularly said to commemorate the search for his body, and he remains one of China's most honoured cultural heroes.

### Songs of Chu (Chuci) (c. 4th century BC – 2nd century AD)

- **Date:** c. 4th century BC – 2nd century AD
- **Type:** Poetry anthology
- **Written / first published:** State of Chu; compiled by Wang Yi, 2nd century AD
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Songs%20of%20Chu%20%28Chuci%29%20Qu%20Yuan)

The Chuci is the second great source of Chinese poetry. Compiled in its present form by the Han scholar Wang Yi around AD 120, it gathers poems traditionally ascribed to Qu Yuan, his follower Song Yu and later Han imitators. Unlike the northern Shijing, its poems use long lines with the exclamatory particle xi, a rich vocabulary of fragrant plants and southern place names, and the imagery of shamans who summon gods and travel through the heavens. Besides the Li Sao, it contains the Nine Songs, hymns to deities of the Chu religion; the Heavenly Questions, a sequence of riddling questions about cosmology and history; and the Summons of the Soul, which calls a dead or dying spirit back from the horrors of the four directions. The anthology gave Chinese literature its language of lament and political frustration, and was the direct ancestor of the Han rhapsody.

### Encountering Sorrow (Li Sao) (c. 300 BC)

[![Encountering Sorrow (Li Sao)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/34/Li_sao_illustr%C3%A9_%28crop%29.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Li_sao_illustr%C3%A9_%28crop%29.png)

<sub>Illustration to the Li Sao from a woodblock-printed edition Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Li_sao_illustr%C3%A9_%28crop%29.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 300 BC
- **Type:** Long poem
- **Written / first published:** State of Chu
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Encountering%20Sorrow%20%28Li%20Sao%29%20Qu%20Yuan)

At about 370 lines, the Li Sao is the longest poem of early China. Its speaker announces his divine descent and noble name, describes how he adorned himself with fragrant herbs as a sign of virtue, and laments that his lord has listened to slanderers and rejected him. Unable to find understanding on earth, he rides a chariot drawn by dragons and phoenixes across the sky, seeks goddesses as brides through unreliable go-betweens, and consults shamans, before resolving to depart, only to look down on his homeland and refuse to leave. A closing envoi says he will follow the example of Peng Xian, interpreted as suicide by drowning. Traditionally read as Qu Yuan's autobiography, the poem's mix of political allegory, mythology and personal integrity created the archetype of the misunderstood loyal official, and its title became a term for any expression of grief over injustice.
