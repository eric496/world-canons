# Poets of the Book of Songs

*詩經作者*

**c. 11th century BC – c. 6th century BC** · Chinese  
Born: Zhou-dynasty states of the Yellow River plain · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Book of Songs & Songs of Chu](../movements/shijing-chuci.md)  

> The anonymous singers, court musicians and ritual poets whose 305 songs make up the Shijing, the oldest anthology of Chinese poetry.

## Biography

The songs of the Shijing were composed over roughly five centuries, from the early Western Zhou to the Spring and Autumn period, by people whose names are almost entirely lost. Some were sacrificial hymns for the royal ancestral temple, some court odes on feasts, hunts and campaigns, and the largest group, the 'Airs of the States', appear to be folk songs of love, courtship, labour and complaint from fifteen regions of north China, though they were probably reworked by court musicians. Tradition held that officials collected songs so that the king could learn the mood of his people, and that Confucius selected the final 305 from some 3,000; both stories are doubted by modern scholars.

Whatever their origins, the songs became one of the Five Classics, memorised by every educated person in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam for more than two thousand years. Their four-character lines, refrains and nature images used to open a feeling (the 'xing' or evocation) shaped all later Chinese poetry, while the allegorical readings imposed by Han commentators turned simple love songs into lessons in political virtue, a habit of interpretation that itself became part of the tradition.

- **Date:** c. 1000–600 BC
- **Type:** Poetry anthology
- **Written / first published:** Zhou-dynasty China; compiled c. 6th century BC
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Book%20of%20Songs%20%28Shijing%29%20Poets%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Songs)

The Shijing collects 305 poems in three sections: the 'Airs of the States' (Guofeng), 160 songs mostly about love, marriage, farming, war service and complaint from fifteen regions; the 'Elegantiae' (Ya), court poems of feasting and history; and the 'Hymns' (Song), sacrificial songs for the Zhou and Shang royal ancestors. Most are in rhymed four-character lines with refrains, and many open with a natural image, such as calling ospreys on a river islet, that sets the emotional key. Probably compiled by court musicians around the sixth century BC and quoted by Confucius, who said the collection could be summed up as having no twisted thoughts, it was canonised under the Han as one of the Five Classics and read through heavy allegorical commentary, the 'Mao' tradition, that made love songs into political lessons. Studied by every educated person in East Asia until the twentieth century, it also became a model for later poets seeking simplicity, and modern scholars from Marcel Granet to Wen Yiduo rediscovered its folk roots.
