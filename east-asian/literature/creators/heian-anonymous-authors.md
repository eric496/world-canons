# Anonymous Heian authors

*平安時代の作者不詳*

**9th century – 12th century** · Japanese  
Born: Heian-kyō (Kyoto) · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Heian court literature](../movements/heian-court-literature.md)  
Also in: [History](../../history/creators/heian-anonymous-authors.md)  

> The unknown writers behind early monogatari such as The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter and The Tales of Ise, and the tale collection Konjaku monogatarishū.

## Biography

Many of the founding works of Japanese prose fiction are anonymous. The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter, which Murasaki Shikibu called the ancestor of all tales, was probably written by a learned man familiar with Chinese texts in the late ninth or early tenth century. The Tales of Ise, a sequence of episodes built around poems, many by Ariwara no Narihira, grew in stages over the tenth century. Konjaku monogatarishū, more than a thousand tales from India, China and Japan, was compiled around 1120 by an unknown hand, perhaps a monk.

These works established the basic forms of Japanese narrative: the supernatural tale, the poem-tale in which prose frames waka, and the anecdotal tale collection. They were endlessly retold in later literature, painting and theatre.

### The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter (late 9th – early 10th century)

[![The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Taketori_monogatari-Tale_of_the_Shining_Princess_MET_DP137389.jpg/1280px-Taketori_monogatari-Tale_of_the_Shining_Princess_MET_DP137389.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taketori_monogatari-Tale_of_the_Shining_Princess_MET_DP137389.jpg)

<sub>Illustrated Taketori monogatari, Metropolitan Museum of Art Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taketori_monogatari-Tale_of_the_Shining_Princess_MET_DP137389.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** late 9th – early 10th century
- **Type:** Monogatari (tale)
- **Written / first published:** Heian-kyō (Kyoto)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tale%20of%20the%20Bamboo%20Cutter%20Anonymous%20Heian%20authors)

An old bamboo cutter finds a tiny girl, three inches tall, glowing inside a stalk of bamboo, and he and his wife raise her. She grows within three months into a woman of radiant beauty, Kaguya-hime, and five noble suitors seek her hand; she sets each an impossible task, such as fetching the Buddha's stone begging bowl or a jewel from a dragon's neck, and they fail in comic ways. Even the emperor's love cannot keep her: she reveals that she is from the moon, and on the night of the full moon a heavenly host descends to take her back, wrapping her in a feather robe that erases her earthly feelings. She leaves the emperor an elixir of immortality, which he orders burned on the highest mountain, explaining the name of Mount Fuji. Called by Murasaki Shikibu the ancestor of all monogatari, it is Japan's oldest surviving prose tale and inspired Takahata Isao's film The Tale of the Princess Kaguya (2013).

### The Tales of Ise (10th century)

[![The Tales of Ise](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/The_Tales_of_Ise_%28Ise_monogatari%29_%28CBL_J_1050.2%2C_ff.11b-12a%29.jpg/1280px-The_Tales_of_Ise_%28Ise_monogatari%29_%28CBL_J_1050.2%2C_ff.11b-12a%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Tales_of_Ise_%28Ise_monogatari%29_%28CBL_J_1050.2%2C_ff.11b-12a%29.jpg)

<sub>Illustrated manuscript of the Tales of Ise, Chester Beatty Library Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Tales_of_Ise_%28Ise_monogatari%29_%28CBL_J_1050.2%2C_ff.11b-12a%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 10th century
- **Type:** Uta monogatari (poem-tale)
- **Written / first published:** Heian-kyō (Kyoto)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tales%20of%20Ise%20Anonymous%20Heian%20authors)

Each episode of the Tales of Ise begins with the phrase 'Once there was a man' and leads to one or more waka. The episodes, many linked to poems by Ariwara no Narihira, sketch a life from coming of age to death: a secret love for a high-born lady who becomes an imperial consort, an affair with the Ise priestess, and a journey east into exile, where at the Sumida River he asks a bird about his love in the capital and, at Yatsuhashi, writes a poem whose syllables spell the name of the iris. The work grew over the tenth century and its authorship is unknown. It became the model of courtly love and elegance, one of the most commented-on texts of Japanese literature, and a favourite subject of painting, from Heian scrolls to the iris screens of Ogata Kōrin.

### Konjaku monogatarishū (c. 1120)

[![Konjaku monogatarishū](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/KonjakuMonogatarisyu_codex_Suzuka.png/1280px-KonjakuMonogatarisyu_codex_Suzuka.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:KonjakuMonogatarisyu_codex_Suzuka.png)

<sub>Suzuka manuscript of the Konjaku monogatarishū Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:KonjakuMonogatarisyu_codex_Suzuka.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1120
- **Type:** Tale collection
- **Written / first published:** Kyoto region
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Konjaku%20monogatarish%C5%AB%20Anonymous%20Heian%20authors)

Each tale in this vast collection begins with the phrase 'Now it is past', from which the title, 'Tales of Times Now Past', is taken. Of the thirty-one books, five tell stories of India, five of China and the rest of Japan, arranged from Buddhist tales of the Buddha, monks and miracles to secular stories of warriors, thieves, ghosts, foxes and ordinary people. Written in a mixed Chinese-Japanese style, terse and vivid, the collection was little known until it was rediscovered in the modern period. Akutagawa Ryūnosuke drew on it for Rashōmon, In a Grove and other stories, praising its raw, untamed vitality.
