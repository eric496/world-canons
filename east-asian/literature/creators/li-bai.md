## Biography

Li Bai (also Li Po) is said to have been born in Central Asia and grew up in Sichuan, where he studied swordsmanship and Daoism. In his twenties he set out on a life of travel along the Yangtze and across north China, making friends, drinking, and seeking patrons. In 742 his fame brought him to the court of Emperor Xuanzong in Chang'an as a member of the Hanlin Academy, but within two years he was eased out. During the An Lushan rebellion he joined the staff of a prince who was then declared a rebel, and he was sentenced to exile in the far south-west, pardoned on the way. Legend says he drowned trying to embrace the moon's reflection; he probably died of illness.

Li Bai excelled in the freer old-style poem and the yuefu ballad, as well as in short quatrains of perfect simplicity. His poems move with sudden leaps of imagination between heaven and earth, celebrating friendship, drinking, mountains and immortals with a sense of boundless self. Paired with his friend Du Fu as the twin summits of Tang poetry, he has been memorised by schoolchildren across East Asia for over a millennium.

### Quiet Night Thought (c. 726)

- **Date:** c. 726
- **Type:** Poem (quatrain)
- **Written / first published:** Yangzhou (traditionally)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Quiet%20Night%20Thought%20Li%20Bai)

In just twenty characters, the speaker sees moonlight before his bed and mistakes it for frost on the ground; raising his head he looks at the bright moon, lowering it he thinks of his home. Traditionally said to have been written when the young Li Bai was travelling in Yangzhou, the poem uses the simplest possible vocabulary and the parallel movement of looking up and looking down to capture the experience of being far from home. Its common modern text differs slightly from the earliest versions, which speak of 'mountain moon' and 'moon's light'; the familiar wording was standardised in Ming anthologies. Learned by virtually every Chinese child, it is a touchstone of the moon as a symbol shared by people far apart.

### The Road to Shu Is Hard (c. 742)

- **Date:** c. 742
- **Type:** Yuefu ballad
- **Written / first published:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Road%20to%20Shu%20Is%20Hard%20Li%20Bai)

Using an old yuefu title, Li Bai evokes the legendary origins of the road from the Qin plains into Shu (Sichuan), built when heroes died hauling at the mountains, and the plank roads, cliffs, gibbering gibbons and plunging torrents that make it harder than climbing to the blue sky, a refrain that recurs throughout. Lines of three to eleven characters break all regular patterns, matching the terrain. The poem may warn a friend about the journey, or the dangers of rebellious governors in Shu; its purpose is debated. When the elder poet He Zhizhang read it in Chang'an, he reportedly called Li Bai a banished immortal, the name that stuck to him.

### Drinking Alone by Moonlight (c. 744)

- **Date:** c. 744
- **Type:** Poem sequence
- **Written / first published:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Drinking%20Alone%20by%20Moonlight%20Li%20Bai)

The first of four poems with this title is among Li Bai's most loved. Among the flowers with a jug of wine and no companion, the poet raises his cup to the moon and, with his own shadow, finds himself in company of three. The moon does not drink and the shadow only follows him, but for the time being they will make merry while spring lasts: he sings and the moon lingers, he dances and the shadow sways; when drunk they will part, and he vows to meet them again far off in the Milky Way. The poem combines loneliness, humour and Daoist fantasy, turning solitude into cosmic companionship. It was probably written during his unhappy years at court, and has been translated into Western languages more often than almost any other Chinese poem.

### Bring in the Wine (c. 752)

- **Date:** c. 752
- **Type:** Yuefu ballad
- **Written / first published:** Songshan, Henan (traditionally)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bring%20in%20the%20Wine%20Li%20Bai)

Written on an old yuefu title, this ballad opens with two famous images: the Yellow River pouring from the sky to the sea, never to return, and a mirror in a high hall showing black hair turned white in a day. The poet urges his friends Cen and Danqiu to drink deeply, declaring that heaven gave him talent for a purpose and that a thousand pieces of silver spent will come back. He dismisses sages as forgotten while only drinkers leave a name, and offers to pawn his fine horse and fur coat for more wine to drown the sorrows of ten thousand ages. Its swinging irregular lines, hyperbole and defiant joy make it the classic expression of Li Bai's persona, and it is still recited and sung.
