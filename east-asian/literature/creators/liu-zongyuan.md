## Biography

Liu Zongyuan rose quickly in the capital and joined the reformist group of Wang Shuwen in 805. When the reform collapsed after a few months, he was banished as a minor official to Yongzhou in Hunan for ten years, and then to Liuzhou in Guangxi, where he governed conscientiously until his early death.

In exile Liu wrote the Eight Records of Yongzhou, short essays on obscure ravines, pools and rocks that turned landscape description into self-portrait, as well as fables and political essays such as the Discourse on Feudalism. With Han Yu he is counted among the founders of guwen prose and one of the Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song. His quatrain River Snow, an old fisherman alone in a snowy world, is one of the most familiar Chinese poems.

### River Snow (c. 810)

- **Date:** c. 810
- **Type:** Poem (quatrain)
- **Written / first published:** Yongzhou, Hunan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=River%20Snow%20Liu%20Zongyuan)

In four five-character lines, Liu describes a scene in which birds have vanished from a thousand mountains and human tracks from ten thousand paths; only an old man in a straw cape and hat sits in a solitary boat, fishing alone in the cold river snow. The poem is built on a progression from vast emptiness to one small figure, and the first characters of its lines have been read as a hidden message. Written in exile, it has been understood as a self-portrait of integrity in isolation. The image of the lone fisherman in snow became one of the commonest subjects of Chinese and Japanese painting.

### Eight Records of Yongzhou (809–812)

- **Date:** 809–812
- **Type:** Prose essays
- **Written / first published:** Yongzhou, Hunan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Eight%20Records%20of%20Yongzhou%20Liu%20Zongyuan)

Banished to Yongzhou after the failure of the reform movement of 805, Liu Zongyuan explored the hills and streams around the town and wrote eight short accounts of his finds: the Western Hill, the Pond of Mr Gumu, the Little Rock Pool, the Yuan family ravine and others. In precise prose he describes clearing weeds, the transparency of water in which fish seem to swim in empty space, and rocks that look like animals drinking. Repeatedly he observes that these beautiful places are neglected in a remote region, an implicit parallel with his own fate. The records turned the landscape essay into an expression of the self and became models for travel writing for centuries.
