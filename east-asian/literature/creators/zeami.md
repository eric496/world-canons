# Zeami Motokiyo

*世阿弥*

**c. 1363 – c. 1443** · Japanese  
Born:  · Died: Sado Island or Kyoto  
Movements & eras: [War tales, recluses & Noh](../movements/kamakura-muromachi.md)  
Also in: [Music & Theatre](../../music/creators/zeami.md)  

> Actor, playwright and theorist who, with his father Kan'ami, created Noh theatre.

## Biography

Zeami was the son of Kan'ami, leader of a sarugaku troupe from Yamato. In 1374 the young shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu saw them perform and took the boy under his patronage, giving him access to court poets and aristocratic culture. After his father's death Zeami led the troupe and wrote many plays and treatises. Under later shoguns he fell from favour; his son and heir died young, and in 1434 he was exiled to Sado Island, where he is thought to have spent his last years.

Zeami's treatises, beginning with the Fūshikaden, are among the earliest works of theatre theory anywhere, built around the ideals of yūgen (subtle grace) and hana (the 'flower' of performance). His dream plays, in which a ghost relives a moment of passion or suffering for a travelling monk, gave Noh its characteristic form. Some fifty plays are attributed to him, many still in the repertory.

### Fūshikaden (The Transmission of the Flower) (c. 1400–1418)

[![Fūshikaden (The Transmission of the Flower)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/Sousetsubon_fushikaden_7_part.PNG/1280px-Sousetsubon_fushikaden_7_part.PNG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sousetsubon_fushikaden_7_part.PNG)

<sub>Early manuscript of the Fūshikaden Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sousetsubon_fushikaden_7_part.PNG).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1400–1418
- **Type:** Treatise on drama
- **Written / first published:** Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=F%C5%ABshikaden%20%28The%20Transmission%20of%20the%20Flower%29%20Zeami%20Motokiyo)

Written over many years and transmitted in secret within Zeami's family until it was published in 1909, the Fūshikaden records the teachings of his father Kan'ami. It covers training at each stage of an actor's life from the age of seven, the imitation of different character types, the history of sarugaku, and above all hana, the 'flower': the fresh, surprising effect a performance has on an audience, which must be cultivated by knowing when and how to show one's art. Zeami writes that a flower that is hidden is a flower, a principle of concealment that runs through Japanese aesthetics. One of the earliest systematic works of drama theory anywhere, it is still studied by actors.

### Atsumori (early 15th century)

[![Atsumori](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Kumagai_Nnaozane_to_Taira_no_Atsumori_LCCN2008660125.jpg/1280px-Kumagai_Nnaozane_to_Taira_no_Atsumori_LCCN2008660125.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kumagai_Nnaozane_to_Taira_no_Atsumori_LCCN2008660125.jpg)

<sub>Kumagai Naozane and Taira no Atsumori, ukiyo-e print, Library of Congress Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kumagai_Nnaozane_to_Taira_no_Atsumori_LCCN2008660125.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** early 15th century
- **Type:** Noh play
- **Written / first published:** Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Atsumori%20Zeami%20Motokiyo)

Based on an episode of The Tale of the Heike, the play brings the monk Rensei, formerly the warrior Kumagai Naozane, to the beach at Ichinotani, where years before he killed the sixteen-year-old Taira no Atsumori and was so moved by the boy's youth and the flute he carried that he renounced the world. A group of reapers playing flutes appears; one stays behind and reveals himself as Atsumori's spirit. In the second half the ghost, in warrior costume, recounts the last festive night of the Taira in exile and dances the story of his death, then, about to strike his killer, recognises that Rensei has prayed for him and asks him to pray again. A classic warrior play (shura-mono) in Zeami's dream form, it embodies Buddhist compassion and release from attachment.

### Izutsu (The Well Curb) (early 15th century)

- **Date:** early 15th century
- **Type:** Noh play
- **Written / first published:** Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Izutsu%20%28The%20Well%20Curb%29%20Zeami%20Motokiyo)

Drawing on episode 23 of The Tales of Ise, the play is set at the ruined temple of Ariwara at Isonokami, where a travelling monk meets a village woman drawing water. She tells him the story of Ariwara no Narihira and the daughter of Ki no Aritsune, who measured their heights against a well curb as children and married when grown, and then reveals that she is the daughter's ghost. In the second half she appears wearing Narihira's hat and robe and dances, finally gazing into the well, where she sees her lover's image in her own reflection, before vanishing at dawn. Zeami considered it one of his finest plays, and it is the model of the 'woman play' of yūgen.
