# Han Music Bureau poets

*樂府詩人*

**c. 2nd century BC – c. 3rd century AD** · Chinese  
Born: Han China · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Han rhapsody & Music Bureau songs](../movements/han-fu-yuefu.md)  

> The anonymous makers of the Han ballads collected by the imperial Music Bureau and of the Nineteen Old Poems, the founding works of five-character verse.

## Biography

The Music Bureau (Yuefu) was an office reorganised by Emperor Wu around 120 BC to supply music for state ritual and entertainment. It collected songs from across the empire as well as composing new ones, and the term yuefu came to mean the ballads themselves. Most were anonymous: songs of soldiers, orphans, abandoned wives and lovers, often told as little dramas with dialogue. To them belong the Nineteen Old Poems of the late Eastern Han, short meditations on parting and the brevity of life by unknown literati, and the long narrative ballad The Peacock Flies Southeast.

These poems established the five-character line as the main vehicle of classical Chinese poetry and gave it a direct, unadorned emotional language quite different from the court rhapsody. Later poets from Cao Cao to Li Bai and Bai Juyi wrote 'new yuefu' on the old titles, and the ballad of the woman warrior Mulan, written down in the following centuries, belongs to the same tradition.

- **Date:** c. 2nd century AD
- **Type:** Poem sequence
- **Written / first published:** Eastern Han China; collected in the Wenxuan, c. 530
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Nineteen%20Old%20Poems%20Han%20Music%20Bureau%20poets)

The Nineteen Old Poems were grouped together in the sixth-century anthology Wenxuan, which presented them as anonymous works of the Han. Each is a short lyric in five-character lines, spoken by a traveller far from home, an abandoned wife, a drinker contemplating graves outside the city gate, or a lover separated from a beloved as the Herd Boy and Weaver Girl stars are separated by the Milky Way. Their language is simple and their emotions universal: the shortness of life, the indifference of time, the urge to seize pleasure. Critics such as Liu Xie and Zhong Rong ranked them the crown of five-character poetry, and they became a model for Cao Zhi, Tao Yuanming and the Tang poets.

### The Peacock Flies Southeast (c. 200)

- **Date:** c. 200
- **Type:** Narrative ballad
- **Written / first published:** Lujiang commandery (Anhui); recorded in Yutai xinyong, 6th century
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Peacock%20Flies%20Southeast%20Han%20Music%20Bureau%20poets)

Also known as 'A Poem Written for the Wife of Jiao Zhongqing', this anonymous ballad of over 350 five-character lines is set at the end of the Han dynasty. Liu Lanzhi, a skilled and dutiful wife, is driven out of her home by her husband's mother. The couple vow to be faithful, but her own family forces her to agree to a new marriage; on the wedding night she drowns herself, and her husband hangs himself in the courtyard. They are buried together, and a pair of birds sings over their graves. With vivid dialogue, repetition and set descriptions of clothing and weddings, the poem shows the oral techniques of yuefu at their most developed, and its criticism of family authority made it popular in modern China, where it has been adapted for opera and film.

### The Ballad of Mulan (c. 5th–6th century)

- **Date:** c. 5th–6th century
- **Type:** Narrative ballad
- **Written / first published:** Northern Dynasties China; recorded in Yuefu shiji, 12th century
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Ballad%20of%20Mulan%20Han%20Music%20Bureau%20poets)

The Ballad of Mulan dates probably from the Northern Wei period and survives in Guo Maoqian's twelfth-century anthology of yuefu. It opens with Mulan sighing at her loom: the emperor has called up troops and her aged father's name is on the list. She buys a horse and equipment in the markets, rides north to the Yellow River and the Black Mountains, and fights for twelve years without her comrades discovering her sex. Offered high office, she asks only for a fast camel to take her home, where she changes back into her old clothes, astonishing her fellow soldiers. The ballad ends with an image of two rabbits running side by side, impossible to tell apart. Its brisk, repetitive style and celebration of filial devotion have made Mulan a heroine of drama, opera and film throughout China and beyond.
