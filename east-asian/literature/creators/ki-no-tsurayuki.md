> Heian poet and critic, chief compiler of the Kokinshū and author of the Tosa Diary.

<sub>Portrait: Later imagined portrait of Ki no Tsurayuki · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ki_no_Tsurayuki.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Tsurayuki came from a minor branch of the Ki clan and held mid-ranking court posts. In 905 Emperor Daigo commissioned him and three others to compile the first imperial anthology of Japanese poetry, the Kokin Wakashū. From 930 to 935 he was governor of Tosa Province on Shikoku, and his voyage home became the Tosa Diary.

His kana preface to the Kokinshū, which begins by saying that Japanese poetry takes the human heart as its seed, is the founding statement of Japanese poetics, and his elegant, witty waka set the standard for court poetry. By writing the Tosa Diary in kana in the voice of a woman, he opened the way for women's diary literature.

### Kokin Wakashū (905)

[![Kokin Wakashū](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/Kokin_Wakashu_Genei.jpg/1280px-Kokin_Wakashu_Genei.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kokin_Wakashu_Genei.jpg)

<sub>Gen'ei-bon manuscript of the Kokinshū, 12th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kokin_Wakashu_Genei.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 905
- **Type:** Poetry anthology
- **Written / first published:** Heian-kyō (Kyoto)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kokin%20Wakash%C5%AB%20Ki%20no%20Tsurayuki)

Commissioned by Emperor Daigo and compiled by Ki no Tsurayuki, Ki no Tomonori, Ōshikōchi no Mitsune and Mibu no Tadamine, the Kokinshū contains about 1,100 poems, almost all tanka, arranged in twenty books by topic: six books of the four seasons, followed by congratulations, parting, travel, names of things, five of love, laments and miscellaneous. Within each book poems are ordered to form a progression, for example from the first haze of spring to the falling of the cherry blossoms. The style is elegant, witty and reflective, rich in wordplay (kakekotoba) and personification, in contrast to the directness of the Man'yōshū. Tsurayuki's Japanese preface and Ki no Yoshimochi's Chinese preface set out the first theory of Japanese poetry. It was the first of twenty-one imperial anthologies, and its vocabulary and seasonal associations shaped all Japanese poetry, art and design until the nineteenth century.

### Tosa Diary (c. 935)

[![Tosa Diary](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3e/Tosa_nikki_copied_by_Teika.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tosa_nikki_copied_by_Teika.JPG)

<sub>Copy of the Tosa Diary made by Fujiwara no Teika Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tosa_nikki_copied_by_Teika.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 935
- **Type:** Diary
- **Written / first published:** Journey from Tosa to Heian-kyō
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tosa%20Diary%20Ki%20no%20Tsurayuki)

The diary covers fifty-five days from late 934 to early 935, as Tsurayuki returned from his governorship of Tosa on Shikoku to the capital, mostly by boat along the coast, delayed by bad weather and fear of pirates. It famously opens by saying that diaries are written by men, but a woman will try her hand at one too: by adopting a female persona Tsurayuki could write in kana rather than Chinese. The narrative records farewells, drinking parties, poems composed by passengers including children, and repeated grief for a little girl who died in Tosa. The diary's mixture of prose and waka, humour and sorrow made it the first literary diary in Japanese and opened the way for the great women's diaries of the following century. A copy made by Fujiwara no Teika survives.
