## Biography

According to Ō no Yasumaro's preface, Emperor Tenmu (r. 673–686), concerned that the genealogies and legends held by the great clans were full of errors, had a court attendant of prodigious memory, Hieda no Are, learn the correct versions. After Tenmu's death the project lapsed until Empress Genmei ordered Yasumaro, a court noble, to record what Are recited; he presented the Kojiki in 712. Little else is known of Are, whose gender is even debated. Yasumaro's existence was confirmed in 1979 when his tomb and bronze epitaph were found near Nara.

The compilers faced the problem of writing Japanese in Chinese characters, mixing Chinese prose with characters used for their sound to preserve names, songs and key words. Their work legitimised the imperial line by tracing it to the sun goddess Amaterasu, and in the eighteenth century the scholar Motoori Norinaga made it the foundation of the 'National Learning' movement.

## Key works

### Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters) (712)

[![Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2d/Shinpukuji-bon_Kojiki_%28%E7%9C%9F%E7%A6%8F%E5%AF%BA%E6%9C%AC%E5%8F%A4%E4%BA%8B%E8%A8%98%29.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shinpukuji-bon_Kojiki_%28%E7%9C%9F%E7%A6%8F%E5%AF%BA%E6%9C%AC%E5%8F%A4%E4%BA%8B%E8%A8%98%29.png)

- **Date:** 712
- **Type:** Chronicle of myths and legends
- **Written / first published:** Heijō-kyō (Nara)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kojiki%20%28Records%20of%20Ancient%20Matters%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Kojiki)

The Kojiki, in three books, was presented to Empress Genmei in 712. The first tells of the creation: the divine pair Izanagi and Izanami give birth to the Japanese islands and many gods; Izanami dies and Izanagi pursues her to the underworld; the sun goddess Amaterasu hides in a cave after the outrages of her brother Susanoo and is lured out by a comic dance; and her grandson descends to rule the land. The second and third books narrate the legendary first emperor Jinmu and his successors down to Empress Suiko (d. 628), with tales of the hero Yamato Takeru. Written in a hybrid of Chinese and Japanese, it preserves over a hundred early songs. Its purpose was to establish the descent of the imperial house from Amaterasu. Neglected for centuries in favour of the Chinese-style Nihon shoki of 720, it was revived by Motoori Norinaga in the eighteenth century and became a sacred text of State Shinto before 1945.
