# Lu Xun

*魯迅*

**1881 – 1936** · Chinese  
Born: Shaoxing, Zhejiang · Died: Shanghai  
Movements & eras: [May Fourth & modern Chinese literature](../movements/may-fourth-modern.md)  

> Founder of modern Chinese literature, whose short stories and essays dissected the ills of a society in crisis.

## Biography

Lu Xun, the pen name of Zhou Shuren, grew up in a gentry family in Shaoxing that declined after his grandfather's imprisonment and his father's long illness. He went to Japan in 1902 to study medicine in Sendai, but, as he later told it, a lantern slide of Chinese onlookers at an execution convinced him that the nation's spirit needed curing more than its bodies. Back in China he worked in the Ministry of Education and taught; in 1918 he published A Madman's Diary in the magazine New Youth. He later lived in Xiamen and Guangzhou before settling in Shanghai in 1927, where he led left-wing writers until his death.

Lu Xun wrote only two collections of short stories and a book of retold legends, but they defined modern Chinese fiction: ironic, compassionate and merciless toward hypocrisy, superstition and the 'cannibalism' of the old moral order. He also wrote hundreds of sharp essays (zawen), prose poems, a pioneering history of Chinese fiction and translations. Canonised after 1949 by the Communist Party, he remains the most studied modern Chinese writer.

### A Madman's Diary (1918)

- **Date:** 1918
- **Type:** Short story
- **Written / first published:** New Youth magazine, Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Madman%27s%20Diary%20Lu%20Xun)

Published in the May 1918 issue of New Youth, the story is framed by a short classical-Chinese preface explaining that the narrator found the diary of an old schoolfriend, since recovered from a mental illness. The diary itself, in vernacular Chinese, records the madman's growing conviction that his elder brother, his neighbours and even his doctor intend to eat him, and his discovery, reading between the lines of a history book full of words about benevolence and righteousness, the words 'eat people'. It ends with a cry to save the children. Modelled partly on Gogol's story of the same title, it was the first important work of fiction in modern vernacular Chinese, and its image of Confucian society as cannibalistic became a slogan of the New Culture movement.

### The True Story of Ah Q (1921–22)

- **Date:** 1921–22
- **Type:** Novella
- **Written / first published:** Morning Post supplement, Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20True%20Story%20of%20Ah%20Q%20Lu%20Xun)

Ah Q is a landless odd-job man in the village of Weizhuang who has no proper name, home or family. Beaten and mocked by everyone, he consoles himself by imagining that he has won, for example by thinking of his tormentors as his sons, and in turn bullies those weaker than himself. When the 1911 revolution reaches the area, he decides to join it, mainly in hope of loot and women, but the local gentry simply rename themselves revolutionaries; Ah Q is arrested for a robbery he did not commit and executed, worrying chiefly that he cannot draw a perfect circle on his confession. Serialised under a pseudonym, the story satirised the self-deception and servility Lu Xun saw in Chinese society, and 'Ah Q-ism' became an everyday term.

### Call to Arms (1923)

- **Date:** 1923
- **Type:** Short story collection
- **Written / first published:** Beijing: Xinchao she, 1923
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Call%20to%20Arms%20Lu%20Xun)

Call to Arms (Nahan) collects fourteen stories written between 1918 and 1922, including A Madman's Diary, Kong Yiji, about an impoverished failed scholar mocked in a tavern, Medicine, in which a family buys a bun soaked in an executed revolutionary's blood to cure their son, My Old Home and The True Story of Ah Q. In its preface Lu Xun recalls his youth and his turn from medicine to literature, and describes a conversation in which he compared China to an iron house without windows, full of sleeping people who will suffocate; to wake a few of them would only make their death more painful, yet one cannot say there is no hope of destroying the house. The collection established modern Chinese fiction and is one of the most influential books of the twentieth century in China.

### Wild Grass (1927)

- **Date:** 1927
- **Type:** Prose poems
- **Written / first published:** Beijing: Beixin shuju, 1927
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Wild%20Grass%20Lu%20Xun)

Written in 1924–26, during a period of political repression and personal crisis, the pieces of Wild Grass are prose poems influenced by Baudelaire and Turgenev. They include dream visions, allegories and dialogues: a traveller who keeps walking toward an unknown destination, a corpse that reads its own epitaph, a soldier advancing alone into a 'formation of nothingness', and a tree in a backyard. Their imagery of shadows, death and despair, and their refusal of easy hope, make them the most modernist side of Lu Xun, and they have attracted increasing critical attention since the 1980s.
