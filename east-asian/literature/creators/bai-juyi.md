## Biography

Bai Juyi passed the jinshi in 800 and became an official at court, where he and his friend Yuan Zhen led a movement of 'new yuefu' poems criticising abuses of power and the sufferings of the poor. His outspoken memorials led to demotion in 815 to Jiangzhou, where he wrote the Song of the Pipa Player. He later served as governor of Hangzhou and Suzhou, building a causeway on West Lake, and retired to Luoyang, where he became a Buddhist and arranged his own collected works.

More than 2,800 of Bai's poems survive, more than any other Tang poet, partly because he carefully preserved them. He believed poetry should be clear enough for an old peasant woman to understand, and his Song of Everlasting Sorrow became one of the best-loved narrative poems in Chinese. In Heian Japan his collected works were the most read Chinese book; Murasaki Shikibu and Sei Shōnagon quote him constantly.

### Song of Everlasting Sorrow (806)

[![Song of Everlasting Sorrow](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Chogonka_Emaki_by_Kano_Sansetsu.jpg/1280px-Chogonka_Emaki_by_Kano_Sansetsu.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chogonka_Emaki_by_Kano_Sansetsu.jpg)

<sub>Kanō Sansetsu, illustrated handscroll of the Song of Everlasting Sorrow, 17th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chogonka_Emaki_by_Kano_Sansetsu.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 806
- **Type:** Narrative poem
- **Written / first published:** Zhouzhi, near Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Song%20of%20Everlasting%20Sorrow%20Bai%20Juyi)

In 120 seven-character lines, Bai Juyi tells the story of the Tang emperor Xuanzong's love for his consort Yang Guifei, which led him to neglect the state. When An Lushan's rebellion drives the court from the capital, the soldiers mutiny at Mawei and the emperor is forced to allow her death. Back in the palace after the war, he is haunted by her memory, and a Daoist adept searches heaven and earth, finding her on an island of the immortals, where she sends back keepsakes and recalls their secret vow on the seventh night of the seventh month to be birds flying wing to wing and trees with intertwined branches. The poem ends with a sorrow that will never end. Written when Bai was a young official, it was immediately popular, inspired a prose tale by Chen Hong, Yuan and Qing plays, and was beloved in Japan, where it shaped the opening of The Tale of Genji.

### New Yuefu (809)

- **Date:** 809
- **Type:** Poem sequence
- **Written / first published:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=New%20Yuefu%20Bai%20Juyi)

Bai Juyi composed the fifty New Yuefu while serving as an imperial censor, declaring in his preface that they were written for the ruler, the ministers, the people, things and events, and not for literature's sake. Each poem treats a social or political problem in plain language: in The Old Charcoal Seller, an old man burns charcoal in the southern mountains and hopes the weather will stay cold, only to have his cartload seized by palace eunuchs for a length of silk; other poems describe a man who broke his own arm to avoid conscription, the oppression of taxes and the waste of court luxury. Together with Yuan Zhen's companion pieces they became the model of Chinese socially engaged poetry.

### Song of the Pipa Player (816)

[![Song of the Pipa Player](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/%E5%90%B4%E5%8E%86_%E7%99%BD%E5%82%85%E6%B9%93%E6%B1%9F%E5%9B%BE%E5%8D%B7.jpg/1280px-%E5%90%B4%E5%8E%86_%E7%99%BD%E5%82%85%E6%B9%93%E6%B1%9F%E5%9B%BE%E5%8D%B7.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%90%B4%E5%8E%86_%E7%99%BD%E5%82%85%E6%B9%93%E6%B1%9F%E5%9B%BE%E5%8D%B7.jpg)

<sub>Wu Li, Bai Juyi by the Pen River, Qing handscroll Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%90%B4%E5%8E%86_%E7%99%BD%E5%82%85%E6%B9%93%E6%B1%9F%E5%9B%BE%E5%8D%B7.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 816
- **Type:** Narrative poem
- **Written / first published:** Jiangzhou (Jiujiang), on the Yangtze
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Song%20of%20the%20Pipa%20Player%20Bai%20Juyi)

Bai Juyi's preface explains that in his second year of demotion to Jiangzhou, seeing off a guest at night, he heard the sound of a pipa from a boat; the player, once a famous musician in the capital, had married a merchant who left her alone. In 88 lines the poem describes her music in a series of brilliant similes, like heavy rain and whispering, pearls falling on a jade plate, a silver vase bursting, and then her story of youthful fame and decline. The poet responds that they are both people fallen to the edge of the world, and that his blue official robe is wet with tears. Admired for its rendering of music in words and its compassion, the poem was as popular as the Song of Everlasting Sorrow.
