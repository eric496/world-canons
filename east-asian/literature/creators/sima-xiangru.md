# Sima Xiangru

*司馬相如*

**c. 179 BC – 117 BC** · Chinese  
Born: Chengdu, Shu · Died: Maoling, near Chang'an  
Movements & eras: [Han rhapsody & Music Bureau songs](../movements/han-fu-yuefu.md)  

> The greatest writer of the Han rhapsody (fu), whose dazzling descriptions of imperial hunting parks delighted Emperor Wu.

## Biography

Born in Shu (modern Sichuan), Sima Xiangru served at the court of Emperor Jing and then with the Prince of Liang, whose literary circle encouraged his rhapsodies. Back home and poor, he famously eloped with Zhuo Wenjun, the young widowed daughter of a rich ironmaster, after playing the zither for her; the couple ran a wine shop until her father relented. Emperor Wu read his Rhapsody of Sir Vacuous and summoned him to court, where he served as a literary attendant and envoy to the south-west.

Sima Xiangru's fu pile up rare words, place names, animals, plants and gems in rhyming, rhythmic catalogues, conjuring the splendour of empire while ending with a gentle moral warning against extravagance. Later critics both admired and mocked the form's excess, but his works defined the rhapsody for centuries, and the romance with Zhuo Wenjun became a favourite subject of poetry and drama.

## Key works

### Rhapsody on the Shanglin Park (c. 135 BC)

- **Date:** c. 135 BC
- **Type:** Fu (rhapsody)
- **Written / first published:** Chang'an, Han court
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rhapsody%20on%20the%20Shanglin%20Park%20Sima%20Xiangru)

This rhapsody continues Sima Xiangru's earlier Rhapsody of Sir Vacuous (Zixu fu): two fictional envoys from the states of Chu and Qi boast of their kings' hunting grounds, until a third speaker, Lord No-such, silences them by describing the emperor's Shanglin Park near Chang'an. Its rivers, mountains, rare beasts, trees, palaces and the emperor's great hunt are evoked in cascades of rhymed lines heavy with obscure binomes and ornate characters, some apparently coined for sound. At its climax the emperor, sated with pleasure, repents of extravagance, opens the park to the people and turns to Confucian virtue. Emperor Wu was so delighted that he appointed the author to court. The work epitomises the grand epideictic fu, which the critic Yang Xiong later condemned for encouraging the very excess it claimed to criticise, but it shaped Chinese literary description for centuries.
