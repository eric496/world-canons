> Late Tang poet of dense, allusive and often enigmatic love poems, including several simply titled 'Untitled'.

## Biography

Li Shangyin passed the jinshi in 837 but became caught in the factional struggles of the late Tang court; his marriage into a family of one faction after patronage from the other blighted his career, and he served mostly as a secretary on the staffs of provincial governors. His wife's early death deepened the melancholy of his later work.

Li's regulated verse is famous for its rich imagery, mythological allusion and deliberate ambiguity: readers still argue whether poems such as The Ornamented Zither are about a lover, a dead wife, political disappointment or poetry itself. Neglected by some moralistic critics, he was admired by the Song and has been the favourite of many modern readers for whom his elusive beauty anticipates Symbolism.

### Untitled Poems (9th century)

- **Date:** 9th century
- **Type:** Poems (regulated verse)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Untitled%20Poems%20Li%20Shangyin)

About twenty of Li Shangyin's poems are titled simply 'Untitled'. They speak of secret meetings, unfulfilled longing and separation: the spring silkworm spins thread until it dies, and the candle sheds tears until it is ash; no colourful phoenix wings let lovers fly together, but their hearts are linked like the rhinoceros horn. Scholars have proposed affairs with Daoist nuns or palace ladies, or political allegories of his career, but the poems resist decoding. Their sensuous imagery, tight parallelism and refusal to explain gave Chinese poetry a new kind of private lyric, and they were imitated as the 'Xikun style' in the early Song.

### The Ornamented Zither (c. 858)

- **Date:** c. 858
- **Type:** Poem (regulated verse)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Ornamented%20Zither%20Li%20Shangyin)

The poem begins with an ornamented zither inexplicably having fifty strings, each recalling a year of youth. Its middle couplets string together allusions: Zhuangzi dreaming he was a butterfly, the ancient king of Shu whose spirit became a cuckoo, a mermaid's tears turned to pearls in the moonlit sea, and the warm jade of Lantian giving off mist in the sun. The poem ends by saying that this feeling might have been remembered later, but even at the time it was already bewildering. Interpreters have read it as a love poem, an elegy for his wife, a preface to his collected poems or a meditation on poetry itself. Its beauty and ambiguity have made it the emblem of Li Shangyin's art.
