## Biography

A sixty-fourth-generation descendant of Confucius, Kong Shangren impressed the Kangxi emperor when he lectured during the emperor's visit to Qufu in 1684, and was given office in Beijing. A posting to the Yangzhou region let him meet survivors of the Ming collapse and visit its sites. Soon after The Peach Blossom Fan was staged in 1699 he was dismissed, perhaps because of the play's sympathy for the fallen Ming.

The Peach Blossom Fan, in forty-four scenes, tells the love story of a scholar and a courtesan against the fall of the Ming court at Nanjing, with unusual attention to historical accuracy. With Hong Sheng's Palace of Eternal Youth it is counted the last great chuanqi drama.

- **Date:** 1699
- **Type:** Chuanqi drama (44 scenes)
- **Written / first published:** Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Peach%20Blossom%20Fan%20Kong%20Shangren)

The play follows the young scholar Hou Fangyu and the courtesan Li Xiangjun in Nanjing in the 1640s. Hou gives Li a fan as a token of love; when corrupt officials of the short-lived Southern Ming court try to force her to marry one of them, she resists and strikes her head, spattering the fan with blood, which a painter friend turns into a spray of peach blossoms. As the Manchu armies advance and the court collapses through factionalism, the lovers are separated and finally meet again in a Daoist temple, where a priest tears the fan and tells them that, with country and ruler gone, love is meaningless; both become recluses. Kong Shangren researched the historical events carefully, and the play's mixture of romance and political lament, its colourful minor characters such as the storyteller Liu Jingting, and its unusual unhappy ending made it the last masterpiece of chuanqi.
