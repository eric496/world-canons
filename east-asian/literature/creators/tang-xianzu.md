## Biography

Tang Xianzu was a precocious scholar whose refusal to join the faction of the powerful grand secretary Zhang Juzheng delayed his success in the examinations until 1583. As an official he criticised court corruption and was demoted to a remote post in Guangdong, then served as magistrate of Suichang in Zhejiang. He resigned in 1598 and returned to Linchuan, where he devoted himself to writing and directing plays.

His four 'dream' plays, of which The Peony Pavilion is the most celebrated, explore the power of feeling (qing) against convention and reason, influenced by the thinker Li Zhi and the Taizhou school. Written for the kunqu style of southern drama, their arias are among the finest in Chinese poetry. He died in the same year as Shakespeare, a coincidence often noted.

## Key works

### The Peony Pavilion (1598)

[![The Peony Pavilion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/81/The_Peony_Pavilion_WDL7112.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Peony_Pavilion_WDL7112.jpg)

<sub>Ming-dynasty printed edition Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Peony_Pavilion_WDL7112.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1598
- **Type:** Chuanqi drama (55 scenes)
- **Written / first published:** Linchuan, Jiangxi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Peony%20Pavilion%20Tang%20Xianzu)

Du Liniang, the daughter of a strict prefect, falls asleep in her family's garden and dreams of a young scholar who makes love to her among the peonies. She pines away for him, paints her self-portrait and dies, and is buried in the garden. Three years later the scholar Liu Mengmei, whom she dreamed of, finds the portrait and is visited by her ghost; with the help of the judge of the underworld he opens her grave, and she returns to life. After many trials, including her father's disbelief, the emperor approves their marriage. Tang Xianzu's preface declares that love can cause death and restore life. The long kunqu play's lyrical arias and celebration of passion against Confucian propriety made it hugely influential; women readers wrote commentaries on it, and the 'Startled Dream' scene is still at the heart of the kunqu repertory.
