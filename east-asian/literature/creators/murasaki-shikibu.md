## Biography

Murasaki Shikibu is a nickname; her real name is unknown. She was the daughter of Fujiwara no Tametoki, a scholar of Chinese and provincial governor, and learned Chinese classics by listening to her brother's lessons. She married an older official, Fujiwara no Nobutaka, around 998 and was widowed with a daughter in 1001. Probably already known for parts of Genji, she was brought around 1005 into the service of Empress Shōshi, daughter of the regent Fujiwara no Michinaga, and her diary records life at court in 1008–10. The date of her death is uncertain.

The Tale of Genji, in fifty-four chapters, follows the loves and losses of an imperial prince and then of his descendants, with a psychological subtlety, sense of impermanence and refinement of feeling summed up by later critics as mono no aware, the 'pathos of things'. It has shaped Japanese literature, painting and aesthetics for a millennium. Her diary and a small collection of her poems also survive.

### The Tale of Genji (c. 1001–1010)

[![The Tale of Genji](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4b/Genji_emaki_01003_002.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Genji_emaki_01003_002.jpg)

<sub>Scene from the Genji monogatari emaki, illustrated handscroll, 12th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Genji_emaki_01003_002.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1001–1010
- **Type:** Monogatari (novel)
- **Written / first published:** Heian-kyō (Kyoto), court of Empress Shōshi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tale%20of%20Genji%20Murasaki%20Shikibu)

The Tale of Genji, in fifty-four chapters, follows Hikaru Genji, the handsome and gifted son of an emperor by a low-ranking consort, who is made a commoner to protect him from court politics. In the first part he pursues many women, among them his father's consort Fujitsubo, who bears his secret son, and the girl Murasaki, whom he raises and makes his great love; he is exiled to Suma, returns and rises to the height of power. In the second, his world darkens with a young wife's betrayal and Murasaki's death. The last chapters, set at Uji after Genji's death, follow the melancholy Kaoru and the impetuous Niou in their love for three sisters, ending inconclusively. Written for the circle of Empress Shōshi, the tale is remarkable for its psychological insight, its use of poetry, and its pervasive sense of impermanence. It was illustrated in the twelfth-century Genji scrolls, studied as a classic from the Kamakura period, translated by Arthur Waley in the 1920s, and is often called the world's first novel.

### The Diary of Murasaki Shikibu (c. 1010)

- **Date:** c. 1010
- **Type:** Diary
- **Written / first published:** Heian-kyō (Kyoto)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Diary%20of%20Murasaki%20Shikibu%20Murasaki%20Shikibu)

The diary opens in autumn 1008 at the Tsuchimikado mansion of Fujiwara no Michinaga, where his daughter Empress Shōshi is awaiting the birth of her first child, the future Emperor Go-Ichijō. It records the rituals of the birth, ceremonies and celebrations in detail, and then turns to a letter-like section of portraits of the ladies of the court, including sharp criticisms of Sei Shōnagon, and frank reflections on Murasaki's own unease at court and her learning in Chinese, which she hid. It is a major source both for Heian court life and for the author of Genji, and parts of it were illustrated in a thirteenth-century handscroll.
