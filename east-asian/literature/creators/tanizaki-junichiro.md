# Tanizaki Jun'ichirō

*谷崎潤一郎*

**1886 – 1965** · Japanese  
Born: Tokyo · Died: Yugawara, Kanagawa  
Movements & eras: [Meiji to early Shōwa modern literature](../movements/meiji-modern.md), [Postwar Japanese literature](../movements/postwar-japanese.md)  

> Novelist of desire, beauty and tradition, author of The Makioka Sisters and In Praise of Shadows.

## Biography

Born into a merchant family in Tokyo, Tanizaki made his name with stories of sensual obsession such as The Tattooer (1910). After the 1923 Kantō earthquake he moved to the Kansai region, whose culture increasingly attracted him. He made three modern-language translations of The Tale of Genji. His long novel The Makioka Sisters was banned by wartime censors and published after the war.

Tanizaki's work moves from Western-influenced decadence to an exploration of traditional Japanese aesthetics, as in the essay In Praise of Shadows (1933), while keeping his fascination with erotic power and masochism, as in The Key and Diary of a Mad Old Man. He was repeatedly shortlisted for the Nobel Prize.

## Key works

- [In Praise of Shadows](#in-praise-of-shadows-1933) (1933)
- [The Makioka Sisters](#the-makioka-sisters-194348) (1943–48)

### In Praise of Shadows (1933)

- **Date:** 1933
- **Type:** Essay
- **Written / first published:** Keizai ōrai magazine, Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=In%20Praise%20of%20Shadows%20Tanizaki%20Jun%27ichir%C5%8D)

Starting from the practical problem of installing electric lighting and plumbing in a traditional Japanese house, Tanizaki contrasts a Western aesthetic of brightness and clarity with a Japanese one that finds beauty in shadow: the glow of lacquerware in candlelight, the patina of old objects, the soft light through paper screens, the darkness of a temple toilet, the faces of women in the gloom of old houses, and the Noh actor's costume. Half serious and half ironic, regretting what modernity had destroyed while admitting its convenience, the essay became one of the most influential texts on Japanese aesthetics, widely read by architects and designers around the world.

### The Makioka Sisters (1943–48)

- **Date:** 1943–48
- **Type:** Novel
- **Written / first published:** Chūō kōron (first instalments 1943; banned); book 1946–48
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Makioka%20Sisters%20Tanizaki%20Jun%27ichir%C5%8D)

Set between 1936 and 1941, the novel follows the Makioka sisters: Tsuruko, the eldest, and Sachiko, married and living in Ashiya with her family; the shy, traditional Yukiko, whose marriage negotiations repeatedly fail; and the modern, rebellious Taeko, whose love affairs scandalise the family. Through flower-viewing at Kyoto, illnesses, a great flood and the approach of war, the novel records with leisurely, detailed elegance the customs of a Kansai bourgeois world on the verge of disappearance. Its serialisation was stopped by the military censors in 1943 as irrelevant to the war effort, but Tanizaki continued writing it privately. It is often considered the greatest Japanese novel of the twentieth century.
