## Biography

Born Urabe Kaneyoshi into a family of Shinto priests at the Yoshida Shrine, Kenkō served at court under Emperor Go-Nijō and took Buddhist vows around 1313, perhaps after the emperor's death. He lived in and around Kyoto, was known as a waka poet, and moved in the circles of warriors and courtiers during the turbulent years of the fall of the Kamakura shogunate.

Essays in Idleness, 243 passages ranging from anecdotes to aesthetic reflections, praises imperfection, impermanence and the beauty of things past their prime. Little noticed at first, it became one of the most widely read Japanese classics from the seventeenth century on.

## Key works

### Essays in Idleness (Tsurezuregusa) (c. 1330)

<sub>Manuscript copy of Tsurezuregusa by the poet Shōtetsu, 15th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tsurezuregusa_Codex_Shotetsu.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1330
- **Type:** Zuihitsu (essays)
- **Written / first published:** Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Essays%20in%20Idleness%20%28Tsurezuregusa%29%20Yoshida%20Kenk%C5%8D)

Tsurezuregusa begins with Kenkō saying that, with nothing to do, he spends the day before his inkstone writing down the trifling thoughts that come into his mind. The sections that follow range from a few lines to a few pages: anecdotes of court and temple life, advice on manners and taste, and reflections on death and impermanence. Kenkō argues that if people lived for ever, things would lose their power to move us; that the moon is best partly hidden by clouds and blossoms after they have scattered; and that a set of books is better left incomplete. His aesthetic of suggestion and imperfection, and his blend of Buddhist, Confucian and Daoist ideas, made the work popular from the Edo period onwards, when it was printed and illustrated, and it remains one of the most widely read Japanese classics.
