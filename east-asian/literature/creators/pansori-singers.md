# Pansori singers

*판소리 광대*

**18th century – 19th century** · Korean  
Born: Jeolla region · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Late Joseon pansori & Silhak prose](../movements/late-joseon-prose.md)  
Also in: [Music & Theatre](../../music/creators/pansori-singers.md)  

> The professional storytellers (gwangdae) who created pansori, sung narratives that became the classic Korean popular novels.

## Biography

Pansori emerged in the south-western provinces in the late seventeenth or eighteenth century, performed by a singer with a fan and a drummer for audiences in markets and at festivals and, later, at yangban gatherings. Twelve stories (madang) were known, of which five survive in performance: Chunhyang, Sim Cheong, Heungbu, the Song of the Underwater Palace and the Song of the Red Cliff. In the 1860s the patron Sin Jae-hyo wrote down and revised texts of six of them.

Pansori combines singing, speech and gesture, mixing vulgar humour with elevated Chinese phrases, and its stories spread as anonymous printed Hangul novels. It was proclaimed a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO in 2003.

### The Tale of Chunhyang (18th century)

[![The Tale of Chunhyang](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c3/Chunhyangjeondo_2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chunhyangjeondo_2.jpg)

<sub>Folk painting of scenes from the Tale of Chunhyang Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chunhyangjeondo_2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 18th century
- **Type:** Pansori narrative / novel
- **Written / first published:** Namwon, Jeolla Province
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tale%20of%20Chunhyang%20Pansori%20singers)

In the town of Namwon, Yi Mongryong, the son of the local magistrate, sees Chunhyang, the beautiful daughter of a retired kisaeng, on a swing at the Dano festival, and they marry in secret. When his father is transferred to the capital, he must leave her. The new magistrate, Byeon Hakdo, demands that Chunhyang serve him; when she refuses, insisting on her fidelity, he has her beaten and imprisoned and plans to execute her at his birthday feast. Mongryong, having passed the state examination and become a secret royal inspector (amhaeng eosa), returns disguised as a beggar, exposes the magistrate and rescues her. The story existed in many pansori and printed versions, mixing earthy comedy and elevated language, and it is the most famous Korean love story, adapted into countless operas and films, including Im Kwon-taek's Chunhyang (2000).

### The Tale of Sim Cheong (18th–19th century)

- **Date:** 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Pansori narrative / novel
- **Written / first published:** Joseon Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tale%20of%20Sim%20Cheong%20Pansori%20singers)

Sim Cheong's mother dies soon after her birth, and she is raised by her blind father, Sim Hakgyu, for whom she begs and works. When a monk promises that her father's sight will be restored if 300 sacks of rice are offered to the Buddha, she sells herself to merchants who need a virgin to sacrifice to the sea god, and throws herself into the waves. The Dragon King, moved by her filial piety, sends her back inside a lotus flower, which is presented to the emperor; she becomes empress, and holds a great banquet for the blind of the kingdom to find her father, whose eyes open at the sight of her. A celebration of filial devotion and one of the five surviving pansori, it is a cornerstone of Korean popular literature.

### The Tale of Heungbu (18th–19th century)

- **Date:** 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Pansori narrative / novel
- **Written / first published:** Joseon Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tale%20of%20Heungbu%20Pansori%20singers)

Nolbu, the greedy elder brother, takes the family inheritance and drives out his kind younger brother Heungbu and his many children, who live in great poverty. Heungbu mends the broken leg of a swallow, which returns the next spring with a gourd seed; the gourds that grow open to reveal rice, gold and a house. Nolbu deliberately breaks a swallow's leg in order to get the same reward, but his gourds release goblins, creditors and floods that ruin him, until Heungbu takes him in. Full of comic catalogues and satire of a society where money was transforming old hierarchies, the story is one of the five surviving pansori and a favourite Korean folk tale.
