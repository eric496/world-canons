> The blind biwa-playing monks (biwa hōshi) and unknown authors who shaped The Tale of the Heike.

## Biography

The authorship of The Tale of the Heike is unknown. The essayist Yoshida Kenkō reported that a former official, Yukinaga, wrote it and taught it to a blind man named Shōbutsu to chant, but this cannot be verified. The tale developed over the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries in many versions, read and recited, and was finally fixed in 1371 by the blind master Kakuichi, whose text is the standard one.

The biwa hōshi chanted the tale to the lute in temples and at gatherings, partly as a Buddhist pacification of the spirits of the dead. Their art gave the war tale its rhythmic prose, its stock scenes of battle and death, and its overarching theme of the impermanence of worldly glory.

## Key works

### The Tale of the Heike (13th century; Kakuichi text 1371)

- **Date:** 13th century; Kakuichi text 1371
- **Type:** Gunki monogatari (war tale)
- **Written / first published:** Kyoto; chanted to the biwa
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Tale%20of%20the%20Heike%20Biwa%20storytellers%20of%20the%20Heike)

The tale narrates the rise of the Taira (Heike) under the arrogant Taira no Kiyomori in the twelfth century and their destruction by the Minamoto (Genji) in the Genpei War of 1180–85. Its opening lines, on the bell of the Gion monastery that rings out the impermanence of all things and the flowers that show the proud must fall, are known to every Japanese. Episodes include the young warrior Atsumori slain by Kumagai Naozane, who then becomes a monk; the brilliant general Minamoto no Yoshitsune; the sea battle of Dannoura in 1185, in which the child emperor Antoku drowns with his grandmother; and, in a final chapter, the life of his mother Kenreimon'in as a nun. Developed by blind biwa-playing monks and fixed in the version of the master Kakuichi in 1371, its rhythmic prose mixes Chinese and Japanese and Buddhist and warrior values. It supplied countless plots for Noh, kabuki and later fiction.
