# Hyangga poets

*鄕歌 작가*

**c. 600 – c. 1000** · Korean  
Born: Silla · Died:   
Movements & eras: [Silla hyangga & Goryeo literature](../movements/silla-goryeo-literature.md)  

> The monks, hwarang youths and others of Silla and early Goryeo who composed the hyangga, the earliest surviving poems in Korean.

## Biography

Hyangga, 'native songs', were composed in Silla from roughly the late sixth to the tenth century. A royal anthology of them, the Samdaemok, was compiled in 888 but is lost; only fourteen survive in Iryeon's Samguk yusa (c. 1281) and eleven Buddhist hymns by the monk Gyunyeo (923–973) in his biography. Named authors include the monk Wolmyeong, author of the Song of Dosol and a requiem for his sister, and Master Chungdam, who wrote the Ode to the Knight Kipa.

The poems are written in hyangchal, a system that used Chinese characters partly for their meaning and partly for their Korean sound, so their exact readings are still debated. Many are Buddhist prayers, elegies or spells believed to have power over nature and spirits, and their four-, eight- and ten-line forms were the first Korean poetic structures.

### Requiem for the Dead Sister (8th century)

- **Date:** 8th century
- **Type:** Hyangga
- **Written / first published:** Silla; preserved in Samguk yusa
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Requiem%20for%20the%20Dead%20Sister%20Hyangga%20poets)

According to the Samguk yusa, the monk Wolmyeong, also a skilled flute player, composed this song at a memorial service for his younger sister, and a wind blew the paper money offering westward, toward the Pure Land. The poem asks how the road of life and death can be so near and yet so fearsome, and why she has gone without a word; it compares the siblings to leaves on one branch, scattered in early autumn wind to unknown places, and ends with the poet resolving to cultivate the Way while waiting to meet her in Amitabha's Pure Land. Combining personal grief with Buddhist faith in its ten-line form, it is regarded as the most accomplished hyangga.

### Hyangga (Native Songs) (c. 600–1000)

<sub>Copy of the Samguk yusa, which preserves fourteen hyangga Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Memorabilia_of_the_Three_Kingdoms_in_museum.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 600–1000
- **Type:** Poems
- **Written / first published:** Silla; preserved in Samguk yusa (c. 1281) and Gyunyeo jeon (1075)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hyangga%20%28Native%20Songs%29%20Hyangga%20poets)

Only twenty-five hyangga survive: fourteen recorded in the monk Iryeon's Samguk yusa, a collection of legends and Buddhist history of the Three Kingdoms, and eleven hymns on the vows of the bodhisattva Samantabhadra by the monk Gyunyeo. The Samguk yusa embeds each song in a story of its making: the Song of Cheoyong, sung by a dragon's son who finds a plague spirit in bed with his wife and drives it away by dancing; the Song of Dosol, which made one of two suns vanish; and the Ode to the Knight Kipa by Master Chungdam. The poems are written in hyangchal, Chinese characters used for meaning and Korean sound, and their reading remains partly conjectural. They show the Buddhist culture of Silla and are the foundation of Korean vernacular poetry.
