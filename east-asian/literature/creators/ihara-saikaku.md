# Ihara Saikaku

*井原西鶴*

**1642 – 1693** · Japanese  
Born: Osaka · Died: Osaka  
Movements & eras: [Edo literature](../movements/edo-literature.md)  

> Osaka merchant-poet who created the ukiyo-zōshi, witty prose fiction of love and money in the 'floating world'.

<sub>Portrait: Portrait of Ihara Saikaku · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ihara-Saikaku-Portrait.png)</sub>

## Biography

Saikaku was probably born into a prosperous merchant family in Osaka. He first won fame as a haikai poet of the Danrin school, renowned for speed composition: in 1684 he is said to have composed 23,500 verses in a day and a night. After the death of his young wife he handed his business to others and turned to writing.

Beginning with The Life of an Amorous Man (1682), Saikaku wrote fiction about the pleasures and passions of townspeople, then turned to warrior tales and to the merchant's pursuit of wealth, as in The Japanese Family Storehouse. His terse, elliptical style, comic realism and close knowledge of the pleasure quarters made him the founder of popular Edo fiction.

### The Life of an Amorous Man (1682)

- **Date:** 1682
- **Type:** Ukiyo-zōshi (novel)
- **Written / first published:** Osaka
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Life%20of%20an%20Amorous%20Man%20Ihara%20Saikaku)

Saikaku's first work of fiction, in fifty-four chapters echoing The Tale of Genji, follows Yonosuke, the son of a wealthy merchant and a famous courtesan, from his first stirrings of desire at seven through a life spent in the pleasure quarters of Kyoto, Osaka and Edo and the provinces, cataloguing thousands of lovers of both sexes. Disinherited and then inheriting a fortune, at sixty he sets sail with friends for a legendary Isle of Women and disappears. Written in a dense, witty, allusive style that parodies the classics, and illustrated by Saikaku himself, the book was an enormous success and created the genre later called ukiyo-zōshi, popular fiction of the urban floating world.

### The Life of an Amorous Woman (1686)

- **Date:** 1686
- **Type:** Ukiyo-zōshi (novel)
- **Written / first published:** Osaka
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Life%20of%20an%20Amorous%20Woman%20Ihara%20Saikaku)

Two young men visit an old woman living in a hermitage outside Kyoto, who tells them the story of her life. The daughter of a minor court official, she was exiled for an affair at thirteen and passed through every kind of role open to a woman of pleasure: dancer, concubine to a lord, high-ranking courtesan in Shimabara, then progressively lower grades of prostitute, servant, seamstress and bathhouse girl, until she ends as a common streetwalker. In a temple filled with statues of the 500 arhats she sees the faces of her past lovers, and resolves to renounce the world. Saikaku's frank and humorous yet bleak portrait was the basis of Mizoguchi Kenji's film The Life of Oharu (1952).

### The Japanese Family Storehouse (1688)

- **Date:** 1688
- **Type:** Ukiyo-zōshi (stories)
- **Written / first published:** Osaka
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Japanese%20Family%20Storehouse%20Ihara%20Saikaku)

Subtitled 'The Millionaires' Gospel Modernised', the book presents thirty stories of townspeople who make fortunes through thrift, ingenuity and luck, or lose them through extravagance and folly. A Kyoto merchant becomes rich by selling cloth by the piece rather than the bolt; an Edo man makes money from scraps; a miser's frugality becomes absurd. Saikaku writes with a mixture of admiration and irony about money as the new value of the merchant class. It is one of the first works anywhere to take commerce as the central subject of fiction and offers a vivid picture of the economy of Genroku-era Japan.
