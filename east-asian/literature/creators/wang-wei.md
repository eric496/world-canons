## Biography

Wang Wei passed the jinshi examination young and had a long official career in Chang'an, rising to the rank of Right Assistant Director of the Department of State Affairs. Captured during the An Lushan rebellion, he was forced to serve the rebel regime, an act for which he was pardoned partly thanks to his brother and a poem expressing loyalty. A lay Buddhist, he spent much of his later life at his estate on the Wang River south of the capital, after the death of his wife and mother.

Wang Wei's short poems on hills, bamboo groves, birds and empty mountains render scenes with such restraint that the observing self seems to vanish, an effect often linked to Chan Buddhism. He was also celebrated as a painter, and Su Shi's remark that his poems contained paintings and his paintings poems made him the legendary founder of literati painting, although no work certainly by his hand survives.

### Wang River Collection (c. 750)

- **Date:** c. 750
- **Type:** Poem sequence
- **Written / first published:** Wang River estate, Lantian, near Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Wang%20River%20Collection%20Wang%20Wei)

Wang Wei and his friend Pei Di each wrote twenty five-character quatrains on twenty named spots of his estate at Wangchuan: a hill, a lake, a grove of bamboo, a magnolia enclosure. The best known, Deer Enclosure, tells of an empty mountain where no one is seen, only the echo of voices heard, and returning sunlight entering the deep wood to shine again on the green moss. The poems avoid overt emotion and the first person, letting sound, light and silence suggest a Buddhist sense of emptiness. Wang Wei is also said to have painted the estate in a handscroll, now lost but copied for centuries, so that the Wangchuan became an ideal landscape for both poets and painters.

### Seeing Off Yuan the Second on a Mission to Anxi (c. 750)

- **Date:** c. 750
- **Type:** Poem (quatrain)
- **Written / first published:** Weicheng, near Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Seeing%20Off%20Yuan%20the%20Second%20on%20a%20Mission%20to%20Anxi%20Wang%20Wei)

Wang Wei's quatrain describes the morning rain in Weicheng, west of the capital, settling the light dust, the inn green with willows (whose branches were given to departing travellers), and urges his friend, sent to the far western garrison of Anxi, to drink one more cup of wine, since west of the Yang Pass there will be no old friends. Set to music, it became known as the 'Three Refrains at Yang Pass', with the last line repeated, and was sung at farewells for centuries. It shows Wang Wei's ability to render emotion entirely through scene and gesture.
