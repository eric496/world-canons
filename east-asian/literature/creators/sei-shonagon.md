## Biography

Sei Shōnagon, a court name, was the daughter of the poet Kiyohara no Motosuke. Around 993 she entered the service of Empress Teishi, consort of Emperor Ichijō, and served her until the empress's death in 1000, after the rival Fujiwara faction of Michinaga had eclipsed Teishi's family. Little is known of her later life.

The Pillow Book, a collection of lists, anecdotes, observations and opinions, is sharp, funny, confident and visually precise, celebrating the elegance of Teishi's salon. It founded the zuihitsu ('following the brush') genre of the miscellany. Murasaki Shikibu's diary criticised her as conceited, and the two women have been compared ever since.

## Key works

### The Pillow Book (c. 1002)

[![The Pillow Book](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/Pillow_Book_Illustrated2.JPG/1280px-Pillow_Book_Illustrated2.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pillow_Book_Illustrated2.JPG)

<sub>Illustrated handscroll of The Pillow Book Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pillow_Book_Illustrated2.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1002
- **Type:** Zuihitsu (miscellany)
- **Written / first published:** Heian-kyō (Kyoto), court of Empress Teishi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Pillow%20Book%20Sei%20Sh%C5%8Dnagon)

The Pillow Book (Makura no sōshi) consists of some 300 sections of three kinds: lists, such as 'Things that make one's heart beat faster', 'Hateful things' or 'Rare things'; diary-like anecdotes of life in Empress Teishi's salon, in which Sei Shōnagon's quick wit and knowledge of Chinese poetry often win the day; and short reflections on nature and the seasons, the most famous being the opening, which names the most beautiful time of each season, dawn in spring. An afterword says that the empress's brother gave her a bundle of paper on which she wrote down whatever came to mind. Written with sharp observation, snobbery, humour and visual precision, it records a brilliant court culture just before its eclipse. It founded the zuihitsu genre and is, with The Tale of Genji, the central classic of Heian prose.
