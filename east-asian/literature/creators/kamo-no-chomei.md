## Biography

Chōmei was the son of the head priest of the Shimogamo Shrine in Kyoto but failed to inherit the post. He was a skilled poet and biwa player and served in Go-Toba's poetry bureau. Around 1204 he took Buddhist vows and eventually built a tiny portable hut on Mount Hino, where in 1212 he wrote his famous essay.

Hōjōki describes the fires, whirlwind, famine and earthquake that struck Kyoto in his lifetime as proof of impermanence, then describes his contentment in his hut, ending with a doubt whether his attachment to it is itself a sin. He also wrote a treatise on poetry and a collection of Buddhist tales.

## Key works

### Hōjōki (An Account of My Hut) (1212)

[![Hōjōki (An Account of My Hut)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Hojyoki_codex_Daifukukohji.jpg/1280px-Hojyoki_codex_Daifukukohji.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hojyoki_codex_Daifukukohji.jpg)

<sub>Daifukukōji manuscript of Hōjōki Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hojyoki_codex_Daifukukohji.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1212
- **Type:** Essay
- **Written / first published:** Hino, south-east of Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=H%C5%8Dj%C5%8Dki%20%28An%20Account%20of%20My%20Hut%29%20Kamo%20no%20Ch%C5%8Dmei)

Hōjōki opens with one of the most famous passages in Japanese: the flowing river never stops and yet its water is never the same, and the foam on still pools vanishes and forms again, like people and their dwellings. Chōmei then describes five disasters he witnessed in Kyoto: the great fire of 1177, a whirlwind, the forced move of the capital to Fukuhara in 1180, the famine of 1181–82, when the dead lay in the streets, and the earthquake of 1185. He tells how he gradually reduced his dwellings until he built a hut ten feet square on Mount Hino, and describes his contented life there with music, poetry and walks. In the final paragraph he asks whether his love of this hut is itself an attachment preventing salvation, and falls silent. Short and perfectly constructed in a mixed Chinese-Japanese style, it is a central text of the medieval literature of reclusion.
