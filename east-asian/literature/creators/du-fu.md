## Biography

Du Fu came from a family of scholar-officials but repeatedly failed to win a post through the examinations. He spent his thirties in the capital Chang'an seeking patronage, met Li Bai, and obtained only a minor office shortly before the An Lushan rebellion broke out in 755. Captured by rebels, he escaped to the court of the new emperor, served briefly as an adviser, and was then sidelined. From 759 he lived as a refugee, notably in a thatched cottage at Chengdu, then down the Yangtze, and died on a boat in Hunan.

Little read in his lifetime, Du Fu was recognised from the ninth century onward as the master of every form, above all the eight-line regulated verse, which he made capable of dense historical reflection. His poems record war, conscription, famine and the fate of ordinary people alongside intimate moments with his wife and children, earning him the title 'poet-historian'. For Confucian readers his compassion and loyalty made him a moral as well as a poetic model.

### Ballad of the Army Carts (c. 750)

- **Date:** c. 750
- **Type:** Yuefu ballad
- **Written / first published:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ballad%20of%20the%20Army%20Carts%20Du%20Fu)

The poem opens with a scene on the bridge outside Chang'an: carts rumble, horses neigh, and conscripts march off with bows at their waists while parents, wives and children run alongside, crying, in a cloud of dust. A passer-by questions a soldier, who explains that men are sent to the frontier at fifteen and still serving with white hair, while fields at home lie untended. He says it is now better to bear a daughter, who can marry a neighbour, than a son who will be buried among the weeds; the poem ends with the ghosts of the dead weeping at Kokonor. Written in the new yuefu manner, directly criticising Emperor Xuanzong's expansionist wars, it established Du Fu's reputation as the poet of the people's sufferings.

### Spring View (757)

- **Date:** 757
- **Type:** Poem (regulated verse)
- **Written / first published:** Chang'an, under rebel occupation
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Spring%20View%20Du%20Fu)

Written in the spring of 757, when Du Fu was held in Chang'an after the city had fallen to An Lushan's rebels, this eight-line regulated poem opens with a celebrated antithesis: the state is shattered, yet mountains and rivers endure; in the city spring comes, and grass and trees grow thick. Flowers seem to shed tears and birds startle the heart at the sorrow of parting. War beacons have burned for three months, and a letter from home would be worth ten thousand pieces of gold. The poem ends with the poet scratching his white hair, so thin that it will scarcely hold a hatpin. Its tight parallelism and fusion of public catastrophe with private grief make it a model of Du Fu's art and one of the most memorised poems in East Asia; Bashō echoed its opening at the battlefield of Hiraizumi.

### Autumn Meditations (766)

- **Date:** 766
- **Type:** Poem sequence (regulated verse)
- **Written / first published:** Kuizhou (Fengjie), Sichuan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Autumn%20Meditations%20Du%20Fu)

Written at Kuizhou above the Three Gorges, where Du Fu lived in poverty and ill health, the Autumn Meditations move between the autumn landscape of the gorges, the sound of washing-stones and the poet's longing for the lost capital, recalled in increasingly dreamlike images of palaces, the Kunming Lake and the Meandering River. The eight regulated poems are linked by recurring images and form a single meditation on memory, history and the decline of the dynasty. Their dense syntax and allusive ambiguity were much admired and analysed by later critics, and they are regarded as the most complex achievement of Chinese regulated verse.
