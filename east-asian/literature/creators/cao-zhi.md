# Cao Zhi

*曹植*

**192 – 232** · Chinese  
Born: Dongwuyang, Han China · Died: Chen, Wei  
Movements & eras: [Six Dynasties poetry](../movements/six-dynasties-poetry.md)  

> Son of the warlord Cao Cao and the most admired poet of the Jian'an era, at the end of the Han dynasty.

## Biography

Cao Zhi was the third son of Cao Cao, who founded the Wei state as the Han collapsed, and was for a time his father's favourite and a possible heir. His brilliance and drinking lost him the succession to his elder brother Cao Pi, who after becoming emperor in 220 kept him under close watch, executed his friends and shifted him from fief to fief. Cao Zhi spent his last years petitioning in vain for an official role and died at forty.

Cao Zhi combined the directness of the yuefu ballads with a new richness of image and personal feeling, and critics such as Zhong Rong ranked him the finest poet of his age. His Rhapsody on the Goddess of the Luo River, about a vision of an unattainable divine woman, inspired a celebrated painted scroll attributed to Gu Kaizhi. A legend that he composed a poem about beans and their stalks in seven paces, to save his life, made him a byword for both talent and fraternal cruelty.

## Key works

### Rhapsody on the Goddess of the Luo River (222)

- **Date:** 222
- **Type:** Fu (rhapsody)
- **Written / first published:** Luo River, near Luoyang
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rhapsody%20on%20the%20Goddess%20of%20the%20Luo%20River%20Cao%20Zhi)

In his preface Cao Zhi says that, returning from the capital, he rested by the Luo River and recalled the ancient story of its goddess, Fufei. The rhapsody describes how he glimpses a woman of supernatural beauty on the riverbank; he praises her in a famous catalogue of comparisons, sends her a jade pendant as a pledge, and she responds, but he hesitates in doubt, and she withdraws with her divine retinue, sorrowing that the paths of gods and men are different. He remains, pining, through the night. Later legend read the poem as an expression of his love for his brother's empress, Lady Zhen, a story most scholars reject; others see it as political allegory. Its lyrical fusion of longing and loss made it one of the most loved fu, and it was famously painted in a scroll attributed to Gu Kaizhi and copied in calligraphy by Wang Xianzhi.
