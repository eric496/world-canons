## Biography

Li Qingzhao was the daughter of a scholar-official in the circle of Su Shi and married Zhao Mingcheng, with whom she shared a passion for collecting books, bronzes and inscriptions. When the Jurchen Jin conquered north China in 1127 the couple fled south, losing most of their collection; Zhao died in 1129. Li spent her later years as a refugee in the southern Song, briefly and unhappily remarried and divorced, and completed her husband's catalogue of inscriptions with a moving afterword about their life together.

Only some fifty of her ci and a few shi and essays survive, but they are among the most admired in the language: precise in sound, with simple, colloquial words used to capture fleeting moods of longing, loneliness and loss. She also wrote a sharp critical essay on the ci, insisting it was a distinct art from shi. Later critics took her as the model of the 'delicate and restrained' (wanyue) school.

### Like a Dream (c. 1100)

- **Date:** c. 1100
- **Type:** Ci (lyrics)
- **Written / first published:** Kaifeng
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Like%20a%20Dream%20Li%20Qingzhao)

Two lyrics to the tune 'Like a Dream' are among Li Qingzhao's best-known early works. In one, she recalls a sunset at a riverside pavilion where, tipsy, she lost the way home and rowed deep into the lotus flowers, startling a flock of egrets and gulls into flight. In the other, after a night of wind and rain and heavy sleep, she asks the maid rolling up the blinds about the crabapple blossoms, is told they are unchanged, and corrects her: the leaves will be fat and the flowers thin. In a few lines each poem captures a mood with colloquial ease and visual precision, revealing the playful, observant young poet before the catastrophes of her later life.

### Afterword to the Records on Metal and Stone (1134)

- **Date:** 1134
- **Type:** Prose memoir
- **Written / first published:** Southern Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Afterword%20to%20the%20Records%20on%20Metal%20and%20Stone%20Li%20Qingzhao)

Zhao Mingcheng, Li Qingzhao's husband, compiled a catalogue of inscriptions on ancient bronzes and stones, the Jinshi lu. After his death she prepared it for publication and added an afterword that is one of the first autobiographical narratives by a Chinese woman. She recalls their early married life, when they pawned clothes to buy books and rubbings and played a game of guessing passages from the classics over tea; the growth of their collection; the flight south in 1127 with fifteen cartloads of books; Zhao's death; and the gradual loss of the remaining objects to war, theft and fraud. She ends reflecting on the vanity of possession. Its intimacy and honesty make it a classic of Chinese prose.

### Slow Slow Tune (c. 1130s)

- **Date:** c. 1130s
- **Type:** Ci (lyric)
- **Written / first published:** Southern Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Slow%20Slow%20Tune%20Li%20Qingzhao)

The lyric begins with seven pairs of reduplicated characters, a daring sequence suggesting searching, coldness, desolation and misery, unprecedented in the ci and much praised by later critics. It goes on to describe a late autumn day when the weather turns from warm to cold, a few cups of weak wine that cannot withstand the evening wind, geese flying overhead that she once recognised, chrysanthemums piled up and withered, and rain falling on the wutong tree at dusk drop by drop. The final line asks how one word, 'sorrow', could ever cover all this. Written after the loss of her husband and her home in the north, it is usually considered her masterpiece.
