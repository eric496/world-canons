## Biography

Born near Ueno in Iga to a family of low-ranking samurai, Bashō served a young lord with whom he studied haikai linked verse. After his master's death he moved to Edo, where he became a professional teacher of poetry. In 1680 he withdrew to a hut in Fukagawa, where a disciple planted a banana tree (bashō) that gave him his name. From 1684 he made a series of journeys on foot, the longest in 1689 through northern Honshū, and he died of illness in Osaka on another journey.

Bashō turned haikai, until then mostly a game of wit, into a serious art infused with Zen and Daoist ideas and the tradition of Saigyō and Chinese poets, using the opening hokku (later called haiku) to capture moments of insight in ordinary things. His ideal of sabi, lonely beauty, and later of karumi, lightness, and his poetic travel diaries made him the central figure of Japanese poetry.

### The Old Pond (1686)

- **Date:** 1686
- **Type:** Haiku (hokku)
- **Written / first published:** Fukagawa, Edo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Old%20Pond%20Matsuo%20Bash%C5%8D)

Bashō's hokku consists of three phrases: an old pond, a frog jumps in, the sound of water. It was composed in spring 1686 at his hut in Fukagawa and first appeared in an anthology of frog poems that year. Its novelty lay in replacing the conventional poetic association of frogs with their croaking by the sudden plop of a leap, and in the stillness of the scene that frames the sound. The poem has been interpreted as an image of Zen insight, of the interaction of the timeless and the momentary, or simply as a fresh observation; it has been translated hundreds of times and is the archetype of the haiku form in world literature.

### The Narrow Road to the Deep North (1694; published 1702)

- **Date:** 1694; published 1702
- **Type:** Haibun (travel journal)
- **Written / first published:** Journey through northern Honshū, 1689; published Kyoto, 1702
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Narrow%20Road%20to%20the%20Deep%20North%20Matsuo%20Bash%C5%8D)

In spring 1689 Bashō and his disciple Sora set out from Edo on a journey of some 2,400 kilometres through the northern provinces and down the Sea of Japan coast to Ōgaki. Oku no hosomichi, written up over several years, is a carefully crafted work of haibun, prose interspersed with haiku. It opens by declaring that the months and days are travellers of a hundred ages, and visits places celebrated in classical poetry: at the ruined battlefield of Hiraizumi Bashō echoes Du Fu and writes of the summer grasses that are all that remain of warriors' dreams; at the mountain temple of Risshakuji, the cicadas' voice seeps into the rocks; at Sado, the Milky Way stretches over a rough sea. A meditation on travel, impermanence and the poetic tradition, it is the most celebrated work of Japanese travel literature, illustrated by Buson in handscrolls and screens.
