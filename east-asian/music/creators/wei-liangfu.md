# Wei Liangfu

*魏良輔*

**c. 1489 – c. 1566** · Chinese  
Born: Yuzhang (Nanchang) · Died: Taicang  
Genres & eras: [Ming chuanqi & kunqu](../movements/kunqu.md)  

> Ming singer and musician of Taicang near Kunshan, traditionally credited with creating the refined kunqu singing style.

## Biography

Wei Liangfu was a singer and physician who lived in Taicang in Jiangsu in the mid sixteenth century. According to later accounts he spent ten years refining the local Kunshan style of singing southern drama, working with the flute player Zhang Yetang and others, and gave it a slow, flowing manner called 'water-mill tune' (shuimo diao), with careful attention to the tones and pronunciation of each syllable.

A short treatise on singing, the Qulü, is attributed to him. The style he shaped, accompanied by dizi flute, sheng and pipa, spread from Suzhou to the whole empire, and Liang Chenyu's Washing Silk (c. 1560s) showed it could carry a full chuanqi play. His importance is partly legendary, as the history of kunqu was written by its admirers, but he is honoured as the founder of the form.

## Key works & forms

### Kunqu (the water-mill style) (c. 1540s–1560s)

- **Date:** c. 1540s–1560s
- **Type:** Theatre and singing style
- **Where performed / held:** Kunshan and Taicang, Jiangsu
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kunqu%20%28the%20water-mill%20style%29%20Wei%20Liangfu)

Kunqu takes its name from the town of Kunshan near Suzhou, one of several local styles for singing southern drama in the Ming. In the mid sixteenth century Wei Liangfu and fellow musicians refined it into a style in which every syllable is sung with its correct tone and articulated in three parts, head, belly and tail, with gracefully ornamented melody over the dizi flute. Because each word is drawn out like water from a mill, it was called shuimo diao. The playwright Liang Chenyu wrote Washing Silk for it, and within a few decades kunqu became the elite theatre of the empire, performed by household troupes of the rich and by professional companies. Its dance, gesture and role types fed into almost every later regional opera. Kunqu declined in the nineteenth century but was saved by a Suzhou school of actors in the 1920s, and in 2001 it was among the first items proclaimed by UNESCO as Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.
