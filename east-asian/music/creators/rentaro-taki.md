# Rentarō Taki

*瀧廉太郎*

**1879 – 1903** · Japanese  
Born: Tokyo · Died: Ōita  
Genres & eras: [Western-style composers](../movements/modern-composers.md)  

> Pioneering Meiji composer of songs and piano music, best known for Kōjō no Tsuki.

## Biography

Taki Rentarō was the son of a government official and grew up in several provincial towns, including Taketa in Ōita. He entered the Tokyo Music School in 1894, aged fifteen, and quickly became its most gifted student, teaching there after graduation. In 1901 he went to study at the Leipzig Conservatory, one of the first Japanese composers to study in Europe, but he contracted tuberculosis within months, returned home in 1902 and died in Ōita the following year, aged twenty-three.

In a career of only a few years he wrote songs for school songbooks, including Kōjō no Tsuki and Hana, and some of the first Japanese piano pieces. His songs, Western in harmony but Japanese in melodic feeling, are still among the best known in Japan.

### Hana (1900)

- **Date:** 1900
- **Type:** Song for two voices
- **Where performed / held:** Part of the cycle Shiki (Four Seasons), Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hana%20Rentar%C5%8D%20Taki)

Hana is the first song of Taki's four-part cycle Shiki (The Four Seasons), published in 1900, and sets a poem by Takeshima Hagoromo about boats rowing on the Sumida River among cherry blossoms. Written as a duet with piano, it is one of the earliest Japanese art songs and remains a standard for school and amateur choirs, sung each spring.

### Kōjō no Tsuki (1901)

- **Date:** 1901
- **Type:** Song
- **Where performed / held:** Published in Chūgaku shōka (Tokyo, 1901)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=K%C5%8Dj%C5%8D%20no%20Tsuki%20Rentar%C5%8D%20Taki)

Commissioned for a songbook for middle schools, Kōjō no Tsuki sets a poem by Doi Bansui contrasting the moonlit cherry-blossom banquets of a castle in its days of glory with its ruins and the unchanging moon. Taki's melody, in a minor-like mode that sounds both Japanese and Western, is said to be inspired by the ruined Oka Castle at Taketa, where he spent part of his boyhood. It was originally published unaccompanied; Yamada Kōsaku later made a piano arrangement, changing a note and slowing it, and this version is often sung today. The song has been recorded by singers, choirs and jazz musicians, including Thelonious Monk.
