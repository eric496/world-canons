# Noh playwrights (anonymous & attributed)

*能作者*

**c. 1350 – c. 1600** · Japanese  
Genres & eras: [Noh & kyōgen](../movements/noh-kyogen.md)  

> The authors of the many Noh plays whose writers are unknown or only traditionally named, from the fourteenth to the sixteenth century.

## Biography

The modern Noh repertory of about 240 plays was largely complete by the early seventeenth century. Many plays were written by Kan'ami, Zeami and their successors Konparu Zenchiku, Kanze Nobumitsu and Konparu Zenpō, but for many others the author is unknown or the traditional attribution is doubtful.

Such anonymous plays include some of the most loved in the repertory, and the ritual Okina predates Noh drama altogether. Texts were handed down in the libraries of the acting families and printed from the early seventeenth century in the luxurious Saga-bon editions.

### Okina (before 14th century)

- **Date:** before 14th century
- **Type:** Ritual Noh

Okina is said to be 'Noh and yet not Noh'. It has no plot. The actors purify themselves before the performance, and in the dance the main actor puts on the white Okina mask on stage, before the audience, to become a god who blesses the realm with peace and abundance; the black-masked Sanbasō, danced by a kyōgen actor, follows with a vigorous rice-planting dance shaking a bell tree. It derives from ritual sarugaku performances at temples and shrines that existed before Kan'ami and Zeami, and it was performed at the start of a formal programme and at New Year. Its masks are among the oldest objects of Noh, and Sanbasō dances passed into kabuki and bunraku.

### Hagoromo (15th century (author uncertain))

- **Date:** 15th century (author uncertain)
- **Type:** Noh play
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hagoromo%20Noh%20playwrights%20%28anonymous%20%26%20attributed%29)

On the pine beach at Miho, with Mount Fuji behind, the fisherman Hakuryō finds a beautiful feather robe hanging on a pine. A heavenly being (tennin) appears and begs for it, since without it she cannot return to the sky. He agrees to give it back if she will dance for him, and when he worries that she will fly away first, she replies that deceit is for humans, not heaven. She dances the dance of the moon palace, then rises over Fuji into the haze. Based on a widespread swan-maiden legend, the play has no tragic element and is loved for its lyricism and its long dance. Its author is unknown; old attributions to Zeami are not accepted.

### Dōjōji (15th–16th century)

- **Date:** 15th–16th century
- **Type:** Noh play
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=D%C5%8Dj%C5%8Dji%20Noh%20playwrights%20%28anonymous%20%26%20attributed%29)

At the Dōjōji temple in Kii province a new bell is being dedicated, and women are forbidden. A dancer arrives and persuades a servant to let her in, then performs a famous slow 'step-dance' (ranbyōshi) with the small drum, in which a single step can take minutes, before leaping up under the stage bell, which falls on her. The abbot tells the legend of a girl whose love for a travelling monk turned her into a serpent who coiled round the bell where he hid and burned him to death. When the bell is raised a serpent demon emerges and is driven off by the monks' prayers. The play, a reworking of the older Kanemaki, is one of the most demanding in the repertory, and the legend was adapted in the kabuki dance Musume Dōjōji.
