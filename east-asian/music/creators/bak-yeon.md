# Bak Yeon

*박연 (朴堧)*

**1378 – 1458** · Korean  
Born: Yeongdong, Chungcheong Province · Died:   
Genres & eras: [Korean aak & hyangak](../movements/korean-court-music.md)  

> Joseon court official who rebuilt the ritual music of Korea under King Sejong, recasting its instruments and pitch standards.

## Biography

Bak Yeon passed the civil examination in 1411 and in 1425 was put in charge of court music under King Sejong. Finding the ritual music inherited from Goryeo in disorder, he studied Chinese ritual texts, reconstructed the ancient pitch pipes, and in 1427 produced a new set of stone chimes (pyeonggyeong) from stone found in Korea at Namyang, instead of relying on imports from China. He submitted dozens of memorials on music and ritual and oversaw the reform of the court orchestras.

His reforms established aak, Confucian ritual music in the Chinese manner, on a solid Korean basis; the music of the Munmyo shrine to Confucius still follows this tradition. He is counted with Ureuk and Wang San-ak as one of the three great musicians of Korea and was a skilled player of the flute (daegeum).

## Key works & forms

- **Date:** 1427
- **Type:** Ritual instrument
- **Where performed / held:** Joseon court, Hanseong; tradition continued at the National Gugak Center, Seoul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Pyeonggyeong%20of%201427%20%28Korean%20stone%20chimes%29%20Bak%20Yeon)

Stone chimes, with bells the fixed-pitch instruments of Confucian ritual orchestras, had to be imported from China, and the set used in Korea was incomplete and poorly tuned. In 1425 a suitable stone was found at Namyang, and Bak Yeon used it to make a new set of sixteen chimes in two rows, hung in a decorated frame and tuned to twelve pipes he had reconstructed from ancient texts. When the set was tested before King Sejong in 1433, the king is recorded as noticing that one chime was slightly high, and on inspection a line of ink was found on the stone where the mason had not finished grinding it, a story often told of Sejong's ear. The pyeonggyeong and the matching bronze bells (pyeonjong) are still central to the aak of the Munmyo and Jongmyo shrines.
