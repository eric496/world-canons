# Takemoto Gidayū

*竹本義太夫*

**1651 – 1714** · Japanese  
Born: Tennōji, Osaka · Died: Osaka  
Genres & eras: [Bunraku & kabuki](../movements/bunraku-kabuki.md)  

> Chanter who founded the Takemoto-za puppet theatre in Osaka; his style of narration, gidayū-bushi, became the voice of bunraku.

## Biography

Born a farmer's son in Osaka, Gidayū trained in the jōruri chanting of Kyoto and in 1684 opened his own puppet theatre, the Takemoto-za, in Dōtonbori. In 1685 he began a long partnership with the playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon, who wrote many of his greatest successes, including The Love Suicides at Sonezaki (1703).

His powerful, emotionally varied style of narration, accompanied by the thick-necked shamisen, absorbed elements of Noh chant and earlier jōruri and gave the chanter (tayū) the voices of every character as well as the narration. Gidayū-bushi became so dominant that 'gidayū' became a general name for puppet chanting, and the Takemoto line survives in the bunraku of today.

When Takemoto Gidayū opened his puppet theatre in Osaka in 1684, he brought a powerful new manner of narration that fused earlier jōruri styles with Noh chant and popular song. The tayū sits at the side of the stage with a text on a lacquered stand, speaking and singing every character as well as the narration, while a shamisen player beside him supports and goads him. The style soon dominated puppet theatre and passed to kabuki, where gidayū musicians accompany plays adapted from bunraku. It was also performed in concert, and from the Meiji period young women sang it in a craze called musume gidayū. In modern bunraku the tayū, shamisen and three-man puppets are equally esteemed, and the art was proclaimed UNESCO Intangible Heritage in 2003 (listed 2008).
