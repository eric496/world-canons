# Bronze Age casters of China

*中國青銅工匠*

**c. 1600 BC – c. 221 BC** · Chinese  
Genres & eras: [Zhou ritual music & bell sets](../movements/zhou-ritual-music.md)  
Also in: [Art](../../art/creators/chinese-bronze-casters.md)  

> The anonymous foundry workers of Shang and Zhou China, whose piece-mould casting produced ritual vessels and tuned bell sets.

## Biography

The bronzes of ancient China were made in workshops attached to royal and princely courts, using clay piece-moulds rather than the lost-wax method common elsewhere. The same craftsmen who cast ritual vessels also cast bells, which were made without clappers and struck from outside with mallets. Their names are not recorded, though inscriptions name the rulers who commissioned the objects.

For music their great achievement was the two-tone bell: an almond-shaped cross-section lets one bell sound two distinct pitches, a minor or major third apart, depending on where it is struck. Tuning was refined by filing the inside of the bell. The set from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng, inscribed with the names of pitches and their equivalents in other states, is the supreme example of their art.

## Key works & forms

- **Date:** c. 433 BC
- **Type:** Bronze bell set
- **Where performed / held:** Hubei Provincial Museum, Wuhan (excavated at Suizhou, 1978)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Bianzhong%20of%20Marquis%20Yi%20of%20Zeng%20Bronze%20Age%20casters%20of%20China)

When the tomb of Marquis Yi, ruler of the small state of Zeng, was excavated at Suizhou in Hubei in 1978, archaeologists found a ritual orchestra laid out as if for a performance: stone chimes, zithers, mouth organs, flutes, drums and, along two walls of the central chamber, a set of sixty-five bronze bells hung on an L-shaped lacquered wooden frame supported by bronze figures of warriors. The bells weigh over two and a half tonnes in all and span about five octaves. Almond-shaped in cross-section, each produces two distinct notes when struck at the centre or the side, and gold-inlaid inscriptions name these pitches and their equivalents in the systems of other states, revealing a sophisticated theory of absolute pitch and transposition. The set was dated by a bell given by the King of Chu in 433 BC. Still playable, it has been reproduced and recorded, and was used in the music for the Hong Kong handover in 1997. The bells are also treated as a masterpiece of bronze casting in the art section.
