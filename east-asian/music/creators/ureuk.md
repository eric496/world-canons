# Ureuk

*우륵 (于勒)*

**6th century – 6th century** · Korean  
Born: Gaya confederacy · Died: Silla  
Genres & eras: [Korean aak & hyangak](../movements/korean-court-music.md)  

> Musician of the Gaya kingdom who, by tradition, perfected the gayageum zither and brought it to Silla.

## Biography

According to the twelfth-century Samguk sagi, King Gasil of Gaya had a twelve-string zither made on the model of the Chinese zheng and ordered the musician Ureuk to compose twelve pieces for it, named after places in Gaya. As Gaya declined, Ureuk defected to Silla around 551, where King Jinheung settled him near Chungju and assigned students to learn his music; Silla conquered the last Gaya states in 562.

Ureuk's pieces are lost, but the instrument, the gayageum, became the most important string instrument of Korean music. A spot by the Namhan River at Chungju, Tangeumdae, is traditionally where he played, and he is honoured as one of the three great musicians of Korean history, with Wang San-ak and Bak Yeon.

- **Date:** 6th century
- **Type:** Instrument and repertory
- **Where performed / held:** Gaya and Silla (Chungju)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20gayageum%20and%20Ureuk%27s%20twelve%20pieces%20Ureuk)

The Samguk sagi records that King Gasil of Gaya, wishing his kingdom to have its own music, had a zither made and commanded Ureuk to compose twelve pieces named after the districts of Gaya. Ureuk brought the instrument to Silla, where it became part of court music, and a Silla zither of the eighth century, the shiragi-goto, survives in the Shōsōin at Nara. The gayageum has twelve silk strings stretched over movable bridges on a long hollow body, plucked with the fingers of the right hand while the left hand presses the strings to bend and vibrate the pitches, giving Korean music its characteristic expressive ornaments. The twelve pieces are lost, but the instrument has been played continuously for fifteen centuries, in court music (pungnyu gayageum) and folk music (sanjo gayageum), and modern versions have up to twenty-five strings.
