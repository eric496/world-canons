# Pansori singers

*판소리 명창*

**c. 1700 – present** · Korean  
Genres & eras: [Pansori & sanjo](../movements/pansori-sanjo.md)  
Also in: [Literature](../../literature/creators/pansori-singers.md)  

> The professional singers (gwangdae) who created and transmitted pansori, from anonymous eighteenth-century performers to the famed master singers (myeongchang) of the nineteenth century.

## Biography

Pansori emerged in the southwestern Jeolla region in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, probably from shamanic songs and the narratives of travelling entertainers. Its singers were low-born professional performers who sang in market squares and at festivals and, by the nineteenth century, for aristocrats and the court. The so-called eight masters of the early nineteenth century, such as Song Heung-rok and Mo Heung-gap, developed the classic styles.

The stories were not written by single authors but grew in performance, each singer adding passages (deoneum) that pupils inherited. Different lineages (je) of the eastern and western schools preserve distinct versions. The tales also circulated as novels, treated in the literature section, and pansori was proclaimed UNESCO Intangible Heritage in 2003.

### Chunhyangga (18th century; texts from 19th century)

- **Date:** 18th century; texts from 19th century
- **Type:** Pansori
- **Where performed / held:** Jeolla Province; performed nationally
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chunhyangga%20Pansori%20singers)

Chunhyangga tells of Chunhyang, daughter of a courtesan in Namwon, who secretly marries Yi Mong-ryong, the son of the local magistrate. When he leaves for the capital, the new magistrate demands her favours, and she is beaten and imprisoned for refusing. Mong-ryong returns as a secret royal inspector, at first disguised as a beggar, and saves her. The singer, standing with a fan, plays all the roles and alternates song (sori), narrative speech (aniri) and gesture (ballim), while a drummer on the buk sets the rhythmic cycles and calls out encouragement (chuimsae). Famous passages include the love song (Sarangga) and the scene of Chunhyang's beating (Sipjangga). It is the longest and most varied of the five surviving pansori and exists in versions of several lineages. The story itself is discussed in the literature section.

### Simcheongga (18th–19th century)

- **Date:** 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Pansori
- **Where performed / held:** Jeolla Province
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Simcheongga%20Pansori%20singers)

Sim Cheong, raised by her blind father, hears that a temple offering of three hundred sacks of rice will restore his sight, and sells herself to sailors who need a maiden to sacrifice to the sea god. Thrown into the waves, she is saved by the Dragon King, returns in a lotus flower and becomes queen, and holds a banquet for all the blind men of the land, where her father regains his sight at the sound of her voice. Simcheongga is renowned for its tragic passages, sung in the sorrowful gyemyeonjo mode, and is often regarded as the most moving of the pansori. Isang Yun's opera Sim Tjong (1972) is based on the story.

### Sugungga (18th–19th century)

- **Date:** 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Pansori
- **Where performed / held:** Jeolla Province
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sugungga%20Pansori%20singers)

The Dragon King of the Southern Sea falls ill, and only the liver of a rabbit can cure him. A turtle is sent to the land, flatters a rabbit with tales of the palace and brings it under the sea, where the rabbit, realising the danger, claims to have left its liver on land to dry and escapes. A satire of officials and courtiers, rooted in an old Buddhist fable, it is the most comic and allegorical of the five surviving pansori.

### Heungboga (18th–19th century)

- **Date:** 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Pansori
- **Where performed / held:** Jeolla Province
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Heungboga%20Pansori%20singers)

Heungbo, cast out by his greedy elder brother Nolbo, mends the broken leg of a swallow, which brings him a gourd seed; the gourds that grow from it are full of treasure. Nolbo breaks a swallow's leg to win the same fortune, but his gourds release goblins and disasters. The pansori is full of humour, especially in the scene of sawing open the gourds, and its tale of kindness rewarded is known to every Korean.

### Jeokbyeokga (18th–19th century)

- **Date:** 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Pansori
- **Where performed / held:** Jeolla Province
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jeokbyeokga%20Pansori%20singers)

Jeokbyeokga retells the Battle of the Red Cliffs of 208, from the Chinese Romance of the Three Kingdoms, in which Cao Cao's fleet is burned by the allied forces of Liu Bei and Sun Quan, and Cao Cao flees in humiliation. Korean singers added scenes of the suffering of common soldiers and a satirical portrait of Cao Cao. It requires a powerful, masculine voice and was long the favourite of male master singers.
