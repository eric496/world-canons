# Yatsuhashi Kengyō

*八橋検校*

**1614 – 1685** · Japanese  
Born: Iwaki (Fukushima) (traditional) · Died: Kyoto  
Genres & eras: [Koto & shamisen music](../movements/koto-shamisen.md)  

> Blind musician who founded the modern koto tradition, credited with Rokudan no Shirabe and the danmono form.

## Biography

Yatsuhashi was a blind musician who first played the shamisen in Osaka, then studied the koto music of the Buddhist temples of northern Kyushu (tsukushi-goto), which was restricted to sighted priests and samurai. He took it to Kyoto and Edo, adapted it for blind professionals and townspeople, and attained the highest rank of kengyō in the blind musicians' guild.

He is credited with devising the hira-jōshi tuning based on the in scale, which made the koto sound distinctly Japanese, with thirteen songs in kumiuta form and with the instrumental danmono pieces Rokudan, Midare and Hachidan, though some attributions are traditional. His followers formed the Ikuta and Yamada schools, and he is honoured as the father of modern koto music.

### Rokudan no Shirabe (17th century (attributed))

- **Date:** 17th century (attributed)
- **Type:** Koto solo (danmono)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Rokudan%20no%20Shirabe%20Yatsuhashi%20Kengy%C5%8D)

Rokudan, 'six sections', is a purely instrumental piece in the danmono form: six sections of equal length, each of fifty-two beats (except the first), beginning slowly and accelerating, with each section a free variation on a common framework. It is played on the thirteen-string koto in the hira-jōshi tuning, and its spare, rising opening phrase is known to almost every Japanese. The attribution to Yatsuhashi rests on tradition, and its early sources are from the eighteenth century. It became the first piece learned by koto students and is often played as a duet with shakuhachi or shamisen. Some scholars have proposed a link with Christian chant heard in sixteenth-century Japan, an idea that remains speculative.

### Midare (17th century (attributed))

[![Midare](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/66/A_koto_or_Japanese_zither_LCCN2009630122.jpg/1280px-A_koto_or_Japanese_zither_LCCN2009630122.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:A_koto_or_Japanese_zither_LCCN2009630122.jpg)

<sub>A koto, woodblock print, Library of Congress Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:A_koto_or_Japanese_zither_LCCN2009630122.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 17th century (attributed)
- **Type:** Koto solo (danmono)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Midare%20Yatsuhashi%20Kengy%C5%8D)

Midare, 'disorder', also called Midare rinzetsu, is a danmono like Rokudan but with sections of unequal length and frequent tempo changes, so that it seems to unfold freely. It is attributed to Yatsuhashi, though the version played today was probably reworked by later players. It is among the most admired works of the classical koto repertory and a test of a player's sense of timing and tone.
