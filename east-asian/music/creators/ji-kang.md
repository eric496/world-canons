## Biography

Ji Kang (also romanised Xi Kang) married into the Cao ruling house of Wei and held a minor court title, but withdrew to live as a Daoist recluse at his estate near Shanyang, where he drank, wrote and played the qin with friends later known as the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove. His refusal to serve the rising Sima clan and his outspoken letters made him dangerous. In 262 he was condemned to death on a trumped-up charge; according to the early histories three thousand students petitioned in vain to study with him, and before he died he asked for a qin and played Guangling san, lamenting that the piece would now die with him.

Ji Kang wrote the 'Rhapsody on the Qin' (Qin fu), one of the finest descriptions of the instrument, and the essay 'Music Has in It Neither Grief nor Joy' (Sheng wu aile lun), which argued against the Confucian view that music carries moral feeling and held that emotions belong to the listener, not to sounds. He became the model of the free-spirited scholar-musician for all later qin players.

### Music Has in It Neither Grief nor Joy (Sheng wu aile lun) (3rd century)

- **Date:** 3rd century
- **Type:** Treatise on music
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Music%20Has%20in%20It%20Neither%20Grief%20nor%20Joy%20%28Sheng%20wu%20aile%20lun%29%20Ji%20Kang)

Confucian tradition, expressed in the Record of Music in the Book of Rites, held that music arises from feeling and shapes the moral character of the listener and the state. In this essay, written as a debate between a host and a guest from Qin, Ji Kang argues instead that sounds are simply harmonious or not, like flavours, and that grief and joy belong to the listener's mind, which is merely stirred by music to express what it already holds. The argument draws on Daoist ideas of naturalness and on the logic of 'pure conversation' (qingtan) popular in his circle. It is the most sophisticated piece of musical aesthetics from early China and is often compared with Western debates about whether music expresses emotion.

### Guangling san (3rd century (score 1425))

- **Date:** 3rd century (score 1425)
- **Type:** Guqin piece
- **Where performed / held:** Earliest score in the Shenqi mipu (1425)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Guangling%20san%20Ji%20Kang)

Guangling san (Melody of Guangling) was already famous in the third century, when Ji Kang is said to have played it at his execution in 262 and lamented that no one would now learn it. The piece is not Ji Kang's composition: he is said to have learned it from a mysterious stranger, and its origins are unknown. A version survived, and was printed in 1425 in Zhu Quan's Shenqi mipu, with forty-five sections whose titles tell the story of Nie Zheng, who avenged his father by assassinating the King of Han, then disfigured himself to protect his family. The music is unusually vigorous and dramatic for the qin, using the low strings forcefully. Guan Pinghu's reconstruction of the 1425 tablature in the 1950s brought it back into performance, and it is regarded as one of the summits of the qin repertory.
