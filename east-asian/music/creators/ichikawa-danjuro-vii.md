## Biography

Danjūrō VII inherited the name as a boy in 1800 and dominated Edo kabuki for decades, playing heroes, villains and ghosts; he created the role of Iemon in Tsuruya Nanboku's ghost play Yotsuya Kaidan in 1825. In 1842, during the Tenpō reforms, he was exiled from Edo for living in luxury, and he spent years performing in Osaka and the provinces.

Around 1832 he announced the Kabuki Jūhachiban, the eighteen great plays of his family, and in 1840 he staged Kanjinchō, adapting the Noh play Ataka to kabuki with nagauta music. Its success opened a whole genre of kabuki dance-dramas based on Noh (matsubame-mono).

## Key works & forms

### Kanjinchō (1840)

[![Kanjinchō](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Ebiz%C5%8D_Ichikawa_V_as_Benkei_and_Danj%C5%ABr%C5%8D_Ichikawa_VIII_as_Tokashi.jpg/1280px-Ebiz%C5%8D_Ichikawa_V_as_Benkei_and_Danj%C5%ABr%C5%8D_Ichikawa_VIII_as_Tokashi.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ebiz%C5%8D_Ichikawa_V_as_Benkei_and_Danj%C5%ABr%C5%8D_Ichikawa_VIII_as_Tokashi.jpg)

<sub>Woodblock print of Ichikawa Ebizō V (Danjūrō VII) as Benkei and Danjūrō VIII as Togashi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ebiz%C5%8D_Ichikawa_V_as_Benkei_and_Danj%C5%ABr%C5%8D_Ichikawa_VIII_as_Tokashi.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1840
- **Type:** Kabuki dance-drama
- **Where performed / held:** Kawarazaki-za, Edo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kanjinch%C5%8D%20Ichikawa%20Danj%C5%ABr%C5%8D%20VII)

Fleeing his brother the shogun, the warrior Yoshitsune and his retainers, disguised as mountain priests, are stopped at the Ataka barrier. To prove they are collecting funds for Tōdai-ji, Benkei reads aloud an empty scroll as if it were a subscription list (kanjinchō). When the guards recognise Yoshitsune, Benkei beats his master to prove he is only a porter, and the barrier keeper Togashi, moved, lets them through. Danjūrō VII adapted the play from Noh in 1840, with a stage set imitating the Noh pine and nagauta music by Kineya Rokusaburō IV. Its climax, Benkei's 'flying six directions' exit along the hanamichi, is one of kabuki's most celebrated moments, and it became the most frequently performed of the Kabuki Jūhachiban.
