## Biography

Cai Yan was the daughter of the scholar and musician Cai Yong and was famed in her own day for her learning and her ear for music. In the chaos at the end of the Han dynasty she was carried off by Xiongnu horsemen, lived for about twelve years in the north and bore two sons there. The warlord Cao Cao, who had been her father's friend, ransomed her, and she returned to China, leaving her children behind. She is said to have written down from memory hundreds of her father's lost texts.

Two long poems on her ordeal are attributed to her, the 'Poem of Grief and Indignation', which most scholars accept as hers, and the Eighteen Songs of a Nomad Flute, which is now generally thought to be a later composition in her voice. Her story of exile and parting became a favourite subject for painters and musicians, and the qin piece that bears her name is still played.

- **Date:** attributed late 2nd century; qin versions Ming
- **Type:** Song cycle / guqin piece
- **Where performed / held:** Qin handbooks from the 15th century onward
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Eighteen%20Songs%20of%20a%20Nomad%20Flute%20%28Hujia%20shiba%20pai%29%20Cai%20Yan)

The Eighteen Songs of a Nomad Flute is a sequence of poems in which Cai Yan tells of her capture by the Xiongnu, her years in the north, her two sons, and her heartbreak at leaving them when she was ransomed back to China. The word pai suggests a 'beat' or stanza, and the 'nomad flute' (hujia) is the reed pipe of the steppe whose sound haunts her. The attribution to Cai Yan herself is doubted by most modern scholars, who date the poem to the Tang or later; it first appears in a Song anthology. Qin pieces with the same title, of which several versions survive in Ming handbooks, set or evoke the cycle, and the story was a favourite subject of painters from the Song onward, with famous handscrolls illustrating its eighteen scenes. It remains one of the most expressive works of the qin repertory.
