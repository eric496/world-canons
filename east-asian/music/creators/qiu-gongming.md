# Qiu Gongming

*丘公明*

**494 – 590** · Chinese  
Born: Kuaiji (Shaoxing) · Died:   
Genres & eras: [The guqin tradition](../movements/guqin-tradition.md)  

> A qin master of the Liang and Chen dynasties, credited with transmitting Solitary Orchid, the oldest guqin piece whose score survives.

## Biography

Qiu Gongming, also known as Qiu Ming, is known almost entirely from the preface to the manuscript of Jieshi diao: Youlan (Solitary Orchid in the Stone Tablet Mode). It says that he came from Kuaiji, lived in seclusion on Jiuyi Mountain in the late Liang dynasty, and was a master of the Chu style of qin playing, and that he passed this piece to Wang Shuming of Yidu shortly before dying aged ninety-seven in 590.

His importance lies in this act of transmission. The melody he taught is the only qin piece written down in the old longhand 'wenzi pu', which describes every finger movement in words, before the abbreviated tablature of the Tang and later centuries replaced it. Later tradition linked the title to a story of Confucius lamenting a fine orchid among weeds, but the attribution of the music itself to Confucius is legendary.

- **Date:** 6th century (manuscript Tang, 7th century)
- **Type:** Guqin piece
- **Where performed / held:** Manuscript in the Tokyo National Museum
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Solitary%20Orchid%20%28Jieshi%20diao%3A%20Youlan%29%20Qiu%20Gongming)

Solitary Orchid in the Stone Tablet Mode survives in a single handwritten scroll copied in Tang China, probably in the seventh century, and kept for over a millennium in Japan, where it is now in the Tokyo National Museum. Its preface credits the piece to Qiu Gongming of the sixth century. Unlike later qin tablature, which uses abbreviated composite characters, the score describes every action in full sentences: which finger stops which string at which stud, how to pluck, slide and ornament. Rhythm is not shown, so the music must be reconstructed by interpretation, and several modern players have recorded versions. The title evokes the image of a noble orchid growing unseen, which tradition associates with Confucius lamenting that his talents went unrecognised. The manuscript is a unique witness to early qin technique and is registered as a National Treasure of Japan.
