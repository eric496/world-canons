# Izumo no Okuni

*出雲阿国*

**c. 1572 – after 1613** · Japanese  
Born: Izumo Province (traditional) · Died:   
Genres & eras: [Bunraku & kabuki](../movements/bunraku-kabuki.md)  

> Dancer who created kabuki in Kyoto in 1603 with her troupe of women performers.

## Biography

Okuni claimed to be a shrine maiden from the Grand Shrine of Izumo, though this may have been publicity. She appears in court diaries from the 1580s as a dancer of children's dances, and in 1603 she performed on the dry bed of the Kamo River in Kyoto with a new style of dance and sketches, playing a dashing young man in outlandish dress who visits a teahouse.

The style was called kabuki, from kabuku, 'to be unconventional', and was soon copied by companies of courtesans. Women were banned from the stage in 1629 and young men in 1652, leaving the all-male kabuki of later centuries. Okuni's own later life is unknown, but she is revered as the founder of the art.

## Key works & forms

### Okuni kabuki (1603)

[![Okuni kabuki](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/Okuni_kabuki_byobu-zu_cropped_and_enhanced.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Okuni_kabuki_byobu-zu_cropped_and_enhanced.jpg)

<sub>Detail of an early 17th-century folding screen showing Okuni kabuki Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Okuni_kabuki_byobu-zu_cropped_and_enhanced.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1603
- **Type:** Dance theatre
- **Where performed / held:** Bed of the Kamo River, Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Okuni%20kabuki%20Izumo%20no%20Okuni)

In the spring of 1603, the year Tokugawa Ieyasu became shogun, Okuni and her troupe performed on a temporary stage on the riverbed of the Kamo in Kyoto. Her programme combined Buddhist nenbutsu dance with a scandalous sketch in which she, dressed as a fashionable young man with a sword and a Christian rosary, visited a teahouse girl played by a man. The novelty, a mixture of dance, song and erotic comedy, was immediately copied, and painted screens and illustrated scrolls of the early seventeenth century depict these performances. The women's kabuki that followed was suppressed in 1629, but the name and many elements of Okuni's art passed into the all-male kabuki of the Edo period.
