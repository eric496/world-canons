# Sejong the Great

*세종대왕 (世宗大王)*

**1397 – 1450** · Korean  
Born: Hanseong (Seoul) · Died: Hanseong (Seoul)  
Genres & eras: [Korean aak & hyangak](../movements/korean-court-music.md)  
Also in: [History](../../history/creators/sejong.md)  

> Fourth king of Joseon, who devised the Hangul alphabet, sponsored science and ritual reform, and composed court music in native Korean style.

## Biography

Yi Do became king in 1418. His reign is remembered as the golden age of Joseon: he founded the Hall of Worthies, promulgated the Korean alphabet Hunminjeongeum in 1446, supported astronomical instruments, rain gauges and agricultural manuals, and strengthened the northern frontier. He died in 1450 after years of illness.

Sejong was a trained musician with a fine ear. He supported Bak Yeon's reform of ritual aak, but he also argued that Koreans should not offer Chinese music to their ancestors, and he composed new pieces based on Korean music, including Botaepyeong and Jeongdaeeop, later adopted for the royal ancestral shrine, and Yeominlak. The mensural notation jeongganbo, the first East Asian system to show rhythm precisely, was created at his court.

### Yeominlak (c. 1445)

- **Date:** c. 1445
- **Type:** Court ensemble suite
- **Where performed / held:** Joseon court, Hanseong
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yeominlak%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

Yeominlak was composed under Sejong to sing the Yongbieocheonga (Songs of the Dragons Flying to Heaven), the first long text written in the new Korean alphabet, which praised the founders of the Joseon dynasty. Its notation in jeongganbo appears in the Sejong sillok. The version played today is purely instrumental and very slow, in several forms for different ensembles, and is one of the representative pieces of Korean court music, with its stately rhythm, the wide vibrato of the oboe piri and the sound of the large bamboo flute daegeum.

### Jeongganbo notation (c. 1447)

[![Jeongganbo notation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ab/%EC%8B%9C%EC%9A%A9%ED%96%A5%EC%95%85%EB%B3%B4_%28%E6%99%82%E7%94%A8%E9%84%95%E6%A8%82%E8%AD%9C%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%EC%8B%9C%EC%9A%A9%ED%96%A5%EC%95%85%EB%B3%B4_%28%E6%99%82%E7%94%A8%E9%84%95%E6%A8%82%E8%AD%9C%29.jpg)

- **Date:** c. 1447
- **Type:** Musical notation system
- **Where performed / held:** Joseon court; scores in the Sejong sillok
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jeongganbo%20notation%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

Chinese and Korean music had long been notated with pitch names, but without a precise way to write duration. Jeongganbo, 'well-square notation', solved this by writing music in columns of squares, each square (jeonggan) representing one unit of time, so that the number of squares a note occupies shows its length. The pitch is written inside the square in Chinese pitch-names or abbreviated syllables, and later forms added ornaments and the rhythm of the hourglass drum. The system is first found in the music scores of the Annals of King Sejong and is traditionally credited to the king himself. It remained in use throughout the Joseon dynasty and is still used to write Korean traditional music today.

### Jongmyo jeryeak (composed 1440s; adopted 1464)

[![Jongmyo jeryeak](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Korean_Royal_Ancestral_Ritual_Music-Jongmyo_Jeryeak-01.jpg/1280px-Korean_Royal_Ancestral_Ritual_Music-Jongmyo_Jeryeak-01.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Korean_Royal_Ancestral_Ritual_Music-Jongmyo_Jeryeak-01.jpg)

<sub>Performance of the Jongmyo ritual music at the shrine Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Korean_Royal_Ancestral_Ritual_Music-Jongmyo_Jeryeak-01.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** composed 1440s; adopted 1464
- **Type:** Royal ancestral ritual music
- **Where performed / held:** Jongmyo Shrine, Seoul
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jongmyo%20jeryeak%20Sejong%20the%20Great)

The rites at Jongmyo, the shrine of the Joseon kings and queens in Seoul, are accompanied by music and the line dance ilmu. The music consists of two suites, Botaepyeong (Preserving the Peace) praising the civil virtues of the dynastic founders, and Jeongdaeeop (Establishing the Great Enterprise) praising their military achievements. King Sejong composed them in the 1440s for banquets, drawing on Korean and Chinese-derived styles, and his son Sejo adopted them for the ancestral rites in 1464. Two ensembles, one on the upper terrace and one below, play bells, stone chimes, drums, oboes, flutes and zithers while a choir sings Chinese texts. The rites were interrupted in the early twentieth century and restored in 1969, and since 1971 they have been held annually in May by the Jeonju Yi clan with the National Gugak Center. In 2001 the Jongmyo rite and its music were proclaimed a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO.
