# Abing

*阿炳*

**1893 – 1950** · Chinese  
Born: Wuxi, Jiangsu · Died: Wuxi  
Genres & eras: [Chinese folk & solo instrumental music](../movements/chinese-instrumental.md)  

> Blind street musician of Wuxi, whose erhu piece The Moon Reflected on the Second Spring is one of the best-loved works of Chinese music.

## Biography

Hua Yanjun, known as Abing, was the son of a Daoist priest and learned ritual music and instruments in his father's temple. After his father's death he fell into poverty, lost his sight by his mid thirties, and earned a living playing erhu and pipa and singing news ballads in the streets and teahouses of Wuxi.

In 1950 the musicologists Yang Yinliu and Cao Anhe recorded six of his pieces on a wire recorder, three for erhu and three for pipa, a few months before his death. They include Erquan yingyue, which became famous across China and was arranged for many forces. Abing became an emblem of the suffering folk artist, though the circumstances of his life have been romanticised.

## Key works & forms

### The Moon Reflected on the Second Spring (Erquan yingyue) (composed c. 1930s; recorded 1950)

[![The Moon Reflected on the Second Spring (Erquan yingyue)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Erhu_in_the_Mets.jpg/1280px-Erhu_in_the_Mets.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Erhu_in_the_Mets.jpg)

<sub>An erhu, Metropolitan Museum of Art Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Erhu_in_the_Mets.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** composed c. 1930s; recorded 1950
- **Type:** Erhu solo
- **Where performed / held:** Recorded in Wuxi by Yang Yinliu and Cao Anhe (1950)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Moon%20Reflected%20on%20the%20Second%20Spring%20%28Erquan%20yingyue%29%20Abing)

Abing played this piece in the streets of Wuxi without a fixed title; when musicologists recorded it in 1950 he and they named it after the 'Second Spring under Heaven' at Wuxi's Huishan temple. It consists of a long melody repeated and varied six times with growing intensity, played in the lower register of the erhu with sliding ornaments that give it a sorrowful, introspective character. After Abing's death the recording was published and the piece was transcribed, taught at conservatories and arranged for Chinese and Western orchestras. Its moving quality, and the legend of the blind musician, made it the emblematic work of the erhu.
