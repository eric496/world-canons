# Kan'ami

*観阿弥*

**1333 – 1384** · Japanese  
Born: Iga Province · Died: Suruga Province  
Genres & eras: [Noh & kyōgen](../movements/noh-kyogen.md)  

> Actor and playwright who, with his son Zeami, turned sarugaku into Noh, winning the patronage of the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu.

## Biography

Kan'ami Kiyotsugu led a sarugaku troupe of Yamato province, the Yūzaki-za, ancestor of the Kanze school. He absorbed the rhythmic kusemai dance-song into sarugaku, giving it a new musical vitality. In 1374 or 1375 the young shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu saw him perform with his son at Imakumano in Kyoto and took both under his patronage, which raised Noh from a popular entertainment to the art of the warrior elite. Kan'ami died in 1384 while on tour in Suruga.

He was famed as a versatile actor who could play anything from demons to beautiful women. Several plays are attributed to him, often in versions revised by Zeami, among them Sotoba Komachi, Jinen Koji and Matsukaze. Zeami's treatises record many of his teachings, so Kan'ami's ideas live on largely through his son.

- **Date:** late 14th century (revised by Zeami)
- **Type:** Noh play
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sotoba%20Komachi%20Kan%27ami)

Priests from Mount Kōya find a ragged old woman sitting on a stupa, a sacred grave marker, and rebuke her, but she argues with them so brilliantly about Buddhist doctrine that they bow to her. She is revealed as Ono no Komachi, once the most beautiful poet of the Heian court, now a hundred years old. Suddenly she is possessed by the spirit of Fukakusa no Shōshō, the suitor who died on the hundredth of the hundred nights he was told to visit her, and she acts out his journeys. The play, by Kan'ami and revised by Zeami, combines debate, madness and possession with a reflection on age and enlightenment. Mishima Yukio wrote a famous modern version in his Modern Noh Plays.

- **Date:** late 14th century (revised by Zeami)
- **Type:** Noh play
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Matsukaze%20Kan%27ami)

At Suma Bay a monk sees a pine that marks the grave of two sisters, Matsukaze and Murasame, who loved the courtier poet Ariwara no Yukihira during his exile there. That night two women carrying salt-water pails come to the hut where he shelters; they are the sisters' ghosts. Matsukaze, overcome by memory, puts on Yukihira's hat and robe and dances, mistaking the pine for her lover, before the ghosts vanish at dawn, leaving only the wind in the pines. Zeami records that the play derives from Kan'ami's version of an older work, and that he himself revised it. With its moonlight, salt water and the motif of the pail reflecting the moon, it was rated by Zeami among the best plays and is one of the most admired works of the repertory.
