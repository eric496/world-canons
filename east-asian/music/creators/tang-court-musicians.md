# Tang court musicians & instrument makers

*唐代樂工*

**618 – 907** · Chinese  
Genres & eras: [Tang court music & Silk Road instruments](../movements/tang-court-music.md)  

> The thousands of musicians, dancers and craftsmen of the Tang court and its Silk Road ensembles, whose instruments and music survive mostly in Japan.

## Biography

The Tang court employed musicians in the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, the Imperial Music Office and the Pear Garden, many of them hereditary or of Central Asian origin, such as the famous pipa families from Kucha and Kashgar. Their repertory ranged from ritual music to large suites of dance music (daqu) and popular entertainments.

Very little of their music was written down in China, but tablatures for pipa and other instruments survive at Dunhuang and in Japan, and the Shōsōin storehouse at Nara keeps instruments sent or brought from Tang China in the eighth century. Through Japanese gagaku some pieces of Tang origin are still performed, though slowed and transformed over twelve centuries.

### The Prince of Qin Breaks the Ranks (Qinwang pozhen yue) (c. 620s)

- **Date:** c. 620s
- **Type:** Court dance music
- **Where performed / held:** Tang court; survives in Japan as Ōdai hajin raku
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Prince%20of%20Qin%20Breaks%20the%20Ranks%20%28Qinwang%20pozhen%20yue%29%20Tang%20court%20musicians%20%26%20instrument%20makers)

Composed to celebrate the victories of Li Shimin, Prince of Qin, who became Emperor Taizong in 626, this was performed at the Tang court by large numbers of armoured dancers with banners and drums. Xuanzang reports that Indian kings asked him about it. The music was later lost in China but was transmitted to Japan, where a gagaku piece called Ōdai hajin raku survives in tablature and performance, much slowed. It illustrates how much Tang music survives only in its Japanese afterlife.

### Five-stringed pipa of the Shōsōin (8th century)

- **Date:** 8th century
- **Type:** Musical instrument
- **Where performed / held:** Shōsōin repository, Tōdai-ji, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Five-stringed%20pipa%20of%20the%20Sh%C5%8Ds%C5%8Din%20Tang%20court%20musicians%20%26%20instrument%20makers)

The Shōsōin, the storehouse of Tōdai-ji in Nara, holds the treasures that Empress Kōmyō dedicated in 756 after the death of Emperor Shōmu, among them some of the finest musical instruments of the eighth century. This lute of red sandalwood has five strings and a straight neck, an Indian and Central Asian form, as opposed to the four-string bent-necked pipa that survived in Chinese and Japanese music. Its body is inlaid with mother-of-pearl and tortoiseshell, and its plectrum guard shows a Central Asian musician riding a camel and playing the pipa among tropical trees. It is generally thought to have been made in Tang China. Together with the Shōsōin's harps, zithers and ruan, it shows the cosmopolitan instruments of the Tang court, and it is shown only occasionally at the annual exhibition in Nara.

### Ruan (genkan) of the Shōsōin (8th century)

[![Ruan (genkan) of the Shōsōin](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Shoso-in.jpg/1280px-Shoso-in.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shoso-in.jpg)

<sub>The Shōsōin storehouse at Tōdai-ji, Nara, where the instrument is kept Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shoso-in.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 8th century
- **Type:** Musical instrument
- **Where performed / held:** Shōsōin repository, Tōdai-ji, Nara
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ruan%20%28genkan%29%20of%20the%20Sh%C5%8Ds%C5%8Din%20Tang%20court%20musicians%20%26%20instrument%20makers)

The ruan, a lute with a round body and long neck named after Ruan Xian, one of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, was played at the Tang court. The Shōsōin keeps one decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay and a painted leather plectrum guard showing figures under trees. Such instruments disappeared from Japanese music but are a key source for reconstructions of Tang instruments, and the modern Chinese orchestra uses several sizes of ruan revived in the twentieth century.

### Dunhuang pipa scores (c. 933)

- **Date:** c. 933
- **Type:** Musical manuscript
- **Where performed / held:** Bibliothèque nationale de France (Pelliot chinois 3808)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Dunhuang%20pipa%20scores%20Tang%20court%20musicians%20%26%20instrument%20makers)

Among the manuscripts sealed in Cave 17 at Dunhuang around 1000 and taken to Paris by Paul Pelliot in 1908 is a scroll whose back carries twenty-five short pieces in a tablature for the four-string pipa, copied around 933. They include tunes with names like 'Qingbei yue' and 'Xiliang yue', related to the banquet music of the late Tang and Five Dynasties. The tablature shows string and fret, but how to read the rhythm marks has been fiercely debated since the 1930s, and Chinese, Japanese and British scholars, notably Laurence Picken and his Cambridge group, have produced very different transcriptions with the help of Japanese gagaku scores. The manuscript is the main written witness to the court music of Tang China.
