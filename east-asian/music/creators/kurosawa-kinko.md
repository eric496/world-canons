# Kurosawa Kinko

*黒沢琴古*

**1710 – 1771** · Japanese  
Born: Fukuoka, Chikuzen Province · Died: Edo  
Genres & eras: [Shakuhachi & the Fuke sect](../movements/shakuhachi-fuke.md)  

> Samurai and Fuke shakuhachi player who collected and arranged the 36 honkyoku of the Kinko-ryū, the leading school of shakuhachi.

## Biography

Kinko was a retainer of the Kuroda clan of Fukuoka who left service and became a komusō of the Fuke sect. He travelled widely through Japan collecting the shakuhachi pieces played at the sect's temples and became an instructor at the Ichigetsuji and Reihōji temples near Edo.

He arranged thirty-six pieces as the core repertory of his tradition, the Kinko-ryū honkyoku, which his descendants and pupils, especially Hisamatsu Fūyō, transmitted and organised into a school. After the sect was abolished in 1871 the school continued as a secular art and became, with the later Tozan-ryū, one of the two main shakuhachi schools.

### Tsuru no Sugomori (18th century (traditional))

<sub>A komusō playing the shakuhachi, woodblock print, Library of Congress Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Komus%C5%8D_LCCN2008660447.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 18th century (traditional)
- **Type:** Shakuhachi honkyoku
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tsuru%20no%20Sugomori%20Kurosawa%20Kinko)

Tsuru no Sugomori is one of the most famous pieces of shakuhachi music, existing in versions in the Kinko, Tozan and regional traditions. It portrays the life of a crane family, from the building of the nest to the raising of the chicks and their flight, using the 'koro-koro' flutter-tonguing and rolling techniques that imitate the cries and wingbeats of cranes. Its origins are unknown and it may have existed before the Kinko-ryū, which transmits a version among its secondary pieces. Because of its descriptive character it is unusual among honkyoku, which are usually meditative, and it is often played at celebrations, the crane being a symbol of long life.

### Shika no Tōne (18th century (traditional))

[![Shika no Tōne](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1c/%E9%A2%A8%E4%BF%97%E5%85%AD%E7%8E%89%E5%B7%9D-A_Young_Komuso_MET_DP144638.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E9%A2%A8%E4%BF%97%E5%85%AD%E7%8E%89%E5%B7%9D-A_Young_Komuso_MET_DP144638.jpg)

<sub>A young komusō, woodblock print, Metropolitan Museum of Art Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E9%A2%A8%E4%BF%97%E5%85%AD%E7%8E%89%E5%B7%9D-A_Young_Komuso_MET_DP144638.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 18th century (traditional)
- **Type:** Shakuhachi honkyoku duet
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Shika%20no%20T%C5%8Dne%20Kurosawa%20Kinko)

Shika no Tōne is one of the core honkyoku of the Kinko-ryū, arranged in the collection that Kurosawa Kinko assembled from the pieces played at Fuke temples. Two players, often standing apart, answer each other in long, sliding phrases, which gradually overlap and come together, evoking the mating call of deer in the autumn mountains, a classical image of loneliness in Japanese poetry. It is one of the most frequently performed shakuhachi works and shows how the honkyoku combine breath, tone colour and silence.
