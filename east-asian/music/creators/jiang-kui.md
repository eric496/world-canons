# Jiang Kui

*姜夔*

**c. 1155 – c. 1221** · Chinese  
Born: Poyang (Jiangxi) · Died: Hangzhou  
Genres & eras: [Song ci songs & court revival](../movements/song-ci-music.md)  

> Southern Song poet and composer, the only Song song-writer whose melodies survive in notation.

## Biography

Jiang Kui never passed the examinations or held office. He spent his life as a guest of wealthy patrons in Hunan, Hubei and around Hangzhou, writing poetry, practising calligraphy and studying music. In 1197 he submitted proposals on ritual music to the court, without success. He died poor in Hangzhou.

His collected works include seventeen ci lyrics with melodies notated in the popular 'suzi' notation of the day, plus a qin song and ritual pieces, the largest body of Song music to survive. Many of his tunes were his own compositions rather than settings of existing patterns. His cool, elegant lyrics, such as 'Dark Fragrance' and 'Sparse Shadows' on plum blossom, were much admired, and modern scholars have transcribed his songs for performance.

## Key works & forms

### Songs of the Whitestone Taoist (Baishi daoren gequ) (c. 1202)

- **Date:** c. 1202
- **Type:** Song collection with notation
- **Where performed / held:** Printed in the Qing from Song editions (1743)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Songs%20of%20the%20Whitestone%20Taoist%20%28Baishi%20daoren%20gequ%29%20Jiang%20Kui)

Jiang Kui's songbook contains lyrics he wrote to his own melodies, together with ritual and qin songs, and above each syllable is a sign in the 'popular notation' (suzi pu) used by Song musicians. The collection was rediscovered and reprinted in the eighteenth century, and since the twentieth century scholars, including Yang Yinliu and Laurence Picken, have decoded the notation and produced transcriptions, though questions of rhythm remain open. The songs include 'Dark Fragrance' (Anxiang) and 'Sparse Shadows' (Shuying), written in 1191 on the plum blossom for his patron Fan Chengda, and 'Yangzhou Slow Song' (Yangzhou man), mourning a city ruined by war. They are the only examples of how the ci of the Song, normally known only as poetry, actually sounded.
