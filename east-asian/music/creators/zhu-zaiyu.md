# Zhu Zaiyu

*朱載堉*

**1536 – 1611** · Chinese  
Born: Huaiqing (Henan) · Died:   
Genres & eras: [The guqin tradition](../movements/guqin-tradition.md)  

> Ming prince and mathematician who first published an exact calculation of twelve-tone equal temperament.

## Biography

Zhu Zaiyu was heir to the princely house of Zheng, a branch of the Ming imperial family. When his father was imprisoned after a dispute he lived for years in a hut outside the palace walls, studying music, mathematics and the calendar. He later renounced his title in favour of a cousin and devoted himself to scholarship.

In works collected as the Yuelü quanshu (Complete Compendium of Music and Pitch), printed around 1606, he showed how to divide the octave into twelve equal semitones using the twelfth root of two, calculated to many digits, and built instruments to demonstrate it. His solution appeared at about the same time as Simon Stevin's in Europe, and whether it influenced later European theory is debated. He also wrote on dance and ritual music.

### Twelve-tone equal temperament (Yuelü quanshu) (1584; printed c. 1606)

- **Date:** 1584; printed c. 1606
- **Type:** Music theory treatise
- **Where performed / held:** Printed in Henan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Twelve-tone%20equal%20temperament%20%28Yuel%C3%BC%20quanshu%29%20Zhu%20Zaiyu)

Chinese theorists had long generated the twelve pitches by the 'add and subtract a third' method, the equivalent of the Pythagorean cycle of fifths, which never quite returns to its starting point. In his New Account of the Science of Pitch-pipes (Lülü jingyi), finished in 1584 and gathered in the Yuelü quanshu, Zhu Zaiyu solved the problem by making each semitone the ratio of the twelfth root of two, which he calculated to twenty-five digits using a large abacus. He described pipes and strings built to these proportions and applied the system to the qin and ritual music. His work was hardly used in Chinese practice, but it is a landmark in the history of tuning; whether knowledge of it reached Europe, where Simon Stevin and Marin Mersenne worked on the same problem, is uncertain.
