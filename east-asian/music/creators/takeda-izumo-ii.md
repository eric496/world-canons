# Takeda Izumo II

*二代目 竹田出雲*

**1691 – 1756** · Japanese  
Born: Osaka · Died: Osaka  
Genres & eras: [Bunraku & kabuki](../movements/bunraku-kabuki.md)  

> Manager of the Takemoto-za and leading author of the collaborative team behind the three great puppet plays of the 1740s.

## Biography

Takeda Izumo II was the son of Takeda Izumo I, who had taken over management of the Takemoto-za, and succeeded him in 1747. He wrote with house playwrights, above all Namiki Sōsuke (Senryū I) and Miyoshi Shōraku, in the collaborative system that had replaced the single author after Chikamatsu.

In three years this team produced Sugawara Denju Tenarai Kagami (1746), Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura (1747) and Kanadehon Chūshingura (1748), the most popular plays of the puppet stage, all of them adapted for kabuki within months. The shares of each author are debated, and the plays are usually credited to the trio together. In this period puppets grew larger and three-man operation was developed.

### Sugawara Denju Tenarai Kagami (1746)

[![Sugawara Denju Tenarai Kagami](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/28/Nakaz%C5%8D_Nakamura_II_as_Matsu%C5%8D-maru_and_as_Noshio_Nakamura_II_Chiyo.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nakaz%C5%8D_Nakamura_II_as_Matsu%C5%8D-maru_and_as_Noshio_Nakamura_II_Chiyo.jpg)

- **Date:** 1746
- **Type:** Bunraku / kabuki play
- **Where performed / held:** Takemoto-za, Osaka
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sugawara%20Denju%20Tenarai%20Kagami%20Takeda%20Izumo%20II)

The play takes the historical exile of the Heian minister Sugawara no Michizane, later worshipped as the god of learning, and builds around it the story of triplet brothers serving different masters. In its most famous act, 'Terakoya' (The Village School), a schoolmaster must deliver the head of Michizane's young son to his enemies, and the retainer Matsuōmaru, sent to identify the head, has secretly sacrificed his own son as a substitute. The play premiered with puppets in 1746 and was soon adapted for kabuki. Its scenes of agonised duty made it, with Chūshingura and Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura, one of the three great masterpieces of the puppet theatre.

### Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura (1747)

[![Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Yoshitsune-Senbon-Zakura-by-Utagawa-Kuniyoshi-1848.png/1280px-Yoshitsune-Senbon-Zakura-by-Utagawa-Kuniyoshi-1848.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yoshitsune-Senbon-Zakura-by-Utagawa-Kuniyoshi-1848.png)

<sub>Utagawa Kuniyoshi, print of Yoshitsune Senbon Zakura, 1848 Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yoshitsune-Senbon-Zakura-by-Utagawa-Kuniyoshi-1848.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1747
- **Type:** Bunraku / kabuki play
- **Where performed / held:** Takemoto-za, Osaka
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yoshitsune%20Senbon%20Zakura%20Takeda%20Izumo%20II)

In this five-act play the fugitive hero Yoshitsune is surrounded by stories of others. The Taira generals reported dead at Dan-no-ura are in fact alive and in hiding, one as a boatman, another as a sushi shop apprentice, and they face their fate in some of the most famous scenes of the repertory. A fox, disguised as Yoshitsune's retainer Tadanobu, follows his lady because the drum she carries is made from the skin of its parents. Written by Takeda Izumo II, Namiki Senryū I and Miyoshi Shōraku, it was first performed by puppets in 1747 and is a cornerstone of both bunraku and kabuki, where the acrobatic fox scenes are a showpiece.

### Kanadehon Chūshingura (1748)

[![Kanadehon Chūshingura](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/Kanadehon-Chushingura-Stage-3-Utagawa-Kuniteru.png/1280px-Kanadehon-Chushingura-Stage-3-Utagawa-Kuniteru.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kanadehon-Chushingura-Stage-3-Utagawa-Kuniteru.png)

<sub>Utagawa Kuniteru, Act III of Kanadehon Chūshingura, woodblock print Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kanadehon-Chushingura-Stage-3-Utagawa-Kuniteru.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1748
- **Type:** Bunraku / kabuki play
- **Where performed / held:** Takemoto-za, Osaka
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Kanadehon%20Ch%C5%ABshingura%20Takeda%20Izumo%20II)

In 1701 a daimyō drew his sword in the shogun's castle against an official who had insulted him and was ordered to commit suicide; in 1703 forty-seven of his former retainers killed the official and then gave themselves up and died by seppuku. Censorship barred direct portrayal of recent events, so the playwrights Takeda Izumo II, Namiki Senryū I and Miyoshi Shōraku moved the story to the fourteenth century and gave the characters medieval names. The play, first performed by puppets in 1748 and staged by kabuki the same year, follows the leader Ōboshi Yuranosuke as he hides his revenge behind a life of dissipation, while ordinary people suffer for their loyalty. In the Edo period it was said that a theatre in difficulty need only stage Chūshingura to recover. It inspired countless prints, novels and films and remains the single best-known story of loyalty in Japan.
