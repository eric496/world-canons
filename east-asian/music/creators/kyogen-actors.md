# Kyōgen actors

*狂言師*

**c. 1350 – present** · Japanese  
Genres & eras: [Noh & kyōgen](../movements/noh-kyogen.md)  

> The comic actors of the Noh stage, whose short farces were transmitted orally for centuries before being written down.

## Biography

Kyōgen grew up alongside Noh from the comic side of sarugaku. Its actors performed farces between Noh plays and took the role of local villagers within them. The repertory was improvised and orally transmitted until the Ōkura, Sagi and Izumi schools wrote down texts in the Edo period; the Ōkura and Izumi schools survive.

Kyōgen is spoken in the colloquial language of late medieval Japan, with song and dance, and its stock characters include the foolish master Daimyō, the clever servant Tarōkaja, priests, sons-in-law and demons. Its humour is gentle and humane, and it remains part of every traditional Noh programme.

### Busu (15th–16th century)

- **Date:** 15th–16th century
- **Type:** Kyōgen play
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Busu%20Ky%C5%8Dgen%20actors)

A master goes out, telling his servants Tarōkaja and Jirōkaja to watch a lacquered tub that contains busu, a deadly poison that kills even if the wind blows from it. Curious, they fan the air away and look inside, and find it is sugar, which they eat to the last grain. To explain themselves they tear a precious scroll and smash a valuable bowl, and when the master returns they weep that, having broken his treasures, they tried to kill themselves with poison but could not die. The story has parallels in Chinese and Japanese folk tales and in Buddhist collections. It is one of the most frequently played kyōgen and is read in Japanese school textbooks.

### Bōshibari (16th century)

- **Date:** 16th century
- **Type:** Kyōgen play
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=B%C5%8Dshibari%20Ky%C5%8Dgen%20actors)

Tired of his servants raiding the sake while he is out, a master ties Jirōkaja's arms to his back and Tarōkaja's to a pole across his shoulders. The two bound servants manage to open the storehouse and feed each other sake from the lid of the cask, then sing and dance in their bonds. The master returns to find them drunk, and they mock him, seeing his face reflected in the sake. The play shows the physical comedy and the sympathy for the servant that are typical of kyōgen, and it is among the best known in the West.
