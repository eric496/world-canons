East Asia has the longest continuous tradition of historical writing in the world. From the Zhou court scribes and Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, China developed the official dynastic history, compiled by each dynasty for its predecessor, which Japan, Korea and Vietnam adapted for their own courts; alongside it grew private chronicles, interpretive histories, war diaries and the evidential scholarship of the Qing. This section gathers those histories together with the codes, edicts, treaties, declarations and constitutions that shaped the region, from the Tang Code to the Constitution of Japan. Manuscripts, early printed editions and original documents are shown from Wikimedia Commons; portraits are paintings or later imagined likenesses, never photographs.

## Notes on this section

- **Two canons in one.** Histories that interpret the past sit alongside codes, edicts, treaties, declarations and constitutions that made it. Documents are credited to their drafters or to the body that issued them; collective entries (Zhou court scribes, the compilers of the Six National Histories, the Joseon historiographers, the signatories of the March First Declaration, the drafters of the Constitution of Japan) stand for anonymous or committee authorship.
- **Official history.** From the Tang onward most Chinese standard histories were compiled by state commissions, each dynasty writing up its predecessor; the eighteenth-century canon of the Twenty-Four Histories is represented here by its landmarks rather than in full. Japan's Six National Histories and Korea's Samguk sagi, Goryeosa and Joseon Annals adapted the model.
- **Traditional attributions.** Ancient works such as the Book of Documents, the Zuozhuan and the Guoyu are credited to their traditional authors or to collective scribes; the descriptions note where modern scholarship differs.
- **Contested history.** Treaties, the Gwanggaeto Stele, the Jinnō shōtōki, the Meiji documents and nationalist histories are read very differently in China, Japan, Korea and Taiwan. The descriptions try to state the main views without taking sides.
- **Shared people.** Zhu Xi, Gu Yanwu, Liang Qichao, Hayashi Razan and Fukuzawa Yukichi also appear in the philosophy section, and the anonymous Heian authors in literature, under the same ids. The Spring and Autumn Annals and the Seventeen-article Constitution are listed in philosophy, the Kojiki in literature.
- **Images.** Manuscripts, printed editions and original documents come from Wikimedia Commons. Portraits are paintings, prints or later imagined likenesses, never photographs, so most modern figures have none. Twentieth-century works still in copyright (such as Gu Jiegang's Gushi bian) are described without images.
