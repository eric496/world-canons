# Liu Zhiji

*劉知幾*

**661 – 721** · Chinese  
Born: Pengcheng (Xuzhou, Jiangsu) · Died: Anzhou (Hubei)  
Traditions & eras: [Tang History Office & the codes](../movements/tang-history-office.md)  

> Tang court historian whose Shitong (Generalities on History) was the first systematic work of historical criticism in any language.

## Biography

Liu Zhiji passed the jinshi examination young and spent much of his career in the Tang History Office, working on the national history and the veritable records under Empress Wu and her successors. He found committee work frustrating: in a famous letter of resignation he complained that compilers were too many, sources hard to get, and powerful ministers interfered in judgements. He finished the Shitong in 710 and died in provincial demotion in 721.

The Shitong analyses the genres, structure and methods of earlier histories, and criticises even revered works, including the Spring and Autumn Annals, for distortion and bias. Liu demanded that a historian combine talent, learning and insight, and praised 'straight writing' that records events without fear. Neglected for centuries, the book was rediscovered by Qing scholars and is now recognised as a landmark of historiographical theory.

## Key histories & documents

### Generalities on History (Shitong) (710)

- **Date:** 710
- **Type:** Historiographical criticism
- **Written / held:** Written at the Tang History Office, Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Generalities%20on%20History%20%28Shitong%29%20Liu%20Zhiji)

The Shitong is divided into inner chapters on the genres and components of history, such as annals, biographies, treatises, comments and prefaces, and outer chapters on the history of historical writing and on errors in specific works. Liu Zhiji argued for factual accuracy, consistency, economy of language and freedom from flattery or superstition, and he questioned the reliability of revered texts, including the Spring and Autumn Annals and the Book of Documents, which brought him criticism from orthodox Confucians. He also recommended treatises on cities, clans and local products. Little read for centuries, the Shitong was revived by Qing evidential scholars and has been compared with the historiographical theory of the European Renaissance, which it precedes by eight hundred years.
