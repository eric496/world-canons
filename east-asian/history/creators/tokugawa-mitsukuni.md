> Second lord of the Mito domain, who in 1657 launched the Great History of Japan (Dai Nihonshi), a project that lasted until 1906.

<sub>Portrait: Edo-period portrait of Tokugawa Mitsukuni · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tokugawa_Mitsukuni.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Mitsukuni was a grandson of Tokugawa Ieyasu. As a youth he is said to have been inspired by the biography of the loyal brothers Boyi and Shuqi in Sima Qian's Shiji to devote himself to learning. He established a historiographical bureau, later named the Shōkōkan, in Edo, gathered scholars including the Ming refugee Zhu Shunshui, and sent researchers across Japan to copy documents. In popular culture he became 'Mito Kōmon', a travelling righter of wrongs, though that image is fictional.

His Dai Nihonshi, written in classical Chinese in the composite style of the Chinese standard histories, emphasised loyalty to the imperial line and recognised the Southern Court as legitimate. The Mito school that grew from it later fed the 'revere the emperor, expel the barbarians' movement that helped bring down the shogunate his family had founded.

## Key histories & documents

### Great History of Japan (Dai Nihonshi) (1657–1906)

[![Great History of Japan (Dai Nihonshi)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Dainihonshi.JPG/1280px-Dainihonshi.JPG)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dainihonshi.JPG)

<sub>Printed volumes of the Dai Nihonshi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dainihonshi.JPG).</sub>

- **Date:** 1657–1906
- **Type:** Composite history (397 chapters)
- **Written / held:** Compiled at the Shōkōkan, Mito domain (Edo and Mito)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Great%20History%20of%20Japan%20%28Dai%20Nihonshi%29%20Tokugawa%20Mitsukuni)

Begun by Tokugawa Mitsukuni in 1657 and continued by the Mito domain and later the Tokugawa family for two and a half centuries, the Dai Nihonshi follows the composite form of the Chinese standard histories: annals of emperors, biographies, treatises and tables. Its annals and biographies were largely finished by 1715; the treatises and tables were completed only in 1906. It is known for three judgements: listing Empress Jingū among consorts rather than sovereigns, recognising Prince Ōtomo as Emperor Kōbun, and treating the Southern Court as legitimate. Its research into documents set high standards, and the 'Mito learning' it fostered, emphasising loyalty to the emperor, fed the late Edo movement that overthrew the shogunate.
