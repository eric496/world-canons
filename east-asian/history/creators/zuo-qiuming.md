## Biography

Almost nothing certain is known about Zuo Qiuming. The Analects mention a man of that name whom Confucius admired, and Han writers, beginning with Sima Qian, made him a court historian of Lu and a contemporary of Confucius who wrote a commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals; Sima Qian also says he was blind when he composed the Guoyu. Later tradition honoured him in the Confucian temple.

Modern scholars generally regard the attribution as traditional rather than historical. The Zuozhuan appears to have been compiled in the fourth century BC from older chronicles and stories, and its link with the Spring and Autumn Annals may have been tightened by Han editors. Whoever its authors were, the works credited to Zuo Qiuming established the patterns of Chinese historical narrative: speeches that reveal character, omens that foreshadow outcomes, and judgements that weigh rulers by ritual propriety.

### Zuo Commentary (Zuozhuan) (c. 4th century BC)

[![Zuo Commentary (Zuozhuan)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c7/Li_Yuanyang_Zuo_zhuan_first_page.png/960px-Li_Yuanyang_Zuo_zhuan_first_page.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Li_Yuanyang_Zuo_zhuan_first_page.png)

<sub>First page of a Ming-dynasty edition of the Zuozhuan Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Li_Yuanyang_Zuo_zhuan_first_page.png).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 4th century BC
- **Type:** Narrative history
- **Written / held:** Compiled in the state of Lu or its neighbours; transmitted in manuscript and later printed editions
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Zuo%20Commentary%20%28Zuozhuan%29%20Zuo%20Qiuming)

The Spring and Autumn Annals record the events of the state of Lu in bare one-line entries; the Zuozhuan, traditionally attributed to Zuo Qiuming, fills them out with detailed narratives of diplomacy, war, ritual and court intrigue across all the Zhou states. Its set speeches, dramatic battle accounts such as Chengpu and Bi, and foreshadowing omens became models of Chinese prose. Unlike the Gongyang and Guliang commentaries, which explain the moral 'praise and blame' hidden in the Annals' wording, it is mainly a work of history, and modern scholars think it was compiled independently from older sources and later aligned with the Annals. It is the richest source for the Spring and Autumn period and one of the Thirteen Classics.

### Discourses of the States (Guoyu) (c. 4th century BC)

- **Date:** c. 4th century BC
- **Type:** Collection of speeches
- **Written / held:** Compiled in Warring States China; earliest commentary by Wei Zhao (3rd century AD)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Discourses%20of%20the%20States%20%28Guoyu%29%20Zuo%20Qiuming)

The Guoyu gathers the counsel of ministers and rulers of Zhou, Lu, Qi, Jin, Zheng, Chu, Wu and Yue, from the tenth to the fifth century BC, in twenty-one chapters. Where the Zuozhuan narrates events, the Guoyu centres on speeches that argue for ritual propriety and humane government. Sima Qian attributed it to a blind Zuo Qiuming, and it was long called the 'outer commentary' to the Spring and Autumn Annals, but its varied style suggests several compilers. The longest sections concern Jin and the struggle between Wu and Yue. Its speeches are an important source for early Chinese political and religious ideas.
