## Biography

Prince Toneri was one of the sons of Emperor Tenmu, who had ordered the compilation of histories of the realm in 681. A senior member of the imperial family, he held high office after Fujiwara no Fuhito's death and was posthumously honoured as emperor (Sudōjinkei) because his son became Emperor Junnin.

The Nihon Shoki was presented under his name in 720, after decades of work by many scholars, some of them immigrants from the Korean peninsula. His role was that of a supervising patron rather than an author, but his name is permanently attached to the founding text of Japanese official historiography.

## Key histories & documents

### Chronicles of Japan (Nihon Shoki) (720)

[![Chronicles of Japan (Nihon Shoki)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/98/Nihonshoki_tanaka_version.jpg/1280px-Nihonshoki_tanaka_version.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nihonshoki_tanaka_version.jpg)

<sub>Tanaka manuscript of the Nihon Shoki, scroll 10, 9th century (Nara National Museum) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nihonshoki_tanaka_version.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 720
- **Type:** Official history (30 scrolls)
- **Written / held:** Presented at the court of Empress Genshō, Heijō-kyō (Nara); oldest manuscripts from the 8th–9th centuries
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chronicles%20of%20Japan%20%28Nihon%20Shoki%29%20Prince%20Toneri)

The Nihon Shoki opens with two scrolls on the creation of the world and the age of the gods, often quoting alternative versions with 'one book says', and then narrates the reigns of the emperors year by year to the abdication of Empress Jitō in 697. Compared with the Kojiki of 712 it is longer, more Chinese in language and form, and more concerned with foreign relations, especially with the Korean kingdoms of Baekje, Silla and Goguryeo, drawing on lost Baekje chronicles. Its early chronology is legendary, extended to place the founding of the state in 660 BC, and its accounts of the peninsula are read critically alongside Korean and Chinese sources. From the seventh century on it becomes increasingly reliable. Lectured on at court from 721, it became the basis of Shinto and national history, and modern historians read it both as evidence and as an ideological construction of the Yamato state.
