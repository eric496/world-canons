# Yuri Kimimasa

*由利公正*

**1829 – 1909** · Japanese  
Born: Fukui, Echizen province · Died: Tokyo  
Traditions & eras: [Meiji constitutionalism & modern historiography](../movements/meiji-documents.md)  

> Fukui samurai and financial reformer who wrote the first draft of the Charter Oath of 1868.

## Biography

Yuri Kimimasa (also known as Mitsuoka Hachirō) was a retainer of the Fukui domain, a student of the reformer Yokoi Shōnan and a specialist in domain finance. He joined the new Meiji government in 1868, where he planned its first paper currency, and later served as governor of Tokyo and as a member of the House of Peers.

His draft of five articles, revised by Fukuoka Takachika of Tosa and finally by Kido Takayoshi of Chōshū, became the Charter Oath proclaimed by the young Emperor Meiji. Its promise of public discussion and of seeking knowledge throughout the world was later cited by advocates of representative government, both in the Meiji era and after 1945.

## Key histories & documents

### Charter Oath (Gokajō no goseimon) (1868)

[![Charter Oath (Gokajō no goseimon)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/67/5jo1.gif)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:5jo1.gif)

<sub>The Charter Oath in the official manuscript Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:5jo1.gif).</sub>

- **Date:** 1868
- **Type:** Imperial oath
- **Written / held:** Proclaimed by Emperor Meiji at the Shishinden, Kyoto Imperial Palace, 6 April 1868
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Charter%20Oath%20%28Gokaj%C5%8D%20no%20goseimon%29%20Yuri%20Kimimasa)

In the early months of the Boshin War the new government sought to win the support of the domains and to signal a break with the shogunal past. Yuri Kimimasa's draft, revised by Fukuoka Takachika and Kido Takayoshi, was sworn by the fifteen-year-old Emperor Meiji before the gods in the presence of the court and daimyo. Its five articles promised that deliberative assemblies would be established and affairs decided by public discussion, that all classes would unite, that common people would be allowed to pursue their callings, that 'evil customs of the past' would be abandoned, and that knowledge would be sought throughout the world. Its wording was vague enough to be cited both by the government and by the movement for popular rights, and in 1946 Emperor Shōwa quoted it in his New Year rescript.
