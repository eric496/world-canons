# Iryeon

*一然*

**1206 – 1289** · Korean (Goryeo)  
Born: Jangsan (Gyeongsan, North Gyeongsang) · Died: Ingaksa, Gunwi (North Gyeongsang)  
Traditions & eras: [Three Kingdoms & Goryeo histories](../movements/korean-dynastic-histories.md)  
Also in: [Religion](../../religion/creators/iryeon.md)  

> Seon Buddhist master of the Goryeo period who compiled the Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms (Samguk yusa).

## Biography

Iryeon became a monk as a boy and passed the monastic examination in 1227. He rose to be National Preceptor in 1283 under King Chungnyeol and spent his last years at Ingaksa temple, where his stupa and memorial stele survive. He lived through the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century and Goryeo's subordination to the Yuan.

The Samguk yusa gathers legends, Buddhist miracle tales, songs and local traditions omitted from the official Samguk sagi. It contains the earliest surviving account of Dangun, the founder of Gojoseon, and fourteen hyangga songs, and it is an irreplaceable source for Korean myth, Buddhism and folk belief.

## Key histories & documents

### Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms (Samguk yusa) (c. 1281–1289)

- **Date:** c. 1281–1289
- **Type:** Collection of history and legend (5 gwon)
- **Written / held:** Compiled at Ingaksa, Gunwi; earliest surviving printings from the early Joseon period
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Memorabilia%20of%20the%20Three%20Kingdoms%20%28Samguk%20yusa%29%20Iryeon)

Where the Samguk sagi is a Confucian official history, the Samguk yusa gathers what it left out. It opens with a chronological table and a section on 'wonders' beginning with the earliest account of Dangun, said to have founded Gojoseon after the union of a son of the heavenly lord with a bear-woman, and continues with stories of the kings of the three kingdoms and Gaya, the introduction and spread of Buddhism, eminent monks, miracles, pagodas and images, and acts of filial piety. It preserves fourteen hyangga, the oldest Korean vernacular poems, written in Chinese characters used for their sounds. Compiled in the aftermath of the Mongol invasions, it is often seen as an affirmation of Korean identity. It is the essential source for Korean mythology and early Buddhism.
