# Kume Kunitake

*久米邦武*

**1839 – 1931** · Japanese  
Born: Saga, Hizen province · Died: Tokyo  
Traditions & eras: [Meiji constitutionalism & modern historiography](../movements/meiji-documents.md)  

> Meiji historian who wrote the official record of the Iwakura Mission and helped found critical history in Japan.

## Biography

A samurai scholar of the Saga domain, Kume accompanied the Iwakura Mission of 1871–73 through the United States and Europe as its secretary and chronicler. He then joined the government's historiographical office and, from 1888, taught at the Imperial University of Tokyo, working alongside Shigeno Yasutsugu and the German historian Ludwig Riess, who taught Rankean source criticism.

His True Account of the Ambassadors' Tour (1878) is a vivid, five-part record of what the mission saw. In 1892 his article describing Shinto as a survival of ancient heaven-worship provoked attacks from Shinto and nationalist circles, and he was forced to resign his professorship, an early landmark in the history of academic freedom in Japan.

## Key histories & documents

### True Account of the Ambassadors' Tour of America and Europe (Bei-Ō kairan jikki) (1878)

- **Date:** 1878
- **Type:** Official travel record (100 volumes)
- **Written / held:** Published by the Daijōkan, Tokyo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=True%20Account%20of%20the%20Ambassadors%27%20Tour%20of%20America%20and%20Europe%20%28Bei-%C5%8C%20kairan%20jikki%29%20Kume%20Kunitake)

The Iwakura Mission, led by Iwakura Tomomi with Kido Takayoshi, Ōkubo Toshimichi and Itō Hirobumi, travelled for nearly two years through the United States and Europe to study institutions and seek revision of the unequal treaties. Kume, its official chronicler, wrote a five-part account describing factories, parliaments, schools, prisons, cities and landscapes, illustrated with copperplate engravings, and reflecting on what Japan should adopt. He noted that Europe's lead was recent, a matter of decades rather than centuries, an observation that encouraged Meiji modernisers. It is a key source for how the Meiji leadership saw the West.
