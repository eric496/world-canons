# Kitabatake Chikafusa

*北畠親房*

**1293 – 1354** · Japanese  
Born: Kyoto · Died: Anō, Yoshino (Nara)  
Traditions & eras: [Historical tales & medieval interpretations](../movements/historical-tales.md)  

> Court noble and general of the Southern Court whose Jinnō Shōtōki argued the legitimacy of Go-Daigo's line from the age of the gods.

## Biography

Chikafusa belonged to a branch of the Murakami Genji court nobility and was tutor and adviser to Emperor Go-Daigo's sons. After Ashikaga Takauji drove Go-Daigo from Kyoto in 1336, Chikafusa became the chief strategist of the Southern Court at Yoshino, campaigning in eastern Japan, where he wrote his history while besieged at Oda castle in Hitachi in 1339.

The Jinnō Shōtōki traces the imperial succession from its divine origins and argues that the throne belongs to the line that combines descent with virtue, which for him was the Southern Court. Its Shinto-Confucian view of Japan as a divine land had a long afterlife: it was read by Mito scholars and Meiji nationalists, and its use in prewar ideology has made it a subject of critical study since 1945.

## Key histories & documents

### Chronicle of the Direct Descent of Gods and Sovereigns (Jinnō shōtōki) (1339; revised 1343)

- **Date:** 1339; revised 1343
- **Type:** Historical and political treatise
- **Written / held:** Written at Oda castle, Hitachi province, for the young Emperor Go-Murakami
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chronicle%20of%20the%20Direct%20Descent%20of%20Gods%20and%20Sovereigns%20%28Jinn%C5%8D%20sh%C5%8Dt%C5%8Dki%29%20Kitabatake%20Chikafusa)

Composed during the civil war between the Southern Court of Go-Daigo's line and the Northern Court backed by the Ashikaga, the Jinnō shōtōki opens with the declaration that great Japan is the land of the gods, and then reviews each reign. Chikafusa argues that Japan alone has preserved an unbroken divine succession, but that true legitimacy lies in the 'direct line' marked by virtue and possession of the three sacred regalia, which for him meant the Southern Court. He also judges rulers and ministers by Confucian standards of virtue. Widely copied in the Edo period, the work influenced the Mito school and Meiji nationalism, and its ideas were used in prewar ideology; modern scholars read it as a political text of its civil-war moment.
