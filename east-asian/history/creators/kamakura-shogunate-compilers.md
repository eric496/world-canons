# Compilers of the Kamakura shogunate

*鎌倉幕府編纂者*

**late 13th century – early 14th century** · Japanese  
Born: Kamakura · Died:   
Traditions & eras: [Historical tales & medieval interpretations](../movements/historical-tales.md)  

> The officials of the Kamakura shogunate, under the Hōjō regents, who compiled the Azuma Kagami, the official chronicle of Japan's first warrior government.

## Biography

The Azuma Kagami was compiled around 1300 by officials of the shogunate in Kamakura, probably within the Hōjō regency's administrative offices. Its authors worked from shogunal documents, court diaries, temple records and family papers of retainers.

The result presents the founding and rule of the shogunate from a Hōjō viewpoint, which historians take into account when reading its portrayal of the Minamoto shoguns and the regents' rivals. It became a model of government for later warrior regimes; Tokugawa Ieyasu is said to have studied it closely.

## Key histories & documents

### Mirror of the East (Azuma Kagami) (c. 1300)

[![Mirror of the East (Azuma Kagami)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9b/Azumakagami_03.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Azumakagami_03.jpg)

<sub>Page from a manuscript of the Azuma Kagami Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Azumakagami_03.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1300
- **Type:** Official chronicle (52 chapters)
- **Written / held:** Compiled in Kamakura; main manuscript lineages include the Hōjō and Kikkawa copies
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mirror%20of%20the%20East%20%28Azuma%20Kagami%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Kamakura%20shogunate)

The Azuma Kagami records the history of the warrior government in Kamakura day by day, from Minamoto no Yoritomo's rising in 1180 to the return of the sixth shogun, Prince Munetaka, to Kyoto in 1266. Written in a Japanese style of classical Chinese, it draws on shogunal documents, court diaries and the records of vassal families, and includes the text of many orders and petitions. It presents the Hōjō regents favourably and is silent on some awkward events, and some years are missing. Despite this it is the basic source for the early warrior age, and later regimes, including the Tokugawa, consulted it as a manual of warrior government.
