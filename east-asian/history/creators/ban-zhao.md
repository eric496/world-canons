## Biography

Ban Zhao was the younger sister of Ban Gu and Ban Chao. Widowed young, she lived at the Eastern Han court, where Emperor He called on her to finish the Book of Han after Ban Gu's death; she is credited with completing its eight tables and, with Ma Xu, the treatise on astronomy. She taught the empress and palace ladies, and the scholar Ma Rong is said to have studied the Book of Han under her. Under the regency of Empress Dowager Deng she was an adviser at court, and she also wrote poems, rhapsodies and memorials.

Her Lessons for Women (Nüjie), addressed to the young women of her family, counsels humility, diligence and deference to husbands and in-laws, and became a foundational text of Chinese women's education. Modern readers have judged it very differently: some see it as entrenching subordination, others note that Ban Zhao also argued for educating girls and herself exemplified a learned woman's public role.

## Key histories & documents

### Lessons for Women (Nüjie) (c. 106)

- **Date:** c. 106
- **Type:** Didactic treatise
- **Written / held:** Written at the Eastern Han court, Luoyang
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lessons%20for%20Women%20%28N%C3%BCjie%29%20Ban%20Zhao)

Ban Zhao wrote the Lessons for Women in old age, addressing her daughters and nieces about to marry. Its seven sections, on humility, husband and wife, respect and caution, womanly qualifications, devotion, obedience, and harmony with in-laws, teach modesty, diligence and accommodation within the household. The text joined the 'Four Books for Women' and was widely printed in China, Korea and Japan, where it was used in women's education into the twentieth century. It was criticised in the May Fourth era as a charter of female subordination; recent scholars read it in its court context and note its plea that girls, like boys, should be educated.
