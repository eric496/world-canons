> The unknown writers behind many Heian works, including the historical tale Ōkagami (The Great Mirror).

## Biography

Many classic works of Heian prose, from early monogatari to tale collections, have no securely known author. In history, the most important is the Ōkagami, probably written in the late eleventh or early twelfth century by a courtier close to the Fujiwara regents; candidates such as Minamoto no Akifusa and Fujiwara no Yoshinobu have been proposed without agreement.

These writers worked in Japanese prose rather than the classical Chinese of the official histories, telling the past through court gossip, poetry and anecdote. Their 'historical tales' (rekishi monogatari) created a lasting vernacular form of history-writing.

## Key histories & documents

### The Great Mirror (Ōkagami) (late 11th or early 12th century)

- **Date:** late 11th or early 12th century
- **Type:** Historical tale
- **Written / held:** Written in Heian-kyō (Kyoto); author unknown
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Great%20Mirror%20%28%C5%8Ckagami%29%20Anonymous%20Heian%20authors)

The Ōkagami is framed as a dialogue overheard at a Buddhist service at the Urin'in temple in Kyoto, where two men said to be about 190 and 180 years old reminisce about the reigns and the great ministers they have seen, while a young samurai questions them. Organised like a Chinese composite history, with sections on emperors, ministers and anecdotes, it centres on Fujiwara no Michinaga, whose power and splendour it celebrates while also recording rivals, scandals and ironies. The dialogue form lets it present differing views, and its lively anecdotes about figures such as Sugawara no Michizane became famous. It was the first of the 'four mirrors' (shikyō), followed by the Imakagami, Mizukagami and Masukagami, and a model for vernacular Japanese history.
