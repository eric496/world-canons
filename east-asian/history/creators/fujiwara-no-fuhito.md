## Biography

Fuhito was the second son of Nakatomi no Kamatari, whose role in the coup of 645 had earned the family name Fujiwara. He rose under Empress Jitō and Emperor Monmu, married his daughter Miyako to Monmu and another daughter, Kōmyō, to the future Emperor Shōmu, setting the pattern of Fujiwara marriage politics. He was a leading figure in the move of the capital to Heijō-kyō (Nara) in 710.

With Prince Osakabe he directed the compilation of the Taihō Code of 701, which established Japan's Chinese-style ritsuryō state, and he later began its revision, the Yōrō Code, which was put into force only in 757. Tradition also connects him with the completion of the Nihon Shoki in the year of his death.

## Key histories & documents

### Taihō Code (Taihō ritsuryō) (701)

- **Date:** 701
- **Type:** Legal and administrative code
- **Written / held:** Promulgated at the court of Emperor Monmu, Fujiwara-kyō; text lost, reconstructed from the Yōrō Code
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Taih%C5%8D%20Code%20%28Taih%C5%8D%20ritsury%C5%8D%29%20Fujiwara%20no%20Fuhito)

Compiled by a commission under Prince Osakabe and Fujiwara no Fuhito and modelled on the Tang codes, the Taihō Code consisted of penal laws (ritsu) and administrative statutes (ryō). It defined a hierarchy of ministries and ranks headed by the Council of State, divided the country into provinces and districts, and set out the allocation of rice land to households, taxes in kind and labour, conscription and the registration of the population. The original text does not survive; it is known largely through the Yōrō Code of 718, enforced in 757, and its ninth-century commentaries, the Ryō no gige and Ryō no shūge. Although many provisions decayed within two centuries, the ritsuryō framework formally lasted until the Meiji era and shaped Japanese court titles and offices for over a thousand years.
