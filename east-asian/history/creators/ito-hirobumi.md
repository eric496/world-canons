# Itō Hirobumi

*伊藤博文*

**1841 – 1909** · Japanese  
Born: Tsukari, Suō province (Yamaguchi) · Died: Harbin, Manchuria (assassinated)  
Traditions & eras: [Meiji constitutionalism & modern historiography](../movements/meiji-documents.md)  

> Meiji statesman, four times prime minister, who led the drafting of the Constitution of the Empire of Japan of 1889.

## Biography

Itō was born into a farming family that rose into the lower ranks of the Chōshū samurai. He studied at Yoshida Shōin's school, was one of the 'Chōshū Five' smuggled to Britain in 1863, and became a key member of the Meiji oligarchy. In 1882–83 he studied constitutional theory in Europe, especially with Rudolf von Gneist and Lorenz von Stein, then built the cabinet system and became Japan's first prime minister in 1885. He founded the Seiyūkai party in 1900.

After the Russo-Japanese War he became the first Resident-General of Korea (1906–09), overseeing the protectorate that preceded annexation. He was assassinated at Harbin station in 1909 by the Korean independence activist An Jung-geun. He is remembered in Japan as the architect of constitutional government and in Korea as a principal agent of colonisation, and both views are part of his history.

### Constitution of the Empire of Japan (Meiji Constitution) (1889)

[![Constitution of the Empire of Japan (Meiji Constitution)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bd/Meiji-Constitution-Empire-of-Japan.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Meiji-Constitution-Empire-of-Japan.png)

<sub>Opening of the Constitution of the Empire of Japan with the imperial seal (National Archives of Japan) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Meiji-Constitution-Empire-of-Japan.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 1889
- **Type:** Constitution
- **Written / held:** Promulgated in Tokyo, 11 February 1889; in force 29 November 1890; original in the National Archives of Japan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Constitution%20of%20the%20Empire%20of%20Japan%20%28Meiji%20Constitution%29%20It%C5%8D%20Hirobumi)

Drafted in secret by Itō Hirobumi with Inoue Kowashi, Itō Miyoji and Kaneko Kentarō, with advice from the German jurists Hermann Roesler and Albert Mosse, the constitution was presented as a gift from the emperor to his subjects. Its seventy-six articles declared the emperor sacred and inviolable and the source of sovereignty, with supreme command of the army and navy, while creating a bicameral Imperial Diet with an elected lower house, a cabinet and courts, and guaranteeing rights 'within the limits of law'. It allowed both the party governments of the 1920s and the military dominance of the 1930s. It remained in force until replaced in 1947 by the Constitution of Japan, which was adopted formally as its amendment.

### Commentaries on the Constitution of the Empire of Japan (Kenpō gige) (1889)

- **Date:** 1889
- **Type:** Constitutional commentary
- **Written / held:** Published in Tokyo under Itō Hirobumi's name; English translation by Itō Miyoji, 1889
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Commentaries%20on%20the%20Constitution%20of%20the%20Empire%20of%20Japan%20%28Kenp%C5%8D%20gige%29%20It%C5%8D%20Hirobumi)

Prepared largely by Inoue Kowashi and issued under Itō's name, the Kenpō gige explains each article of the Meiji Constitution, presenting the emperor's sovereignty as rooted in Japan's unbroken imperial line while describing the Diet, cabinet and courts as instruments of constitutional government. An English translation by Itō Miyoji appeared the same year to present Japan's new order to Western powers. It was the starting point for later debates between scholars such as Hozumi Yatsuka, who emphasised imperial sovereignty, and Minobe Tatsukichi, whose theory of the emperor as an organ of the state dominated in the 1920s before being banned in 1935.

### Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895)

[![Treaty of Shimonoseki](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Japan_China_Peace_Treaty_17_April_1895.jpg/1280px-Japan_China_Peace_Treaty_17_April_1895.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Japan_China_Peace_Treaty_17_April_1895.jpg)

<sub>The Treaty of Shimonoseki, 17 April 1895, with the signatures and seals of the plenipotentiaries Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Japan_China_Peace_Treaty_17_April_1895.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1895
- **Type:** Peace treaty
- **Written / held:** Signed at the Shunpanrō inn, Shimonoseki, 17 April 1895
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Treaty%20of%20Shimonoseki%20It%C5%8D%20Hirobumi)

Negotiated by Itō Hirobumi and Mutsu Munemitsu for Japan and Li Hongzhang for the Qing, after Li had been wounded by a Japanese assailant during the talks, the treaty required China to recognise the full independence of Korea, ending the tributary relationship; to cede Taiwan, the Penghu Islands and the Liaodong peninsula; to pay an indemnity of 200 million taels; and to open further ports to Japanese trade. Within days Russia, France and Germany forced Japan to return Liaodong in exchange for a larger indemnity, the Triple Intervention. The treaty marked Japan's emergence as an imperial power, began fifty years of Japanese rule in Taiwan and stimulated reform movements in China. It is remembered very differently in Japan, China, Taiwan and Korea.
