## Biography

Born into a masterless samurai family, Hakuseki studied under Kinoshita Jun'an and became tutor to the lord of Kōfu, who became the sixth shogun Tokugawa Ienobu in 1709. Hakuseki then served as Ienobu's chief adviser and that of his young successor Ietsugu, reforming the currency, regulating the Nagasaki trade and revising diplomatic protocol with Korea. He lost influence under the eighth shogun Yoshimune and spent his last years writing.

His Tokushi yoron, from lectures given to Ienobu, explained the transfer of power from court to warriors through 'nine changes' of court rule and 'five changes' of warrior rule, a rational, cause-seeking account. His Seiyō kibun, based on his interrogation of the Italian missionary Giovanni Battista Sidotti in 1709, was a pioneering study of the West, and his autobiography Oritaku shiba no ki is a classic.

### Reading History and Its Lessons (Tokushi yoron) (1712)

- **Date:** 1712
- **Type:** Interpretive history
- **Written / held:** Based on lectures given to the shogun Tokugawa Ienobu in Edo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Reading%20History%20and%20Its%20Lessons%20%28Tokushi%20yoron%29%20Arai%20Hakuseki)

Hakuseki explains Japanese history from the ninth century to the founding of the Tokugawa shogunate as a series of 'nine changes' in the government of the realm by the court and 'five changes' in rule by warriors, culminating in the Tokugawa peace. He looks for causes in the actions and abilities of rulers and ministers rather than in fate or the gods, and judges the decline of the court as a consequence of its own failings. The work served to justify Tokugawa rule historically, and is admired as one of the most analytical pre-modern Japanese histories.

### Tidings of the West (Seiyō kibun) (1715)

- **Date:** 1715
- **Type:** Account of the West
- **Written / held:** Written in Edo; circulated privately in manuscript
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tidings%20of%20the%20West%20%28Seiy%C5%8D%20kibun%29%20Arai%20Hakuseki)

In 1708 the Sicilian priest Giovanni Battista Sidotti landed secretly on Yakushima, disguised as a samurai, hoping to revive Christianity in Japan. Brought to Edo, he was questioned by Arai Hakuseki in 1709. Hakuseki recorded his answers on European geography, politics and customs and on Christian doctrine. He admired Sidotti's learning and recommended that he be sent home rather than executed; Sidotti was instead confined and died in 1714. The book, kept within official circles, combined respect for Western knowledge with rejection of Christianity, and became a foundation of Edo Western learning.

### Told Round a Brushwood Fire (Oritaku shiba no ki) (1716)

- **Date:** 1716
- **Type:** Autobiography
- **Written / held:** Written in Edo after Hakuseki's retirement
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Told%20Round%20a%20Brushwood%20Fire%20%28Oritaku%20shiba%20no%20ki%29%20Arai%20Hakuseki)

Written after the death of the shogun Ietsugu and Hakuseki's loss of office, the memoir begins with portraits of his stern samurai father and his childhood studies, then gives a detailed, self-justifying account of the policies he pursued for Ienobu and Ietsugu: currency reform, judicial cases, the regulation of the Nagasaki trade and his reforms of protocol for the Korean embassy. Intended for his descendants, it is an insider's view of shogunal government and a portrait of a Confucian statesman's mind, and it is often regarded as the first great Japanese autobiography.
