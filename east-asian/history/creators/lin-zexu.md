## Biography

Lin Zexu passed the jinshi in 1811 and built a reputation as an able and honest provincial official. Appointed imperial commissioner to suppress the opium trade in 1838, he arrived in Canton in 1839, forced foreign merchants to surrender over twenty thousand chests of opium and destroyed them publicly at Humen. Britain responded with war, and after early defeats Lin was made a scapegoat and exiled to Ili in Xinjiang. Later pardoned, he died on his way to suppress the Taiping rebels.

Lin had translators compile information on Western countries, gathered as the Gazetteer of the Four Continents, which he passed to Wei Yuan. In China he is honoured as a national hero who resisted foreign drug trafficking; historians also discuss how far his confrontational tactics and misreading of British power contributed to the outbreak of war.

### Letter to Queen Victoria (1839)

- **Date:** 1839
- **Type:** Diplomatic letter
- **Written / held:** Written at Canton (Guangzhou); English translations printed in the Chinese Repository and the London press
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Letter%20to%20Queen%20Victoria%20Lin%20Zexu)

In 1839 Lin Zexu, as imperial commissioner at Canton, wrote to Queen Victoria arguing that the opium trade was poisoning China while Britain itself banned the drug, and asking her to forbid its cultivation and export. The letter appeals to a shared moral order and warns that foreign merchants caught with opium would be punished under new Qing laws. Translated into English, it was published in Canton and London, though there is no evidence the queen ever received it. Its mixture of moral argument and the Qing tributary framework, which treated Britain as a distant vassal, has made it a classic document for teaching the clash between Chinese and Western ideas of international order on the eve of the Opium War.

### Gazetteer of the Four Continents (Sizhou zhi) (c. 1841)

- **Date:** c. 1841
- **Type:** World geography
- **Written / held:** Compiled at Canton (Guangzhou) from translated Western sources
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gazetteer%20of%20the%20Four%20Continents%20%28Sizhou%20zhi%29%20Lin%20Zexu)

During his time at Canton Lin Zexu employed translators, among them Liang Jinde, to render Western newspapers, legal texts and books into Chinese, so that he could understand his opponents. The core of the Sizhou zhi is a translation of parts of Hugh Murray's Encyclopaedia of Geography, describing the geography, governments and customs of the countries of Asia, Europe, Africa and the Americas. Lin passed the manuscript to Wei Yuan when he went into exile, and it became the core of the Haiguo tuzhi. It marks the first serious Qing effort to gather systematic knowledge of the West.
