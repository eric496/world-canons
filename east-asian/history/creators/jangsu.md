# King Jangsu of Goguryeo

*長壽王*

**394 – 491** · Korean (Goguryeo)  
Born: Goguryeo (probably Gungnae, Ji'an) · Died: Pyongyang  
Traditions & eras: [Three Kingdoms & Goryeo histories](../movements/korean-dynastic-histories.md)  

> Twentieth king of Goguryeo, who erected the great stele to his father Gwanggaeto in 414 and moved the capital to Pyongyang.

## Biography

Jangsu, whose name means 'long life', reigned for nearly eighty years from 413 to 491. He moved the capital from Gungnae to Pyongyang in 427, maintained relations with both the Northern and Southern dynasties of China, and in 475 captured the Baekje capital at Hanseong, pushing Goguryeo's frontier south to the Han River basin.

In 414 he set up the stele at his father's tomb near Gungnae (now Ji'an, China), whose inscription is the oldest substantial historical text from Korea's Three Kingdoms period.

## Key histories & documents

### Gwanggaeto Stele (414)

[![Gwanggaeto Stele](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/20230605_Stela_for_Gwanggaeto.jpg/960px-20230605_Stela_for_Gwanggaeto.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20230605_Stela_for_Gwanggaeto.jpg)

<sub>The Gwanggaeto Stele at Ji'an, Jilin, in its protective pavilion Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20230605_Stela_for_Gwanggaeto.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 414
- **Type:** Monumental inscription
- **Written / held:** Ji'an, Jilin province, China, near the tomb of King Gwanggaeto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Gwanggaeto%20Stele%20King%20Jangsu%20of%20Goguryeo)

King Jangsu set up the stele two years after his father's death. Its roughly 1,800 Chinese characters on four faces recount the descent of the founder Jumong from the Lord of Heaven and the daughter of the river god, list the campaigns of Gwanggaeto (r. 391–412) against Baekje, the Wa (Japanese), the Khitan and others, and end with regulations for the households guarding the royal tombs. Rediscovered in the 1870s and first studied through rubbings made for the Japanese army, it became the focus of a long dispute: a passage on the Wa crossing the sea in 391 was read by prewar Japanese scholars as evidence of Japanese rule in southern Korea, while Korean and Chinese scholars have proposed other readings and questioned whether rubbings were altered. Most scholars today read the passage within Goguryeo's own propaganda against Baekje. The stele is part of the UNESCO World Heritage site of the Koguryo capital cities and tombs.
