# Compilers of the Six National Histories

*六国史編纂者*

**8th century – 10th century** · Japanese  
Born: Heijō-kyō (Nara) and Heian-kyō (Kyoto) · Died:   
Traditions & eras: [Ritsuryō codes & the Six National Histories](../movements/ritsuryo-histories.md)  

> The court nobles and scholars who compiled Japan's six official histories, from the Nihon Shoki of 720 to the True Records of Three Reigns of 901.

## Biography

After the Nihon Shoki, the court continued to commission official histories modelled on Chinese practice. Commissions of nobles and Confucian scholars, such as Fujiwara no Tsugutada and Sugano no Mamichi for the Shoku Nihongi (797), Fujiwara no Fuyutsugu and Fujiwara no Otsugu for the Nihon Kōki (840), and Fujiwara no Tokihira and Sugawara no Michizane for the Nihon Sandai Jitsuroku (901), worked from court diaries, edicts and archives.

The six histories are written in classical Chinese and record events year by year from the viewpoint of the court. When the series ended in 901 the court did not resume it; history-writing passed to private chronicles, diaries and historical tales, a shift often linked to the rise of Fujiwara regency and the decline of ritsuryō institutions.

- **Date:** 797
- **Type:** Official history (40 scrolls)
- **Written / held:** Compiled at the court of Emperor Kanmu, Heian-kyō (Kyoto)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Continued%20Chronicles%20of%20Japan%20%28Shoku%20Nihongi%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Six%20National%20Histories)

Compiled in several stages and completed in 797 by Fujiwara no Tsugutada and Sugano no Mamichi, the Shoku Nihongi records the century of the Nara court: the building of Heijō-kyō, the Taihō and Yōrō codes, the casting of the Great Buddha at Tōdai-ji, the rise of the monk Dōkyō and the move of the capital under Emperor Kanmu. Written in classical Chinese, it preserves sixty-two imperial edicts (senmyō) in a script recording the Japanese language, invaluable for the history of the language. It is far more sober and documentary than the Nihon Shoki and is the indispensable source for eighth-century Japan.
