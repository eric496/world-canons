The religious life of China, Japan and Korea: Shang divination and ancestor cults, Confucian ritual, religious Daoism, the many schools of East Asian Buddhism, Shinto, Korean shamanism, popular religion, the arrival of Christianity and the new religions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In East Asia these traditions were rarely exclusive; most people drew on several at once, and this section treats them as overlapping strands of one religious landscape. It is written as history of religion, describing what each tradition teaches and practises and what modern scholarship says about authorship and dating, without taking sides. Manuscripts, early prints, temple paintings and portrait sculptures come from Wikimedia Commons.

## Notes on this section

- **Approach.** This is written as history of religion: it describes what each tradition teaches and practises, and what modern scholarship says about authorship and dating. It does not judge which teachings are true. Revelations, miracles and lineages are reported as the traditions tell them ("according to tradition", "he reported a revelation"), and the critical view is noted where it differs, as with Bodhidharma, Huineng and the Platform Sutra, the origin of the Heart Sutra, the Five Books of Shinto and Yoshida Kanetomo's claimed authorities.
- **Many traditions at once.** In East Asia people seldom belonged to just one religion. Ancestor rites, Buddhist funerals, Daoist exorcism, shrine festivals and shamanic healing were often all part of the same family's life. The traditions below are separate strands of that one landscape, not rival churches, and many figures belong to more than one.
- **Overlap with philosophy.** Confucius, Laozi, Zhiyi, Fazang, Huineng, Kūkai, Shinran, Dōgen, Wonhyo, Uisang, Jinul, Choe Je-u and Han Yong-un keep the ids they have in the philosophy section. Here they appear as founders, ritualists and objects of cult. A few core texts (Book of Rites, Platform Sutra, Sokushin jōbutsu gi, Kyōgyōshinshō, Shōbōgenzō, Donggyeong daejeon) are listed in both sections. Their entries here focus on religious use and point to philosophy for the ideas. Zhuangzi is left to philosophy.
- **Overlap with literature and art.** The Kojiki and Journey to the West are in the literature section and are only mentioned here. The Nihon Shoki has its own entry here. Buddhist sculpture and cave temples are in the art section, and shrine and temple buildings such as Ise belong to architecture, so the Ise entries here cover the priesthood, its theology and the rebuilding rite. Shared collective ids: `nara-buddhist-workshops` and `yamato-e-painters` (art). Shared person ids: `han-yong-un` (literature and philosophy).
- **Collective entries.** Anonymous or communal work is listed under group entries: Shang diviners, the compilers of the Book of Rites and the Engishiki, anonymous early Daoist authors, the Ming editors of the Daoist Canon, authors of morality books, the monks of Dunhuang, the carvers of the Tripitaka Koreana, the Ise priesthood, Korean shamans and the Kakure Kirishitan communities.
- **Names.** Pinyin, Hepburn with macrons and Revised Romanization, family name first. The original script is given for every figure and tradition. Kumārajīva uses `kumarajiva` so that a future South Asian section can share it.
- **Pictures.** Manuscripts, early prints, mandalas, handscrolls and temple portraits come from Wikimedia Commons. Portraits are paintings, prints or sculptures, never photographs. Most portraits of early monks are later idealised likenesses. Modern figures with no public-domain painted portrait (Nakayama Miki, Sotaesan, Taixu, D. T. Suzuki and others) have none, and works still in copyright are not illustrated.
