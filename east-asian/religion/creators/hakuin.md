# Hakuin Ekaku

*白隠慧鶴*

**1686 – 1769** · Japanese  
Born: Hara, Suruga Province · Died: Hara, Suruga Province  
Traditions & eras: [Chan, Seon and Zen](../movements/chan-zen-seon.md)  

> Reviver of Rinzai Zen in the Edo period, who systematised kōan training and spread Zen through popular writings and ink paintings.

<sub>Portrait: Hakuin Ekaku, self-portrait, 1767 · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zen-Master-Hakuin-Ekaku-Self-Portrait-1767.png)</sub>

## Biography

Hakuin was the son of an innkeeper at Hara on the Tōkaidō road and became a monk at fifteen. After a dramatic awakening and a later breakdown that he said he cured through a meditation taught by a hermit, he returned to the small temple Shōin-ji in Hara, where he taught for the rest of his life, attracting students from all over Japan.

He insisted on intense kōan practice and 'great doubt', and his disciples developed the graded kōan curriculum used in Rinzai to this day; virtually all Japanese Rinzai lineages descend from him. He wrote in vernacular Japanese for lay people, composed popular songs, and painted thousands of bold ink images of Bodhidharma and others, now prized as Zen art. 'Sound of one hand' is his kōan.

### Song of Zazen and Orategama (c. 1750)

- **Date:** c. 1750
- **Type:** Vernacular Zen writings
- **Where written / held:** Shōin-ji, Hara, Suruga
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Song%20of%20Zazen%20and%20Orategama%20Hakuin%20Ekaku)

The Zazen wasan, a hymn of some 44 lines in Japanese, begins by declaring that all beings are originally buddhas, like water and ice, and praises zazen as the gate to awakening; it is chanted in Rinzai temples to this day. The Orategama, 'Embossed Tea Kettle', printed in 1751, collects letters to a feudal lord, a nun and a sick monk explaining how to practise kōan meditation amid daily affairs and describing methods of healing exhausted body and mind. Like his other vernacular works and songs, they show how Hakuin carried Zen beyond the monastery.

### Daruma paintings (c. 1740s–1768)

[![Daruma paintings](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Hakuin_Ekaku_-_Portrait_of_Daruma_-_2015.500.9.3_-_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art.jpg/1280px-Hakuin_Ekaku_-_Portrait_of_Daruma_-_2015.500.9.3_-_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hakuin_Ekaku_-_Portrait_of_Daruma_-_2015.500.9.3_-_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art.jpg)

<sub>Hakuin Ekaku, Daruma, ink on paper (Metropolitan Museum of Art) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hakuin_Ekaku_-_Portrait_of_Daruma_-_2015.500.9.3_-_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1740s–1768
- **Type:** Ink paintings
- **Where written / held:** Many collections, e.g. Metropolitan Museum of Art, Eisei Bunko, Manju-ji (Ōita)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Daruma%20paintings%20Hakuin%20Ekaku)

Hakuin took up painting and calligraphy seriously in his sixties and produced thousands of works, which he gave to visitors and lay followers. His Daruma figures, with glaring eyes and thick robes drawn in a few heavy strokes, are often inscribed with phrases such as 'Point directly at the human mind; see your nature and become Buddha'. Unconcerned with professional polish, they belong to the tradition of zenga, Zen paintings by monks. Rediscovered by collectors in the twentieth century, they are now among the best-known images of Zen.
