# Kuoan Shiyuan

*廓庵師遠*

**fl. 12th century** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died:   
Traditions & eras: [Chan, Seon and Zen](../movements/chan-zen-seon.md)  

> Song Chan monk whose ten verses and pictures of searching for an ox became the classic image of the path to awakening.

## Biography

Kuoan Shiyuan was a twelfth-century Chan monk of the Linji lineage, about whom little is known beyond his authorship of a version of the oxherding sequence. Several Song monks produced such series; his ten stages, ending with an empty circle and a return to the marketplace, became the standard one.

The pictures and verses travelled to Japan, where painters such as Tenshō Shūbun copied them in the fifteenth century, and they remain one of the best-known teaching images of Zen.

## Key texts & works

### Ten Oxherding Pictures (Shiniu tu) (12th century)

[![Ten Oxherding Pictures (Shiniu tu)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a9/Shubun_-_looking_for_the_ox.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shubun_-_looking_for_the_ox.jpg)

<sub>Tenshō Shūbun (attributed), Searching for the Ox, from the Ten Oxherding Pictures, 15th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Shubun_-_looking_for_the_ox.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 12th century
- **Type:** Illustrated verse sequence
- **Where written / held:** Song China; best-known version painted by Tenshō Shūbun in 15th-century Japan (Shōkoku-ji, Kyoto)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Ten%20Oxherding%20Pictures%20%28Shiniu%20tu%29%20Kuoan%20Shiyuan)

Kuoan's sequence begins with a herdsman searching for a lost ox, the true mind, finding its tracks, glimpsing it, catching, taming and riding it home; then the ox is forgotten, both ox and self are forgotten in an empty circle, there is a return to the source, and finally the sage enters the marketplace 'with helping hands'. Its ending, where awakening returns to ordinary compassion, distinguishes it from earlier sequences that ended in emptiness. Painted by Shūbun and many others in Japan and still used in teaching, it is one of the most widely reproduced images of Zen.
