## Biography

Almost nothing is securely known about Laozi, the 'Old Master'. The historian Sima Qian, writing around 100 BC, already offered several identifications, the main one being Li Er, an archivist at the Zhou court and an older contemporary of Confucius who departed westward after writing a book in two parts. Most scholars now regard the Daodejing as a composite text of the fourth century BC. The philosophy section treats the text and its ideas.

In religious history Laozi became a god. By the later Han he received imperial sacrifice, and the Celestial Masters revered him as Lord Lao (Laojun), the Dao itself in personal form, who had revealed himself repeatedly through history, including to Zhang Daoling in AD 142. Scriptures attributed to him, such as the Classic of Purity and Stillness, multiplied, and the Tang emperors, surnamed Li, honoured him as their ancestor. He is worshipped today as one of the Three Pure Ones.

## Key texts & works

### Classic of Purity and Stillness (Qingjing jing) (c. 8th–9th century)

- **Date:** c. 8th–9th century
- **Type:** Daoist scripture
- **Where written / held:** Tang China; attributed by tradition to Lord Lao
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Classic%20of%20Purity%20and%20Stillness%20%28Qingjing%20jing%29%20Laozi)

The Qingjing jing, about 400 characters long, teaches that the Dao is formless and nameless and that the human spirit loves purity, but desire disturbs it; when desire is removed the mind becomes still and spirit returns to purity. Its language shows Buddhist influence from Madhyamaka and the Heart Sutra. Though framed as Laozi's teaching, it was composed in the Tang. It became one of the most widely recited Daoist texts, part of the daily liturgy of the Quanzhen order, and was the subject of many commentaries.
