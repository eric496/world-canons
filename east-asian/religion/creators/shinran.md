## Biography

Shinran trained for twenty years on Mount Hiei before becoming a disciple of Hōnen in 1201. Exiled to Echigo in 1207, he declared himself 'neither monk nor layman', married Eshinni and raised a family, then spent some twenty years teaching in the Kantō region before returning to Kyoto, where he wrote and corresponded until his death at ninety.

He taught that even the nenbutsu is not one's own effort but a gift of Amida, so that faith (shinjin) itself is given, and that the wicked are the very object of the vow. The philosophy section treats his thought. His descendants built the Honganji, which under Rennyo became Japan's largest Buddhist organisation; Jōdo Shinshū's married clergy set a pattern for Japanese Buddhism.

## Key texts & works

### Teaching, Practice, Faith and Realisation (Kyōgyōshinshō) (c. 1224–1250s)

[![Teaching, Practice, Faith and Realisation (Kyōgyōshinshō)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/41/Kyogyoshinsho.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kyogyoshinsho.jpg)

<sub>Shinran's autograph manuscript of the Kyōgyōshinshō (Bandō edition) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kyogyoshinsho.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 1224–1250s
- **Type:** Doctrinal anthology
- **Where written / held:** Inada, Hitachi Province, and Kyoto; autograph Bandō manuscript at Higashi Hongan-ji
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Teaching%2C%20Practice%2C%20Faith%20and%20Realisation%20%28Ky%C5%8Dgy%C5%8Dshinsh%C5%8D%29%20Shinran)

In six books on the true teaching (the Sutra of Infinite Life), practice (the name), faith (shinjin), realisation, the true Buddha and land, and the provisional and false, Shinran assembles quotations from sutras and the seven Pure Land patriarchs of India, China and Japan, interpreting them so that every element of salvation becomes the work of Amida's 'other power'. He wrote and revised it over decades; the date 1224 often taken as Jōdo Shinshū's founding comes from a passage within it. The hymn 'Shōshinge' from its second book is chanted daily by Shin Buddhists. Shinran's autograph copy survives.
