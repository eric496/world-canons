## Biography

Wonhyo set out twice for Tang China with his friend Uisang; on the second attempt, according to a famous story, he drank from what he thought was a gourd in the dark and found at dawn that it was a skull, realising that all things arise from the mind, and turned back. He wrote some eighty works of doctrinal commentary, treated in the philosophy section. He fathered a son, Seol Chong, with a royal princess, after which he lived as a layman.

As a religious figure Wonhyo is remembered for taking Buddhism into villages and markets, dancing and singing with a gourd and teaching people to call on Amitābha. His exhortation to practice and his Pure Land commentaries were read across East Asia. He is venerated as the founder of an ecumenical Korean Buddhism and appears in many temple legends.

## Key texts & works

### Arouse Your Mind and Practise (Balsim suhaeng jang) (7th century)

- **Date:** 7th century
- **Type:** Exhortation
- **Where written / held:** Silla
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Arouse%20Your%20Mind%20and%20Practise%20%28Balsim%20suhaeng%20jang%29%20Wonhyo)

In rhythmic Chinese prose, Wonhyo contrasts the buddhas, who abandoned desires and practised austerities in the mountains, with ordinary people who cling to wealth and pleasure, reminds the reader that life is short and death certain, and urges immediate practice and precepts. Attributed to Wonhyo by tradition, it was combined with texts by Jinul and Yaun into the Chobalsim jagyeongmun, the primer that Korean novices memorise to this day, so that it is among the most widely read Korean Buddhist texts.
