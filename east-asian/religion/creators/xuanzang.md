> Tang monk who journeyed to India for seventeen years and translated some 75 Buddhist texts, among them the most recited version of the Heart Sutra.

<sub>Portrait: Xuanzang carrying sutras, Japanese hanging scroll, Kamakura period (Tokyo National Museum) · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xuanzang_w.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Xuanzang left Chang'an in 629 in defiance of a ban on foreign travel, crossed Central Asia and studied at the great monastery of Nālandā under Śīlabhadra, mastering Yogācāra philosophy. He returned in 645 with 657 texts, relics and images and was received with honour by Emperor Taizong, who asked him to write an account of the western lands.

Until his death he directed a translation bureau in Chang'an, producing about 1,335 fascicles, including the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sutra; the Heart Sutra version attributed to him is chanted across East Asia, though its origin is debated. His disciple Kuiji founded the Faxiang (consciousness-only) school, carried to Japan as Hossō. His journey became the core of the novel Journey to the West, treated in the literature section.

### Great Tang Records on the Western Regions (Da Tang Xiyu ji) (646)

[![Great Tang Records on the Western Regions (Da Tang Xiyu ji)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/%E5%A4%A7%E5%94%90%E8%A5%BF%E5%9F%9F%E8%A8%98%EF%BC%88%E5%9B%9B%E5%BA%AB%EF%BC%89.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%A4%A7%E5%94%90%E8%A5%BF%E5%9F%9F%E8%A8%98%EF%BC%88%E5%9B%9B%E5%BA%AB%EF%BC%89.jpg)

- **Date:** 646
- **Type:** Travel record
- **Where written / held:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Great%20Tang%20Records%20on%20the%20Western%20Regions%20%28Da%20Tang%20Xiyu%20ji%29%20Xuanzang)

Written at the emperor's request after Xuanzang's return, the twelve-fascicle record describes, country by country, the geography, climate, people, rulers, customs, languages, monasteries, schools of Buddhism and sacred sites from the Tarim Basin to Afghanistan, India and Sri Lanka. It is careful and largely sober, with legends recorded as such. Nineteenth-century archaeologists, among them Alexander Cunningham, used it to identify Buddhist sites such as Sarnath, Kuśinagara and Nālandā. Its narrative, with that of the biography by his disciple Huili, was transformed into the fantasy of the novel Journey to the West.

### Heart Sutra (Bore boluomiduo xin jing) (649)

[![Heart Sutra (Bore boluomiduo xin jing)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/Falongsibeiye.png/1280px-Falongsibeiye.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Falongsibeiye.png)

<sub>Palm-leaf manuscript of the Sanskrit Heart Sutra preserved at Hōryū-ji, Japan Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Falongsibeiye.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 649
- **Type:** Mahāyāna scripture (translation)
- **Where written / held:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Heart%20Sutra%20%28Bore%20boluomiduo%20xin%20jing%29%20Xuanzang)

In about 260 Chinese characters, the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara explains to Śāriputra that the five aggregates are empty, that there is no arising or ceasing, and that through the perfection of wisdom all buddhas attain awakening; it ends with a mantra. The Chinese text traditionally ascribed to Xuanzang (649) is chanted in Chan, Zen, Seon and many other temples and copied as a devotional practice. Jan Nattier argued in 1992 that the sutra was first composed in Chinese from passages of Kumārajīva's Large Sutra and back-translated into Sanskrit, a thesis that remains debated. Sanskrit copies include the palm leaves of Hōryū-ji.
