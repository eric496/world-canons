## Biography

The earliest sources, from the sixth and seventh centuries, describe Bodhidharma as a foreign meditation teacher, perhaps from South India or Persia, active in northern China around 500, whose disciple Huike passed on his teaching. Later Chan histories added the stories now associated with him: an interview with Emperor Wu of Liang, nine years facing a wall at the Shaolin monastery, and Huike cutting off his arm to show his sincerity. Scholars treat these as legends developed to support Chan lineage claims.

A short text on the 'Two Entrances and Four Practices' preserved in Dunhuang manuscripts is the earliest teaching attributed to him. As Daruma he became one of the most recognisable figures in East Asian art and popular culture, from Zen ink paintings to the round, limbless Daruma dolls of Japan.

## Key texts & works

### Two Entrances and Four Practices (Erru sixing lun) (c. 6th century)

- **Date:** c. 6th century
- **Type:** Meditation text
- **Where written / held:** Northern China; survives in Dunhuang manuscripts and in the Xu gaoseng zhuan
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Two%20Entrances%20and%20Four%20Practices%20%28Erru%20sixing%20lun%29%20Bodhidharma)

Preserved with a preface by Bodhidharma's disciple Tanlin, the short text distinguishes entering the Way by principle, through 'wall contemplation' in which one realises that all beings share the one true nature, from entering by practice: accepting suffering as retribution, adapting to conditions, seeking nothing and acting in accord with the Dharma. Its attribution to Bodhidharma is accepted by many scholars as reflecting his circle, unlike much later Chan lore. Rediscovered at Dunhuang, it gives the most reliable glimpse of the earliest Chan movement.
