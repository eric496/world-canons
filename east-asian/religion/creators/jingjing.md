# Jingjing (Adam)

*景淨*

**fl. 781** · Persian or Chinese  
Born: Chang'an · Died:   
Traditions & eras: [Christianity in East Asia](../movements/christianity-east-asia.md)  

> Priest of the Church of the East in Tang Chang'an who composed the text of the Xi'an Stele of 781.

## Biography

Jingjing, whose Syriac name was Adam, was a priest, chorepiscopus and 'papash of China' of the Church of the East in the Tang capital. The stele he composed names him as author, and Buddhist sources record that he collaborated with the Indian monk Prajña on a translation of a Buddhist sutra, which the emperor rejected as mixing teachings.

His stele inscription tells how the monk Aluoben brought the 'Luminous Teaching' (Jingjiao) to Chang'an in 635 and was welcomed by Emperor Taizong, summarises Christian doctrine in Buddhist and Daoist vocabulary, and lists clergy in Chinese and Syriac. Several Chinese Christian texts found at Dunhuang are also associated with him.

## Key texts & works

### Xi'an Stele (Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin) (781)

[![Xi'an Stele (Stele of the Propagation of the Luminous Religion of Daqin)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Xian_Stele_%28detail%29.jpg/1280px-Xian_Stele_%28detail%29.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xian_Stele_%28detail%29.jpg)

<sub>Head of the Xi'an Stele with its cross and title, Forest of Steles Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Xian_Stele_%28detail%29.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 781
- **Type:** Inscribed stone stele
- **Where written / held:** Chang'an; now in the Forest of Steles Museum, Xi'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Xi%27an%20Stele%20%28Stele%20of%20the%20Propagation%20of%20the%20Luminous%20Religion%20of%20Daqin%29%20Jingjing%20%28Adam%29)

The black limestone stele, nearly three metres tall, has a cross above a lotus and clouds at its head. Its Chinese text summarises Christian teaching on the Trinity, creation, the fall, the incarnation and baptism in vocabulary borrowed from Buddhism and Daoism, then recounts how the monk Aluoben arrived in 635, how Emperor Taizong approved the faith and how later emperors supported it; Syriac lists name the clergy. Buried perhaps after the anti-foreign persecutions of 845, it was unearthed around 1625, when Jesuits hailed it as proof of Christianity's ancient roots in China, and its authenticity, once questioned in Europe, is now accepted. It is the main monument of the first Christian presence in East Asia.
