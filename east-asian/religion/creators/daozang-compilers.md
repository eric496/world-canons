# Ming compilers of the Daoist Canon

*正統道藏編纂者*

**1406 – 1445** · Chinese  
Born: Beijing · Died: Beijing  
Traditions & eras: [Inner alchemy, Quanzhen and the Daoist canon](../movements/later-daoism.md)  

> The Celestial Masters and court editors who assembled and printed the Zhengtong Daoist Canon in 1445.

## Biography

Earlier Daoist canons, compiled under the Tang, Song and Yuan, were largely lost, the last in a Yuan burning of Daoist books in 1281. In 1406 the Yongle emperor commissioned the forty-third Celestial Master, Zhang Yuchu, to compile a new canon; the work continued under his brother Zhang Yuqing and was completed and printed in 1445 under the Zhengtong emperor, with a supplement in 1607.

The editors arranged some 1,400 texts in the traditional Three Caverns and Four Supplements and had them cut on woodblocks in the format of Buddhist sutras. Their canon preserved most of what survives of early Daoist literature, from scriptures and liturgies to alchemy, medicine and philosophy, and it remains the basic source for the study of Daoism.

- **Date:** 1445 (supplement 1607)
- **Type:** Scriptural canon
- **Where written / held:** Beijing; woodblock print
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Zhengtong%20Daoist%20Canon%20%28Zhengtong%20Daozang%29%20Ming%20compilers%20of%20the%20Daoist%20Canon)

Commissioned in 1406 and printed in 1445, with a Wanli-period supplement of 1607, the Zhengtong canon contains about 1,500 titles in over 5,300 fascicles. It is arranged in the Three Caverns, Perfection (Shangqing), Mystery (Lingbao) and Spirit (Sanhuang), and four Supplements, a classification going back to Lu Xiujing, although the placement of many texts is inconsistent. It includes scriptures, liturgies, talismans, alchemical and medical works, hagiographies, temple gazetteers and philosophical classics such as the Daodejing and Zhuangzi. Few complete sets survived the Qing; photographic reprints from the 1920s made it available to modern scholarship, which has catalogued it in Kristofer Schipper and Franciscus Verellen's historical companion (2004).
