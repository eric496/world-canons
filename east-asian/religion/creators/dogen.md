## Biography

Born into the high aristocracy and orphaned young, Dōgen trained on Mount Hiei and at Kennin-ji, then went to China in 1223, where he received the Caodong transmission from Rujing at Mount Tiantong. He returned in 1227, taught near Kyoto, and in 1243 moved with his community to Echizen, where he founded Eihei-ji.

His thought, recorded in the Shōbōgenzō, is treated in the philosophy section. As a religious founder he set out rules for every part of monastic life, from the meditation hall to the kitchen and the toilet, treating ordinary work as practice. Sōtō, spread widely among rural people by his successors such as Keizan, is now Japan's largest Zen school.

### Treasury of the True Dharma Eye (Shōbōgenzō) (1231–1253)

- **Date:** 1231–1253
- **Type:** Collection of sermons and essays
- **Where written / held:** Kōshō-ji near Kyoto and Eihei-ji, Echizen
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Treasury%20of%20the%20True%20Dharma%20Eye%20%28Sh%C5%8Db%C5%8Dgenz%C5%8D%29%20D%C5%8Dgen)

Dōgen wrote the 75 and 12 fascicles of the Shōbōgenzō over two decades, in a dense, inventive Japanese full of quotations of Chinese kōans. Beside essays such as 'Being-Time' and 'Genjō kōan', many fascicles prescribe practice: how to sit, how to wash the face, how to wear the robe, how to receive the precepts, on the merits of leaving home. For the religious history of Sōtō it was long a treasure guarded by the monasteries and hardly read; the 95-fascicle printed edition of 1796–1815 and twentieth-century philosophers made it famous. The Shushōgi, an anthology from it compiled in 1890, became the standard Sōtō lay liturgy.

### Instructions for the Cook (Tenzo kyōkun) (1237)

- **Date:** 1237
- **Type:** Monastic instructions
- **Where written / held:** Kōshō-ji, near Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Instructions%20for%20the%20Cook%20%28Tenzo%20ky%C5%8Dkun%29%20D%C5%8Dgen)

Dōgen recalls how, on arriving in China in 1223, he met an old monastery cook who had walked miles to buy mushrooms and told him that cooking was his practice, and another drying seaweed in the midday sun who would not leave the task to others. He then sets out the cook's duties: planning meals with the officers, washing rice, handling each ingredient with care, and cultivating 'joyful mind, nurturing mind and great mind'. Later included in the Eihei shingi, Dōgen's monastic code, it remains a guide for Zen kitchens and a popular text in the modern world.
