# Kyōkai

*景戒*

**fl. c. 787–822** · Japanese  
Born: Kii Province (probably) · Died:   
Traditions & eras: [Asuka and Nara Buddhism](../movements/nara-state-buddhism.md)  

> Monk of Yakushi-ji who compiled the Nihon ryōiki, Japan's first collection of Buddhist miracle tales.

## Biography

Kyōkai (also read Keikai) is known only from his own book, in which he describes himself as a monk of Yakushi-ji in Nara who had once lived as a layman with a family and in poverty. He records a dream in 787 and finished his collection in the early ninth century.

His Nihon ryōiki gathers 116 stories, set mostly in the eighth century, of karmic reward and retribution, miraculous images, and wandering holy men, including the mountain ascetic En no Gyōja later revered by Shugendō. It is the first great source for Buddhism among ordinary people in Japan and the ancestor of later Japanese tale literature.

## Key texts & works

### Miraculous Tales of Japan (Nihon ryōiki) (c. 822)

[![Miraculous Tales of Japan (Nihon ryōiki)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/de/Nihon_Ryoiki_Kofukuji.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nihon_Ryoiki_Kofukuji.jpg)

<sub>Kōfuku-ji manuscript of the Nihon ryōiki Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nihon_Ryoiki_Kofukuji.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 822
- **Type:** Buddhist tale collection
- **Where written / held:** Nara; oldest manuscripts at Kōfuku-ji and Raigō-in
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Miraculous%20Tales%20of%20Japan%20%28Nihon%20ry%C5%8Diki%29%20Ky%C5%8Dkai)

The full title, Nihonkoku genpō zen'aku ryōiki, means 'records of miraculous karmic retribution of good and evil in Japan'. Written in Chinese with Japanese elements, the tales, arranged chronologically from the fifth to the early ninth century, tell of people reborn as oxen for unpaid debts, hunters punished, images that bleed or speak, women saved by devotion to Kannon, the ascetic En no Gyōja flying over mountains, and the monk Gyōki teaching the people. Kyōkai aimed to prove that karma operates in Japan as in China and India. The collection shaped later setsuwa literature such as the Konjaku monogatarishū and is a basic source for early Japanese popular religion.
