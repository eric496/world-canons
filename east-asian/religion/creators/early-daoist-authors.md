# Anonymous Han–Jin Daoist authors

*漢晉道經作者*

**c. 100 – c. 400** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: China  
Traditions & eras: [Celestial Masters and early religious Daoism](../movements/early-daoism.md)  

> The unnamed authors and revealers behind early Daoist scriptures such as the Taiping jing and the Yellow Court Scripture.

## Biography

Many of the founding scriptures of Daoism were presented as revelations from gods or immortals rather than as human compositions. The Scripture of Great Peace (Taiping jing) was said to have been received by Gan Ji or Yu Ji in the Later Han, and the Yellow Court Scripture (Huangting jing) is linked by tradition to the Shangqing matriarch Lady Wei Huacun (252–334). Modern scholars see these texts as layered works produced by circles of practitioners over generations.

This entry groups those authors honestly rather than assigning the texts to legendary figures. Their writings set out visions of a just world order, of the body as a landscape inhabited by gods, and of meditation, breathing and visualisation as ways to longevity and transcendence, themes that run through all later Daoism.

### Scripture of Great Peace (Taiping jing) (2nd century and later)

[![Scripture of Great Peace (Taiping jing)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Daozang_edition_of_Taipingjing.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daozang_edition_of_Taipingjing.png)

<sub>Opening of the Taiping jing in the Ming Daoist Canon Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Daozang_edition_of_Taipingjing.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 2nd century and later
- **Type:** Daoist scripture
- **Where written / held:** China; survives in the Ming Daoist Canon in 57 of 170 chapters
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Scripture%20of%20Great%20Peace%20%28Taiping%20jing%29%20Anonymous%20Han%E2%80%93Jin%20Daoist%20authors)

Texts called Taiping jing were presented to the Han court in the second century, said to have been revealed to Gan Ji or Yu Ji. The surviving version, partly of later date, takes the form of dialogues between a Celestial Master and his disciples on how to restore cosmic balance: through the cooperation of Heaven, Earth and humanity, the confession of faults, care for the body's spirits and the 'inherited burden' (chengfu) that accumulated wrongdoing places on later generations. The Yellow Turbans, who rose in 184 under Zhang Jue proclaiming a 'Way of Great Peace', drew on related ideas. It is a major source for Han religious thought and social criticism.

### Yellow Court Scripture (Huangting jing) (c. 3rd–4th century)

[![Yellow Court Scripture (Huangting jing)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fd/Wang_Xizhi_Huang_Ting_Jing.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wang_Xizhi_Huang_Ting_Jing.jpg)

<sub>Rubbing of the Yellow Court Scripture in small regular script, attributed to Wang Xizhi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wang_Xizhi_Huang_Ting_Jing.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 3rd–4th century
- **Type:** Daoist meditation scripture
- **Where written / held:** China; transmitted in the Shangqing tradition
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Yellow%20Court%20Scripture%20%28Huangting%20jing%29%20Anonymous%20Han%E2%80%93Jin%20Daoist%20authors)

The Huangting jing exists in an 'Outer' and an 'Inner' version, in seven-character verse. It describes the body as a landscape of palaces and gods, centred on the Yellow Court in the abdomen and the cinnabar fields, and instructs the adept to visualise these deities, circulate breath and saliva and preserve essence. The Inner version is associated with the Shangqing revelations and with Lady Wei Huacun; the Outer is perhaps earlier. A famous calligraphy version was attributed to Wang Xizhi, and the text was recited by Daoists, studied by physicians and quoted by poets. It shaped Daoist inner cultivation through to modern qigong.
