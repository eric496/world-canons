## Biography

Ricci entered the Jesuits in Rome, studied mathematics under Christopher Clavius, and reached Macau in 1582. With Michele Ruggieri he settled at Zhaoqing in 1583, first dressing as a Buddhist monk and later, from 1595, as a Confucian literatus. He gained admiration for his world map, clocks and memory techniques and in 1601 obtained permission to reside in Beijing, where he died.

Ricci argued that ancient Confucianism had known the one God, called Shangdi, and that ancestor and Confucius rites were civil rather than religious, a position that later caused the Chinese Rites controversy and was condemned by Rome in 1704 and 1742. With converts such as Xu Guangqi he translated Euclid. His catechism and his journals, published in Latin by Nicolas Trigault, shaped both Chinese Christianity and Europe's image of China.

## Key texts & works

### The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven (Tianzhu shiyi) (1603)

- **Date:** 1603
- **Type:** Catechetical dialogue
- **Where written / held:** Beijing
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20True%20Meaning%20of%20the%20Lord%20of%20Heaven%20%28Tianzhu%20shiyi%29%20Matteo%20Ricci)

Written in classical Chinese after years of discussion with literati, the book argues by natural reason, quoting the Confucian classics, that there is one creator, the Lord of Heaven, identical with the Shangdi of the ancient texts; that the human soul is immortal and distinct from animals; and that Buddhist emptiness and Neo-Confucian principle cannot be the source of all things. It says little about Christ, leaving revelation for later instruction. It was widely read by scholars, included in the Siku quanshu catalogue of the Qing, and carried to Korea, where it inspired the first Catholic converts, and to Japan and Vietnam. Its method of accommodation was at the heart of the Chinese Rites controversy.
