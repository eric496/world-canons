# Authors of morality books

*善書作者*

**12th century – 19th century** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: China  
Traditions & eras: [Chinese popular and sectarian religion](../movements/popular-religion.md)  

> The mostly anonymous lay authors, editors and sponsors of shanshu, the popular Chinese 'books that exhort to goodness'.

## Biography

Morality books are short works, usually distributed free by donors as an act of merit, that teach how deeds are rewarded and punished by the gods, in this life and after death. The first great example, the Treatise on Action and Response, appeared in the Southern Song and was attributed to Lord Lao; later texts were revealed through spirit-writing, compiled by local literati, or attributed to gods such as Guandi and Wenchang.

Their authors drew freely on Confucian ethics, Buddhist karma and Daoist accounting of merit, and the books were reprinted in vast numbers in the Ming and Qing and are still handed out in temples in Taiwan and Southeast Asia. Historians treat them as among the most widely read Chinese texts and a key source for everyday morality.

### Treatise of the Most High on Action and Response (Taishang ganying pian) (12th century)

- **Date:** 12th century
- **Type:** Morality book
- **Where written / held:** Southern Song China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Treatise%20of%20the%20Most%20High%20on%20Action%20and%20Response%20%28Taishang%20ganying%20pian%29%20Authors%20of%20morality%20books)

The Ganying pian opens by declaring that fortune and misfortune have no gate but are summoned by people themselves, then lists several hundred good and bad actions, from filial piety and kindness to animals to cheating in trade, and explains that the Director of Destiny, the spirits of the body and the Kitchen God report them to Heaven and shorten or lengthen life accordingly. First mentioned in the twelfth century and printed with commentary by Li Changling, it was endorsed by emperors and literati and became a staple of free distribution. It shows the blending of Daoist, Buddhist and Confucian ethics in popular religion.

- **Date:** c. 18th–19th century
- **Type:** Illustrated morality book
- **Where written / held:** Qing China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jade%20Record%20%28Yuli%20baochao%29%20Authors%20of%20morality%20books)

The Yuli presents itself as a revelation of the infernal judges, transmitted through a monk in the Song, but scholars date it to the Qing. It describes the ten courts of the underworld, each ruled by a king, where the dead are judged, punished for specific sins in vivid detail, and finally given the soup of forgetfulness before rebirth, and it promises remission through good deeds, vegetarianism and spreading the book itself. Widely reprinted with woodcut illustrations, it shaped popular images of the afterlife, temple murals and Ghost Festival displays across the Chinese world.
