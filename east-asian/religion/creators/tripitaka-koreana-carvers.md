# Carvers of the Tripitaka Koreana

*팔만대장경 판각자*

**1236 – 1251** · Korean  
Born: Ganghwa Island and Namhae, Goryeo · Died: Goryeo  
Traditions & eras: [Buddhism in Silla and Goryeo](../movements/korean-buddhism.md)  

> The monks, scholars and craftsmen who carved the entire Buddhist canon onto over 81,000 woodblocks during the Mongol invasions.

## Biography

The first Goryeo canon, carved from 1011 to seek protection against Khitan invasions, was burned by the Mongols in 1232. The Goryeo court, in refuge on Ganghwa Island, set up a Directorate for the Canon in 1236, with a branch on Namhae; the monk Sugi led the editing, comparing the Song Kaibao, Khitan and first Goryeo editions. Hundreds of carvers, whose names are sometimes recorded on the blocks, cut the text by 1251.

The 81,258 blocks, on which some 52 million characters are carved with remarkably few errors, were moved in 1398 to Haeinsa, where they are still kept in fifteenth-century storage halls designed for ventilation. Their critical edition served as the base text of the modern Japanese Taishō canon. The blocks and halls are on UNESCO's lists.

## Key texts & works

### Tripitaka Koreana (Palman daejanggyeong) (1237–1251)

[![Tripitaka Koreana (Palman daejanggyeong)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Tripitaka_Koreana_02.jpg/1280px-Tripitaka_Koreana_02.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tripitaka_Koreana_02.jpg)

<sub>Woodblocks of the Tripitaka Koreana at Haeinsa Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tripitaka_Koreana_02.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1237–1251
- **Type:** Woodblock Buddhist canon
- **Where written / held:** Haeinsa, South Gyeongsang Province
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Tripitaka%20Koreana%20%28Palman%20daejanggyeong%29%20Carvers%20of%20the%20Tripitaka%20Koreana)

Begun to win the Buddha's protection against the Mongols after the first Goryeo canon was burned in 1232, the canon comprises 1,516 titles in some 6,800 fascicles. The blocks, of birch and other woods seasoned in seawater and coated with lacquer, carry 23 lines of 14 characters per side and are reinforced at the ends with metal. The editor Sugi collated earlier Chinese and Korean editions and recorded his corrections in a separate work. Since 1398 the blocks have been stored at Haeinsa in the Janggyeong panjeon halls, designed with slatted windows and charcoal-laced floors to control humidity. Prints from them were sought by Japanese rulers in the Joseon era, and they underlie the modern Taishō canon. Both the blocks and the halls are UNESCO heritage.
