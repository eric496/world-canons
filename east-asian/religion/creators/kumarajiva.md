# Kumārajīva

*鳩摩羅什*

**344 – 413** · Kuchean  
Born: Kucha, Tarim Basin · Died: Chang'an  
Traditions & eras: [Buddhism's arrival and the translators](../movements/buddhism-arrives.md)  

> Kuchean monk whose translations of the Lotus, Diamond and Vimalakīrti sutras became the standard Chinese Buddhist scriptures.

## Biography

Kumārajīva was born in the Silk Road kingdom of Kucha to an Indian father and a Kuchean princess, became a monk as a child, and studied in Kashmir and Kashgar, turning from mainstream Buddhism to Mahāyāna. When Kucha fell to a Chinese army in 384 he was carried off and held for seventeen years in Liangzhou, where he learned Chinese. In 401 the Later Qin ruler Yao Xing brought him to Chang'an and set up a translation bureau with hundreds of monk-assistants.

Over a dozen years the team produced around 35 works in some 300 fascicles, including the Lotus Sutra, Diamond Sutra, Vimalakīrti Sutra, the Amitābha Sutra and the Madhyamaka treatises of Nāgārjuna. His fluent, readable versions displaced earlier ones and are still the texts recited across East Asia. Sources record that he was compelled to take wives, a matter on which Buddhist biographers were uneasy; his disciples Sengzhao and Daosheng laid the foundations of Chinese Buddhist thought.

### Amitābha Sutra (Amituo jing) (402)

- **Date:** 402
- **Type:** Mahāyāna scripture (translation)
- **Where written / held:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Amit%C4%81bha%20Sutra%20%28Amituo%20jing%29%20Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva)

The Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra describes the jewelled trees, lotus ponds and singing birds of Sukhāvatī, the Land of Bliss, and states that anyone who single-mindedly holds the name of Amitābha for one to seven days will be met by him at death. Kumārajīva's translation became, with the Larger Sutra of Infinite Life and the Contemplation Sutra, one of the three Pure Land scriptures, and it is chanted daily in Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Vietnamese temples. Shandao reputedly copied it many thousands of times.

### Lotus Sutra (Miaofa lianhua jing), Kumārajīva's translation (406)

- **Date:** 406
- **Type:** Mahāyāna scripture (translation)
- **Where written / held:** Chang'an; Dunhuang manuscripts in the British Library and elsewhere
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Lotus%20Sutra%20%28Miaofa%20lianhua%20jing%29%2C%20Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva%27s%20translation%20Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva)

The Saddharmapuṇḍarīka Sūtra, composed in India around the first and second centuries AD, presents the Buddha revealing that his earlier teachings were skilful means and that all beings are destined for buddhahood through a single vehicle. Its parables, the burning house, the prodigal son, the phantom city, and its chapter on the bodhisattva Guanyin (Avalokiteśvara), who saves all who call on him, became part of East Asian culture. Kumārajīva's translation of 406 superseded that of Dharmarakṣa (286). It was the central scripture of Tiantai and Tendai, of Nichiren and his successors, and of lay movements such as Sōka Gakkai and Risshō Kōseikai; copying, reciting and illustrating it were acts of merit.

### Vimalakīrti Sutra (Weimojie jing) (406)

[![Vimalakīrti Sutra (Weimojie jing)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/Vimalakirti_debating_Manjusri%2C_Tang_Dynasty.jpg/1280px-Vimalakirti_debating_Manjusri%2C_Tang_Dynasty.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vimalakirti_debating_Manjusri%2C_Tang_Dynasty.jpg)

<sub>Vimalakīrti debating Mañjuśrī, Tang-dynasty painting Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Vimalakirti_debating_Manjusri%2C_Tang_Dynasty.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 406
- **Type:** Mahāyāna scripture (translation)
- **Where written / held:** Chang'an
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Vimalak%C4%ABrti%20Sutra%20%28Weimojie%20jing%29%20Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva)

The Vimalakīrtinirdeśa tells how the wealthy householder Vimalakīrti of Vaiśālī feigns illness, and the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī leads the Buddha's disciples to visit him. In their dialogue he overturns their understanding of purity, gender and attainment, and when asked how to enter non-duality he answers by remaining silent. Kumārajīva's elegant translation made it a favourite of Chinese scholars and aristocrats, who saw in Vimalakīrti a model of enlightened life in the world, and the debate scene was painted in many Dunhuang caves. Prince Shōtoku's commentary on it is one of the earliest Japanese Buddhist writings.

### Diamond Sutra, printed copy of 868 (868 (translation 402))

[![Diamond Sutra, printed copy of 868](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Diamond_Sutra_of_868_AD_-_The_Diamond_Sutra_%28868%29%2C_frontispiece_and_text_-_BL_Or._8210-P.2.jpg/1280px-Diamond_Sutra_of_868_AD_-_The_Diamond_Sutra_%28868%29%2C_frontispiece_and_text_-_BL_Or._8210-P.2.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Diamond_Sutra_of_868_AD_-_The_Diamond_Sutra_%28868%29%2C_frontispiece_and_text_-_BL_Or._8210-P.2.jpg)

<sub>Frontispiece and opening text of the Diamond Sutra of 868 (British Library) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Diamond_Sutra_of_868_AD_-_The_Diamond_Sutra_%28868%29%2C_frontispiece_and_text_-_BL_Or._8210-P.2.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 868 (translation 402)
- **Type:** Printed scroll of a Mahāyāna scripture
- **Where written / held:** Found in the Library Cave, Dunhuang; British Library, Or.8210/P.2
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Diamond%20Sutra%2C%20printed%20copy%20of%20868%20Kum%C4%81raj%C4%ABva)

The Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā, a short Perfection of Wisdom scripture in which the Buddha teaches Subhūti that all phenomena are empty and that a bodhisattva liberates beings without the notion of beings, was translated by Kumārajīva in about 402 and became one of the most recited texts in East Asia; Huineng is said to have awakened on hearing it. The Dunhuang copy is a scroll over five metres long, printed from seven woodblocks with a frontispiece of the Buddha preaching, and its colophon records that Wang Jie had it made on the fifteenth day of the fourth month of the ninth year of Xiantong (868) on behalf of his parents. Taken from Dunhuang by Aurel Stein in 1907, it is a landmark of the history of printing as well as of piety.
