# Prince Toneri

*舎人親王*

**676 – 735** · Japanese  
Born: Asuka · Died: Nara  
Traditions & eras: [Kami worship and Shinto](../movements/shinto.md)  
Also in: [History](../../history/creators/prince-toneri.md)  

> Imperial prince who presented the Nihon Shoki, Japan's first official history and a basic text of Shinto myth, to the throne in 720.

## Biography

Prince Toneri was a son of Emperor Tenmu, who had ordered the compilation of national histories in 681. He held high office at the Nara court and was the father of the later Emperor Junnin. The Shoku Nihongi records that in 720 he presented the Nihon Shoki in thirty scrolls with a genealogy.

He headed a team of court scholars who wrote in classical Chinese and drew on Chinese histories, clan records and Korean sources. The work begins with the age of the gods and the descent of the imperial line from Amaterasu, so that his name is attached to one of the two founding texts of Shinto mythology.

## Key texts & works

### Chronicles of Japan (Nihon Shoki) (720)

- **Date:** 720
- **Type:** Official history and myth
- **Where written / held:** Nara; earliest fragments 9th century, Tanaka manuscript (Nara National Museum)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Chronicles%20of%20Japan%20%28Nihon%20Shoki%29%20Prince%20Toneri)

Presented to the throne in 720 by Prince Toneri, the Nihon Shoki was written in classical Chinese on the model of Chinese dynastic histories. Its first two books tell of the creation of the islands by Izanagi and Izanami, the sun goddess Amaterasu and her brother Susanoo, and the descent of her grandson to rule Japan, giving many variant versions ('in one writing it says…'); the rest narrate reigns from the legendary first emperor Jimmu to Empress Jitō in 697, including the official introduction of Buddhism from Baekje in 552. The parallel Kojiki of 712, a literary classic, is treated in the literature section. The Nihon Shoki was lectured on at court from 721, was the object of medieval Shinto commentaries, and was used in the Meiji period to support emperor worship; historians treat its early chronology as legendary.
