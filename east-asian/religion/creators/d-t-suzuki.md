# D. T. Suzuki

*鈴木大拙*

**1870 – 1966** · Japanese  
Born: Kanazawa, Ishikawa · Died: Tokyo  
Traditions & eras: [Religion and modernity](../movements/modern-religion.md)  

> Japanese scholar whose English books made Zen known to the Western world in the twentieth century.

## Biography

Suzuki Daisetsu Teitarō studied Zen at Engaku-ji under Shaku Sōen and in 1897 went to the United States, where he worked for eleven years with the publisher Paul Carus. Back in Japan he taught at Otani University and wrote in English and Japanese on Zen, Pure Land and Mahāyāna, then lectured widely in America and Europe after 1950.

His Essays in Zen Buddhism and later books presented Zen as an experience of awakening at the heart of all religion and of Japanese culture, influencing writers, artists and psychologists from Thomas Merton to John Cage. Later scholars have criticised his picture as romanticised and entangled with Japanese nationalism, but his role in the global reception of Zen is undisputed.

## Key texts & works

### Essays in Zen Buddhism (First Series) (1927)

- **Date:** 1927
- **Type:** Study of Zen in English
- **Where written / held:** London (Luzac and Company)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Essays%20in%20Zen%20Buddhism%20%28First%20Series%29%20D.%20T.%20Suzuki)

In this collection, followed by second and third series in 1933 and 1934, Suzuki introduced the history of Chan and Zen, the meaning of satori, the kōan exercise and the Zen monastery, drawing on Chinese sources he was among the first to study, including Dunhuang texts. He presented Zen as the essence of Buddhism and of Eastern spirituality, beyond doctrine and ritual. The books shaped the understanding of Zen among Western intellectuals, artists and the 1950s counterculture. Scholars since the 1980s have questioned his emphasis on experience and his links to nationalist ideas, while recognising his pioneering role. The text is still under copyright.
