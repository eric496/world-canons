## Biography

Kūkai left the state academy to wander as a Buddhist ascetic and in 804 sailed to Tang China, where in Chang'an the master Huiguo initiated him into the esoteric lineages of the Womb and Diamond Realms shortly before his death. Returning in 806 with scriptures, mandalas and ritual implements, he became a favourite of Emperor Saga, received Tō-ji in Kyoto, and in 816 founded a monastery on Mount Kōya.

His teaching that one can become a buddha in this very body through the 'three mysteries' of body, speech and mind is treated in the philosophy section. As a religious figure he became the object of a vast cult: pilgrims believe he did not die but remains in eternal meditation in his mausoleum at Kōya, and the 88-temple Shikoku pilgrimage follows his legendary footsteps. He was also a famed calligrapher and is credited by legend with many feats.

### Catalogue of Imported Items (Go-shōrai mokuroku) (806)

[![Catalogue of Imported Items (Go-shōrai mokuroku)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d7/Catalogue_of_Imported_Items.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Catalogue_of_Imported_Items.png)

<sub>Kūkai's Catalogue of Imported Items, Tō-ji copy Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Catalogue_of_Imported_Items.png).</sub>

- **Date:** 806
- **Type:** Catalogue and report
- **Where written / held:** Dazaifu; copy attributed to Saichō at Tō-ji, Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Catalogue%20of%20Imported%20Items%20%28Go-sh%C5%8Drai%20mokuroku%29%20K%C5%ABkai)

Writing from Dazaifu in Kyushu after returning early from China, Kūkai listed 216 works in 461 fascicles, many newly translated by Amoghavajra, as well as mandalas and portraits of patriarchs, ritual implements and relics, all received from his master Huiguo. He explained that the esoteric teaching was the swiftest path to buddhahood and described his initiation. The catalogue was meant to justify his early return and win court support, which it did after a delay. A copy traditionally attributed to Saichō, who borrowed texts from Kūkai, is a National Treasure at Tō-ji.

### Attaining Enlightenment in This Very Body (Sokushin jōbutsu gi) (early 9th century)

- **Date:** early 9th century
- **Type:** Doctrinal treatise
- **Where written / held:** Kyoto or Mount Kōya
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Attaining%20Enlightenment%20in%20This%20Very%20Body%20%28Sokushin%20j%C5%8Dbutsu%20gi%29%20K%C5%ABkai)

Kūkai's treatise interprets a two-stanza verse to show that the six elements, earth, water, fire, wind, space and consciousness, that make up all things are the body of Mahāvairocana, that the four kinds of mandala express it, and that through the 'three mysteries', mudrā, mantra and meditative union, the practitioner's body, speech and mind become one with the Buddha's. For the history of religion it is the charter of Shingon practice and of the wider Japanese idea that buddhahood is immediate. The cult of Kūkai as one who entered eternal meditation in his body at Kōya made this doctrine visible to pilgrims.

### Mandalas of the Two Realms (Ryōkai mandara) (806 (brought from China); Takao Mandala c. 829–833)

[![Mandalas of the Two Realms (Ryōkai mandara)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a2/Taizokai.jpg/1280px-Taizokai.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taizokai.jpg)

<sub>The Womb Realm (Taizōkai) mandala, Tō-ji, Kyoto Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taizokai.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 806 (brought from China); Takao Mandala c. 829–833
- **Type:** Esoteric mandala pair
- **Where written / held:** Tō-ji and Jingo-ji, Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mandalas%20of%20the%20Two%20Realms%20%28Ry%C5%8Dkai%20mandara%29%20K%C5%ABkai)

The Womb Realm (Taizōkai) mandala, based on the Mahāvairocana Sutra, shows Mahāvairocana at the centre of an eight-petalled lotus surrounded by concentric courts of buddhas, bodhisattvas and guardians, expressing the principle of compassion; the Diamond Realm (Kongōkai), based on the Vajraśekhara texts, arranges nine assemblies in a grid expressing wisdom. Kūkai brought a pair painted in Chang'an under Huiguo's direction; the Takao Mandala at Jingo-ji, in gold and silver on purple silk, is the oldest surviving Japanese version. In Shingon ritual the mandalas are used for initiation, where the initiate throws a flower onto the mandala to find a personal deity, and for meditation.
