# Shang royal diviners

*商代貞人*

**c. 1250 BC – c. 1046 BC** · Chinese  
Born: Anyang, Henan · Died: Anyang, Henan  
Traditions & eras: [Shang and Zhou religion](../movements/shang-zhou-religion.md)  

> The diviners of the late Shang court, whose questions to the ancestors and spirits, carved on bone and shell, are the oldest Chinese writing.

## Biography

At the late Shang capital near modern Anyang, from about 1250 BC until the Zhou conquest, the king and a staff of named diviners put questions to the royal ancestors, nature powers and the high god Di. They applied heat to prepared ox shoulder blades and turtle plastrons, read the cracks, and often carved the question, the forecast and sometimes the outcome beside them. Over a hundred names of diviners are known, grouped by scholars into chronological 'groups' that help date the inscriptions.

The inscriptions reveal a religion of exchange: the living fed the dead with sacrifices of cattle, grain, wine and sometimes human captives, and the ancestors in turn affected harvests, war, illness and childbirth. Rediscovered only in 1899, when 'dragon bones' sold as medicine were recognised as inscribed, the oracle bones confirmed the Shang king lists recorded a thousand years later and remain the foundation for the study of early Chinese religion and writing.

- **Date:** c. 1250–1046 BC
- **Type:** Divination records
- **Where written / held:** Yinxu, Anyang, Henan; now in many collections, notably the Institute of History and Philology, Taipei, and the National Library of China
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Oracle%20bone%20inscriptions%20Shang%20royal%20diviners)

Some 150,000 inscribed fragments of ox scapulae and turtle plastrons have been recovered from the late Shang capital at Anyang since 1899. Diviners drilled hollows in the prepared bone, applied a hot point to produce cracks, interpreted them as answers from the ancestors or the high god Di, and carved the charge, often in paired positive and negative forms, with the king's forecast and sometimes the verification. They ask about sacrifices owed to particular ancestors, harvests, rain, hunts, campaigns, childbirth, dreams and toothache. The inscriptions show a carefully scheduled cycle of ancestral offerings and reveal the scale of Shang sacrifice, including human victims. They are the foundation of the history of Chinese religion and writing.
