# Compilers of the Book of Rites

*禮記編者*

**c. 400 BC – c. 50 BC** · Chinese  
Born: China · Died: China  
Traditions & eras: [Confucian ritual and the state cult](../movements/confucian-ritual.md)  
Also in: [Philosophy](../../philosophy/creators/liji-compilers.md)  

> The anonymous Confucian ritual specialists whose essays on mourning, sacrifice and ceremony were gathered into the Book of Rites in the Han dynasty.

## Biography

The Book of Rites (Liji) is a compilation of forty-nine chapters written by many hands, mostly between the late Warring States period and the Former Han. Tradition credits its arrangement to the Han scholar Dai Sheng, a nephew of Dai De, in the first century BC. Its authors were the ru, specialists in ritual and the classics who served as masters of ceremonies at funerals and sacrifices and who looked back to Confucius and his disciples.

The compilers recorded the practical rules and the meaning of ritual: degrees of mourning, the ancestral temple, sacrifices to Heaven and Earth, the calendar of state observances. Their collection became one of the Five Classics and, through the Great Learning and Doctrine of the Mean, also fed Neo-Confucian philosophy. They are treated as a group in the philosophy section too.

- **Date:** c. 300 BC – 1st century BC
- **Type:** Ritual classic
- **Where written / held:** Compiled in the Former Han, traditionally by Dai Sheng
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Book%20of%20Rites%20%28Liji%29%20Compilers%20of%20the%20Book%20of%20Rites)

The Liji's forty-nine chapters mix detailed rules with essays explaining why rites matter. For the history of religion its most important parts deal with mourning grades and periods, the ancestral temple and its tablets, the sacrifices to Heaven, Earth, mountains and rivers, the seasonal ordinances of the Monthly Commands, and the 'Meaning of Sacrifices', which describes how the son fasts and visualises his dead parents until he seems to see and hear them. It became one of the Five Classics, the basis for state and family ritual in China, Korea, Vietnam and Japan, and was commented on by Zheng Xuan and Kong Yingda. The philosophy section treats it, and the Great Learning and Doctrine of the Mean drawn from it, as philosophy.
