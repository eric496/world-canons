> Monk of Mount Lu, honoured as the first patriarch of Chinese Pure Land, who defended the independence of the monastic order from the throne.

<sub>Portrait: Huiyuan, later Chinese portrait · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hui_Yuan.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Huiyuan was trained in Confucian and Daoist learning before becoming a disciple of the great monk Dao'an. Around 380 he settled at the Donglin monastery on Mount Lu, where he lived for over thirty years without, it was said, ever crossing the Tiger Brook below the monastery. He corresponded with Kumārajīva on doctrine and welcomed visiting translators.

In 402 he and 123 lay and monastic followers vowed before an image of Amitābha to be reborn together in the Pure Land, an event later remembered as the founding of the White Lotus Society and of Pure Land Buddhism, although his practice was a meditative visualisation rather than the later recitation of the name. His treatise arguing that monks need not bow to rulers set the terms of church-state debate in China.

## Key texts & works

### A Monk Does Not Bow Before a King (Shamen bujing wangzhe lun) (404)

- **Date:** 404
- **Type:** Treatise
- **Where written / held:** Mount Lu, Jiangxi
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=A%20Monk%20Does%20Not%20Bow%20Before%20a%20King%20%28Shamen%20bujing%20wangzhe%20lun%29%20Huiyuan)

When the warlord Huan Xuan demanded in 402 that monks bow to the ruler, as all subjects did, Huiyuan replied in five essays. He argued that laymen owe obedience to the ruler, but monks have left the household and pursue a goal beyond this world, so that their non-conformity actually serves the ruler's ends; he also argued that the spirit does not perish with the body. Huan Xuan withdrew the order. The question was raised again many times under later dynasties, and the treatise is the classic expression of the claim to monastic autonomy in a Confucian empire.
