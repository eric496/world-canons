## Biography

Kanetomo came from the Urabe family, hereditary diviners at the Office of Kami Affairs and priests of the Yoshida shrine in Kyoto. During and after the Ōnin War, which devastated the capital, he built in 1484 the Daigengū, an octagonal hall enshrining all the kami of Japan, and claimed, on doubtful grounds, that the sacred objects of Ise had flown there.

His Essentials of Yuiitsu Shinto taught a 'primal' Shinto that preceded and contained Buddhism and Confucianism, inverting the medieval view that kami were local manifestations of buddhas. His family gained the right to license shrine priests and grant ranks to kami, which they held through the Edo period, making Yoshida Shinto the first Shinto institution of national reach.

## Key texts & works

### Essentials of Yuiitsu Shinto (Yuiitsu Shintō myōbō yōshū) (late 15th century)

- **Date:** late 15th century
- **Type:** Shinto doctrinal treatise
- **Where written / held:** Yoshida shrine, Kyoto
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Essentials%20of%20Yuiitsu%20Shinto%20%28Yuiitsu%20Shint%C5%8D%20my%C5%8Db%C5%8D%20y%C5%8Dsh%C5%AB%29%20Yoshida%20Kanetomo)

Framed as a dialogue and attributed by Kanetomo to his remote ancestor Urabe Kanenobu, the treatise distinguishes three kinds of Shinto: that combined with Buddhism, that based on shrine origin legends, and the 'primordial' Yuiitsu Shinto transmitted secretly in the Urabe-Yoshida line. It describes esoteric rites modelled on Shingon ones and quotes a saying attributed to Prince Shōtoku that Shinto is the root, Confucianism the branches and Buddhism the fruit. Though based on invented authorities, it gave Shinto an independent doctrine and liturgy, and the Yoshida house used it to dominate shrine priesthood until the Meiji Restoration.
