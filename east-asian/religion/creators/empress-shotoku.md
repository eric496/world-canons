## Biography

The daughter of Emperor Shōmu, builder of the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji, she reigned as Empress Kōken from 749 to 758, abdicated, and returned to the throne as Empress Shōtoku from 764 until her death. Her favour for the monk Dōkyō, whom she made Dharma King and whom an oracle reportedly proposed as emperor, provoked a crisis at court; the oracle of Usa Hachiman was eventually reported against it.

After the suppression of the rebellion of Fujiwara no Nakamaro in 764, she vowed to have a million miniature pagodas made, each containing a printed Buddhist charm, and distributed them to ten great temples. The episode shows how fully Buddhism had been woven into Nara statecraft; after her reign the court took care to keep monks away from power.

## Key texts & works

### Hyakumantō Darani (One Million Pagoda Charms) (764–770)

[![Hyakumantō Darani (One Million Pagoda Charms)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/43/Hyakumant%C5%8D_Darani.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hyakumant%C5%8D_Darani.jpg)

<sub>A Hyakumantō pagoda and its printed dhāraṇī Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hyakumant%C5%8D_Darani.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 764–770
- **Type:** Printed dhāraṇī charms in miniature pagodas
- **Where written / held:** Ten great temples of Nara; about 40,000 pagodas survive at Hōryū-ji
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Hyakumant%C5%8D%20Darani%20%28One%20Million%20Pagoda%20Charms%29%20Empress%20K%C5%8Dken%20/%20Sh%C5%8Dtoku)

After the defeat of Fujiwara no Nakamaro's rebellion in 764, Empress Shōtoku vowed to make a million pagodas; according to the Shoku Nihongi they were completed in 770. Each three-storey pagoda, turned on a lathe and about 21 centimetres high, contains a small paper roll printed with one of four dhāraṇī from the Sutra of the Great Dhāraṇī of Stainless Pure Light, a text promising long life and the removal of sins. Whether they were printed from wood or metal plates is debated. The pagodas were distributed to ten temples; Hōryū-ji kept many thousands, some of which were sold in the Meiji period and are now in museums worldwide. They show the protective, ritual purpose of early Buddhist printing.
