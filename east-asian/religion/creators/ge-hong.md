## Biography

Ge Hong came from a southern gentry family with a tradition of occult learning; his great-uncle Ge Xuan was a renowned adept. After service as a military officer in the civil wars that followed the fall of the Western Jin, he devoted himself to study, writing and alchemy, and late in life sought a posting in the far south to obtain cinnabar. He died on Mount Luofu near Guangzhou.

His Baopuzi argues that immortality can be attained by study and effort, reviews methods from breathing and diet to talismans, and gives the most detailed early Chinese account of elixir alchemy, while its outer chapters defend Confucian ethics. His Biographies of Divine Immortals collected the lives of transcendents. Ge Hong is also remembered as a physician; a fourth-century remedy in his handbook of emergency prescriptions guided Tu Youyou to the antimalarial artemisinin in the 1970s.

### Biographies of Divine Immortals (Shenxian zhuan) (early 4th century)

- **Date:** early 4th century
- **Type:** Hagiography
- **Where written / held:** Eastern Jin China; survives in later reconstructions
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Biographies%20of%20Divine%20Immortals%20%28Shenxian%20zhuan%29%20Ge%20Hong)

Ge Hong compiled these biographies to show by example that people had in fact become immortal. They tell of adepts who refined elixirs, mastered magical arts, healed, vanished from coffins or rose to Heaven in broad daylight, and include early accounts of Laozi, of Zhang Daoling as a Celestial Master, and of the goddess-like Magu. The original was lost and the versions now read were reconstructed from quotations, so their exact content is debated. The work became a model for later Daoist hagiography and a quarry for fiction and drama.

### The Master Who Embraces Simplicity (Baopuzi) (c. 317)

[![The Master Who Embraces Simplicity (Baopuzi)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/Laojunfu.gif)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Laojunfu.gif)

<sub>Talismans of Lord Lao for entering the mountains, from the Baopuzi Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Laojunfu.gif).</sub>

- **Date:** c. 317
- **Type:** Treatise on immortality and alchemy
- **Where written / held:** Jiankang (Nanjing), Eastern Jin
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20Master%20Who%20Embraces%20Simplicity%20%28Baopuzi%29%20Ge%20Hong)

The Baopuzi is in two parts. The twenty 'Inner Chapters' argue that transcendence is real and can be learned, and survey methods: breathing and gymnastics, diet and herbs, sexual techniques, talismans for entering mountains, protection against demons, and above all the preparation of elixirs from cinnabar, gold and other minerals, which Ge Hong ranks above all other paths. The fifty 'Outer Chapters' are Confucian essays on government and morals. Ge stresses that moral merit is also necessary for long life. The book is a key source for the history of Chinese science and medicine as well as of Daoism, and was widely read by later adepts.
