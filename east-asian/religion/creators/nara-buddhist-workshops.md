# Asuka and Nara Buddhist workshops

*奈良仏所*

**7th century – 8th century** · Japanese  
Born: Nara · Died: Nara  
Traditions & eras: [Asuka and Nara Buddhism](../movements/nara-state-buddhism.md)

> The state and temple workshops of Asuka and Nara Japan that produced Buddhist images, paintings and textiles.

## Biography

In the seventh and eighth centuries Japanese temples and the court ran workshops, often staffed by immigrants from Korea and China and their descendants, which cast bronze buddhas, modelled clay and dry-lacquer statues, painted murals and wove large textiles. The government Office for the Construction of Tōdai-ji was the largest of them. The same collective entry appears in the art section.

Their works, including those preserved at Hōryū-ji, Tōdai-ji, Taima-dera and in the Shōsōin, are the earliest surviving monuments of Japanese Buddhism and often the best evidence for lost Tang Chinese styles.

## Key texts & works

### Taima Mandala (8th century (original); later copies)

[![Taima Mandala](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3f/Taima_Mandala%2C_14th_century%2C_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art%2C_57.156.6.jpg/1280px-Taima_Mandala%2C_14th_century%2C_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art%2C_57.156.6.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taima_Mandala%2C_14th_century%2C_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art%2C_57.156.6.jpg)

<sub>Painted copy of the Taima Mandala, 14th century (Metropolitan Museum of Art) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taima_Mandala%2C_14th_century%2C_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art%2C_57.156.6.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 8th century (original); later copies
- **Type:** Woven mandala of the Pure Land
- **Where written / held:** Taima-dera, Nara Prefecture; copies in many collections
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Taima%20Mandala%20Asuka%20and%20Nara%20Buddhist%20workshops)

The original Taima Mandala, about four metres square and now badly damaged, is a tapestry probably woven in Tang China or by immigrant weavers in Japan in the eighth century. Its centre shows Amitābha's paradise with palaces, ponds and bodhisattvas; its borders depict Queen Vaidehī's story and the sixteen contemplations, following Shandao's reading of the sutra. In the medieval legend of Chūjōhime, a princess wove it with the help of Amida in a single night in 763. From the thirteenth century Pure Land preachers, especially of the Seizan branch, circulated painted copies and used them in sermons, so it became one of the most reproduced images of Japanese Buddhism.
