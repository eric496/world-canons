# Hirata Atsutane

*平田篤胤*

Born: Kubota, Dewa Province · Died: Kubota, Dewa Province  
Traditions & eras: [Kami worship and Shinto](../movements/shinto.md)  

> Nativist scholar whose theology of the afterlife and the hidden world shaped Restoration Shinto and the Meiji religious settlement.

<sub>Portrait: Hirata Atsutane, self-portrait · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hirata_Atsutane_Jigazo.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Born to a samurai family in Akita, Hirata went to Edo, was adopted into the Hirata family, and in 1801 declared himself a posthumous disciple of Motoori Norinaga, whose kokugaku (national learning) is treated in the philosophy section. He lectured widely to samurai, merchants and village headmen and wrote voluminously; in 1841 the shogunate ordered him home to Akita, where he died.

Unlike Norinaga, Hirata gave Shinto a doctrine of the soul and the afterlife: the dead go to an unseen realm ruled by Ōkuninushi and remain near the living. He also studied Christian writings in Chinese, Daoism and folk beliefs. His thousands of disciples supported the Meiji Restoration and its campaign to separate kami from buddhas in 1868, though his followers were soon sidelined by the new state.

## Key texts & works

### The True Pillar of the Soul (Tama no mihashira) (1812)

[![The True Pillar of the Soul (Tama no mihashira)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9b/Tama-no-Mihashira.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tama-no-Mihashira.jpg)

<sub>Diagram from the Tama no mihashira Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tama-no-Mihashira.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 1812
- **Type:** Shinto cosmology and theology of the afterlife
- **Where written / held:** Edo
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=The%20True%20Pillar%20of%20the%20Soul%20%28Tama%20no%20mihashira%29%20Hirata%20Atsutane)

Using diagrams in the manner of Hattori Nakatsune's Sandaikō, Hirata describes the formation of heaven, earth and the underworld from the creative kami and argues that the dead do not go to the polluted land of Yomi, as Motoori Norinaga held, but remain in an unseen realm (yūmei) within this world, ruled by Ōkuninushi, close to their descendants. He presents this knowledge as the foundation of a steadfast Japanese spirit. The work was widely read among his disciples, helped shape Shinto funerals and ancestor rites, and influenced the religious policy of the early Meiji government.
