# Lu Xiujing

*陸修靜*

**406 – 477** · Chinese  
Born: Wuxing (Zhejiang) · Died: Jiankang (Nanjing)  
Traditions & eras: [Celestial Masters and early religious Daoism](../movements/early-daoism.md)  

> Daoist master of the Liu Song dynasty who edited the Lingbao scriptures, catalogued the first Daoist canon and reformed liturgy.

## Biography

Lu Xiujing came from an aristocratic southern family, left his household to pursue Daoism, and lived for years on Mount Lu. In 467 the Liu Song emperor Ming invited him to the capital Jiankang, where a monastery, the Chongxu guan, was built for him. In 471 he presented a catalogue of 1,228 scrolls of scriptures, medicines and talismans, the first attempt to define a Daoist canon, arranged in what became the Three Caverns.

Lu edited and authenticated the Lingbao scriptures, composed rituals of fasting and confession (zhai) that became the basis of later Daoist liturgy, and criticised the laxity he saw in Celestial Master communities, calling for proper registers, ranks and conduct. His work joined the southern revealed traditions with the older Celestial Master church and gave Daoism the institutional shape of a rival to Buddhism.

## Key texts & works

### Abridged Codes for the Daoist Community (Lu xiansheng daomen kelüe) (5th century)

- **Date:** 5th century
- **Type:** Daoist community rules
- **Where written / held:** Liu Song China; preserved in the Daoist Canon
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Abridged%20Codes%20for%20the%20Daoist%20Community%20%28Lu%20xiansheng%20daomen%20kel%C3%BCe%29%20Lu%20Xiujing)

In this short text Lu Xiujing describes how Celestial Master households should be organised under their libationers, register their members, gather at the three annual assemblies and keep their oratories, and complains that priests promote themselves, mix with popular cults and take fees. He insists on the covenant of orthodox unity, under which the gods require no payment and blood sacrifices are forbidden. Together with his liturgical works and canon catalogue, the codes show how fifth-century Daoism remade itself as an organised religion with clergy, rules and scriptures, partly in response to Buddhism.
