> The ritual specialists, mostly women, who perform the gut rituals of Korean shamanism and transmit its sacred narratives.

<sub>Portrait: Shin Yun-bok, A Shaman's Dance, from the Hyewon pungsokdo album, late 18th century · [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hyewon-Munyeosinmu.jpg)</sub>

## Biography

Mudang are of two broad kinds: initiated shamans, mostly in the north and centre, who become possessed after an illness interpreted as a call from the spirits, and hereditary shamans in the south, who perform without possession. They learn songs, dances, costumes and the order of ritual from senior shamans, and work for clients in homes, shrines and on the shore. Male shamans (baksu) also exist.

Joseon officials taxed, regulated and periodically expelled shamans from the capital, and colonial and post-war modernisers campaigned against 'superstition', but shamanism remained widespread. Since the 1970s several rites have been designated as intangible cultural heritage and mudang have become respected performers as well as healers. Their oral narratives, such as that of Princess Bari, are a major part of Korean oral literature.

### Princess Bari (Barigongju) (oral tradition; recorded from the 1930s)

[![Princess Bari (Barigongju)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Spirit_of_a_Noble_Woman_%28Probably_Princess_Pari%29_and_Attendant_LACMA_M.2000.15.11.jpg/1280px-Spirit_of_a_Noble_Woman_%28Probably_Princess_Pari%29_and_Attendant_LACMA_M.2000.15.11.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spirit_of_a_Noble_Woman_%28Probably_Princess_Pari%29_and_Attendant_LACMA_M.2000.15.11.jpg)

<sub>Shaman painting of a noble woman, probably Princess Bari, with attendant (Los Angeles County Museum of Art) Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spirit_of_a_Noble_Woman_%28Probably_Princess_Pari%29_and_Attendant_LACMA_M.2000.15.11.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** oral tradition; recorded from the 1930s
- **Type:** Shamanic narrative song (muga)
- **Where written / held:** Sung in rituals for the dead across Korea
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Princess%20Bari%20%28Barigongju%29%20Korean%20shamans%20%28mudang%29)

Sung in the rituals that send the dead to the other world, such as the Seoul saenamgut, the narrative tells of a king who abandons his seventh daughter because he wanted a son. When he falls mortally ill, his other daughters refuse to help, but Bari, raised by an old couple, travels to the realm of the dead, serves a guardian for years, marries him and bears sons, and brings back the water of life that revives her father. Refusing worldly reward, she becomes the deity who guides souls and the ancestress of shamans. Recorded in over a hundred versions, it is a major work of Korean oral literature and has inspired modern novels and plays.

### Shamanic deity paintings (musindo) (18th–20th century)

[![Shamanic deity paintings (musindo)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Mansindo_%28%E8%90%AC%E7%A5%9E%E5%9C%96%29%2C_early_19th-c._Korean_shamanic_painting.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mansindo_%28%E8%90%AC%E7%A5%9E%E5%9C%96%29%2C_early_19th-c._Korean_shamanic_painting.jpg)

<sub>Mansindo, painting of the shamanic pantheon, early 19th century Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mansindo_%28%E8%90%AC%E7%A5%9E%E5%9C%96%29%2C_early_19th-c._Korean_shamanic_painting.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** 18th–20th century
- **Type:** Ritual paintings
- **Where written / held:** Shaman shrines; collections such as the Gahoe Museum, Seoul, and Musée Guimet, Paris
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Shamanic%20deity%20paintings%20%28musindo%29%20Korean%20shamans%20%28mudang%29)

A shaman's shrine (sindang) is hung with paintings of the deities she serves: the Mountain God with his tiger, the Heavenly King Jeseok, the Seven Stars of the Big Dipper, generals such as Choe Yeong, the Dragon King, the Smallpox spirit and the shaman's own ancestors. Painted by professional or amateur artists and sometimes by shamans, they are consecrated and believed to house the gods. Many were destroyed in campaigns against superstition or burned when a shaman died, so most surviving ones date from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The early nineteenth-century Mansindo, 'picture of ten thousand gods', shows the whole pantheon at once.

### Jeju Chilmeoridang Yeongdeunggut (traditional; 2nd month of the lunar calendar)

[![Jeju Chilmeoridang Yeongdeunggut](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5c/Korea-culture-gut-jeju.folk.nature.museum.jpg/1280px-Korea-culture-gut-jeju.folk.nature.museum.jpg)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Korea-culture-gut-jeju.folk.nature.museum.jpg)

<sub>Museum display of a Jeju shamanic ritual, Jeju Folklore and Natural History Museum Image: Wikimedia Commons. [Full resolution, credit and licence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Korea-culture-gut-jeju.folk.nature.museum.jpg).</sub>

- **Date:** traditional; 2nd month of the lunar calendar
- **Type:** Shamanic ritual
- **Where written / held:** Chilmeoridang shrine, Jeju City
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Jeju%20Chilmeoridang%20Yeongdeunggut%20Korean%20shamans%20%28mudang%29)

Each year in the second lunar month the people of Jeju's fishing villages welcome the wind goddess Yeongdeung, who visits the island bringing seeds of seaweed and shellfish, and send her off two weeks later. At the Chilmeoridang shrine shamans conduct the welcoming rite, prayers for the sea and divination with millet, and launch a small straw boat to carry the goddess away. The rite is closely tied to the haenyeo, the island's women divers. Designated a national intangible heritage in 1980, it was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List in 2009.
