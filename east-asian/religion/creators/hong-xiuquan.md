## Biography

Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka from near Guangzhou, failed the civil examinations repeatedly. After a breakdown in 1837 he had visions of a heavenly father and elder brother who charged him to destroy demons, which he later interpreted through Liang Fa's Christian tracts as God and Jesus. With Feng Yunshan he founded the God Worshipping Society in Guangxi, which rose in 1850.

Proclaiming the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom in 1851, his forces took Nanjing in 1853 and held much of the Yangzi valley, enforcing iconoclasm, sexual segregation and an idiosyncratic Bible. The civil war against the Qing, among the deadliest in history with perhaps 20 million or more dead, ended with Hong's death and the fall of Nanjing in 1864. Historians debate whether to see Taiping religion as a Chinese form of Christianity or as a new religion.

## Key texts & works

### Taiping Imperial Declarations (Taiping zhaoshu) (c. 1852)

- **Date:** c. 1852
- **Type:** Religious proclamations
- **Where written / held:** Taiping Heavenly Kingdom (printed in Yongan and Nanjing)
- **Read more:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?search=Taiping%20Imperial%20Declarations%20%28Taiping%20zhaoshu%29%20Hong%20Xiuquan)

The Taiping zhaoshu gathers texts that Hong wrote in the 1840s, such as the 'Ode on the Original Way for Saving the World', in which he combines the worship of one God, called Shangdi as in Ricci's usage, with Confucian moral teaching and an account of an ancient China that had worshipped God before idolatry. Later Taiping publications, printed in Nanjing, included a revised Bible, the Ten Commandments and the account of Hong's vision of 1837. Most were destroyed after 1864 and survive chiefly in copies sent abroad by Western observers. They are the basic sources for the religious ideology of the largest civil war of the nineteenth century.
